When Workers, Driving Customers or Saving Lives in the ER, Become Independent Contractors

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Boston DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-15T20:41:19+00:00

[[{“value”:” By: Matt Wolfinger BOSTON – There’s a loophole in our labor laws spreading across the economy. From delivery drivers to nurses, corporations are classifying workers as independent contractors and using the promise of flexible work to cudgel labor rights. Nearly 12 million people in the United States, or seven percent of the workforce, describe independent contractor work as their primary source of income, according to the most recent data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Independent contractors aren’t protected under the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA), so they can’t unionize, aren’t entitled to benefits, a minimum wage, or overtime and can be fired at will. Companies like Uber, Lyft and Amazon have spent hundreds of millions to keep their workers classified as self-employed independent contractors, allowing them to cut costs and indirectly union-bust. By blurring the line between employee and contractor, employers save money they’d otherwise be required to spend on unemployment insurance, benefits and employer-paid payroll taxes. Meanwhile, workers lcassified as contractors then pay exorbitantly higher taxation to account for both employee and employer contributions. If corporations can successfully “gigify” their industries, they can circumvent responsibilities and laws that would otherwise prevent them from mistreating their workers. 1099 tax filing, the paperwork for independent contractors (Working Mass) Uber, Lyft and the Legacy of Gigification Delivery and rideshare apps like Doordash, Lyft and Uber are poster children for operating in this gray area. Emerging in the shadow of the Great Recession when jobs were hard to come by, companies like Uber, Lyft and the now-defunct Sidecar pioneered the modern understanding of gig work by taking advantage of the dire economic landscape. In Massachusetts, most workers are considered employees unless a company can prove their work meets three different criteria. According to Massachusetts Attorney General Andrea Joy Campbell’s office, the work must be: Done without the direction and control of the employer Performed outside the usual course of the employer’s business Done by someone who has their own, independent business or trade doing that kind of work Companies constantly find workarounds. In one landmark case, Massachusetts residents voted in late 2024 to pass a ballot measure affording rideshare drivers the right to unionize. Notably, neither Uber or Lyft spent a cent to oppose it. In contrast, they paid more than $200 million in California to help pass Proposition 22, a bill cementing California gig workers’ misclassification as independent contractors. The reason why these companies did not perceive Question 3 as a threat became clear in May, when over 70,000 Lyft and Uber in Massachusetts drivers successfully voted to certify their App Drivers Union (ADU). As the first union of its kind in the country, it’s a big deal—but there’s a catch: The union ceded on classification. Their official stance is that they’ve chosen to remain independent contractors. This limits the union’s ability to advocate for its members, since benefits like healthcare or a minimum wage are off the bargaining table. As Working Mass’s Editorial Board reported at the time, Question 3 “allowed drivers to vote for a collective bargaining representative, with which Uber and Lyft would then have to negotiate over wages and working conditions… [but only covers] app workers transporting passengers, leaving out take-out and grocery delivery workers.” As a result, “the system as written allows drivers to vote for or against collective bargaining but does not mandate any driver rights within the “union” once it is recognized.” This has led to credible accusations against the ADU of company unionism. The ADU’s FAQ page on their website states that remaining independent contractors allows members to “keep our flexibility and independence,” since bosses cannot legally dictate when independent contractors work and do not work. The ADU did not respond to requests for comment on how they reached this decision. App Drivers Union (ADU) marching in Labor Day 2026 (Working Mass) The flexibility messaging is reminiscent of a June 2026 report from the Flex Association, a national association for app-based work, that found that nearly two-thirds (63 percent) of the 10,000+ app-based workers they surveyed are doing gig work to supplement W-2 jobs. Flex’s 2024 Economic Impact Report found that “exactly half of driver and delivery partners would rather be able to choose their own hours, than receive a 50% increase in earnings with a fixed schedule.” While these studies seem to indicate gig workers would largely rather stay as independent contractors and prefer lower pay, there’s two fundamental flaws underpinning their results. First, drivers could advocate for having flexibility to choose their own hours and an increase in pay. There could also be an industry association for app-based work whose members include Uber, Lyft, Instacart, Doordash and Grubhub, Flex is incentivized to only share positive viewpoints on gig work. In reality, drivers aren’t a monolith—some have become disillusioned with the promises made to them by rideshare companies once the curtain is pulled back and their exploitation becomes indisputable. In July, a Massachusetts Uber driver filed a class action lawsuit against the company, alleging misclassification of its drivers as independent contractors. Uber uses this misclassification to avoid covering expenses like gas, insurance, and vehicle maintenance, since they were the responsibility of their “self-employed” contractor. It’s argued that the three qualifications in Massachusetts for independent contractor status aren’t met because: Uber controls the routes and pay of their drivers while requiring them to meet a set of standards dictated by the company, so the work is not done “without direction or control of the employer.” The services provided are not “performed outside the usual course of the employer’s business” (Uber is a company providing transportation to riders, which is fulfilled through the labor of their drivers). Drivers do not have “their own, independent business or trade doing that kind of work” as Uber sets pay rates, can change them at their sole discretion and requires a set of standards its drivers and vehicles must meet (and can change those at will). Food delivery and rideshare companies want to straddle the fence on employment, controlling every aspect of how drivers work through algorithms like employees, while stifling their ability to fully advocate for the same rights and benefits afforded to employees in Massachusetts. In 2024, More Perfect Union and Rideshare Guy put seven rideshare drivers in a room to see what fares they would be offered for identical trips and found that both Uber and Lyft’s algorithms routinely offered different prices to each driver. Uber’s average wage gap difference was roughly 12 cents. “You know, to the naked eye, it may not be as much,” driver and Senior Contributor to Rideshare Guy Sergio Avedian told More Perfect Union. However, “A penny’s difference on 2.7 billion trips that Uber does every quarter is the idea.” Nurses on strike during the lockout of July 2026 (Working Mass) The Gig Economy Taking Healthcare Executives in other industries have observed the chokehold platforms like Uber and Lyft have on their workers once they cannot do their job without it. Taking those lessons to heart, industries like healthcare have spent millions building their own. When nurses at Brigham and Women’s Hospital (BWH) with Brigham Nurses United (BNU) went on a one-day strike in July, the hospital locked them out for 4 additional days as retaliation, hiring 1,300 scab nurses to fill in for the 4,700 nurses on strike. Many of the scabs came from out-of-state, sourced from Colorado-based US Nursing (USN) who specialize in hiring nurses to scab during ongoing strikes. Similar to Amazon, they’ve begun outsourcing the labor to third-parties. Their website hosts a portal where nurses can apply to fill shifts at hospitals where “Potential Strikes” loom. The nurses taking over these positions are paid comparable wages than the nurses they’re replacing, but their lack of hands-on knowledge that comes from working full time at a hospital can place patients at an increased risk of harm. The job listings on “gig nursing” apps like Ingenovis Health’s often don’t list the facility in question, but anyone can make an account—no proof of credentials needed. Select “Strike Jobs” and set the location to Boston, MA to find nearly 300 USN listings, many of them starting October 11, for 2-week strike coverage at a hospital with a bed count of 846 and 59,715 emergency room visits. Two screenshots from the Ingenovis Health app listing an ICU RN position (start date October 11) and the facility details, captured October 8 (Working Mass). According to the American Hospital Directory, BWH has a bed count of 846, and their ED’s About page says they “care for nearly 60,000 patients each year.” The listing has since changed from an “Active Strike” to “Potential Strike” with a date of “TBA” after the MNA announced the strike’s postponement. 93 percent of the 4,000 workers with BNU recently voted in favor of an open-ended, indefinite strike, originally slated to start October 14. The decision came after their historic strike in July had yet to yield results at the bargaining table, with negotiations between the MNA and MGB leadership at a standstill. The strike has since been postponed pending a vote on what MGB calls its “last, best and final offer,” though the MNA has emphasized that MGB refuses to budge on many key issues or engage in more bargaining sessions. “Nurses continue seeking improvements on several key issues,” said an MNA spokesperson in a statement , including: … Limits on MGB’s reliance on temporary nurses to support continuity and quality of care; affordable health insurance with meaningful plan choice; a differential for nurses required to float among units; and a meaningful cost-of-living wage increase. Another strike is still on table if a contract fails to be ratified, even as MGB planned to once again work with USN to hire scabs for the strike. Since USN pays for each scab’s hotel and travel costs, they’re not classified as independent contractors. However, other gig nursing apps like CareRev have come under fire for allegedly misclassifying users as independent contractors. These platforms allegedly use a form of dynamic pricing to pay each nurse that looks at a listing an algorithmically generated pay rate, a difference of cents or even dollars apart. It’s unclear how these gig nursing algorithms function, but it’s eerily similar to the ones used by Uber and Lyft. The “gigification” of healthcare has earned gig nursing platforms the name “Uber for Nurses.” Central to the argument for gig nursing is in response to a so-called “nursing shortage.” But as recent data analysis from Time Magazine revealed , “there are substantively more actively licensed nurses than there are nursing jobs.” Framing the issue as a shortage obscures the real culprit—nurses are refusing to tolerate worsening working conditions. Dr. Katie Wells, a Senior Fellow at the AI Now Institute researching tech in healthcare, spoke to Working Mass : When I dug into the history of that, and I looked at all the scholars who had been studying labor markets for nurses, it became very clear that the rise of corporate medicine and the for-profit nature of a lot of hospital systems coincided with the degradation of working conditions for healthcare workers. Dr. Wells is the co-author of a two-part “Uber for Nursing” study detailing the rise of gig work in the healthcare industry. Although these platforms can be used to supply facilities with scabs, many are hired to account for lower levels of staffing. These platforms pit nurses against each other in a race to the bottom, where the nurse that wins a shift is the one willing to accept the lowest pay rate. Data the platform collects on nurses, such as credit card debt, helps it identify which nurses are more likely to accept lower pay. A portion of the travel nurses using these apps are classified as independent contractors. They don’t receive health benefits, despite providing others with healthcare themselves. These gig nursing platforms have been pushing for legislation that would classify the nurses who use their platforms as independent contractors. They’d be defined as different entities than existing healthcare staffing agencies, as AI Now’s study explains: “These platforms are trying to convince policymakers that their business model is not that of a healthcare staffing agency, and that they instead should be recognized as a ‘healthcare worker platform’ or a ‘healthcare technology platform.’” The language is similar to arguments made by Uber to Massachusetts legislators in 2024—that they’re closer to a “travel agency” than a transportation company—giving them the right to keep their drivers misclassified (a European court saw through this argument). That defense was part of a case brought against Uber and Lyft by then-Attorney General Maura Healey, who alleged they were misclassifying their drivers. The Classification Fight for Worker’s Rights In exchange for a $175 million settlement with Uber and Lyft, current AG Andrea Joy Campbell dropped the case before the court could rule whether rideshare drivers count as independent contractors. Campbell’s deal forced Uber and Lyft to abandon a similar ballot initiative to Prop. 22 in Massachusetts and afford drivers a few benefits. One of those benefits, which seemingly required the companies to pay drivers $32.50/hour, falls apart after reading the fine print . That pay rate only applies to “active hours,” meaning it counts the time when drivers are either on their way to pick up a rider or taking them to their destination, but not the time in-between, referred to as “online working hours.” “Time spent between trips, waiting for another ride request, or deadheading back from low-demand drop-off areas is neither compensated, nor counts towards the work time thresholds to qualify for sick time, health insurance stipends, and other benefits,” reporting from Forbes explains. Drivers have attested online that “staying busy the entire hour is impossible due to the oversaturation of drivers.” By comparing active time to online working hours, Forbes calculated the utilization rate (the percentage of time drivers actually get paid for): For example, if Uber pays $8 for a 12-minute trip, the driver’s pay rate per active hour would be $40 – above the mandated minimum. But if this is the only offer a driver receives and accepts over the next 48 minutes, the driver’s utilization rate would be 12/60= 20% (low!) and the gross pay per working hour would only be $8 (awful!). So, utilization rate is a critical determinant of driver earnings. This doesn’t include the burden on drivers of paying for gas and insurance, taking another chunk out of their pay. With employee status, drivers could bargain for a contract to cement a firm minimum wage. To their credit, Campbell’s office has kept their finger on the pulse of independent contractor misclassification. The AGO’s 2026 Labor day report released by its Fair Labor Division shows that in the last year, 1,500 workers across 60 companies were misclassified, leading to more than $2.1 million in penalties and restitution. “When workers are misclassified as independent contractors instead of employees, they are denied basic workplace rights, including guaranteed minimum wage, overtime pay, timely compensation, accrued sick leave, and more,” Campbell’s office stated in an emailed statement to Working Mass. “Addressing this harmful practice and ensuring that every worker receives the protections they deserve is one of my top priorities.” But until a firm decision is reached on how to classify rideshare and delivery drivers, a comprehensive answer on gig workers’ rights and the next steps organized labor will need to take will remain in limbo. There aren’t signs that federal reclassification is on the docket anytime soon, making the state level (for now) the best way forward. Although Campbell’s settlement took a similar bill to Prop. 22 off the ballot, gig work platforms have proven they’ll spend millions to tip the scales in their favor. Gig nursing platforms aren’t introducing legislation in Massachusetts that would reclassify their business like they have in other states, but that could always change. The conversation surrounding independent contracting finds itself in a sticky spot: many gig-workers prefer the flexibility provided by the work, and use it to supplement their income when their W-2 pay doesn’t cut it. Flexibility is attractive to workers, Dr. Wells explained, due to a lack of stable, guaranteed hours and a set schedule across the labor market. Knowing their schedules ahead of time gives workers more autonomy—scheduling a doctor’s visit or picking a child up from school becomes much easier. “I think the reality is that there is a problem with existing labor markets in so many of these sectors and, at large, that has real purchase,” said Dr. Wells. “It has so many things at play that it means people are willing to trade all kinds of protections for the allure or the possibility of flexible schedules.” The national minimum wage has sat at $7.25 since 2009, and the lack of universal healthcare is smothering everyday Americans in medical debt. The leading reported cause of bankruptcy in the U.S. is medical debt , and we’re the only major country without a form of universal healthcare. A long-term pathway to ending the exploitative nature of gig work and doing away with the independent contractor classification needs to come alongside ensuring workers don’t need to take on extra work to afford basic necessities, and without the threat of medical or student debt held over their heads. In a world where everyone is guaranteed a living wage and health benefits aren’t up to the whims of your employer, gig workers could stick with their 9 to 5 or even keep driving if that’s what they’d prefer. It may sound like a pipe dream, but the system as it stands puts the working class at a severe disadvantage. Drivers can choose to pick their main job that now pays them a living wage or stay a driver, earning a living wage and receiving the same health benefits that their other job afforded them. As companies celebrate record profits, the workers who generate that value see none of it reflected in their own paychecks. It’s undeniable that working as an independent contractor provides unmatched levels of autonomy, but that should not come at the cost of the benefits and workplace protections afforded as the bare minimum to every other worker in the country. “The rise of [gig nursing] is a symptom of really broken labor conditions,” Dr. Wells said. That’s how I try to think about it. And so when people say, ‘Well, let’s fix gig nursing,’ I think, ‘No, let’s fix the conditions that gave rise to gig nursing.’ Matt Wolfinger is a data journalist and a contributing writer to Working Mass. Why I Started Judging the Judges: The Election That Changed How I Vote

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Sharon Kyle ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-15T20:40:48+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. When I watched a tough-on-crime prosecutor reinvent himself before a room full of progressive voters, I realized how little we really knew about the people we were electing to the bench. That experience eventually led me to create LA Progressive's Judging the Judges series. Every election season, millions of Americans confront a familiar problem. They are asked to make decisions about candidates they know almost nothing about. Nowhere is this problem more apparent than in judicial elections. Most voters can tell you something about the candidates running for president, governor, or mayor. They may have heard them speak, watched them debate, or read about their positions on issues that matter to them. But when they reach the judicial contests on their ballots, the information often disappears. There are names. There are ballot designations. Occasionally, there are carefully crafted candidate statements. But there is precious little that tells voters how these individuals think, how they have exercised power, or what kind of judgment they bring to decisions that can alter people's lives. For years, I was one of those voters. I understood the importance of the judiciary. I understood that judges make consequential decisions about incarceration, family separation, housing, civil rights, and the application of the law. Yet when I looked at the judicial candidates on my ballot, I frequently knew almost nothing about them. Then something happened that changed my approach to judicial elections. Two Encounters With the Same Prosecutor More than a dozen years ago, I had been summoned for jury duty. During jury selection, I listened to a prosecutor whose presentation struck me as decidedly tough on crime. His tone and approach left an impression. Although I was not ultimately selected to serve on the jury, I remembered him. A short time later, I encountered that same prosecutor again. This time, he was running for a seat on the Los Angeles County Superior Court. He came to speak at my local Democratic club, an organization with a reputation for being a progressive bastion. What struck me was the contrast. The prosecutor I had observed during jury selection presented one face of himself in the courtroom. The judicial candidate standing before my fellow Democratic club members presented a decidedly different one. The courtroom prosecutor had emphasized a tough-on-crime approach. The candidate seeking the support of progressive voters sounded quite different. I couldn't ignore the disconnect. Perhaps both presentations reflected genuine aspects of his professional outlook. Prosecutors and judicial candidates operate in different settings, with different responsibilities. But the contrast raised a question I could not shake: How could voters know who they were really electing if they encountered candidates only in carefully managed campaign settings? My fellow club members had not necessarily seen what I had seen in that courtroom. They were evaluating the candidate based on his presentation to them. I had the unusual advantage of having observed him in a different professional setting. And it occurred to me that this was precisely the problem with judicial elections. Most voters never get to see candidates exercising the authority they already possess. They rarely observe them prosecuting cases, representing clients, ruling on motions, negotiating pleas, or interacting with the people whose lives are caught up in the justice system. Instead, voters encounter a campaign version of a professional whose record may be difficult to examine. That realization became the foundation of LA Progressive's Judging the Judges series. The Problem Isn't That Voters Don't Care I've been thinking a great deal about why Americans don't vote. Research shows that some nonvoters are discouraged by the available candidates, some believe their participation will make little difference, and others face practical barriers involving work, transportation, health, or family responsibilities. But there is another explanation that deserves greater attention: Sometimes people don't vote because they don't have enough information to make a responsible choice. Judicial elections illustrate that problem particularly well. When someone leaves a judicial contest blank, it's more likely than not that they don't know anything about the candidate. I understand that predicament because I experienced it myself. The distinction matters. If someone doesn't care about an election, persuading them to participate may require changing their sense of political engagement. But if someone wants to participate and lacks information, the immediate problem is different. They need access to credible, understandable research. That is a problem journalists can help address. Judicial Elections Demand More Than a Campaign Biography California's judicial elections are especially challenging. Superior Court judges serve six-year terms. Many first reach the bench through gubernatorial appointment, and uncontested incumbents generally do not appear on the ballot. When voters do face a contested judicial election, the candidates frequently have considerably lower public profiles than candidates for legislative or executive office. Yet the consequences of judicial decisions are anything but obscure. Judges decide whether people remain incarcerated while awaiting trial. They make rulings that can affect the admissibility of evidence, the conduct of criminal proceedings, child custody, civil disputes, and the protection of constitutional rights. Their work is not abstract. For someone facing the loss of freedom, housing, or family relationships, the person sitting on the bench can have an enormous impact. A judicial candidate's campaign presentation cannot tell us everything we need to know about how that person will exercise such authority. Neither can a résumé listing years of experience. Experience matters. Legal knowledge matters. Professional competence matters. But so do judicial temperament, fairness, impartiality, and a demonstrated commitment to applying the law faithfully. Those qualities deserve careful examination. Building Judging the Judges The experience with that prosecutor motivated me to begin researching judicial candidates rather than accepting the limited information placed before voters. That work developed into LA Progressive's Judging the Judges series. The series has appeared across multiple election cycles, with published installments dating back at least to 2008 and continuing through the 2026 elections. Over the years, our work has drawn on judicial candidate evaluations, public records, professional backgrounds, available interviews, and other sources that can help voters understand the people seeking seats on the bench. The goal has been to make judicial elections less mysterious. Research does not eliminate disagreement. Two informed people can review the same judicial candidate's record and reach different conclusions. And no amount of research can perfectly predict how someone will perform as a judge. But voters should not have to confront these elections armed with little more than a candidate's name and ballot designation. The public deserves accessible information about the professional experience, qualifications, and records of those seeking judicial authority. That belief continues to guide the series. What Voter Abstention Taught Me About Journalism The broader debate about voter participation has reinforced something I learned from that first experience. We often talk about voting as though the entire burden rests on the individual. Citizens are told that they have a responsibility to study the issues, research the candidates, and participate in elections. There is merit in that expectation. But what responsibility do our political institutions, news organizations, and civic associations bear for ensuring that meaningful information is available? Democracy asks citizens to exercise judgment. That judgment requires knowledge. And knowledge is not distributed equally. People with professional connections, legal expertise, flexible schedules, and access to political networks may have advantages in learning about candidates that other voters do not. Even someone who follows politics closely can struggle to find reliable information about judicial races. That information gap has consequences. When voters know little about the candidates, endorsements, ballot designations, name recognition, and campaign resources can assume greater importance. Those signals may provide useful information, but they are not substitutes for examining a candidate's professional record. This is also why I resist the easy characterization of nonvoters as apathetic. A voter who leaves a judicial race blank because the available information is inadequate has identified a real democratic problem. The answer cannot simply be to shame that person for not voting. We must also ask why such an important election presents such a difficult information challenge in the first place. From Frustration to Information I have never found a particularly convincing argument that simply staying home on Election Day will, by itself, improve our political system. I understand why people become frustrated. I understand why they lose confidence in candidates and institutions. And I understand why someone might refuse to cast a vote in a race where they cannot make an informed choice. But refusing to participate does not automatically produce a more accountable political system. Nor does casting an uninformed vote necessarily improve democracy. What I learned from my encounters with that prosecutor was that there is another response to the problem: investigate. Ask questions. Look beyond the campaign presentation. Examine the available record. Compare what candidates say with what they have actually done. Make the information accessible to others. That is what Judging the Judges grew out of. I did not begin researching judicial candidates because I believed I had all the answers. I began because I realized how much I didn't know. And because I had witnessed firsthand how differently the same candidate could present himself in a courtroom and before a room of progressive voters. That experience transformed a personal frustration into an ongoing publishing project. Years later, the lesson remains just as important. A democracy cannot reasonably expect informed participation while leaving voters uninformed about the people asking to exercise power over their lives. If we want to understand why people don't vote, we should certainly listen to their explanations. But we should also examine what we are asking them to do—and whether we have given them the tools to do it responsibly. For me, that inquiry began with jury duty, a prosecutor, and an unexpected second encounter at a Democratic club. It continues with every edition of Judging the Judges . Turkey Risks Being Drawn Into Saudi Arabia’s War in Yemen

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Oğul Tuna ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-15T20:02:16+00:00

There are few places outside Turkey that occupy such a mournful place in the country’s popular memory as Yemen. For generations, Turks who knew little about the politics of the Arabian Peninsula nevertheless knew Yemen through folk songs. They were ballads about young men sent from Anatolia to fight in Yemen, many of whom never returned. They told of mothers waiting for sons, women waiting for husbands, and a distant land associated above all with death. One of the most haunting was recorded by Ruhi Su, the great communist interpreter of Turkish folk music. Its refrain addresses the country with a bitter grandeur: Yemen, Yemen, glorious Yemen, Yemen, Yemen, your soil soaked in blood. The glory belonged to the empire. The dead belonged overwhelmingly to ordinary families. More than a century after the Ottoman withdrawal from Yemen, this old history has acquired an unexpected relevance. Turkey recently signed a new defense pact with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan. As the regional war has spread to Saudi territory, Ankara has condemned Houthi attacks and has already begun providing Saudi Arabia with mainly defensive and technical support . As the fighting has escalated, the alliance has moved from planning toward implementation, while Turkish officials have begun openly discussing what further assistance Riyadh might require. Turkey is not at war in Yemen. But it has taken the first steps toward assuming obligations in a conflict that is not its own. The Turkish government and its allies would do well to remember one thing: Turks have been down this road before. The Empire Goes South The Ottoman presence in Yemen long predated the nineteenth century. But after the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 transformed the strategic importance of the Red Sea, Istanbul made a renewed effort to consolidate imperial control over the region, as a coalition of local tribes revolted in the 1870s. Among the officers sent south was Ahmed Muhtar Pasha. He would later become one of the Ottoman Empire’s most celebrated generals, particularly for his campaigns against the Russian army in 1877. But before earning fame on the Caucasian front, Muhtar Pasha served in Yemen, where Ottoman forces sought to extend Constantinople’s authority over territory that existed much more convincingly on imperial maps than it did on the ground. His memoirs describe the difficulties of campaigning through Yemen’s mountains and dealing with a political landscape structured by tribal loyalties, religious authority, local rivalries, and an unforgiving geography. Yes, the Ottoman army could capture towns. That did not mean the Ottoman state controlled the countryside around them. Military victories repeatedly failed to settle political questions. Rebellion was suppressed only to soon flare up again. More troops were dispatched. More money was spent. More men died. And the soldiers who bore this burden were generally not the men who had decided that Yemen was indispensable to the security of the empire. They were conscripts. That distinction, between those who define the strategic necessity of a war and those who actually pay for it, survived the Ottoman Empire itself. The generals and ministers disappeared. The popular memory remained, hence the songs. Saudi Arabia Learns the Same Lesson A century later, a vastly wealthier and technologically superior power encountered a variation of the same problem. When Saudi Arabia launched its military intervention in Yemen in 2015, there was little apparent symmetry between the two sides. Riyadh and its allies possessed modern aircraft, precision weapons, enormous financial resources, and crucial backing from the United States and other Western powers. Yet years of bombing failed to eliminate Ansar Allah, better known as the Houthis. The consequences for Yemen were catastrophic. Saudi Arabia’s US-backed campaign devastated civilian infrastructure and contributed to one of the world’s worst humanitarian crises. American and European arms manufacturers, meanwhile, profited from the weapons flowing toward the coalition. The Houthis have not only survived a decade of war but developed greater military capabilities than they began with. None of this makes Ansar Allah a progressive force. It remains, however, among the very few forces willing to confront American and Israeli military power directly — not merely rhetorically — and, in doing so, to challenge the US-led regional order. But there is equally little reason to accept the fantasy that military superiority allows an outside power to rearrange Yemen according to its wishes. The Ottomans learned this the hard way, and so did the Saudis after 2015. Turkey now risks stepping into the same political geography. A New Alliance On August 7, Turkey, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan signed the Mecca Joint Defense Agreement, announcing that an armed attack against one member would be considered an attack against all three. Hakan Fidan compared the pledge to the North Atlantic Treaty Organization’s (NATO) Article 5. But in his account, assistance would follow a request from the threatened member and decisions by the relevant institutions; it would not automatically mean sending troops. He listed intelligence, logistics, ammunition, and potentially military units. Even Article 5 leaves NATO members discretion over how to assist an attacked ally. Ankara has made a commitment, but it still has choices. The pact also envisages cooperation before a crisis. On August 8, Fidan described plans for joint training and exercises, intelligence sharing, coordinated threat assessments, and cooperation in arms production. The aim is to make the three armed forces capable of operating together, with arrangements in place before a member calls for help. Some machinery is already taking shape. A committee of the three countries’ foreign and defense ministers and military chiefs met on August 31; a secretariat is planned in Riyadh. On September 19, Fidan said preparations for it and an associated military headquarters were nearly complete, but decisions on staffing and operations still lay in the future. NATO is the model, but this alliance is still being built. On September 18, the Financial Times reported that Turkish parliamentary ratification was still pending. Events have since moved considerably faster. On October 5, the three governments agreed to activate collective deterrence measures and prepare for the rapid deployment of military forces and capabilities in support of Saudi Arabia. Two days later, Turkish officials confirmed that Ankara had begun providing mainly defensive and technical assistance. Fidan has ruled out Turkish participation in offensive operations inside Yemen, but Ankara is considering air defense, electronic warfare, and other forms of military support. Recep Tayyip Erdoğan has meanwhile said that the agreement will be submitted to parliament for ratification. Ankara insists the pact is defensive. But whose regional order might it defend? Iran’s setbacks in Syria and Lebanon have opened space for rival powers. Donald Trump has publicly welcomed the agreement. One possible outcome is, in effect, a division of labor: Israel attacks Iran and its allies, while US-aligned regional powers expand their influence at Tehran’s expense. Their rivalry with Israel could coexist with policies that reinforce an order favorable to Washington. The NATO analogy would then extend to the consolidation of a US-led order beyond the promise of mutual defense. The pact’s immediate test, however, is in Yemen. Renewed Houthi attacks on Saudi territory and Riyadh’s military response are now testing what its promises mean in practice. The pressure is, however, no longer coming from Riyadh alone. On October 13, Trump publicly suggested that he might ask Erdoğan to help Saudi Arabia against the Houthis. Turkey is thus confronted not with an abstract treaty provision but with the possibility that its new ally might request assistance during an actual war. On September 19, Fidan addressed precisely that possibility. He condemned attacks against Saudi sovereignty and territorial integrity and said that Riyadh might have military requirements, particularly of a technical nature. Under the new agreement, he indicated, Turkey would have no problem meeting such needs. “Technical” no longer describes merely a hypothetical form of assistance. Turkey has already begun providing mainly defensive and technical support to Saudi Arabia. Intelligence and logistical support can help sustain a military campaign without a single Turkish battalion entering Yemen. Deeper involvement would still require further decisions. But each commitment can give Ankara another reason to make the next one. Whose War Is It? There is a deeper contradiction in Ankara’s position. Fidan has simultaneously argued that Saudi Arabia should not be dragged into the confrontation between the United States and Iran. Turkey has called for diplomacy and attempted to present itself as an actor capable of communicating with the different sides. But if Saudi Arabia should not be dragged into someone else’s war, the same question must surely be asked of Turkey. The present regional conflagration was not initiated by an attack on Turkey. Turkish territory is not under Houthi occupation. The Turkish people have no dispute with the people of Yemen. The current regional escalation has grown out of the US-Israeli confrontation with Iran and the network of alliances surrounding it. Ansar Allah has positioned itself firmly on Tehran’s side of that struggle. Saudi Arabia, willingly or not, has become another arena in it. Now the pact gives Riyadh a basis for seeking Turkish help and Ankara a reason to treat Saudi security as its own. That does not make intervention inevitable. It makes refusing further involvement a political choice that the language of alliance solidarity can make harder to defend. The Turkish government may believe that its increasingly close military relationship with Riyadh increases Ankara’s regional influence. Turkey’s defense industry may also find new opportunities in Saudi demand for drones, missiles, electronic warfare systems, and other military technology. But what appears from Ankara as strategic influence looks rather different from below. There is no single “regional interest” at stake here. The Saudi monarchy and the Turkish state are pursuing their own interests, as are Washington and Israel in their confrontation with Iran, and Ansar Allah and Tehran on the other side. Defense contractors have interests of a more profitable kind. The people who die in these struggles rarely have much say in defining any of them. The Price of “National Interest” This is where the Ottoman experience matters. Not because Erdoğan is some latter-day sultan, as he is still too often caricatured in the Western press. Nor because modern Turkey is the Ottoman Empire, or because every Turkish move in the region can be explained away as “neo-Ottomanism.” And not because twenty-first-century Yemen is identical to the Yemen encountered by Ahmed Muhtar Pasha. Such analogies explain very little. The continuity lies elsewhere. States have always been extraordinarily good at converting the interests of rulers, strategic establishments, and economic elites into something called the “national interest.” Once that transformation has been accomplished, ordinary people can be asked to sacrifice themselves for objectives they had no role in determining. Ottoman officials had perfectly sophisticated strategic arguments for holding Yemen. Control of the Red Sea mattered. Rival European empires mattered. The security of the holy cities mattered. Imperial prestige mattered. None of these arguments made the dead conscript less dead. Today, the vocabulary has changed. Politicians and pundits say that regional security and alliance credibility and defense cooperation matter, or that Turkey’s influence in the Persian Gulf matters. Maritime trade matters. But before accepting these abstractions as reasons to go to war, there is a more elementary question: Who benefits, and who pays? Turkey’s rapidly expanding arms industry would certainly benefit from deeper military cooperation with the Gulf monarchies. Political elites may benefit from the prestige of projecting Turkish power beyond the country’s borders. The young soldier ordered to defend that projection will experience it differently. So will the Yemeni family living underneath the drones. Yemen, Yemen . . . The Ottoman Empire eventually disappeared from Yemen. The official case for holding Yemen has largely faded from popular memory. The officials who sent troops south are remembered mostly by specialists. Many of the battles mean almost nothing to modern Turks. Yet the songs survived. That should tell us something about how war enters popular memory. The state remembers campaigns, commanders, and victories. Ordinary people remember the son who did not return. Ruhi Su understood the difference. When he sang the old Yemen songs, he was not celebrating imperial strategy. His voice belonged to another tradition, one that preserved the suffering concealed beneath the language of glory. That is why an Ottoman general’s memoir and a communist folk singer unexpectedly belong in the same story. Ahmed Muhtar Pasha shows us the view from the campaign, while Ruhi Su gives us the view from home. Between them lies the history that Ankara should remember before “technical assistance” becomes something larger. Turkey has no need to return to Yemen. Its people have already paid for that lesson once before. Yemen, Yemen, glorious Yemen, Yemen, Yemen, your soil soaked in blood. Financial institutions manage client relationships through onboarding, verification, account servicing, reviews, and closure. Each stage creates tasks, decisions, records, and compliance obligations that require consistent handling. When teams manage these activities separately, information becomes fragmented, and delays increase. That is where a structured, end-to-end process adds the most value. Client lifecycle management connects the full relationship into one structured process, giving teams clearer ownership, better data, and stronger oversight. The approach becomes easier to understand by examining its main stages and practical value. Here is a closer look at what the process involves and the problems it helps solve. What Client Lifecycle Management Covers Client onboarding begins when a prospective client enters the organization’s pipeline. The process captures identity details, business information, ownership structures, risk factors, and required documentation during client lifecycle management. This structure connects onboarding with later verification, due diligence, monitoring, review, and closure tasks, so teams maintain a consistent record throughout the relationship. Know Your Customer checks and identity verification confirm who the client is. Due diligence then assesses factors such as ownership, location, products requested, and expected activity. The depth of review depends on the client’s risk profile and applicable compliance requirements. After onboarding, the relationship requires ongoing attention. Teams update client information, conduct periodic reviews, monitor transactions, manage documentation, and respond to changes in ownership or risk. The lifecycle ends when the institution closes the relationship and records the required offboarding actions. A structured process connects these activities through shared information and defined workflows. Instead of asking different teams to collect the same details repeatedly, the institution reuses approved data across relevant tasks. That structure reduces avoidable handoffs and gives staff a clearer record of what happened, when it happened, and who approved it. Why It Matters A well-structured lifecycle process delivers measurable improvements in three key areas. Faster, More Consistent Onboarding Client onboarding often involves relationship managers, compliance specialists, operations teams, and clients. Without coordinated workflows, each group may request information through separate channels, creating delays and duplicate work. A lifecycle process assigns tasks based on client type, risk level, product, and jurisdiction. Straightforward cases follow a defined route, while higher-risk cases receive additional review. Teams spend less time sorting requests and more time resolving issues that require judgment. Consistency also improves the client experience. Clients receive clearer requests, fewer repeated questions, and better visibility into outstanding requirements. Better Compliance Control Compliance depends on accurate information, complete records, and timely reviews. A fragmented process makes it harder to confirm whether teams followed the right policy for each client. Lifecycle management places rules and approval steps inside operational workflows. The process records submitted documents, verification results, risk assessments, review dates, and decisions. Managers gain a clearer audit trail, while compliance teams can identify overdue actions before they become larger problems. This control matters because client risk changes after onboarding. A change in ownership, unusual transaction activity, or a new regulatory requirement can affect the relationship. The institution needs ongoing due diligence that supports action throughout the client’s tenure, not only during account opening. Lower Operating Costs Repeated data entry creates unnecessary work and increases the chance of errors. Staff members also lose time searching across email threads, spreadsheets, shared drives, and separate systems. A connected process gives approved client information a usable home. Integrations then move relevant data between systems without requiring manual copying at every step. Fewer handoffs reduce processing time and limit corrections caused by inconsistent records. The financial effect extends beyond labor. Better data quality reduces rework, supports faster decisions, and helps teams manage larger client volumes without matching increases in administrative effort. What a Strong Process Requires A useful lifecycle process starts with clear ownership. Every task should have an assigned team, a defined deadline, and an escalation route when information is missing or risk changes. Data standards matter just as much. Institutions need consistent client fields, document requirements, risk categories, and review triggers. Without shared standards, automation only moves inconsistent information faster. Integration is also necessary. Client lifecycle management works best when onboarding, identity verification, Know Your Customer checks, transaction monitoring, and account records exchange relevant data. Staff should see the information needed for a decision without rebuilding the client record manually. Finally, reporting should show more than completed tasks. Managers need visibility into bottlenecks, aging cases, exception volumes, review backlogs, and recurring documentation problems. These measures help teams correct process weaknesses with evidence rather than assumptions. Conclusion Client lifecycle management gives financial institutions a structured way to manage relationships after the initial application. It connects onboarding, verification, due diligence, monitoring, reviews, and offboarding through shared data and assigned workflows. The practical next step is to map every client touchpoint, identify repeated requests, and record where ownership becomes unclear. That review creates a concrete basis for improving process design, selecting supporting technology, and measuring better operational control.

Climate policies can provide immediate relief by focusing on cutting costs. Take electricity, for example, a popular focus in affordability conversations. Electricity costs have exploded in recent years because of the massive data center build-out, extreme weather, and foreign wars increasing already-volatile fossil fuel prices. As residential electricity rates have risen, more and more people have fallen into utility debt. One in six households are behind on their power bills. Meanwhile, utilities are working hand in glove with data center developers and tech companies, creating huge bill increases for everyday people. The federal government can create an Electricity Debt Cancellation Program , which would provide $20 billion in forgivable loans to utility companies conditioned on eliminating all residential ratepayer debt, reconnecting users, and submitting a concrete affordability plan that tackles the root causes of increased electricity prices, like the climate crisis and data center explosion. This would save people in the United States millions in utility bills and also show them that the federal government can and should intervene more explicitly in managing utilities to keep rates affordable and make electricity green and reliable during extreme weather events. Beyond financial instability, climate policy can also protect people from the threats to their survival exacerbated by the climate crisis. Extreme heat, hurricanes, wildfires, and severe storms are not just increasing costs for communities already facing a broader affordability crisis but damaging homes and infrastructure while endangering and displacing people. Federal responses to disaster is one salient part of this picture: instead of building out the public institutions that deliver security, like the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA), the Trump administration has been gutting the already under-resourced agency in order to divert funds for staffing and capacity to US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), even as FEMA was facing increased demands for emergency disaster response. The consequences are twofold: not only are ICE’s operations violating people’s rights, but FEMA is forced to help carry out deportations and immigration enforcement rather than disaster relief. Congress can alleviate these harms by reducing the ICE budget and redirecting resources to FEMA to better fund disaster response as the climate crisis intensifies. Redirecting federal investment from militarized police to disaster response would not only keep people safe from ICE’s intensely unpopular and violent enforcement tactics, but it would allow Department of Homeland Security funding to go to much-needed scaling up of the public institution responsible for protecting people facing hurricanes, wildfires, extreme heat, and flooding. As of June 2026, more than $100 billion was provided to ICE through budget reconciliation that has gone unused. Moving that money to be used by FEMA would demonstrate that safety can be built through state capacity and preparedness rather than coercive enforcement. A Right to Thrive Beyond bringing down the knock-on financial costs from the climate crisis and mitigating direct harm, immediate relief should also support the right to thrive in the fullest sense. Most working people are not only struggling to pay for electricity bills and home insurance premiums, they are suffering from lower quality of life overall. People are working more, making less, and lonelier than ever — and the climate crisis is making this worse. One way to fix this is through policies that work to adapt communities. The federal government can invest just $10 billion in public parks that would provide outdoor space for community interaction, increase flood absorbency, and offer heat relief in the summer months. Access to public works is not just important for people’s sense of quality of life; it can provide much-needed green infrastructure as cities and towns adapt to a changing climate. As the United States faces a dire cost-of-living crisis, affordability should be an anchoring priority to provide immediate relief to working people. Climate policy should be central to any agenda that provides this economic relief, and it can also go beyond cutting costs here and there to bringing immediate relief across the spectrum of everyday life. By acting quickly to move federal investments, Congress can show that government can accomplish the first steps of a capacious agenda that brings relief to millions of people who need it. Historian Eric Hobsbawm Was a “Communist Polymath”

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Emile Chabal ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-15T15:10:22+00:00

Eric Hobsbawm’s life began just as the “long nineteenth century” was drawing to a close. Born in Alexandria, Egypt, and formed as a youth in Red Vienna and Weimar-era Berlin, he came to intellectual maturity in interwar Cambridge and postwar London. His Ages tetralogy firmly established him on course syllabi and on the bookshelves of what he called, in The Age of Revolution , “the intelligent and educated citizen, who is not merely curious about the past, but wishes to understand how and why the world has come to be what it is today and whither it is going.” And he was, paradoxically for someone who rose to such popular heights, a proud communist who, for better or worse depending on whom you ask, bound himself to a particular vision of a better future as he bore witness to an epoch he dubbed the “age of extremes.” Yet despite being, as his biographer Emile Chabal puts it, a “communist polymath, with a remarkable reach over the theoretical and empirical landscape of modern history,” Hobsbawm, unlike many of his continental counterparts, “rarely features in the canon of left-wing or Marxist thought.” Why that is so is one of the questions animating Chabal’s fascinating and wide-ranging new intellectual history, The Age of Hobsbawm . Chabal is a professor of contemporary history at the University of Edinburgh, and the book’s publication brought him up to St Andrews, Scotland, for an author event at our local independent bookstore . It is a testament to Hobsbawm’s enduring appeal that even now, in a town dominated by golf enthusiasts and posh students, a crowd of unreconstructed Marxists from the 1970s, left-wing autodidacts, and curious graduate students turned up for a spirited discussion of the theory and politics of the twentieth-century left and their prospects today. What follows is an extension of our conversation that evening and an introduction to Hobsbawm’s world and legacy. Michael Schapira You mention in the preface that you initially had designs on “writing a history of postwar French Marxism, which would capture some of the richness and complexity of radical politics in France.” But you soon realized you could “achieve the same goals by looking closely at Hobsbawm.” Hobsbawm had strong connections to French intellectual life and Eurocommunism more generally, but both he and British Marxism have a very different flavor than their French variants. In what sense did Hobsbawm satisfy your initial aspirations, and how did shifting the focus to him open new avenues to explore radical postwar politics? Emile Chabal When I started working on Hobsbawm in late 2012, I was a freshly minted postdoc. I’d spent the previous five years working on contemporary French history and politics, so it was perhaps inevitable that French concepts like the “intellectual” and the Sartrean idea of engagement [in English, “commitment”] loomed so large in my mind. You are right though: postwar British Marxism has a very different flavor from its French (or continental) counterpart. It was less abstract, less ideological, and less overtly philosophical. What is interesting about Hobsbawm is that he straddled the British and continental European intellectual traditions much more than his other British Marxist contemporaries. As a student in the late 1930s, he was profoundly shaped by the peculiar environment of Cambridge, but he also saw himself as an integral part of a global communist movement with its base in Paris. After World War II, when he wanted to make his name as a historian, he continued to move between Britain and continental Europe in an effort to find useful interlocutors and mentors. As I learned more about Hobsbawm’s incessant back-and-forth between the UK and Europe, I was grateful for having been a scholar of France in my younger days; it gave me a unique perspective on his intellectual formation and identity. And it also helped me bridge the often rather sterile divide between the intellectual histories of radical politics in the English-speaking world and continental Europe. Michael Schapira Toward the end of his life, Hobsbawm returned to his formative years in Vienna and Weimar Berlin, framing his experience as that of a generational cohort whose worldview was fading. However, he also developed habits of mind and manner that clearly reflected his years at Cambridge and became recognizably British in so many aspects of his writing and work. This reminded me of Owen Hatherley’s wonderful profile of Central European émigrés who arrived in Britain around the same time as Hobsbawm and were, in the words of Walter Gropius, shocked by the “sentiment-laden inartistic world” that greeted them. This may be unfairly reductive, but did Hobsbawm meaningfully retain this “alienation effect,” as Hatherley puts it, or did aspects of his biography and personality make him especially suited to British cultural and political life? Emile Chabal I think it’s important to emphasize that Hobsbawm was not a Central European émigré or refugee. He followed a similar trajectory (in his case, from Vienna to Berlin to London in the mid-1930s), and he certainly felt that London in 1933 was desperately boring compared to Berlin. But he was a British national and a native English speaker. When he arrived in London, he had, on paper, come “home,” even if he had never lived in the UK. He was therefore an outsider, not a foreigner. It would be more accurate, then, to see the young Hobsbawm engaged in a very rapid process of acculturation and assimilation after his arrival in London — and, since he was only a teenager, he discovered that the school system was a very good way of becoming British. Inevitably, this meant that some of his most obviously British qualities — his intellectual agility, his commitment to concision, his facility with self-deprecating rhetoric and debate — were forged during his final years of high school in London and as a student at Cambridge. These intellectual traits were easily recognizable for the rest of his life. In other ways, however, he was not very British at all. It is noteworthy, for instance, how little class discomfort he appears to have felt. Most British left-wing intellectuals of his generation — whether communist or not — were resentful, if not angry, about the straitjacket of the British class system, but Hobsbawm’s class coordinates lay elsewhere, in the bourgeois universe of Central Europe. Crucially, he missed the vital period between late childhood and early teenage years when the British class system is most aggressively inculcated through schooling and wider society. By the time he arrived, he could simply transcend class prejudices by being extremely good at school (and, later, at university). This made him remarkably tolerant of manifestations of class prejudice in Britain’s higher educational system. If he disliked High Table at Cambridge, it was because he found the people boring, not because it was an expression of class inequality! Michael Schapira Hobsbawm is unusual in his commitment to communism — especially of a Leninist, party-led variant. Whereas so many took 1956 as an off-ramp from the cause completely or charted a new path (including those who would found influential journals like New Left Review ), Hobsbawm stayed the course to the best of his abilities. This is most clearly on display in his 1994 BBC interview with Michael Ignatieff, where he defends what Ignatieff considers an indefensible position: supporting the aspirations of communism in the wake of the events of the twentieth century. Do you think this helps explain why he never really found a home in the canon of Marxist thought? And how does it square with his elevation to the status of a celebrity intellectual and best-selling author? Emile Chabal Following on from the previous question, one could argue that Hobsbawm’s commitment to communism was another decidedly un-British character trait of his. Rather than adopt a pragmatic position toward the future of the Left by moving away from the Communist Party after 1956, he maintained his attachment to a political idea, which is exactly the kind of “dogmatism” that many in Britain saw (and still see) as a pathology of continental European politics. Hence, I think, why many of his interviews in the 1990s — including the famous one with Ignatieff — were so awkward. He and Ignatieff were talking past each other: Hobsbawm was trying to explain his deep commitment to an ideal, a future utopia, something that transcended him, whereas Ignatieff was insisting he make a moralistic judgment. It’s interesting to reflect on how Hobsbawm’s communism might have affected the reception of his work and his mass-market success. I don’t actually believe that his communism is the reason for his relative neglect in the Marxist “canon.” I think this has much more to do with the fact that he always presented himself as a “historian,” not as a “theorist.” In the book, I try to make the case that Hobsbawm was quite a serious thinker and that he had a strong theoretical grounding, but some might disagree with me, simply because he worked so hard to downplay his theoretical credentials. What is unquestionably true is that he was a mass-market publishing sensation, and this may well have had to do with his communism. Paradoxically, though, it’s probably not because people saw his writings as dogmatically communist — a novice reader of Age of Extremes might not even realize he was a Marxist! — but because his communist framing of history provided a powerful basis for his work. Many people have been attracted to Hobsbawm’s writings because they feel as if history is in motion in his texts; this derives, in large part, from his underlying philosophy of history. Michael Schapira You have extensive chapters devoted to Hobsbawm’s work as a critic (e.g., of jazz, on which he wrote a regular column) and his travels around the world. These inclusions expanded the project tremendously, including interviews with figures from around the world but also opening up a whole new archive of writings that many might not take to be central to his work. When did these become an important part of your project? Emile Chabal Yes, I am proud of these chapters in the book! In both my exploration of him as a cultural critic and in the section devoted to his travels, I wanted to widen the frame of intellectual history to take in different kinds of writing. It was particularly important to me to show that Hobsbawm was as much a journalist as he was an academic — and that the two identities were mutually reinforcing. Given that he was a lifelong academic and very clearly a professional historian, it might seem odd to put his journalistic and scholarly writings on the same level, but all you have to do is look at his bibliography: he published hundreds and hundreds of journalistic op-eds, essays, and reviews. They were a huge part of his published corpus. In the pages of the New Statesman , Rinascita , or the Times Literary Supplement , you can see him commenting on current affairs and working out new ideas (about the role of culture in society, for example). I expect it was the work I did on the complete Eric Hobsbawm online bibliography in 2019–2020 that really brought home to me the importance of these supposedly ephemeral writings. Michael Schapira As part of the chapters on his travels, you have descriptions of Hobsbawm’s extensive use of interviews and conversations, sometimes in methodologically questionable ways. In the back of your book, there is a long list of interviews you conducted with historians, activists, politicians, and others over several years and in various locales. What inspired you to extend beyond traditional archival research and embark on this ambitious interview project? Emile Chabal I always wanted the book to be global so that it would reflect the global character of its subject. But, as Hobsbawm himself knew very well, one’s global ambitions are limited by the hard cash that is available. And so it was for me: it was only when I was awarded a major fellowship grant in 2017 that I was able to undertake interviews in India, Brazil, Peru, Chile, Argentina, Mexico, the United States, France, Germany, and the UK. These were primarily with Hobsbawm’s friends, students, colleagues, and those influenced by him, and they were tremendously useful to me as I was trying to frame Hobsbawm’s life within a global context. But they also did something more: they helped me understand how Hobsbawm approached foreign countries. He and I did similar things. We both arrived in a new city with an address book and sought out interesting people with whom to talk. In this way, the time I spent interviewing members of the twenty-first-century left intelligentsia gave me a unique insight into how Hobsbawm did more or less the same thing half a century before. Michael Schapira In our talk at Topping & Company Bookshop in St Andrews, an audience member reminisced about the Marxist grandees who made up his curriculum at Warwick in the 1970s. And of them, Hobsbawm has had the most enduring power. Bernie Sanders and Jeremy Corbyn, arguably responsible for the biggest injection of energy into left-wing electoral politics in recent times, were also relics of a less fashionable era of leftist thought, closer to Hobsbawm. Why do you think this is? Does it have something to do with another topic you discuss in the book, namely the Left’s history of dealing with disappointment and defeat? Emile Chabal There’s no question that being on the Left — whatever we take that to mean — involves accepting and rationalizing defeat. Enzo Traverso has written very eloquently about the “left-wing melancholia” that has been part of the story of the Left since the defeat of the Paris Commune in 1871. Hobsbawm embodied this tradition: he understood the history of the Left to be one of adversity. This is part of the reason why he did not take the myriad “failures” of the Left during his lifetime — from the [Treaty of Non-Aggression between Germany and the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics] of 1939 through to the crushing defeats meted out to the British Labour Party in the 1980s — as a sign that he should jump ship. Having said that, I agree with Traverso when he says that the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 was a more fundamental and unsettling kind of defeat than anything that had happened before. You can see this even in Hobsbawm’s private correspondence in the early 1990s, where he was forced to reckon with the total failure of his lifetime of political commitment. And yet he came out fighting: in public, at least, he defended communism, and he refused to recant his beliefs. I think this sort of steadfast commitment to a political ideal is something that is very difficult to understand for left-wing activists born into the post–Cold War world. They look at Hobsbawm — or Sanders or Corbyn — and see a certainty about the future that they don’t have. Some admire this certainty and want it to be the foundation of a renewed left, but it’s also important to recognize that it was the product of a very particular set of mid-twentieth-century historical circumstances, none of which exist today. Michael Schapira Perhaps we can conclude by returning to the present. By his own admission, Hobsbawm was not someone whom you’d find shoulder to shoulder with factory workers or miners on the front lines of class struggle. But one area where he was directly active was in the university, an institution that nurtured and formed the basis of his scholarly output. At the moment, you are several weeks into a strike at the University of Edinburgh as conditions across the “higher education sector” continue to deteriorate across Great Britain and elsewhere. How did working on this project over the past decade inform your understanding of continuities and changes in the status and nature of the university in the past seventy years? Emile Chabal Hobsbawm entered the university system as a student at Cambridge in 1936, and he remained in it until the end of his post-retirement visiting professorship at the New School in New York in the mid-1990s. He never took extended sabbaticals beyond the standard allowance, and he continued teaching in some way or another throughout his career. He was as fully institutionalized as was possible within the higher educational structures of the time. The fact that he was able to be productive and successful while depending on his university for financial support and logistical infrastructure should give those of us who are in a similar situation today some reassurance. Hobsbawm too got tired of marking essays, chasing collaborators, and doing university paperwork! And yet something feels different about the twenty-first century. This could be because universities in Western Europe and North America are now contracting, whereas they were expanding in the 1950s, ’60s, and ’70s. This could also be because wage deflation has made it increasingly difficult to aspire to a comfortable middle-class life on a university salary. To me, though, the real difference lies in the dramatically enhanced role of public-sector institutions — like universities — in postwar Europe, a point Hobsbawm himself discussed in the late 1970s in his famous lecture “The Forward March of Labour Halted?” When Hobsbawm first became a professional academic in the late 1940s, universities were small and elite institutions, but their rapid growth made them — especially in Europe — essential components of local and regional economies. Today they are every bit as important as factories were in the early twentieth century, and the closure or bankruptcy of a university risks the same catastrophic effects that many European countries experienced during deindustrialization in the 1970s and ’80s. That is why today’s fight to defend higher education feels existential in a way that it did not for Hobsbawm and his generation. How Bernie’s Campaigns Reorganized the Organized Left

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Hadas Thier ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-15T14:07:23+00:00

Ten years ago, while socialist Bernie Sanders was making his historic run for the Democratic presidential nomination that drew millions of supporters and promised a “political revolution” to live long past his campaign, I was part of a small revolutionary organization that pooh-poohed him as a faux renegade. The socialist left at the time had been largely marginal to the American political landscape. This was not only because so few socialists sat in political offices. Organized socialists were unable to insert our ideas, strategies, or demands beyond small meetings and study groups, only occasionally influencing national discourse by being part of movements that showed themselves on the street in mass numbers. But movements naturally ebb and flow, and in their absence, much of the Left would retreat back into itself time and again. In 2016, Bernie Sanders showed that socialists could be relevant to mainstream discourse, could move the needle on major policy issues, and could do so on the basis of a class politics that resonates widely and deeply around the country. Though he didn’t win that primary nor the following one in 2020, Bernie’s runs transformed the mainstream political landscape and, along with it, the organized left. The section of the Left that threw itself in with Bernie’s campaigns — most significantly, the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) — grew exponentially in both numbers and influence and developed a deep well of experience and savvy. The section of the Left that scorned his campaigns in favor of independence from the Democratic Party and time-honored revolutionary principles stagnated or fell apart, leaving DSA and electoral projects at the forefront of an ascendant movement. The Left was thus reorganized to take the lead from Bernie’s “political revolution,” where electoralism was one important piece of a broad strategy to contend for power and build a mass politics. Ten years on, that political revolution continues to grow, while the approach of smaller socialist sects that had characterized so much of the pre-2016 left has receded into the background. We need not return to the path of permanent outsiders ever again. With DSA a powerful political force in the country and democratic socialist elected officials making concrete strides forward for working people, the intraleft debates that surfaced in 2016 are largely settled. They offer important lessons about the pitfalls of a sectarian approach that places organizational identity above building the biggest and most powerful left and holds rigidly onto traditional principles above shifting political realities. 2016 Was a Turning Point 2016 was an odd time for the radical left. For those of us standing outside of the most transformational development within the American political landscape to have taken place in many years, having to explain that Bernie wasn’t a true socialist to scores of newly energized and activated young people made us feel like killjoys, no matter how we tried to spin it. The group that I was a member of, the International Socialist Organization (ISO), insisted that we could relate to the movement around Bernie without supporting its candidate, by showing up to rallies with our newspaper and by engaging in organizing work around demands like Medicare for All. The main thrust of our argument served as a wet blanket. The radical left quibbled with some of Bernie’s positions, but his cardinal sin was collaboration with the Democratic Party and the fact that, despite decades of holding political office as an independent, he was running for president within the Democratic primary. The reasoning behind the argument isn’t entirely wrong: the Democratic Party is a party controlled by big business. Ruling elites have traded political power among themselves through the Democratic and Republican parties for over 150 years. The entrenchment of the two-party system in the United States is unique in comparison to other capitalist democracies. Because of antidemocratic and restrictive barriers to entry into the electoral arena, independent parties of the working class, poor farmers, and the oppressed have struggled heroically — and largely failed — to assert themselves or break the stranglehold of the two parties. In that context, Bernie Sanders and others who have followed in his footsteps since have opted to run for political office utilizing the Democratic Party and its open primary elections. Despite the conservative leadership of the party, Sanders wagered, socialist politics could appeal to the base of the party and create a movement from within. Sanders understood it was a serious problem that workers didn’t have their own party and were largely stuck with the Democrats, but also that the structural limitations of American politics made a break with them impossible in the short term. So he developed a strategy of running for president within the Democratic Party and engaging mass numbers of Americans while remaining independent of and often explicitly hostile to the party’s elites. Many on the already existing radical left weren’t buying it. Bernie was just another “sheepdog,” according to Bruce Dixon of Black Agenda Report , “charged with herding activists and voters back into the Democratic fold who might otherwise drift leftward and outside of the Democratic Party.” Howie Hawkins, a Green Party leader who himself had run for various offices on the Green Party line, argued at the time: “Sanders is violating the first principle of socialist politics: class independence.” Bernie’s positions on the issues, he explained, are “secondary to the question of whether his politics are helping the working class act for itself or subsume itself under the big business interests in charge of the Democratic Party.” According to this line of argument, the party leadership actually benefits from such campaigns, because the campaigns reengage otherwise disaffected voters. The “well-trodden path” worn by Dennis Kucinich was the prime example. Running in the Democratic primaries for president twice, in 2004 and 2008, on a platform opposing war in Iraq and in support of single-payer health care, he garnered less than 4 percent of votes, eventually throwing his support behind John Kerry and then Barack Obama. The radical left assumed the same fate for Bernie Sanders in 2016. “The Democratic establishment can breathe a collective sigh of relief,” argued one Socialist Worker article. “It doesn’t, in fact, fear liberal Democrats like Kucinich or Sanders, but third-party challenges like [Ralph] Nader’s that have the prospect of breaking their stranglehold on votes from workers and the oppressed, as several local and statewide campaigns have shown over the last few years.” From there flowed a familiar refrain of arguments: No sustained movement would come of it. The Democrats have always functioned as the “graveyard of social movements.” Radicals attempting to change the Democratic Party would only end up changed themselves, ostensibly into toothless reformists. And the old favorite, quoting historian Howard Zinn: “What matters most is not who is sitting in the White House, but ‘who is sitting in’ — and who is marching outside the White House, pushing for change.” The only revolutionary socialist organization to endorse Sanders was Socialist Alternative. It took the awkward tack of endorsing Bernie while urging him to run as an independent in the general election, in the likely event that he lost the primary (although he had made it clear, in no uncertain terms, that he would not do so). It tried to distinguish its politics from the larger DSA, which was much more integrated into Bernie’s campaign. Meanwhile, the American Stalinist organization the Party for Socialism and Liberation (PSL) ran its own candidate for president but more enthusiastically greeted the Bernie movement as a positive step for the socialist left, frowning upon the strategy of other socialists for calling out Bernie as “not a real socialist.” By 2020, it would argue for critical support of Sanders’s campaign. Of course, what actually happened in 2016 didn’t follow the expected script. Entering the campaign with no name recognition and polling just 3 percent at the start, Sanders went on to win twenty-three primaries and caucuses and about 46 percent of pledged delegates at the Democratic National Convention. Leaked emails among Democratic National Committee members (who, as it turned out, were much more afraid of Sanders than they ever were of third-party challengers like Nader) exposed behind-the-scenes work in the committee to help Hillary Clinton secure the nomination. Bernie did endorse and campaign for Clinton in the general election, as promised. But that didn’t stop millions of his supporters from learning a bitter lesson about the party’s establishment. The word “socialist” went from a slur to a point of pride. The “political revolution” that Sanders promised came to be through a growing number of democratic socialist campaigns, from Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez’s upset victory over ten-term incumbent Joe Crowley in 2018 through to Zohran Mamdani’s New York City mayoralty today. But while the Left as a whole was buoyed by Bernie’s 2016 run, some radical socialist organizations floundered. DSA grew exponentially by the tens of thousands, and groups like the ISO shrank. Three years later, when it came time to decide how to relate to the 2020 election, most of the revolutionary left opted to just run it back. What could arguably have been considered an understandable mistake in 2016 was by 2019 a different ballgame. Sanders had proven that it was possible to make a serious run; that millions would be radicalized rather than sheepdogged; that tens of thousands would become politically activated in a more long-term way, whether through joining an organization like DSA or by taking inspiration to organize their own workplace . But despite the fact that reality did not play out in the way that the theory had said that it would, the revolutionary left doubled down. Unwilling to adapt the theory to fit new realities or even to acknowledge obvious truths standing right in front of it, the revolutionary left made clear that opposition to the Democratic Party was its core political identity. That identity was hard to part with, and it precluded a serious political and strategic discussion of how to go about rebuilding the Left in a country where the undemocratic two-party system has a stranglehold over electoral politics. So much so that a remotely viable third-party challenge, on a national level, has not happened in a very long time. Lessons for Today As the Left reorganized, three critical lessons emerged. First, theory, even that which has been elevated to first principles, is good only insofar as it explains the world around us. If we can’t question our a priori assumptions when they no longer fit reality, then we have become pure propagandists. Second, if those principles become your identity and the organization holding that identity becomes more important than the health of a broad, effective left, then you have become a sect rather than a serious political project. In the ISO, which I was still a member of in 2019, a small group of us attempted to shift the organization’s electoral strategy to allow for endorsing Bernie Sanders and other socialist candidates running on the Democratic Party ballot line and were met with dismay from the majority of the organization. One document written against our proposal understandably argued that willingness to work within Democratic Party campaigns “logically spells the end of the ISO as a distinct organization on the left.” Pointing out that without a hard abstention from the Democratic Party, the ISO’s politics would be “virtually indistinguishable from the left-wing socialist currents inside DSA,” the document asked: “Why would someone eschew joining a vibrant and growing organization of 40,000 socialists — which includes a growing faction that accords with their politics completely — in favor of joining a political facsimile which is not only outside of DSA but boasts a membership that is a mere 3% of the size of DSA?” The assumption that carried the argument was that the ISO’s brand, identity, and survival as an organization were paramount. This was not because members of the ISO, or any other radical socialist organization, were particularly inclined toward sectarianism. But the many years and decades of operating in isolation, which were imposed by a historically weak Left and labor movement, had left their mark. Detached from working-class movements, the Left developed deeply harmful habits and a mindset that assumes that your organization’s preservation is critical above all else. Lastly, while revolutionary socialists weren’t wrong in their assessment that it’s a huge problem that the Left doesn’t have its own party, they were wrong in their prescription for how we get there and wrong in their assumption that a third party is on the agenda at this moment. Some pointed to polls showing widespread support for a third party. In fact, at least 40 percent of Americans have told Gallup a third party is needed every time it has asked the question since 2003, and majorities have said so in every poll since 2013. But the idea of a third party is very different from actually voting for a third party that has no chance of winning in a system stacked against it. Thus in 2016, while millions donated to, and over 13 million voted for, Bernie Sanders, Green Party candidate Jill Stein received 1.5 million votes, just over 1 percent of the total. The high points of third-party electoralism included Ralph Nader’s 2000 run, which garnered less than 3 percent of the vote and in which Nader was excluded from ever appearing at a presidential debate. As a socialist, I helped lead a Nader 2000 committee at the time, and the experience showed a huge potential for organizing vibrant electoral campaigns. But Nader’s relegation to “spoiler” status by our undemocratic electoral system made it impossible to make substantive headway on Election Day. Worse, the backlash against Nader’s campaign once George W. Bush won (arguably stole) the election by a slim margin only further set back the left electoral project. Even Eugene Debs, the American socialist movement’s greatest popularizer, won 6 percent of the vote in 1912. The Left has grown quickly over the past decade. Despite largely settling the question of whether we can work within the Democratic Party, which was so anathema to many radicals before 2016, we must still grapple with questions of how to do so effectively while avoiding the pitfalls that so many previous American leftists have fallen into in engaging with the party. In the early 1970s, when many student activists were migrating from campus to industrial jobs to promote working class radicalization, no labor film was more influential than Salt of the Earth. This pioneering docudrama was made two decades earlier by a producer, writer, director, and several actors who were unemployable in Hollywood, due to McCarthy era-blacklisting. This left-wing creative team recruited a cast of workers and their families from the International Union of Mine, Mill, and Smelter Workers (IUMMSW) to play themselves in a fictional account of a long strike at the Empire Zinc Company in Grant County, New Mexico. Local 890 members knew something about red-baiting too. Shortly before their 1951 fight for better conditions and an end to wage disparities between Anglo and Chicano workers, Mine, Mill was expelled from the Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO), along with the left-led United Electrical Workers (UE) and west coast longshore union, the ILWU. In In real life, and later on screen, the key to the success of this mining community struggle was the militant participation of strikers’ wives, mothers, daughters and sisters after their own mass picketing is enjoined by a local judge. In Salt of the Earth, this picket-line replacement strategy, which challenged and changed traditional gender roles in a socially conservative company town, is heatedly debated by film’s two lead characters. Blacklisted and Deported Playing the role of Ramon Quintera, Juan Chacon, an actual IUMMSW local president, initially objects to the strike activism of his fictional wife Esperanza, played by Mexican actress Rosaura Revueltas (who was deported for making the film). Other members of an entirely male and largely Latino workforce are similarly conflicted about the emergence of a vocal and empowered women’s auxiliary. When Esperanza and her fellow female pickets are arrested, she takes her small baby behind bars with her. The resulting boisterous jail house protest—which prefigures southern civil rights and peace movement tactics in the 1960s--forces the sheriff to release them all. The miners’ subsequent defense of the Quintera family--when the company tries to evict them--becomes a turning point in the long strike which ends in a contract settlement. Berkeley journalist and now first-time novelist Barry Bergman was among the many young radicals much inspired by this now iconic depiction of Mexican-American labor struggle. Before Bergman relocated to California and began a long career as a newspaper reporter, magazine writer/editor, and communications specialist for UC Berkeley and numerous non-profits, he worked for several years in a copper smelter near Tucson. That experience as a rank-and-file member of the United Steel Workers (USW)--which gobbled up the IUMMSW, after years of raiding it--led to his writing Proles . (Serving House Books). Bergman’s novel is a funny, moving, and historically accurate account of workplace “colonizing”—or “salting” as it’s called today-- told from the point of view of the author’s fictional alter-ego. Via a college course entitled “Celluloid and Social Change,” Simon Bussbaum learns about a film called City of Emeralds. He is captivated by its depiction of “dirt-poor Chicano miners in the Southwest” and their humble but charismatic leader, Naldo Galvin. Art Imitates Life Simon does further research and discovers that Galvin—like the real-life Juan Chacon in the 1970s—has become a dissident in the Steel Workers. The former strike organizer—and Salt of the Earth co-star-- is now rallying his co-workers against “a class collaborationist union which muscled its way into zinc and copper mines in the Sun Belt” by marketing itself as a labor-management partner, rather than a class struggle foe. The bookish film buff from Queens (the author’s hometown as well) leaves anti-war politics behind for a very different blue-collar life in open shop Arizona. Arriving for his first shift at Cobra Copper Company (aka “ThreeCo”) near Turcson, Simon walks into a scene lifted from the film that inspired his turn toward the working class. “Outside the giant garage doors that opened out from the smelter’s shell, hundreds of men clustered in small groups, coveralls gone or dangling at their waist. Some were standing, others lying on bare ground or using their hardhats for seats. A handful were shirtless, glistening in the sun. It might have been a disorderly line at the Fillmore East, save for the work clothes and industrial boots.” A new co-worker, a “hard-looking Chicano with a missing tooth, a Fu Manchu mustache and a cobra tattoo on his right forearm” tells Simon what’s going on. “It’s a ‘wildcat,’” explains Snake, who seems happy to follow the crazy "white dudes” who started it all by “waving their arms, walking out, and shouting, ‘Join us!”. Other hard hats in the now idle crowd are pissed off about losing a few hours pay. “Anarchists,” one grumbles. “Commies and agitators. They don’t like it here, can’t blame them for that. But you can’t strike till the contract’s up. That’s the law.” A manager addresses the crowd with a bull-horn and repeats the same message. “This is an unlawful work stoppage. Your local union has not authorized this action, and joins us in ordering you to immediately cease and desist. This is your final warning.” A Tactical Error? The “cadre” organization members--who have inducted Simon into their small circle at a local radical bookstore--are among “the twelve people pegged as troublemakers and fired.” After this “tactical error,” Simon attends his first Steelworkers local union meeting. There, he encounters bureaucratic lethargy and business union routine familiar to many rank-and-file activists, then and now. Only a smattering of members show-up—at a VFW hall with an “American flag on the pole outside the building and another hung on a wall behind the podium.” Much time is spent on the financial report and a reading of minutes from the last meeting. The biggest agenda item is the e-board’s unanimous decision “not to pursue grievance arbitration cases for employees who were terminated.” As the union treasurer explains, this will result in “a big savings for the local because we could not have prevailed in defending an action that went against the ‘no strike’ clause in our own contract.” (“Good riddance,” blurts out the local’s business manager, “a pudgy man in a short sleeved white shirt,” who gavels the meeting to a close soon thereafter.) Bergman’s workplace descriptions are equally on target. Some resemble the best passages in The Jungle, Upton Sinclair’s fictional account of job hazards in mid-western meat-packing plants a century ago or in Emperor of Gladness , Ocean Voung’s new novel about low wage work in 21st century New England. “The smelter, known as ‘Freako,’ was a cavernous, ramshackle Big Top featuring spark-spewing furnaces, splashing cauldrons dangled from the cranes like murderous marionettes, rapids of liquid fire tumbling down in harrowing dayglow ribbons. Danger was everywhere, lurking beneath the dull roar and the rotten eggs stench. Everything black and orange, darkness and firelight, a waking nightmare Halloween that left him in terror of any possible misstep.” Unfortunately, the hottest shop floor issue is not the threat of being scalded to death or maimed and killed in many other ways. It’s ThreeCo’s controversial decision to hire its first female laborer, a feisty former high school athlete, who is a half-Latino and a single mother. It’s 1973, after all, and the era of equal opportunity is dawning. As the fictional reincarnation of Esperanza, Samantha Tanager is not just showing that she can, in a pinch, take a man’s place on the picket-line; she’s going to join them on the shop floor, in a fire-resistant orange jump suit, aluminum hard-hat, and steel-toed boots. But the sudden arrival of a “fetching copper miner’s daughter,” as a local newspaper describes her, proves to be very triggering for Bullock. He’s the big, tobacco-chewing redneck with “a natural talent for cruelty” that Bussbuam is assigned to work with. Already suspicious of Simon’s rather obvious East Coast Jewishness and college boy ways, Bullock views “Tanager’s admission to the sacred sanctum of the smelter as sacrilege, a grievous affront to the Code of the Working Stiff, Nonferrous Division.” Besides, he believes that Samantha is a lesbian. (Spoiler alert: much to Simon’s love-struck chagrin, Bullock’s gaydar is right.) Casual Racism Simon’s New Left idealism is now further tested amid Freako’s “dark, depraved beauty, which reeked of musty maleness that gave rise to a loose-knit, shambling ‘esprit de corps, wrapped in a kind of casual racism and sexism so expansive, so deeply rooted, it had started to feel—Simon not being of this world, and without standing perhaps to judge his co-workers’ cultural values—like just another flavor of assholism.” When that “assholism” takes the form of a nasty campaign by Bullock to make Tanager’s life miserable on the job, Simon’s own personal class struggle ends in a parking lot brawl between two “union brothers.” It doesn’t end well for the new hire and little resembles more heroic scuffles, between labor and management, in Salt of The Earth. But it does lead Bussbaum to bid blue-collar life adieu and head for the greener pastures of California. (Tanager and her female partner leave for the more tolerant Texas oasis of Austin.) Proles was definitely not written as a how-too manual for members of the Rank-and-File Project , a present-day effort to “recruit, educate, and support a new generation of activists who will join the fight for working-class power by taking union jobs on the shop floor.” But the book should be required reading, in such circles anyway, due to its deft exploration of the nexus between the personal and the political. Any left-wing “salt” trying to befriend un-class conscious (and sometimes weird) co-workers, while dealing with draining night shifts, imperious managers and not much nicer union bosses, will find humorous respite in Barry Bergman’s fictional rendering of his real-life experience with the “salt of the earth” in Arizona fifty years ago.

Sometimes it’s the humble actions that matter, an important lesson for our dangerous time. For years, I’ve volunteered to get out the vote for my preferred candidates. In one election, knocking on doors in a Seattle neighborhood, I spoke with three people who supported my candidate for governor but wouldn’t have voted had I not contacted them: One forgot it was Election Day. Another didn’t know how to submit an absentee ballot. The third needed a ride to the polls. My impact had nothing to do with eloquence or skill and everything to do with simply showing up. After three recounts, my candidate won by 133 votes in a state of 6.2 million. I’d personally gotten one-fiftieth of the margin. If just a handful of the volunteers on my side had stayed home, or there had been a few more on the other side, Washington State would have had a different governor. Related: Poll Workers, Essential for Our Democracy Another time, in a US Senate race, I walked Election Day precincts until my feet hurt, and then continued until the polls closed. The margin this time was 2,229 votes, and I had no idea it would be that close. I also once invited my neighbors to a house party for my friend Lisa, who was running for Seattle City Council. Fifteen people RSVP’d, but only five showed, plus Lisa and her campaign manager. I apologized for wasting the campaign’s time, but my friend Shep, who’d just retired, was one of the five. He bombarded her with questions, but then, profoundly impressed by her answers, made volunteering for her campaign his full-time job over the next five weeks. Lisa won by 39 votes. Had we not held the house party, she most likely would have lost. Many of us spend hours a day inhaling the bad news of our dark reality. Or we follow the polls like we do our favorite sports teams, elated when our candidates rise, deflated when they fall. Knowing what’s going on is important, but if we put at least some of that energy into electing candidates we believe in, we can change the results, including America’s critical upcoming midterms. The research confirms our potential impact. A 2024 analysis by political scientists Christopher B. Mann and Katherine Haenschen found that in high-visibility elections like midterms, each potential voter you talk with adds 3 percentage points to the likelihood that they’ll vote. So if you reach 100 people from a list predisposed toward your candidate, you’ll likely generate three additional votes from people who’d probably otherwise stay home. Volunteer phone calls produce a shade less, 2.3 points per person reached. Neither sounds like much, but if 10,000 volunteers each spend two days and reach 100 voters, that will yield roughly 30,000 votes. That’s larger than the margin for a half dozen of the 2022 Senate and governor races, during which only 31% of eligible young voters showed up . If you still feel too bleak to volunteer, or the candidates don’t meet your perfect standard, remember that people have far more room to act before would-be dictators consolidate power. Recently, I met a young Russian woman who joined the protests around Putin’s jailing of dissident Alexei Navalny. “But then they got smaller and the risks got greater,” she said. So she stopped. As Navalny, who died in a Siberian prison, wrote in his memoir, “While we were immersed in our petty squabbles to decide whom to label as belonging to which faction, and whether it was appropriate for us to be photographed in their company, we suddenly found ourselves living in a country where people were being thrown in prison for no reason or even murdered.” Courageous and impactful action is possible even under dictatorships, as when Argentina’s Mothers of the Disappeared demanded the military tell them the fate of their children and helped bring down that murderous regime. But it’s far easier when dictatorial power is still contested and doesn’t control every major institution. So if we want to preserve our democracy, this election is a maximum point of leverage to act. And that means doing all we can now to impact it by knocking on doors, making phone calls, sending letters and postcards, texting our friends and asking others to do the same, supporting grassroots efforts in key states and districts. Pollsters talk about “the margin of error,” which means their projections aren’t cast in stone. Organizers talk about the “margin of effort,” which is what happens when enough ordinary people join a campaign and reach out to others. The more each of us can become part of that margin, the better our chances. Burkina Faso moves to decolonize education through curriculum reform

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Nicholas Mwangi ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-15T05:01:23+00:00

Burkina Faso has begun reviewing its history, geography and civic education curricula. The government says the aim is to “decolonize” the country’s schools by removing colonial-era portrayals of African history and giving more space to national history, African resistance, cultural values, and local knowledge. Speaking during a meeting with education officials in Ouagadougou, Secondary Education Minister Moumouni Zoungrana criticized the language used in school textbooks to describe European colonialists. “Those who came to conquer Africa are called explorers, conquerors. Were they conquerors? They were spies…. They were invaders,” the minister said, according to APA News . The minister said certain terms in the current materials serve to “infantilize” and “animalize” Africans in the teaching of their own history. “We have made a firm resolution to reread these curricula, to reread the content and to remove the words that are infantilizing and animalizing us,” he said. He added that the reform would go beyond removing offensive language. “We have made the same decision to add other chapters that promote our values and eliminate the chapters that are dragging us down,” he said. Further, during the second phase of Burkina Faso’s National Days of Patriotic Commitment and Citizen Participation (JEPPC), where officials presented education reforms and responded to concerns raised by citizens. It was emphasized that beyond the revision of historical narratives, the reforms seek to make education more responsive to Burkina Faso’s sociocultural and environmental realities. Measures include strengthening civic and moral education, introducing English progressively from the second grade, expanding instruction in national languages, and promoting bilingual education and cultural activities in schools. “The logical continuation” of decolonizing reforms Souleymane Kimbié Armel, of the Comité international Mémorial Thomas Sankara, told BreakThrough News that the curriculum overhaul follows directly from the country’s wider political project. Armel said the reforms sought to decolonize the minds of younger generations, arguing that previous curricula glorified European colonialists while presenting African societies through degrading descriptions. He cited the expression “the flight of the Moogho Naaba,” referring to the Mossi king of Ouagadougou at the arrival of invading forces, as an example of terminology that requires critical examination. “The change in the curriculum is the logical continuation of reforms aimed at decolonizing mindsets, beginning with the youngest members of society,” Armel said. He also challenged what he described as the longstanding portrayal of Burkina Faso as an inherently poor country, arguing that such narratives obscure the country’s capacities and resources. According to Armel, the educational system has historically failed to recognize the value of Burkina Faso’s cultural diversity, sometimes presenting ethnic differences as a source of division rather than a strength. Instead, he said, the reforms aim to restore popular confidence and pride by helping students understand their own histories, cultures and collective capacities. “All these reforms aim to restore the people’s pride and confidence in their own abilities,” he said. The emphasis on national history and indigenous knowledge is therefore intended not only to change what students learn but also to challenge inherited assumptions about African societies and their capacity for self-directed development. Liberation struggles key to the new curriculum Liberation struggles are at the centre of the new curricula, Armel said. “African states and countries of the Global South have always fought for their emancipation. The history of these struggles must be known by present and future generations,” he said. “It is in this spirit that the Haitian Revolution, the Chinese Revolution, and Thomas Sankara’s revolution are being taught.” “The objective is to sow the seeds of revolution among young students,” he said. He noted that the Ministry has developed teaching modules and curricula and distributed them to teachers. He added that the reforms are not limited to political education. “Beyond teaching these revolutions, the transformation also involves technical and scientific education, with the aim of ensuring that young Burkinabè are both politically conscious and technically competent.” Armel linked the reforms to economic sovereignty, drawing on the South African anti-apartheid leader Steve Biko. “As Steve Biko rightly said, what the master has conquered in the colonized is their mind. No development can take place without the decolonization of mindsets,” he said. “This will help prevent the economy from being outward-oriented and dependent on external forces. It will also help stem the brain drain to Western countries.” “Teaching in national languages and incorporating indigenous knowledge will contribute to achieving political sovereignty,” he continued. “In a context where the people must remain continuously mobilized, the education system should enable them to rely on their own capabilities to take charge of their country’s development.” Challenges ahead Armel acknowledged that implementing the reforms will be difficult. “Implementing these curricula also presents challenges, including the need to train teachers in the new concepts and ideas being introduced. This will enable teachers to understand and teach in the spirit of these changes,” he said. “In addition, textbooks and teaching materials for both teachers and students must be developed to support the reforms and improve their effectiveness.” He also pointed to political opposition. “As with any reform, there is resistance from Francophiles who are ardent supporters of the French system. These groups represent obstacles that must be taken seriously,” he said. “Ultimately, the state must provide teachers with training and greater motivation so that they can sustain the revolution within the school system.”
