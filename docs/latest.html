Trump’s Politicized Deportations Are Becoming the Global Norm

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Branko Marcetic ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-08T21:57:20+00:00

Given all the headlines about the US government trying to deport one or another migrant over their foreign policy views, you’d be forgiven for thinking this is a uniquely Trumpian innovation. Sadly, as the recent developments out of Latvia show, it’s far from it. Yesterday news broke that Leonid Ragozin, an exiled Russian journalist who has lived in the Baltic country for over a decade (and is also a Jacobin contributor who I interviewed for a forthcoming piece) has been declared persona non grata by the Latvian government, which intends to swiftly deport him. The Latvian foreign minister cited unspecified “information” received from “the competent authorities” to justify this extraordinary measure, but you barely have to even read between the lines to see that Ragozin’s offense was simply having opinions and doing reporting that the Latvian elite don’t like. For one, there’s the fact that Latvia’s own interior minister, Jānis Dombrava, came right out and said this, complaining last week that Ragozin had “over a prolonged period of time, publicly expressed opinions that are in blatant contradiction to the interests of the Latvian state.” You’ll note that this rationale is eerily similar to the words that Donald Trump’s State Department has used to claim the power to strip permanent residency from and deport pro-Palestinian activists Mahmoud Khalil and Mohsen Mahdawi: that they hold views that might have “serious adverse foreign policy consequences” for the United States. That Ragozin is being targeted for his journalism and opinions is made even less in doubt by the fact that several news reports based on information fed by European officials all mention a recent interview Ragozin did with Aaron Maté for the podcast Useful Idiots , clips of which have then been circulated online and cited by anti-Russia accounts that exist to harass anyone not toeing a sufficiently pro-war line on Ukraine. Ragozin’s critics have charged variously that he’s an “ apologist of Moscow’s war against Ukraine,” that he “push[es] aggressively pro-Russian lies ,” or, in the words of the Latvian politician who suggested he be deported in the first place, says things that reflect Russian propaganda about the war in Ukraine and so was actually playing a role in Russian “hybrid warfare” activities. (Yes, a politician really said this). You may not be shocked to find out that this is all complete and utter nonsense. Ragozin has done a lot of writing, so it is not remotely difficult to quickly bring up a long paper trail of all the times he has criticized Vladimir Putin and his horrendous war. The day Russia invaded Ukraine, Ragozin called it “a crime against Ukraine” and a “brazen violation of international law” that “will mark a dark chapter in Russia’s history.” He also called it “insanely criminal” and “ all-out aggression ” and accused Putin, who he has called “horrible,” of having “unleashed evil.” In a piece for The Washington Post early in the war, he dismantled he war propaganda fed by the Kremlin to the Russian public, telling readers that “Russians are not facing an existential threat now, of course” but rather “it is their own country that’s posing an existential threat to a neighbor.” In other posts, he’s charged “the vote was blatantly unfree” in Russian elections, mocked the nonsense math involved in Putin’s rigged elections, pointed out the gulf between the Russian leader’s sanctimonious appeal to “traditional values” and his own private behavior, and frequently lauded and defended the liberal Russian opposition leader Alexei Navalny, who was tortured and likely killed by the Putin regime. You could go on and on with this, but you get the idea. Look at what Ragozin actually said in the interview, and it’s obvious his critics are doing what the war lobby always does , including throughout the nearly five years the Ukraine war has raged: twisting his words to make his analysis seem like partisan cheerleading. Far from justifying Moscow’s actions — or lobbing a threat against Latvia, as some have claimed particularly dishonestly — Ragozin was doing what analysts and journalists do and explaining the mindset that is driving the current Russian “hybrid warfare” campaign in Europe. No, if you want to understand what Ragozin has done wrong, Latvia’s interior minister helpfully laid it out : Ragozin has, among other things, “blamed the West” for the war, “criticized the West and Ukraine,” “discredited” Ukraine’s politicians and armed forces, called the anti-Russian sanctions ineffective, and questioned if simple imperialism was really the only motivating factor behind Putin’s invasion, as Western politicians tend to insist. In other words, he has done reporting, voiced opinions, and highlighted information that are politically inconvenient for a hawkish Latvian elite — no different from how those who said September 11 was blowback for US foreign policy have been labeled pro-terrorist, or how those pointing out that the seeds for October 7 were sown in previous decades of Israeli brutalization of Gaza were labeled antisemitic pogromists. Or, as an editor at the Berlin-based Zentrum Liberale Moderne — a think tank that styles itself as “stand[ing] for the defense and renewal of liberal democracy” — helpfully summed it up : “Since 2022 he gradually transformed into a rancorous peacenik.” Weaponizing Immigration On the one hand, this is all the culmination and natural outgrowth of a toxic political discourse that’s developed around the Ukraine war and Russia. Ragozin, like all critics of this war, has for years been on the receiving end of these kinds of spurious and ugly smears, meant to cast basic foreign policy analysis as treasonous and even criminal, a trend hardly limited to the Baltics but sadly one we’ve seen across Europe and the Western world. The difference is, until this current Latvian government, no government was thuggish and shameless enough to treat these kinds of internet smears as the legal basis for something as serious as deporting someone. But there’s something else going on here. As Ragozin himself wrote yesterday, Latvian authorities are resorting to declaring him persona non grata out of “the desire to deny me justice in the court system where the case — as senior politicians involved in attacking openly admit — won’t hold water.” In other words, weaponizing the immigration system against a dissident and war critic means a much easier threshold to punish someone for doing journalism. This, too, is part of an alarming and accelerating trend, a different one. More and more, particularly in countries that are meant to be liberal democracies, we are seeing immigration policies leveraged not for their actual purpose — for managing the millennia-old, natural movement of human beings across borders in a secure and orderly fashion — but as an extralegal way to punish and censor speech. The Trump administration’s use and abuse of this move is probably most famous, targeting activists and journalists as the verbal threat of denaturalization and deportation for political views becomes a shockingly regular part of right-wing political rhetoric. But we’re seeing it pop up elsewhere. Recently, the journalists behind NAZA , the recent documentary featuring Israeli soldiers recounting the war crimes they carried out in Gaza for the past three years, were threatened by both the Israeli prime minister and culture minister with being stripped of their citizenship over their work, despite the fact that both filmmakers were born in Israel. Before that, Israeli authorities denied entry to and deported a French journalist explicitly because her work criticized Israel’s war in Gaza and used the word “apartheid” to describe its treatment of Palestinians. This past June, the French government placed under house arrest and deported Xenia Fedorova, the former head of Russian state television network RT France, who had become a critic of the Ukraine war for a conservative billionaire’s media empire, on the basis that she was a potential risk to “ national security ” and that she was acting as a “conduit for disinformation campaigns orchestrated by the Russian authorities,” even as media outlets readily admitted there was no evidence she was paid by or had any links to the Kremlin. Unlike Ragozin, Fedorova really did hew close to Moscow’s talking points — calling the war a “special military operation” for instance, the Kremlin’s preferred term for its invasion. But the rhetoric and reporting on Fedorova folded this kind of thing into legitimate points she’s raised about how NATO expansion helped spark the conflict and the Western role in prolonging the war, as if they’re all one and the same — points that are far from unique to her and have been made by many voices not friendly to the Kremlin. Her deportation opens the door to the French government deeming anyone making those inconvenient criticisms a national security threat subject to expulsion. In the UK, British conservatives demanded that recently released British-Egyptian democracy activist Alaa Abd El Fattah be stripped of his British citizenship for decade-old intemperate tweets praising the murder of Zionists — even as Israeli officials who have called for and actually carried out much worse violence are welcomed to the country. Last year, a German climate activist who already served a stiff prison sentence over a peaceful protest was facing the “ double punishment ” of being deported for it, too, and being separated from his British partner and stepfamily in the process, a case he ultimately successfully appealed . Whatever you think of each of these cases or the politics of the people involved, they reflect a rapid degradation in the Western world when it comes to the right to speak freely that’s meant to be core to liberal democracy. And they suggest Ragozin’s case, rather than being unique, is a preview of something that will become increasingly common. The Bell Will Toll for Thee Anyone cheering this should, first of all, be embarrassed. But they should also not be so naive to think that, in systems where power regularly changes hands and swings constantly between Left and Right, the targets of a policy of vindictive, politically motivated expulsion are going to stay limited to the viewpoints they personally disagree with. As I’ve written before , stripping citizenship from or expelling government critics and other dissidents used to be the behavior of the authoritarian states that Western politicians hold up as examples of what we must stop our societies from becoming, including Nazi Germany and, ironically, Putin’s Russia . But because the immigration system typically lacks the due process, evidentiary standards, and checks and balances of the criminal justice system, those same Western politicians are now increasingly turning to it as a way to punish critics while sidestepping legal protections or even having to mount a case that stands the test of a proper criminal trial. It is also one of the sad and predictable ironies of war fever, warned about at the start of the Ukraine invasion, as Western governments embarked on a program of Putin-like censorship to fight the so-called “information war” over the actual war. On that topic, maybe it’s best to give Ragozin himself the last word. “Putin’s greatest — and perhaps unintended — success in this conflict has been a profound Putinization of the Western discourse when it comes to Ukraine and Russia,” he wrote earlier this year, complaining about how it had been taken over by black and white thinking, loss of nuance, and an atmosphere where people in democracies denounced each other like they were living in occupied East Germany. “I can’t see how one can defeat the rising authoritarianism embodied by Putin with the help of Kremlin-styled intimidation, threats, online mob attacks and vile propaganda,” he concluded. “I don’t want to live in a world that’s made of different versions of Putin.” This article originally appeared in Jacobin , a democratic socialist magazine publishing long-form essays and analysis on politics, economics, and culture. Locals 228 and 728 Defy Odds to Win Substantial Wage Increases

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: UE NEWS Updates | UE ／ 著者: 不明 ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-08T21:36:36+00:00

On September 28 and 29 respectively, UE Locals 728 and 228 ratified new one-year agreements. The locals collectively represent over 800 workers who process visa petitions and provide petitioner support at the U.S. Department of State’s (DOS) Kentucky Consular Center (KCC) in Williamsburg, KY (Local 728), and National Visa Center (NVC) in Portsmouth, NH (Local 228). They are sister locations whose members are both employed by federal contractor LDRM under DOS’s Visa Support Services contract. The Trump Administration’s drastically increased visa application fees, regional travel bans, and newly-imposed visa bonds have driven workloads at both facilities to historic lows. Taking that into consideration, along with an impending surge in the cost of health insurance premiums, the bargaining committees of both locals made the decision to pursue one-year extensions of their current contracts and bargain exclusively over wages and fringe benefits. Despite the odds, Locals 228 and 728 won new union contracts with substantial, combined flat hourly increases of $2.10 and $1.60, which represent average gains of seven and six percent, respectively. Local 728 negotiations kicked off in Williamsburg on September 15 but got a burst of energy the following day when members onsite at KCC held a rally just minutes before the committee presented a counter-proposal. By the time the employer delivered their last, best, and final offer, several members had shown up to observe negotiations firsthand. Recalling the experience, Local 728 First Shift Chief Steward Daulton Couch noted, “Through member support and attendance backing the bargaining committee while at the table, we collectively fought and won a substantial wage increase to help combat the increased costs of everyday living as well as assist with the harsh reality that is the healthcare system in America.” Local 228 began their round of negotiations in Portsmouth, NH on September 22 by reminding the employer just how astronomically high the cost of living on the New England Seacoast had become. Determined to steer the course of negotiations the following day, members onsite at NVC took action. “When management submitted their first two passes, their proposals were identical to the first two passes they presented to our sister Local 728 a week prior,” said Local 228 Sergeant-at-Arms Dave Kirby. “When the committee called them out on their ‘mirrored bargaining’ as not bargaining at all, the membership took action. They held a walkout at their breaks on both shifts. After that, the real negotiation began. Direct action is one of the only tried and true methods to get management’s attention,” The employer delivered their last, best, and final offer to the Local 228 committee on September 23 in a packed, standing-room-only conference hall. Looking back on the locals’ joint contract campaign, Local 728 Second Shift Chief Steward Kala Collett said, "No matter how bleak the circumstances may seem, you can never forget how powerful [our] locals become when the members work together as one…and [we] have proven that during every contract bargaining." The Local 228 Bargaining Committee consisted of President Jane Shepard-O’Connor , First Shift Chief Steward Shane Tassinari , Second Shift Chief Steward Ed Martin , Sergeant-at-Arms Dave Kirby, and Stewards Dee Towne , Courtney Smith , and Danny McNair . The Local 728 Bargaining Committee consisted of President Caroline Gambrel , First Shift Chief Steward Daulton Couch, Second Shift Chief Steward Kala Collett, and stewards Brian Bradley and Ashley Perkins . Local 115 Wins Wage Increases, Firm Limits on Mandatory Overtime in Second UE Pact

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: UE NEWS Updates | UE ／ 著者: 不明 ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-08T21:36:36+00:00

The members of UE Local 115 ratified a new three-year agreement on August 27 after four months of negotiations. Refresco employees bottle beverage products like Gatorade, Bodyarmor, Tropicana, and generic sodas for various supermarket chains. The contract, Local 115’s second with Refresco, includes an average 14 percent wage increase across the life of the agreement, establishes firm limits on mandatory overtime, and lowers penalties for attendance infractions. Members making less than $24.00 an hour are set to receive general wage increases of 5.5, 4.25, and 4 percent in each year respectively, with all other workers receiving 4, 3.25, and 3 percent increases. The local also won longevity pay increases, an increase to the third shift differentials, and adjustments to lead pay. Some workers are receiving as much as a 12 percent increase in the first year of the contract, 6.7 percent in the second year, and 6.3 percent in the third year. Local 115 won a limit of three mandatory same-day overtime shifts in a week, a large problem during the life of the first contract. Employees were sometimes being mandated up to five or six days during the high season. The agreement establishes reporting pay and schedule posting procedures. The local also won the right to advance notice of and the right to bargain over permanent schedule changes. This contract saw a major win on attendance procedures, an issue which was very important for the membership. Attendance infractions for arriving late or leaving early from the overtime portion of the employee’s shift will be halved going forward. An employee will now have to be tardy or leave early during the overtime position twenty-four times in a year before receiving a step one write up. Employees will also be given a ninety-minute grace period to get to work if there are government-imposed travel restrictions, and will be given seven business days to bring in medical documentation before being written up for attendance policy violations. Overall, almost half of the contract is being changed, with two brand new articles regarding ADA accommodations and immigration leave & rights ( read the full summary here ). The company entered negotiations saying they did not want to “rewrite” the contract. The local organized t-shirt days and produced union stickers saying “No te metas con nosotros. Unidos para un segundo contrato” (“Don’t tread on me. United for a second contract”). The local began collecting strike pledges when they received the Company’s first economic offer. By the last day of bargaining, over 75 percent of the plant had signed the pledges. Local 115 members also met with representatives of the New York City and New York State pension funds to discuss various issues during bargaining. Most importantly, the contract has no concessions. The union beat back attempts by the company to increase the notification period for vacation requests and the removal of holiday pay counting towards overtime calculations. Local 115 was represented in negotiations by President Ivan Rios , Plant Chief Steward Jose Rivera , John Mata , and Alexander Prendas . They were assisted by UE Field Organizer Eric Cortes-Kopp . Eastern Region President Janet Gray , General Executive Board Member Dean Pacileo from Local 243, and Local 506 President Ryan Mudger and Business Agent John Miles provided valuable advice. The Private Equity Shareholder Project coordinated various meetings with representatives of the NYS and NYC pension funds.

UE Members Win Collective Bargaining Ordinance Despite Virginia Governor’s Veto

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: UE NEWS Updates | UE ／ 著者: 不明 ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-08T21:36:36+00:00

On September 22, the Portsmouth City Council passed a collective bargaining ordinance allowing city workers “the right to organize, form, join, assist, and pay dues or contributions to employee organizations, to bargain collectively through an exclusive bargaining representative of their own choosing, and to engage in other concerted activities for the purpose of collective bargaining or other mutual aid and protection.” The language in the ordinance covers all city employees and prohibits retaliation for organizing. “The City will refrain from any intimidation, coercion, or harassment of employees in the exercise of their rights under this division.” The ordinance also allows workers to use work property for union activities off duty or during breaks and allows for the use of city communications and email to conduct union business. UE members and other unions in the state have been organizing around collective bargaining rights for years. The success in Portsmouth, according to UE member and Mental Health Case Manager Hope Cuffee , came from consistency. She said, “Persistence, patience, and not giving up has been so important.” Cuffee said the city firefighters who are affiliated with IAFF have been key partners in the fight. “I think that we got here because of the firefighters and us. We were constantly writing letters to city council, attending meetings, and communicating with them to let them know we wanted to be heard.” Workers from Portsmouth also attended “organizing blitzes” in North Carolina — learning alongside other union members in the South. The win in Portsmouth came only four months after Virginia Governor Abigail Spanberger vetoed a state collective bargaining bill that she initially promised to pass. In a newsletter from the Virginia UE members, Tangela Randall , an Independent Living Coordinator with Portsmouth Social Services, responded to the governor’s betrayal by saying, “The truth is that public-sector workers in Virginia have been deeply underpaid and under-appreciated for generations. This was a critical opportunity to correct a historic, racist law that was established in the Jim Crow era to hold down Black workers. It now holds back all workers.” The same newsletter said, “During her bid to win the election last fall, she looked many unionists in the eyes and committed her support for public-sector collective bargaining.” The betrayal by the governor is a reminder that UE’s policy of independent political action is the only way to advance the rights of working people. When asked about the recent actions that led up to the ordinance being passed, Randall said, “The consistency of the city workers showing up has been most important. Me and some other workers have been at city council meetings constantly to just let our presence be known. Some of the things we did were not approved but we still continued to gather.” When asked about the recent win in Portsmouth, Randall said, “We have a renewed sense of hope.” She also said that she and the other members of UE will continue to “get things rolling because one thing we do know is that election season is here… Now is not the time to slack off. It’s time to put our best foot forward, keep up the pressure, and continue to organize.” Cuffee added, “I would like to thank everyone involved. It started with a few of us and even though we started off small, our persistence made it happen. We still got more fights to go to be official but I want to thank everyone for not giving up. Even though I might retire in four years, it wasn’t about me, it was about people behind me. It was about the whole city.”

More Legal Cannabis

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Paul Armentano ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-08T19:34:30+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. Most Americans now live in a jurisdiction where cannabis is legal — and use by teens has never been lower. Yes, you read that right. According to the latest findings from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, fewer young people nationwide are using marijuana today than at any time in the last 30 years. In the 24 states where adult-use cannabis markets are legally regulated, teen use has plummeted . For example, cannabis use by high schoolers fell over 60 percent in Colorado following legalization. In Washington, use by 12th graders has declined over 65 percent . (Colorado and Washington were the first two states in the nation to enact adult-use legalization laws.) In Ohio, the most recent state to enact legalization, teen use has fallen 33 percent in just two years. In short, legalization has accomplished something that prohibition never could. It took marijuana products off the street corner and placed them behind the counter — largely out of the reach of young people. This outcome shouldn’t come as a surprise. Licensed retailers check IDs and deny service to underage patrons. Criminal entrepreneurs do not. Related: Here's What Critics Still Get Wrong About Marijuana Legislation In fact, compliance check data from legal cannabis states finds that licensed marijuana retailers do a better job barring minors from their premises than liquor stores do. For example, year-to-date data from Oregon reports that underage patrons posing as adults are denied access at marijuana dispensaries nearly 100 percent of the time. Among alcohol retailers, compliance is only 80 percent. In Illinois , police in 2025 attempted underage purchases at more than 1,400 licensed dispensaries. Their efforts were rejected over 99.5 percent of the time. Data from California , Colorado , Washington , and other legalization states tell a similar story. That’s why scientists writing in JAMA (the Journal of the American Medical Association) Pediatrics concluded : “Marijuana use among youth may actually decline after legalization for recreational purposes. This latter result is consistent with … the argument that it is more difficult for teenagers to obtain marijuana as drug dealers are replaced by licensed dispensaries that require proof of age.” Related: Want Safer Cannabis? Put It Behind Counters, Not on Streets Of course, legalization has resulted in other positive outcomes as well. State-legal marijuana markets have created over 400,000 jobs and raised more than $28 billion in new tax revenue. In addition, hundreds of thousands of Americans are no longer having their lives disrupted by low-level cannabis arrests. (A case in point: In New Jersey, low-level marijuana possession arrests fell from over 30,000 annually to less than 300 post-legalization. In Maryland, arrests declined from over 16,000 per year to less than 500 .) In legal jurisdictions, regulated markets are displacing the unregulated ones — with most cannabis consumers now saying they get their products from licensed brick-and-mortar retailers. Unregulated markets will be further displaced as legal markets evolve and excise taxes on legal marijuana products decrease over time. Where Are the Democratic Socialists of America Going?

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Paul Heideman ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-08T18:02:38+00:00

The history of the American left is marked by imposing peaks and valleys. Over the last century and a half, organizations have sprung up and, often within a decade of their founding, become major institutions of US politics. They have typically declined nearly as quickly, with few sustaining their positions in American society longer than a decade or so. The Populist Party emerged in the early 1890s, won nearly 10 percent of the vote in the 1892 presidential election, and had largely disappeared by 1900. The Communist Party, founded in 1919, reached about seventy-five thousand members in the mid-1940s, only to collapse, through repression and its own mistakes, to under ten thousand by the end of the 1950s. The trajectories of the Knights of Labor, the Socialist Party, and Students for a Democratic Society, despite their different structures and purposes, are all strikingly similar. In the last decade, the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) has formed a new peak in this landscape. Growing rapidly since 2016, the group jumped from about 6,500 members in 2014 (with a median age of sixty-eight) to over one hundred thousand members (with a median age of about thirty-three) in 2026. At the same time, the group has elected hundreds of members and endorsed candidates to offices from county boards to US Congress. In its size and impact, it now sits with the most significant institutions of the American left. What accounts for this ascent, and what does it portend? Answering these questions requires resisting two temptations that have shaped much of the commentary on DSA. The first is triumphalism, the assumption that the organization’s growth is the leading edge of an irreversible socialist revival and that its trajectory can only continue upward. The second is the mirror image of this, a declinism that reads each of the group’s many internal crises as the beginning of the end. Both err in the same way: they mistake the story of a single organization for that of a whole political conjuncture. DSA’s rise cannot be understood apart from the institutional structure of American electoral politics, the specific sequence of events that revived the American left after 2008, and the strategic choices the organization has made in response to both. Its future will be determined by the same forces. This article will place DSA in the context of the electoral projects of the American left and assess both its achievements and its limitations. It begins by arguing that the institutional transformation of US elections over the twentieth century — above all, the introduction of the direct primary and the proliferation of ballot access restrictions — has made third-party politics a dead end and has channeled left electoral energy into the Democratic Party. This structural fact, more than any strategic error or ideological failing, explains the shape DSA has taken. The article then traces the organization’s revival through the two Bernie Sanders presidential campaigns, which transformed a stagnant remnant of the old socialist movement into the largest left organization in the United States in generations. From there, it turns to the strategic debates that have convulsed the group since 2016, the remarkable string of electoral victories its chapters have won, and the internal crises — over centralization, over the Democratic Party, and over Palestine — that have accompanied its growth. It closes by weighing the organization’s most serious limitations: the enormous variation in political culture across its chapters, its isolation from large sections of the working class, and its difficulty establishing a presence outside the electoral arena. These are not incidental weaknesses but structural ones, and whether DSA can overcome them will determine whether it becomes a lasting force in American politics or simply the highest peak yet in a landscape defined by valleys. The Limited Relevance of Pre–World War II Left Political Projects Discussions of DSA’s strategy are rife with comparisons to other leftist electoral projects. The Socialist Party of Eugene Debs’s era in particular forms a key point of comparison for writers from various political positions. Yet what often goes unexamined in these comparisons is the profound institutional transformation of electoral politics that separates the Debsian Socialist Party as well as efforts that predate it, such as the People’s Party (more commonly known as the Populists), from DSA. The introduction of primary elections and the spread of ballot access restrictions have drastically changed the strategic calculus for electoral politics and ensured the abject failure of third-party efforts since their introduction. It may be objected that discussing these twentieth-century institutional changes is unnecessary, as the adamantine nature of the two-party system in the United States can be explained as a simple consequence of Duverger’s law. Named after the political scientist Maurice Duverger, the rule states that in a winner-take-all, first-past-the-post electoral system, such as exists in the United States, a two-party system will result. This is because the system places third parties in the inevitable role of spoilers, where voting for them only makes more likely the election of one’s least-favored alternative. But Duverger’s law is insufficient to explain two-party dominance in the United States, either historically or comparatively. Historically, third parties, particularly in the late nineteenth century, had considerable success, electing nearly sixty representatives, five senators, and more than a dozen governors. From a comparative perspective, two-party dominance is far more pronounced in the United States than in the United Kingdom, Canada, or India. Something else is at work. One piece of the puzzle is the introduction of the direct primary in the early twentieth century. Prior to the direct primary, candidate nomination was managed through some combination of party nominating conventions and simple diktat by party leaders. Direct primaries, by contrast, involve holding an election among some class of voters: either voters signed up with the party, which is called a closed primary, or any voter who chooses to vote in the primary, which is called an open primary. These elections are typically administered by the state rather than the party. During the Progressive Era, when politics in most states were dominated by a single party, challengers, who had no hope of ousting incumbents on the opposing party ticket, turned instead to attacking the power of parties themselves. The direct primary, by expropriating parties’ control over their ballot lines, was one of the chief lines of attack. Primaries drastically change the incentives for those who wish to challenge the policies of a political party. Rather than building an entirely new party apparatus, they make it possible to challenge an incumbent party leadership in a lower-turnout election that requires mobilizing far fewer voters. Once a primary is won, the challenger simply inherits large portions of the existing machinery of the party. In parts of the country that are dominated by one party, winning a primary effectively guarantees victory in the general election. As such, the political entrepreneurs who hope to challenge an incumbent party leadership, and the voters dissatisfied with that leadership, tend to run and vote in primaries rather than building a third party. The incentive to compete within existing parties is heightened by the legal hurdles to such endeavors that have been thrown up since the early 1900s. Chief among these are ballot access laws, mandating a certain number of signatures be collected by third parties who wish to appear on the ballot. The Democrats and Republicans are often exempted from such requirements, creating one set of laws for the two major parties and another for everyone else. These laws began proliferating as states attempted to repress the Communist Party in the 1930s but have been used frequently since then, as major parties fear third-party competition. Combined with primaries, ballot access laws further disincentivize challenging parties from without. They create uncertainty about whether it is even possible to appear on the ballot and ensure challengers need to spend their limited resources on gathering signatures and navigating the inevitable legal challenges the two major parties mount regarding the signatures. Primaries open the door to challenging the party from the inside, and ballot access laws close the door to challenging it from the outside. Given this combination, it is little surprise that the postwar period has never seen a sustained third-party challenge reach the level of the Debsian Socialist Party, let alone that of the People’s Party. Instead DSA, as the most successful US socialist project of the post–World War II era, has grown primarily by organizing itself as a force within the Democratic Party. The Sanders Campaigns and the Rebirth of DSA DSA’s origins go back to the 1970s. It was founded out of a fracture in the Socialist Party, which by the 1960s had dwindled to a group with some influence among unions and intellectuals but little mass base. Much of the group recoiled in horror at the development of the New Left, seeing them as spoiled middle-class children embracing Stalinist totalitarianism. In 1972, the group split. The majority embraced hawkish Cold War anti-communism, while a minority led by Michael Harrington, who had achieved fame as a socialist writer and speaker, founded the Democratic Socialist Organizing Committee (DSOC). Leveraging close ties with some of the more progressive union leaderships, the organization achieved some real success in the late 1970s by cohering a socialist pole within the Democratic Party. In the early 1980s, the group merged with the New American Movement, veterans of the New Left who had reached similar political conclusions to DSOC members. This merger created DSA. Though the new group was larger than DSOC, the political environment of the 1980s proved inhospitable to socialist politics, and membership remained largely stagnant over subsequent decades. The American left experienced some rejuvenation following the turn of the century, and this set the stage for DSA’s rebirth. First, the movement against the Iraq War was a crucial politicizing context for many in the cohort that would emerge as DSA’s new leadership. Second, the financial crisis of 2008 and the glacial recovery that followed gave a new prominence to economic inequality in US politics. Finally, the Occupy Wall Street movement showed that mobilizing large numbers of people around this issue was possible. Bernie Sanders’s 2016 primary campaign transformed this growing radicalization into a force that would reshape both DSA and US politics as a whole. In his first presidential campaign, Sanders won over thirteen million votes in the state primaries, and his steadfast advocacy for democratic socialism throughout the campaign helped to coalesce a new left pole in American politics. The organizational expression of this was the rebirth of DSA. Organizers in the group were quick to recognize the potential of a Sanders campaign. DSA had supported Sanders earlier, including in his campaign for the Senate in 2006, raising about $60,000 (or 1 percent of his fundraising) for him. Sanders himself declared his interest in running for president in an interview with The Nation at the beginning of March 2014. Within weeks, DSA members were discussing a petition campaign encouraging him to run and laying the basis for DSA’s participation in his campaign. By the beginning of April, they had launched their petition . Over the course of the campaign, DSA members participated at all levels. Local chapters organized tablings and social events (“ Beers for Bernie ”), and members played a central role in the Labor for Bernie group that worked to recruit union members to the campaign. Much of this was organized through the group We Need Bernie, which DSA members formed as an independent expenditure group to support Sanders’s run. Even at the Democratic National Convention in the summer of 2016, where Sanders’s defeat by Hillary Clinton was officially pronounced, DSA members organized a Socialist Caucus at the DNC, bringing the fifty-five DSA members who had been elected delegates together with other Sanders delegates. DSA’s energetic participation in the campaign dovetailed with other dynamics to supercharge the group’s growth. Sanders was emphatic about identifying as a democratic socialist, and DSA had the phrase in its name. It also, unlike most other existing socialist groups, had basically no barrier to entry, requiring no political education to become a member. As a result of all this, by mid-2017, the group’s membership had roughly tripled to twenty-five thousand, turning it from a group consisting largely of retirees to the biggest socialist organization in the United States. When Sanders began gearing up for his 2020 campaign, DSA was well-prepared to support him again. Already in October 2018, the DSA National Political Committee (NPC, the group’s elected leadership body) had convened an exploratory committee to discuss possible orientations toward the 2020 primaries, with an eye to preparing for a Sanders run. This committee proposed a process for endorsing Sanders in which the NPC would run a membership poll immediately after he announced his candidacy, and if the membership voted to endorse, the NPC would then issue an endorsement and begin organizing a campaign. This is precisely what happened in March 2019. Of the 24 percent of members who voted, 76 percent voted to endorse. Two days after Sanders officially announced his campaign, the NPC had endorsed him. At this point, DSA’s membership had grown to about fifty thousand members. As a result of the organization’s growth since 2016, it was able to play a much larger role in the 2020 campaign. In cities where the Sanders campaign did not have a field office, like Chattanooga, Tennessee, and New Orleans, Louisiana, DSA was effectively the local campaign, organizing canvassing and phone banking. Overall, the DSA for Bernie campaign knocked on over half a million doors in the race. Despite all of this, Sanders’s defeat came quickly. After he won the first three primary contests, most of the Democratic candidates dropped out and endorsed Joe Biden, who seemed to be the only one who could plausibly stop Sanders. After running an unimpressive showing in the first three states, Biden won a decisive majority of delegates from the Super Tuesday primaries, and Sanders never recovered. Despite the gut punch that Sanders’s defeat represented, DSA grew considerably throughout the campaign. By March 2021, it had reached ninety-one thousand members, bringing it close to the Socialist Party’s peak over a century earlier. What historian Matt Karp has called “Bernie Sanders’s Five-Year War” transformed DSA from a stagnating group of aging leftists to the primary organization of a new generation of the American left. The process was not without growing pains. Strategic Debates DSA was transformed by the Sanders campaigns. As Meagan Day and Micah Uetricht wrote , DSA had become “a different entity than the one that existed two years prior.” Previously DSA had been a relatively politically homogeneous group, with broad agreement on the political approach Harrington had articulated in the 1970s and ’80s. By 2017, DSA contained within it nearly every tendency of any significance on the US left, from Trotskyists and Marxist-Leninists to anarchists. These tendencies were at first mostly unorganized, but DSA quickly developed a vigorous culture of internal factionalism, carried out largely through different caucuses. The group’s evolution since 2016 is, in part, the evolution of the political debates within it, which have focused on questions ranging from the group’s internal organization to its relationship to elected officials. Two essays have been touchstones for many of the discussions within DSA and are worth briefly reviewing before getting into the specifics of factional debates. The first is Seth Ackerman’s piece from late 2016 entitled “A Blueprint for a New Party.” Ackerman argues that aspects of American election law, such as those described earlier in this essay, foreclose third-party politics as a route for building an electorally competitive left. Instead leftists need to construct a kind of party within a party: an organization that uses the ballot line of one of the major parties but has its own program, membership, organizational structure, and fundraising apparatus. Though Ackerman himself does not use the phrase, this proposal has come to be commonly referred to as the party surrogate model and has been referenced widely in debates in DSA. The other article is Eric Blanc’s “The Ballot and the Break,” from late 2017. Blanc’s article is a history of the Minnesota Farmer-Labor Party (FLP), which was built in the 1920s and became, for a time, a successful state-level labor party, independent of the Republicans and the Democrats. Blanc argues that the FLP was established along the lines that Ackerman suggested: by building up independent organization in Minnesota’s politically dominant Republican Party. But the crucial moment in the process came when FLP supporters left the GOP and established their own party. Blanc calls this strategy the “dirty break,” contrasting it with the demand from many socialist groups for a “clean break” with the Democratic Party. Rather than embark on the mission of forming a third party immediately, Blanc argues, socialists should follow the FLP’s example and use the Democratic ballot line to build up their forces until they are strong enough to launch a credible electoral vehicle of their own. It would take time for these to become orienting points in the debate. At the first DSA National Convention held after the 2016 Bernie campaign, in August 2017, factionalism in the organization remained muted, and the positions that would divide the organization were just beginning to take shape. In the months leading up to the convention, two main groupings appeared. First, the Momentum slate of NPC candidates argued for unified national priorities , most centrally a campaign for Medicare for All, and for an electoral approach that largely followed the strategy suggested by Ackerman. The group also embraced several proposals that would break with past practices, most notably endorsing the Boycott, Divestment, and Sanctions (BDS) campaign against Israel and disaffiliating DSA from the Socialist International. A second grouping, calling itself Praxis , also coalesced. Praxis rejected Momentum’s focus on nationwide priorities, preferring to let local chapters set their own courses, and pushed for NGO-style skills trainings as a means to democratize the organization, by which they meant giving members the skills to do their own organizing without relying on official DSA structures. The convention delegates ultimately elected six NPC candidates from Momentum, five from Praxis, and five from DSA’s pre-2016 membership. It also voted for endorsing BDS and leaving the Second International. Proposals to commit DSA to a more hostile posture toward the Democratic Party failed but received about 40 percent of the vote, portending the organization’s leftward shift over these questions. After the convention, the factional ecosystem grew even more dense. At the convention itself, the Libertarian Socialist Caucus was formed, consolidating an explicitly anarchist current in DSA. A few months later, DSA Refoundation was launched as an explicitly “revolutionary socialist” caucus, which largely consisted of members from various Trotskyist currents. Many supporters of Praxis, meanwhile, reformed as a caucus simply called Build, reflecting a mostly nonideological approach that continued Praxis’s emphasis on decentralization. The Momentum slate launched the Spring Caucus, which soon split over internal disagreements, resulting in the formation of Bread & Roses, which generally continued the commitments that had defined Momentum. Some of these currents, like DSA Refoundation, would dissolve in internal acrimony before the next convention. More generally, this was a period of real instability in the new organization, with some NPC members resigning and leaving the organization. Despite this, local chapters achieved a great deal in their organizing and successfully laid the foundations for DSA’s intervention in the 2020 Sanders campaign. Yet the success of many DSA chapters reinforced a de facto decentralization that only intensified after the 2017 convention. Though the Momentum slate, which had been the strongest advocate of centralization, won the most seats on the NPC, over the next two years, the NPC was largely consumed with internal crises of various sorts and, as a consequence, failed to provide national political leadership to the organization. Immediately after the 2017 convention, there was a furor when it was revealed that one NPC member, a union organizer and longtime member of DSA, had done work for police unions in the past. The next year, a member of the NPC was accused of sexual misconduct, precipitating an investigation. Beyond these episodic crises, the atmosphere on the NPC was, by all accounts, distrustful and hostile , with little possibility for collaborative leadership. In this atmosphere, chapters largely went their own ways, and the organization developed tremendous heterogeneity among chapters in dimensions like ideology, culture, and organization. Some chapters wanted to go all out in organizing for national Medicare for All marches. Others preferred to do free brake light repair events to build a base through mutual aid (the mutuality of these kinds of community service projects was always unclear). A hundred flowers bloomed. The 2019 convention was polarized along similar lines, with centralization and posture toward the Democratic Party remaining key axes of division. The Build caucus was the successor to the Praxis slate, carrying the banner for decentralization. Bread & Roses was the successor to Momentum, committed to the party surrogate as a vehicle for the dirty break, the rank-and-file strategy in labor, and an appropriate degree of political centralization to achieve both. A couple other caucuses had also sprung up in the meantime. The first was the Socialist Majority Caucus ( SMC ), which prioritized running socialist candidates for office, generally on the Democratic ballot line, and expressed skepticism of the rank-and-file strategy embraced by Bread & Roses. SMC quickly developed a reputation as the right wing of DSA due to its lack of angst over the question of the Democratic ballot line and its willingness to work in coalition with mainstream Democrats to keep Donald Trump and the rest of the GOP out of office. The second was the Collective Power Network , a grouping that combined SMC’s position on electoral politics with support for Build’s decentralization agenda and strong opposition to the rank-and-file strategy. At the 2019 convention , the forces arguing for centralization and for working within the Democratic Party won out, for the time being. Resolutions to redirect a far greater share of the organization’s funds to local chapters failed. An SMC resolution to continue prioritizing electoral work passed, as did Bread & Roses’ resolution requiring candidates to campaign as open socialists and “use their public profile to popularize a class struggle perspective.” Though a fourteen-question litmus test for candidates failed, a “Bernie or Bust” resolution, forbidding the organization from endorsing any other Democrat should Sanders fail to win the 2020 nomination, did pass and would have consequences the following year. In the NPC elections, Bread & Roses elected three candidates, SMC elected four, and Build and some allied caucuses elected five, resulting in a decisive victory for the centralizers. While DSA was immensely successful in its support of the Sanders campaign in 2019 and 2020, the twin shocks of Sanders’s loss and the COVID pandemic disrupted much of the group’s work for the following two years. The 2021 convention was held online, and its results largely indicated political continuity for the organization. At the same time, the number of resolutions calling for some variety of break, either clean or dirty, with the Democratic Party, had increased and come from a wider variety of factional positions. Though the convention results favored continuity, they also suggested that the organization’s current path was becoming more controversial . Storming the Statehouses Even as the national leadership was unable to set a line of march for the organization as a whole, there was significant dynamism in many local chapters. This most often manifested in electoral campaigns, generally the group’s most successful means of reaching large numbers of nonmembers. Between 2016 and 2021, dozens upon dozens of DSA members won office, eclipsing the success DSOC had achieved in the 1970s and establishing the organization as the most successful left electoral project since the Socialist Party. New York City, long the heartland of American socialism, led the way. The chapter had been one of the first to embrace running candidates for local offices in the Sanders moment. In 2016, they ran Debbie Medina, a housing organizer, for a state senate seat in Brooklyn. Though Medina lost, out of this campaign came the NYC-DSA Electoral Working Group, which would form the nucleus of the chapter’s electoral operations over the coming years. Two years later, the chapter achieved two impressive victories. In 2018, Julia Salazar beat a sixteen-year incumbent (the same senator who had defeated Medina two years earlier) for a seat in the state senate. Even more significantly, Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez unseated Joe Crowley, the fourth-ranking Democrat in the House of Representatives. Her victory attracted significant national press attention, and on the day after she won her primary, over 1,100 people joined DSA, about thirty times the normal daily recruitment. Two years later, in 2020, the New York City chapter built further on this success. The Electoral Working Group coordinated a huge effort among volunteers from the chapter, and, together with liberal insurgent groups like the Justice Democrats and the Working Families Party, succeeded in electing two state senators, three state assembly members (including future New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani ), and one member of congress, Jamaal Bowman . A year later, the chapter ran six candidates for city council and elected two. For a group with fewer than a thousand members in the city just five years earlier, it was a period of stunning victories. Other chapters also achieved considerable electoral success in this period. The most notable after the New York chapter was Chicago’s, which won the election of six members to city council. Here coalition politics were even more central than in New York. The chapter’s electoral effort was closely coordinated with United Working Families (UWF), which had been founded five years earlier by the Chicago Teachers Union and SEIU health care workers. In the 2019 election, all of DSA’s candidates were also endorsed by UWF, which devoted significant resources to the races. Whereas in New York the chapter had to build its campaign largely without union support, as most unions were uninterested in taking a chance on challengers to the Democratic establishment, in Chicago socialist success was built on a partnership with the most powerful unions in the city. Beyond these major cities, electoral victories were more scattered. In 2018, DSA member Rashida Tlaib won the Democratic primary for the House in Dearborn, Michigan, joining Ocasio-Cortez as the second socialist elected to Congress after the Sanders campaign. In 2020, St Louis DSA got Cori Bush, a former nurse, elected to Congress. In 2018, the Metro DC chapter helped elect two candidates to the Maryland House of Delegates. In Minneapolis, the Twin Cities chapter elected three city councilors in 2021, amounting to about a quarter of the council. All told, DSA has helped elect some three hundred candidates to office across the country since 2016. The period of 2016 to 2021 was unquestionably one of ascent for the organization. In 2021, DSA could claim nearly ninety-five thousand members and had experienced about six consecutive years of growth. Though the organization still functioned as more of a loose collection of local groups rather than anything like a unified nationwide group, this configuration did not prevent it from achieving considerable success. As the group began to confront life after Sanders’s five-year war and the challenges of left politics with a Democrat in the White House, problems that were left unresolved in this initial period of success would soon make themselves felt. A Miserable Fit of the Blues Even as DSA was becoming an undeniable force in US politics, its limitations and weaknesses were also becoming clear. From 2021 onward, factionalism would intensify in the group, leading to damaging internal conflicts. At the same time, the group’s demographic profile remained more or less constant, revealing the limitations of its penetration into major labor unions and the US working class more broadly. The major factional flash point since 2021 has been on the question of Palestine. DSA’s revival was marked by a break with the organization’s longtime position on this question. In the 1980s and ’90s, DSA was distinguished on the US left by a deeply pro-Israel position. As mentioned above, the 2017 convention effectively repudiated this with its endorsement of the BDS movement. This proved complicated, however, as the ranks of DSA-endorsed elected officials grew. Most notably, Representative Jamaal Bowman, in late 2021, first voted for a bill that provided funding to Israel’s Iron Dome missile interceptor system and then went on a trip to Israel and Palestine sponsored by J Street, a liberal Zionist organization. Both actions were met with swift condemnation from DSA’s national BDS Working Group, which had been established in 2019 to coordinate support for BDS. The working group demanded that the NPC expel Bowman. When the NPC declined to do so, the group and its supporters turned their campaign against the NPC, denouncing it as “racist” and “imperialist.” When the group refused to adhere to the DSA code of conduct and continued campaigning against the elected leadership, the NPC ultimately voted to de-charter the working group, removing its status as an official DSA body. In the aftermath , some fifty DSA chapters (out of about two hundred) passed resolutions supporting the BDS Working Group. Out of this episode coalesced the political alignments that have largely defined DSA over the last five years. The primary political divide in DSA ceased to be over the organization’s degree of centralization and instead concerned its relationship to the elected officials it had endorsed. Caucuses like SMC, still the biggest bloc of votes on the NPC at that point, argued for a more collaborative approach, while others foregrounded “ accountability ” as a key principle for the organization’s interaction with elected officials. As the borrowing from the pseudotherapeutic lexicon of contemporary liberalism here suggests, the discourse of accountability in DSA often functions as an attempt to wish away some basic problems of the political terrain. The most obvious of these is the fact that elected officials in DSA, by virtue of being elected, need to represent, in some form, the constituencies who elected them. Any attempts to enforce accountability to DSA, carried out through sanctions like censure, the reversal of endorsements, or expulsion, will be of little consequence because, unlike the officials’ constituencies, DSA does not have the ability to remove an official from office. This is the fundamental limitation of accountability as a framework. It is maniacally focused on the kinds of negative sanctions the organization can impose on elected officials, when the limits of these sanctions should be painfully obvious. Alternative ways of exercising organizational influence over officials, such as finding ways for positive-sum outcomes in which both the organization and the official solve problems for each other and are thus invested in each other’s success, go unexamined in the squall for accountability. The Bowman affair provided a fillip to the currents in the organization most dissatisfied with the group’s direction since 2016. The hegemony of caucuses that wanted, more or less, to generalize the NYC-DSA approach nationally provided a focal point for discontent. The 2023 convention demonstrated the new strength of these countercurrents. The newly formed Red Star caucus, descended from Refoundation, won three seats on the NPC, while the Marxist Unity Group, influenced by the British Marxist Mike Macnair and firmly supportive of the effort to expel Bowman, won two. They were joined by three candidates from Bread & Roses, which had moved from an alliance with SMC toward one with the dissidents. SMC, meanwhile, ran six candidates for the NPC and elected only two. For the first time since 2016, factions of the organization that did not look to the Sanders campaign as the most important model for socialist politics led the organization . This period coincided with stagnation in DSA’s growth, which in turn fed dissatisfaction with the existing leadership. Membership had peaked in 2021 and by mid-2023 had fallen to about fifty thousand. The Biden presidency proved barren ground for the growth of the Left, with only brief exceptions, such as when the Supreme Court overruled Roe v. Wade . October 7 and its aftermath further inflamed tensions in the organization. The NPC immediately put out a statement condemning all violence against civilians and arguing that Operation al-Aqsa Flood was a response to the Israeli regime of occupation and dispossession. For some older members of DSA, this proved a bridge too far. They condemned the organization for its alleged “indifference to human pain and suffering, and . . . its failure to demonstrate empathy for the victims of Hamas.” In their telling , DSA had compromised its moral authority and thus its ability to contribute to building democratic socialism. Around the same time, some were drawing the same conclusion from the other direction. The Bowman affair and its fallout convinced some that DSA was firmly captured by reformists unwilling to take a principled stand on anything. One observer from this camp declared in late 2023 that “the DSA moment is over.” Centrists watching the drama agreed, crowing that “the war in Gaza is tearing” DSA apart. Obituaries began to be written . The Road to Power The doomsayers were proved wrong in 2025. Zohran Mamdani’s victory in the New York City Democratic mayoral primary proved a massive boon to an organization whose morale was flagging. The recruitment bump the organization experienced in the wake of the victory was even larger than the one in 2018 following Ocasio-Cortez’s unexpected win. Thanks in part to Mamdani’s victory, DSA reached one hundred thousand members for the first time in early 2026. Some of the obituary writers had plainly mistaken the optative for the indicative. This was particularly the case for those who interpreted the NPC’s refusal to expel Bowman as the organization’s crossing of a reformist Rubicon. While some tried to argue that DSA’s stagnation around 2022 was due to disgust with the organization’s conduct in this matter, sober examinations of membership trends revealed a rather more banal dynamic. Membership tended to spike in response to external events, like Ocasio-Cortez’s election or the 2020 Bernie campaign, but a large portion of members who joined during such episodes did not renew their memberships the following year, leading to an “exodus” exactly a year later. The membership decline that followed the Bowman affair was a year after DSA’s 100K Drive recruitment campaign in late 2020. Rather than an expression of disgust with reformist betrayal, DSA’s stagnation in the second half of the Biden administration was instead a consequence of the membership surge of late 2020 and early 2021 and an absence of electoral successes, like Ocasio-Cortez’s and Mamdani’s, that provide the organization with its main membership boosts. Yet if DSA has proven more resilient than many had feared (or hoped) in the dark days of 2024, the limitations that the organization confronts remain formidable. Two in particular stand out: the group’s massive variation in political culture across chapters and its isolation from the working class. Rhetorical Maximalism, Political Minimalism The decentralization that developed organically in the wake of DSA’s revitalization has never abated. As one critic of the national leadership observed during the Bowman affair, “National leaders oversee the work of our hundreds of chapters less than a semi-separate ‘National chapter,’ which contains national campaigns . . . . Though the DSA constitution states that the National Political Committee is the ‘highest decision-making body of the organization between meetings of the Convention,’ in practice this rarely filters its way down to local chapters.” The fights over national leadership in the organization may be intense, but the group’s political structure has barely changed since the less electorally focused caucuses won a majority on the NPC in 2023. Individual chapters vary wildly in their organization and conduct, with some chapters holding “Maoism 101” discussions led by video game streamers, while others organize the canvassing of tens of thousands of voters to send members to Congress. Chapters in more conservative areas, in particular, appear more likely to devolve into subcultural study groups. In these places, successful electoral campaigns are considerably more difficult. This has two effects. First, it deprives the chapters of the kind of victories that are DSA’s primary vehicle for recruiting members. Second, as these chapters tend to engage in less electoral work, they face less pressure to adjust their culture in ways that allow them to effectively engage people outside of activist subcultures. Both of these factors reinforce subcultural dynamics. Because of this variance in political culture, some DSA chapters are engines for socialists to break out of their marginality in American political life, while others actively reinforce that marginality. Moreover, the marginality embraced by some sections of the organization actively hinders those sections seeking to break out from it. Many currents in the organization embrace the most self-indulgent forms of expressive politics, like rhetorical maximalism that has zero connection to any actual political goals. This kind of maximalism is all too easily deployed as a cudgel against the more politically adroit sections of the organization by conservative media. If DSA is to be more than an idiosyncrasy of urban Democratic politics in the United States, this political culture must be overcome. But given how strongly self-reinforcing it is, the prospects for doing so without the national structure imposing a common political culture are slim. As yet, there is unfortunately little indication this will happen. The devotees of rhetorical maximalism and political minimalism retain a majority of the NPC seats. There is also a possibility that a healthier political culture can be built via a more decentralized route. An example of how this can occur comes from the Green Party. In the 1980s, the Green movement in the United States was captured by a leadership that was ideologically opposed to participating in elections. However, state-level organizations embraced electoral campaigns and were able to essentially take over the national organization by the late 1990s. A similar path could be envisioned for DSA. But any hopes in this direction must be tempered by the fact that much of the dysfunction in the organization is locally based and self-reproducing. Beyond the Electoral Sphere The second limitation the group must confront is its continued isolation from large sectors of the working class. In 2021, the organization conducted a membership survey including some information on members’ occupations and a few other demographic characteristics. The results were largely unsurprising. About 80 percent of members age twenty-five or older have a college degree, and most live in cities. In terms of occupational composition, a few categories are overrepresented, most notably teachers and academics, who make up about 10 percent of DSA’s membership, compared with about 3 percent of the workforce as a whole. Others are underrepresented, most glaringly retail, food service, and other service sector industries, which represent about 20 percent of total employment but only 6 percent of DSA members. Blue-collar workers are even less represented in DSA, making up nearly one-third of total employment but only about 4 percent of members. DSA members are concentrated in the salariat, with relatively little penetration down into the layers that make up the bulk of the US working class. The picture is similar on the subject of unions. Overall, about 20 percent of DSA members who are currently working are members of unions. While this is about twice the rate of unionization in the labor force as a whole, it is less impressive in light of the union density of previous organizations of the US left. In 1938, for example, the leadership of the Communist Party complained that only about 27,000 out of 75,000 party members were in unions. Despite the relatively low union density of the group, however, it has made some significant interventions in labor. Most notably, the Emergency Worker Organizing Committee (EWOC), which was launched with the United Electrical, Radio and Machine Workers of America in 2020 to support workers interested in unionizing, has played a role in hundreds of unionization drives over the last six years. Dozens of DSA members hold union leadership positions, and the group has built relationships with important union leaders such as Sara Nelson of the Association of Flight Attendants and Shawn Fain of the United Auto Workers. But again, compared to organizations like the Socialist Party and the Communist Party, this is a skimpy record. The Socialist candidate challenging Samuel Gompers for president of the American Federation of Labor in 1912 received a third of the vote. In the late 1930s, about 40 percent of the unions in the Congress of Industrial Organizations were led by Communists or close allies of the party. DSA’s relative isolation from much of the working class limits the organization in two ways. First, it contributes to the reproduction of subcultural activist organizational norms. With such a heavy concentration of college-educated members, it is inevitable that the organization will reproduce some of the pathologies of campus politics, from extreme moralism to obsession with different identity groupings. These dynamics perpetuate marginalization and impotence. Even more important, however, DSA’s isolation from large sectors of the working class has meant that, for the most part, the beneficiaries of the policies the organization fights for are not its members. When DSA members are part of successful campaigns to raise the minimum wage, for example, the group’s class composition means that very few of its members directly benefit. Yet this point should not be overstated. Where DSA has been most successful, like in New York, the group has won reforms like tenant protections, whose benefits extend through all layers of the working class. But it remains the case that, unlike the Socialist Party of a century ago or its fellows in the Second International, DSA chapter meetings by and large are not debating issues of immediate consequence to the organization’s members. This last point dovetails with a final one. DSA has had more success with electoral politics than any other socialist group in the United States in the last hundred years. At the same time, however, it has struggled to find its footing outside the electoral arena. To be sure, DSA’s focus on electoral politics has paid rich dividends. At the most basic level, any serious attempt to win an electoral campaign necessitates large-scale engagement with sections of the population that are not part of the activist subculture. To win their votes, organizers need to show they can prioritize the concerns of these voters, which will often require discarding ways of talking about some issues or abandoning some positions altogether. Electoral work, in other words, serves to discipline organizations, forcing them to abandon at least the most obviously self-marginalizing aspects of their political culture. This has undoubtedly had a salutary effect on DSA in those chapters that have done large-scale, sustained electoral work. To the extent that the organization has broken out of left subcultures since the 1980s, its commitment to electoral organizing has been responsible. Yet at the same time, DSA has basically failed to establish a real presence outside this realm. This is in sharp contrast with other organizations of the modern American left. The Socialist Workers Party of the 1960s and ’70s, for example, while never reaching even three thousand members, played a crucial role in organizing the movement against the Vietnam War. The Communist Party famously played a leading role in the struggle against racism in the 1930s and ’40s. The International Socialists, a group whose membership probably never reached five hundred, played a crucial role in establishing Labor Notes and Teamsters for a Democratic Union. DSA, despite being significantly larger than these groups, has established no such footprint in the labor movement or various social movements. While these groups shaped US politics well out of proportion to their actual numbers through their work in various movements, DSA has not been able to leverage its much larger membership in a similar way. But the consequences of this go beyond DSA leaving money on the table, so to speak. They also affect the group’s politics and potential. First, they influence the trade-offs DSA confronts as a socialist electoral project. Socialists have long recognized that the class structure of capitalism itself is not sufficient to guarantee that workers will identify their shared class interests and unify politically. Their propensity to do so depends on the strategies adopted by other political actors, like parties and unions, that are dedicated to this project. If these actors succeed, there will be a high degree of class consciousness among workers, and electoral and industrial initiatives will have much stronger chances of success. If they fail, workers will instead embrace political identities organized around race or nation, or simply political quietude. As political scientist Adam Przeworski has argued, political parties in particular face a trade-off here. No socialist party has ever won an election with the votes of workers alone; they have always depended on winning some layers of the middle class as well. To win these votes, parties must de-emphasize their class appeal and instead build a nonclass mass appeal to win voters outside the working class. But doing so has a cost. As parties de-emphasize their class appeal, they disorganize their key voting base by ceasing to organize them on a class basis. Yet Przeworski argues there is a way out of this dilemma, as parties are not the only organizations that organize workers as a class. Unions can also perform this function. Where unions are strong and centralized, he argues, the trade-off that socialist parties face between being mass parties and being class parties is not so harsh. Parties can afford to dilute their class appeal without risking their foundations in the class consciousness of workers because that class consciousness is sustained through other institutions. In this situation, seeking the vote of sectors of the middle class does not necessarily mean losing votes among workers. For DSA, this trade-off has the potential to be more severe than it was for the European socialist parties. First, DSA is already based primarily on a relatively thin stratum of the working class, consisting primarily of college-educated workers. While this stratum, partially in response to its increasing proletarianization, has trended left over the last decade, there are signs of limits to this radicalization. Most notably, there has been limited enthusiasm for the broad-based taxation necessary to build the comprehensive welfare state it seeks. DSA will simply not be able to sustain its agenda without more working-class votes. Second, the fact that DSA’s strategy is to run on another party’s ballot line does have real costs in terms of its ability to build class consciousness. The party’s elected officials may proclaim their socialism loudly and proudly, but to many voters they will simply be Democrats, because that is the ballot line on which they run. Critics of the party surrogate strategy have a point when they argue that it inhibits cohering a generalized socialist political identity among the electorate. Finally, of course, there is the abysmal union density in the United States. When Przeworski and his coauthor John Sprague wrote about the ability of strong unions to soften the trade-off between creating a mass party and retaining a class base, they were comparing Swedish unionization with French unionization in the 1960s and ’70s. The distance between those two cases is almost negligible in comparison to the United States of the 2020s. Today the majority of the US working class has no experience whatsoever with unions, and the existing unions are far more oriented toward sectional interests than class-wide solidarity. As such, DSA’s ability to become a force not merely in electoral politics but in the union movement as well is of particular urgency. Without a massively revived labor movement, DSA will face particularly unpleasant versions of the trade-offs that have confronted all mass socialist parties over the last century and a half. Of course, it is too much to expect DSA to single-handedly revive the American union movement. To appreciate the scale of the task, consider that even today there are over one hundred union members in the United States for each member of DSA. Yet it is inarguable that previous socialist groups had a bigger impact on the labor movement with a far smaller membership. Though initiatives connected to DSA like EWOC and the Rank-and-File Project have done excellent work, the organization’s ability to grow and sustain itself will be dependent on doing much more in the labor movement. The second drawback of the organization’s overriding focus on the electoral sphere is the familiar Marxist case that the state in capitalist society is structurally biased against socialist policies. Because of this, even if a pro-socialist government can get elected, it will, sooner or later, be compelled to abandon its commitments in the face of resistance from capitalists. To be sure, this critique has often been leveled with a glibness that belies the considerable power socialist parties amassed at different points in the last century, as well as the complications that bedevil some of the canonical cases of the dynamic it describes. But even with appropriate reservations, the argument retains considerable force. Though it is often framed as a warning about how periods of socialist governance end, this argument is just as useful for understanding how they begin. In the United States, for example, the period of reform at the height of the New Deal came about because the capitalist class, under pressure from an insurgent working class, split into a section that preferred compromise with workers and another that opposed it. With capital thus split, workers were able to win significant reforms . The broader lesson here is dispiritingly familiar in studies of subaltern insurgency: when elites remain unified, the triumph of subaltern groups is unlikely. In an advanced capitalist democracy like the United States, industrial disruption by workers remains the most plausible way of generating these kinds of divisions. Though DSA’s ambitions for transforming American society run through electing more socialists to government, the success of this endeavor will depend at least in part on US workers’ ability to mobilize. In the abstract, almost all members of DSA would affirm a version of this thesis. But it remains true that DSA has found little success in the second half of this formula, even as its success in the first has been extraordinary. Conclusion: To the Mountaintop Richard Nixon, in the final address to his staff before resigning the presidency, observed that “only if you’ve been in the deepest valley can you ever know how magnificent it is to be on the highest mountain.” It is an apt, if unlikely, epigraph for the story of DSA. The organization has traversed the full distance between the valley and the mountain in a single decade. In 2014, it was a moribund remnant of a bygone left, a few thousand aging members presiding over the embers of Michael Harrington’s project. By 2026, it was the largest socialist organization in the United States since the days of Eugene Debs, its members seated in Congress and statehouses across the country, with one of them governing the nation’s largest city. Few could have predicted this ascent, and it would be churlish not to recognize it for the genuine achievement it is. Yet the peaks and valleys with which this essay began should counsel against any easy confidence about what comes next. The history of the US left is a history of organizations that reached comparable heights or higher, only to collapse within a decade or two. What distinguishes DSA from its predecessors is instructive. On the one hand, DSA has been spared the two forces that destroyed so many earlier left organizations. It has not faced serious state repression on the scale that broke the Communist Party, and its decision to work within the Democratic Party has insulated it from the strategic cul-de-sac of third-party politics that doomed most of the postwar left. The institutional story of DSA is, in this sense, one of adaptation. DSA has discovered a way to build a durable socialist presence within the peculiar constraints of the US electoral system. On the other hand, the very adaptation that has allowed DSA to survive has also limited what it can become. Running on the ballot line of another party, drawing its membership overwhelmingly from a thin stratum of the educated salariat, and struggling to establish itself in the labor movement and other areas of struggle outside the electoral arena, DSA remains, despite its size, a shallow-rooted organization. The limitations surveyed in this essay — the wild heterogeneity of its chapters, its distance from the bulk of the working class, and its difficulty acting outside the electoral sphere — are not the sort of problems that resolve themselves with time or growth. If anything, DSA’s structure tends to reproduce them. Its decentralization allows self-marginalizing chapters to persist and even flourish alongside its most effective ones; its class composition draws it back toward the pathologies of campus and activist subcultures; and its electoral successes, precisely because they are so much easier to achieve than durable institutional presence in unions or social movements, continually tempt the organization to define itself by them alone. None of this is destiny. The comparison with the Greens suggests that a decentralized organization can, over time, be reshaped from below by its more serious tendencies, and the comparison with earlier socialist organizations suggests just how much a determined minority can accomplish in the labor movement with even modest numbers. But these outcomes will not arrive on their own. They will have to be fought for, against the grain of the organization’s own proclivities. Whether DSA can win those fights is, at present, an open question, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. What can be said is that the organization has already outlasted its own obituaries more than once, and that its survival through the barren years of the Biden administration and the bitter conflicts over Palestine testifies to a resilience its critics have repeatedly underestimated. Under Trump Pressure, Bolivia Arrests Its Own Attorney General

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Joseph Bouchard ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-08T18:02:38+00:00

Early last week, the Trump administration’s State Department announced visa restrictions on a group of Latin American officials it accused of corruption and of “undermining democratically-selected governments.” Of the twenty-one people it targeted in Bolivia, Washington chose to name only one: Bolivia’s sitting attorney general, Roger Mariaca. A little over twenty-four hours later, on September 30, Bolivian police detained Mariaca at Viru Viru International Airport in Santa Cruz while agents raided prosecutors’ offices in Sucre and Santa Cruz. He was taken without a warrant before the legislature had taken any steps to remove him from office, as required by the constitution and, as the government itself later acknowledged , with intelligence supplied by the US Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA), an agency with a particularly sordid history in Bolivia. It looked like an order sent straight from Washington. “The arrest of Bolivia’s attorney general just after the US rescinded his visa,” journalist Benjamin Swift, based in La Paz, told Jacobin , “is a clear signal of the United States’ growing influence in Bolivia.” At the time of his arrest, Mariaca was investigating the far-right Argentine political operative Fernando Cerimedo, “ MAGA’s man in Latin America,” who was arrested just over a month prior at the same airport while attempting to flee to Buenos Aires. Cerimedo was taken in for allegedly ordering a hit against his pregnant partner, the lawyer Nadia Beller, who survived the attack by two gunmen outside a Santa Cruz hotel. Mariaca’s office soon also began digging into Cerimedo’s alleged ties to drug-trafficking networks. Cerimedo, who has close connections to Donald Trump’s innermost circle, has reportedly had a hand in virtually every recent far-right electoral victory in the region, including that of Javier Milei in Argentina, José Antonio Kast in Chile, Nasry Asfura in Honduras, and Abelardo de la Espriella in Colombia. He was also deeply involved in the disinformation campaign that claimed the 2022 Brazilian election was stolen from Jair Bolsonaro, crafting a narrative that — as Sunday’s first-round election results show — continues to sway voters. In Bolivia, he was not only a strategist in right-wing President Rodrigo Paz’s campaign but became one of his top aides once in government, holding his own office in Bolivia’s presidential palace. After Cerimedo’s allies arrested Attorney General Mariaca, US Secretary of State Marco Rubio signaled the White House’s involvement and support, declaring that the Trump administration “stands firmly” with President Paz as his government takes “bold and decisive actions to combat narco-trafficking, systemic corruption, and transnational organized crime.” Attacking the Judiciary Mariaca was arrested along with thirteen others from the Public Prosecutor’s Office and the justice system, including Santa Cruz departmental prosecutor Alberto Zeballos and prosecutor Martín Irusta, in a direct attack on Bolivia’s judiciary. Former socialist President Evo Morales called the operation unconstitutional and accused the DEA, banned from the country until recently for its meddling in politics and involvement in cocaine trafficking in Bolivia, of running it. President Paz’s minister of government, Marco Antonio Oviedo, who oversees the police, pointed to Mariaca as the leader of “a criminal organization embedded in the justice system and in the administration of a very important institution in our country, the Public Ministry.” “Either you stand with the law,” Oviedo said, “or you stand with crime, with narcoterrorism” — a term borrowed directly from the Trump administration. The day before his arrest, Mariaca publicly attributed the initial US sanctions against him to his office’s investigations into Cerimedo and the gasolina basura (junk gasoline) case over contaminated fuel imports. There have been massive fuel shortages in the country since the president slashed fuel subsidies, hitting the poor the hardest. “We know the work we have been doing has not been to the liking of certain political sectors, both outside and inside our country,” Mariaca said. “The timing of Mariaca’s arrest is alarming,” Thomas Becker told Jacobin . Becker is an international human rights lawyer who led the landmark US civil case that found former Bolivian President Gonzalo Sánchez de Lozada (1993–97, 2002–3), a close Washington ally, liable for extrajudicial killings. “The US is meddling in Bolivia’s internal affairs in plain sight,” he said. “Washington is no longer even pretending to hide its hand. This is the ‘Donroe Doctrine’ in action.” Legal Maneuverings Two days after the arrest of Mariaca, both chambers of the Plurinational Legislative Assembly passed Law 1773 . It amends Article 25 of the Organic Law of the Public Ministry to let lawmakers, by a two-thirds vote, permanently suspend an attorney general who has been formally charged or placed under precautionary measures for serious crimes and name an interim replacement. Paz signed it on Friday. In a session that ran more than twenty hours , into early Saturday, 113 of the 128 legislators present voted to remove Mariaca for good, and the Assembly installed José Armando Urioste Viera as interim attorney general for ninety days. It was, in effect, a post hoc legalization of an unconstitutional removal. Vice President Edmand Lara, a populist figure who has broken with President Paz, argued that “the law is not retroactive” and that suspending officials on the strength of charges alone violates the constitutional presumption of innocence. He has so far refused to swear in Urioste. A US official attended the session as part of the Shield of the Americas , which some have dubbed the new Plan Condor. Senator Ana Crispín accused him of handing lawmakers QR codes that she alleged were tied to payments for their votes. Kathryn Ledebur, director of the Andean Information Network in Cochabamba, speculated “that the Paz government will hand Mariaca over to the DEA [very quickly], just like they did with Marset, no extradition process or anything,” referring to Uruguayan kingpin Sebastián Marset, captured in a DEA-backed operation in Bolivia in March and sent to the United States. “Apparently, getting your visa revoked doesn’t mean you won’t get to know the US — quite the opposite.” She may be right. The day after the sanctions list was released, Defense Minister Ernesto Justiniano said the case could lead to “a judicial step in the United States, probably an extradition request.” Vice President Lara Says He’s the Next Target In the following days, sitting Vice President Edmand Lara, a former police captain who was pushed out of the Bolivian National Police after publicly denouncing corruption inside it, sounded the alarm that he could be next. Speaking from Madrid, Lara said he received warnings “from within the executive” that police were preparing to plant evidence in order to arrest him, and he named a police colonel, Marcelo Ferrufino, as the man behind it. “I fear for my physical integrity and for my life,” Lara said. “This would be a frame-up for political ends, to silence me.” The government has not responded publicly. Lara was elected on Paz’s ticket, likely winning him the election, but he broke with the president in December 2025, declaring himself in “constructive opposition” as the president abandoned his moderate positions to align himself with the far-right Santa Cruz business interests that he had defeated electorally. Lara has since become one of the most vocal critics of Paz and his security cabinet. He backed the union-led protests in May, pressed for transparency over Cerimedo’s role in the government, released audio recordings implicating the adviser, and has demanded that Washington’s hand be pulled back from Bolivia’s security and politics. After Mariaca’s arrest, the vice president warned that “no foreign police agent, whether from the DEA or any other institution, can exercise arrest powers in our territory outside Bolivian law,” refusing to treat a US sanctions list as grounds for prosecuting Bolivian officials. Rule by Emergency Since June 20, the Paz administration has governed under a “ state of exception ,” curtailing civil and political rights, including the right to protest. Road blockades are banned, mass gatherings are restricted, and the military is deployed alongside the police. The emergency powers were declared after fifty days of protests and blockades by Bolivian unions, campesino and indigenous organizations, and the opposition. The protests were triggered by Law 1720, which allowed small agricultural holdings, legally protected from seizure, to be converted into medium-size properties that could be mortgaged to banks. Indigenous and campesino groups saw it as a first step toward handing their lands, redistributed after the 1952 Revolution, to “more productive” sectors; opposition politicians said it was written on behalf of the large and politically dominant agribusiness sector concentrated in Santa Cruz, the country’s conservative economic capital. After protesters marched more than a thousand kilometers from Pando to La Paz, President Paz repealed the law on May 14. But by then, the movement had widened into a revolt against the end of fuel subsidies, the imported “junk gasoline” that was wrecking engines, and the worst economic crisis in decades. As the government moved toward emergency rule, Claudia Herbas, a deputy for the Alianza Popular opposition who introduced the bill to repeal the decree, warned that coercive measures would only escalate the violence, urging a return to the negotiating table. Instead, the blockades were broken by force. Bolivia’s Ombudsman’s Office recorded twenty-two deaths over the course of the conflict, along with eighty-eight injured and 583 arrested. The Inter-American Commission on Human Rights found that police used excessive force, in some cases in coordination with far-right vigilante groups . The protests were broken within days, but the state of exception continues. On September 17, the Assembly extended it through mid-December, with Paz arguing it would let the government act against “narcoterrorism.” The Mariaca affair fits neatly into that project. It hands an embattled president, whose approval rating had collapsed to 21 percent by September, a fresh justification for emergency rule long after the blockades have ended. And it lets him centralize power and delegitimize a judiciary that has occasionally checked him, even after he had already abolished the Ministry of Justice and turned the machinery of the state against his political enemies. This is the first time a state of exception has been invoked under Bolivia’s 2009 constitution; the last comparable measure was the 2008 state of siege in Pando , declared after the Porvenir massacre of campesinos. These emergency powers echo darker chapters of Bolivian history, both of them provoked by Washington. In 1971, Hugo Banzer seized power in a US-supported coup and ruled by decree until 1978, banning unions, jailing and disappearing opponents, and joining Plan Condor; when he returned as an elected president, he declared a state of siege during the 2000 Cochabamba Water War. And in 2019, after the coup d’état that forced Morales from office, the interim government of Jeanine Áñez issued a decree shielding the armed forces from prosecution; within days, security forces killed at least twenty people in the Sacaba and Senkata massacres. The Trump White House hailed the coup against Morales as “a significant moment for democracy in the Western Hemisphere.” MAGA’s Man in La Paz Mariaca was elected by the Assembly in October 2024, during the government of former socialist President Luis Arce, to a six-year term. Arce himself has been jailed in preventive detention since December by the pro-US Paz administration; just last week, a judge refused to release him despite illness. The other socialist former president, Evo Morales, has been holed up in his compound in the Chapare coca-growing region, where he is shielded by supporters from an arrest warrant. Paz has threatened that Morales’s “days are numbered .” Morales has interpreted Mariaca’s arrest as evidence of who is really running the country. “It confirms that in Bolivia, the United States governs,” he said . “It is open and direct interference.” The point of the operation, he added, was to decide “whom they will persecute and detain in order to save Cerimedo — and saving Cerimedo is saving Rodrigo Paz and his family.” The investigation Mariaca was leading goes to the heart of the Paz government. “ MAGA’s man in Latin America” kept an office on the twenty-third floor of the presidential palace, sat in on cabinet meetings, and controlled the president’s communications; one Bolivian analyst called him “the closest thing to a prime minister the country had.” Cerimedo also worked for Milei and Kast, and de la Espriella’s campaign has ties to his former business partner. Milei, Kast, and de la Espriella are all now presidents of their respective countries. At a ceremony at Mar-a-Lago in February, where Cerimedo was honored as the year’s top Hispanic political consultant, he thanked his business partner Brad Parscale, Donald Trump’s former campaign manager, who has since registered as a foreign agent for Israel . Photos have also surfaced of him with Donald Trump Jr . Since his arrest, the powerful in Bolivia have kept their distance; Paz insists that “Mr Cerimedo never had the authorization to represent [him].” But Cerimedo first cut his teeth in Brazil. Between the two rounds of the 2022 presidential election, Eduardo Bolsonaro, son of then President Jair Bolsonaro, traveled to Buenos Aires to meet Cerimedo . フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Jan Kandiyali ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-08T16:07:55+00:00

Not too long ago, a wave of books written by left-wing and liberal commentators appeared celebrating a future in which work would be a thing of the past. The increased productivity of technology would free humans from drudgery and allow them to focus on cultural pursuits and spend more time making friends or finding love, these polemics alleged. Amid the near-daily stream of stories warning about the disastrous social effects that artificial intelligence may have, not just on the labor market but on people’s literacy and numeracy, this earlier mood seems at best misguided. In his recently published book , Flourishing Together: Karl Marx’s Vision of the Good Society , Jan Kandiyali, a professor of philosophy at Durham University in England, argues that his subject’s views about work and the good life have been deeply misunderstood. Yes, Marx was a profound critic of capitalism. He saw the system as exploitative and harmful to human relations of friendship and love. But he also thought that contributing productively to society was a vital component of the good life. In a wide-ranging discussion, Kandiyali tells Jacobin why socialists mustn’t abandon this vision. The path to building a better society runs through, rather than past, recognizing the social value of work. Hugo de Camps We usually think of Marx mainly as a critic of capitalism. In Flourishing Together , you argue that his work also contains a positive account of human flourishing. What is, in your view, a good society for Marx? Jan Kandiyali I agree that people generally think of Marx as a critic of capitalism. The book is motivated by two thoughts: First, that Marx was more concerned with the question of what a good society would look like than people tend to think. And second, that it is a mistake for socialists to confine themselves to criticism of existing society without offering an account of an alternative. As G. A. Cohen once said, unless you write recipes for the cookshops of the future, there is no guarantee you will get the food you like. I understand a good society as one that enables its members to flourish. The word “enables” is important here. It is not about guaranteeing that people flourish or forcing them to, but about giving them the real opportunity to do so. Moreover, in Marx’s view, a vital component of human flourishing is self-realization in work. So, a good society is one that enables self-realization in work, among other things. Hugo de Camps How, exactly, do you understand work in this context? Jan Kandiyali Defining work is difficult, and I do not offer a strict definition. I am interested in self-realization in work, understood as activity that enables people to develop their powers and capacities while also meeting the needs of others. That differs from employment understood simply as wage labor. In my sense of the term, people can work without being employed, for example through charity work. Conversely, someone can be employed without working in this sense. Think of David Graeber’s “bullshit jobs” or work that is actively harmful, such as maximizing the addictiveness of gambling technologies. You can receive a wage without engaging in the kind of self-realizing work that I am interested in. Hugo de Camps At the center of the book is what you call the mutualist interpretation, contrasted with the parallelist view associated with G. A. Cohen and Jon Elster. On the parallelist view, individuals may flourish alongside one another, but not necessarily through one another. What would you say is missing from this reading, and why do you find the mutualist interpretation more convincing? Jan Kandiyali The parallelist reading, which is my term, says that people realize themselves simply by developing their powers and capacities. They may do so in ways that benefit others, but this is not required: their activity might simply benefit themselves. Cohen illustrates this with the example of a jazz band. Cohen says: one way of understanding Marx’s vision of the good society is to imagine a jazz band in which each player is motivated by their own self-realization. Now, in this jazz band, each player needs their fellow musicians to provide the music for their self-realization. But they do not value their cooperation or the fact that they are providing the conditions for one another’s self-realization. On the parallelist view, then, we achieve self-realization alongside others, through our parallel projects of self-realization, but not necessarily through contributing to one another’s flourishing. I contrast it with the mutualist interpretation. On the mutualist interpretation, people realize themselves through providing others with the various goods and services they need to pursue their flourishing. So, the mutualist interpretation is a more social and relational vision of the good society. Now, to turn directly to your question, the short answer is that the parallelist vision leaves out the value of solidarity. By solidarity, I mean concern for others and finding value in activities through which we contribute to their lives. I think Marx and the socialist tradition more generally saw these solidaristic relationships as part of human flourishing. Without them, your life may go well in some respects, but you are missing something important. In a nutshell, that is what the parallelist account leaves out. Hugo de Camps You describe mutual self-realization as developing our capacities in ways that provide others with the goods and services they need to flourish. Voluntary care seems a clear case, but what about entertainment or luxury goods? Put differently: Does mutual flourishing depend on distinguishing genuine human needs from preferences shaped by the market? Jan Kandiyali I understand needs as things required for people to flourish. That means that we can distinguish genuine needs from preferences shaped by the market — what Marxists, particularly in the tradition of the Frankfurt School, have called false needs. We might desire certain things we do not need. But at the same time, we should not think of needs in too restricted a way. Marx had a broad and rich understanding of what we need to flourish. In his early writings, he contrasts the wealth and poverty of capitalism with the “rich human being” and “rich human need” associated with communism. The ideal is not merely to satisfy basic physical needs. Cultural goods, aesthetic enjoyment, and entertainment, for example, also count as needs. Marx’s ideal was not one of an austere life of self-denial, nor should ours be. Hugo de Camps Recognition by others forms part of your account of socialization through productive activity, but recognition can also be gained through friendship, care, political activity, or artistic creation. Is there anything distinctive, in your view, about work as a source of self-realization? Jan Kandiyali I am not claiming that the recognition we receive through friendship, family, or other activities is unimportant. But there is, I think, something distinctive about the recognition we receive for our productive contributions. Recognition within the family or among friends typically has a certain character. Especially within the family, it tends to be relatively unconditional: it does not typically depend upon your specific achievements. In productive activity, by contrast, you are recognized primarily for what you contribute. If I write a book, for example, other people assess its merits and shortcomings and recognize me on the basis of that contribution. So, my answer to your question would be quite Hegelian: we have needs for different types of recognition — as family members, friends, producers, and citizens. These forms are distinct and cannot simply substitute for one another. Hugo de Camps You present Marx’s vision of socialism as an alternative to liberal egalitarian theories of justice such as John Rawls’s. While liberal egalitarians focus primarily on distribution, your reading of Marx emphasizes production, self-realization, and social relations. What can this account explain that a theory centered on distribution cannot? Jan Kandiyali The liberal egalitarianism of Rawls and his immediate followers is an impressive body of work, but I think it has two limitations. First, its focus on distribution leaves out the sphere of production. Perhaps surprisingly, one of the first people to make this point was the libertarian philosopher Robert Nozick, who argued that liberal egalitarians such as Rawls can write as though resources exist in a “big social pot” waiting to be distributed. But obviously enough, those goods have to be produced in the first place. So, liberal egalitarianism has traditionally lacked a theory of what Lucas Stanczyk calls “productive justice.” This lacuna has, to a large degree, been addressed in recent years. (For a Marxist theory of it, I recommend Paul Gomberg’s excellent book , How to Make Opportunity Equal: Race and Contributive Justice .) Second, the focus on justice leaves out a concern with the good society and what it means for people’s lives to go well. Justice is sometimes glossed as giving people their due. But people can receive their due while living bad lives. For example, they might get their due but pursue worthless conceptions of the good, such as amassing an endless supply of consumer goods. Or they might be treated fairly by social institutions but lack any solidarity with their fellows. Concerns with justice and distribution are obviously important, but they leave out important dimensions of human flourishing. So Marx gives us a way of bringing a concern with work and human flourishing back into debates in political philosophy. Hugo de Camps In the book, you present a critique of post-work politics and argue that socialism should aim to transform work rather than reduce its role. Can you explain why? Jan Kandiyali There is a divide between socialists who seek to transform work and those who seek to reduce its importance. My book belongs squarely in the former camp, as I think that work can be an important good for us when it is organized in the right way. I believe that a life in which everything we needed appeared like manna from heaven would be far from ideal. In such a world, human beings would no longer need one another to provide the goods and services required for flourishing. We could, of course, still read poetry, play football, or watch films together, but the collective activity of meeting one another’s needs would disappear. This implies that a post-work world would be, in one respect, worse than one in which our labor is required. Current anxieties about AI and technologically driven unemployment seem to me to support this view. Part of the worry about AI is that people will lose an income, but I do not think this is their sole concern. People are also worried about what they would do if AI took their jobs. Of course, many people also want to work less and have more time for family, friends, and other activities, but I do not think that most people want to eliminate work altogether. Hugo de Camps If productive contribution is an important part of human flourishing, does this risk creating new hierarchies between forms of work? And how does your account accommodate those who, because of disability or illness, may be unable to participate in productive activity? Jan Kandiyali I will take up the issue of hierarchy and the issue of disability and illness separately. With regard to hierarchy between different forms of work, the book offers a simple solution. A standard Marxist line is that automation will solve the problem of drudgery. I am not against automating drudgery, but I am skeptical that it can completely solve the issue here. My view is that the drudgery should be shared among everyone able to perform it. That would help overcome hierarchies between different forms of work because drudgery would then be done by everyone. With regard to those who cannot work because of illness and disability, we should be cautious of thinking that the ill or the disabled cannot work. As proponents of the social model of disability have argued, much depends on the social environment. Maybe some people cannot work now. But with appropriate adjustments, they might be able to. Of course, it is true that even with all the adjustments, some people still will not be able to work. In Marx’s vision of the good society, the relevant principle is “From each according to their abilities, to each according to their needs!” This means that those who cannot work will not be materially any worse off than those who can, since needs are met irrespective of prior labor contribution. However, it is an implication of the view — and perhaps a difficult one — that people who are unable to produce miss out on a vital component of human flourishing. Hugo de Camps How do you see the relationship between ideal theory and more immediate political questions? Jan Kandiyali Flourishing Together is primarily a work of ideal, or utopian, political theory. It asks what a genuinely good society might look like and uses that ideal to critique capitalism. The final chapter does discuss issues of transition, but it discusses transition from what Marx called the lower phase of communism to the higher. It does not provide a detailed route from present-day capitalism. Clarifying the level at which the argument operates is important when we turn to more immediate political questions. My criticism of universal basic income (UBI), for example, does not mean that UBI could not be useful now, perhaps as a way of reducing poverty. Questions like that depend on various concerns about institutions, public support, trade unions, and political organization. I do not deal directly with these issues in the book, but I think it is a very good thing that more work within political philosophy is now considering these questions. Overall, I see nonideal political philosophy and the ideal project of imagining a good society as complementary. El enorme electorado de São Paulo proporcionó una ventaja particularmente importante a la derecha. Estas divisiones no pueden explicarse mediante una oposición entre una población ilustrada y otra ignorante. On September 20, 2026, Die Linke achieved a historic result. With 25.7% of the vote, the party became the largest in the Berlin parliament for the first time. The traditional parties of power (the CDU, the SPD, and the Greens) are left standing. A left-wing government with Elif Eralp as governing mayor is, in theory, within reach. The reason for this success is no mystery. It is substance. During the campaign, thousands of Die Linke volunteers went out into the neighborhoods. Door to door. Building to building. Up one stairwell, down the next. They came not with shiny brochures, but with one fundamental question: what keeps people awake at night? For hundreds of thousands of Berliners, the answer is painfully clear: rent. The envelope that lands on the doormat every month. The knowledge that an ever-larger share of their wages flows straight into the bank account of some anonymous slumlord. Die Linke wants to make rents affordable, break the power of listed real estate giants, and finally implement the outcome of the referendum. In that 2021 referendum, more than one million Berliners voted for the socialization of large housing stock. Berliners consider no other party as competent on housing. The figures speak for themselves: after the election, 37% of all Berliners say Die Linke has the best solutions for affordable housing. The establishment strikes back: the great reversal of the debate Die Linke won on material issues: housing, rent, ownership. But within no time, the press and the talk shows have all but stopped talking about any of it. Suddenly the political debate is dominated by accusations of antisemitism. By stories about the Rote Hilfe, a left-wing solidarity organization that provides legal support to activists and prisoners. By alleged connections to criminal “clans.” By the suggestion that parts of the party lie outside the acceptable democratic framework. Suddenly, the burning question in the media is no longer how to lower rents, but whether Die Linke is “even fit to govern at all.” It is the great reversal of the debate. Center stage no longer belongs to the unaffordable rents, the power of real estate corporations, and the housing crisis, but to the legitimacy of the party that won precisely on those issues. Meanwhile, the federal establishment works hard behind the scenes to make any possible socialization of real estate giants, the so-called Vergesellschaftung, legally impossible. Yet Article 15 of the German Constitution makes such socialization possible in principle. What we see here is an organized counterattack from established economic and political power blocs. They open multiple fronts simultaneously – economic, legal, political, and ideological. Let us take a closer look at the nine forces that have now mobilized against Die Linke in Berlin. 1. Real estate capital The most direct clash is with real estate capital. Die Linke wants to expropriate profit-driven housing companies that own more than three thousand units each. That concerns a total of over 200,000 housing units in the city. Among them is Europe’s largest real estate company Vonovia, which alone owns 138,000 units in Berlin. On September 21, the morning after the election, BFW Berlin-Brandenburg, the lobby club of private project developers, pulls the emergency brake. The real estate group warns of “a step backward in housing policy.” Expropriation would slow new construction. Private capital would flee. The BBU, the association of housing companies in Berlin-Brandenburg, also springs into action. In Handelsblatt, Welt, Die Zeit, and Tagesspiegel it warns of “massive investment risks.” In short, as soon as its property and profit model is even slightly threatened, the real estate lobby pulls out all the stops and lets the organ roar. Here you see class struggle in its most material form. Die Linke wants to shift power from large real estate owners to tenants and public ownership. The real estate sector wants to preserve the existing regime of private property, rental income, and investment returns. What is at stake is concrete: who owns the apartments, who controls rental income, where does public money go, and who may decide politically about it? 2. Financial capital Where there is real estate, there are banks. On that same September 21, Die Deutsche Kreditwirtschaft, the powerful umbrella organization of the German banking sector, issues an official warning. Socialization of housing would deter private capital. It would make real estate financing more difficult. It would drive risk premiums sky-high. It would – for hypocrisy knows no bounds – “reduce funds for education and social policy,” as if the German banking sector had ever been guided by such concerns in past decades. Financial markets also react immediately. Vonovia’s stock price takes a hit and falls 1.7% against the trend, to its lowest level since June 2023. Deutsche Wohnen takes a beating too. Why? Because for these guys, housing in Berlin are not places where people live, love, and raise children. For big capital, housing units are investment vehicles and collateral for gigantic mountains of debt. If you remove those homes from the private market, you hit not only the slumlord but also the wealth manager, the banker, and the speculator behind the entire parasitic ecosystem of Frankfurt. That too is class struggle. 3. The Berlin business establishment Traditional Berlin capital had already dug in before the election. The four major employer organizations – the Chamber of Commerce (IHK Berlin), the Handwerkskammer, the VBKI, and the UVB – closed ranks in their so-called Wahlarena der Berliner Wirtschaft, a joint election forum where the main parties had to defend their economic programs before organized Berlin business. Their lobbying does not stop on election day. After Die Linke’s victory, VBKI chairman Harald Christ mounts his warhorse. His message? “Anyone who wants to expropriate private property in this city puts our future at stake.” The VBKI is one of the most elite clubs of Berlin businessmen, of the sort that lives more on returns from property and the labour of others than on their own labour. According to Harald Christ, who himself holds no elected public mandate, a party “that does not clearly distance itself from antisemitism” should not govern either. Here you see how the two lines of attack of the establishment merge perfectly. On one hand, defence of sacred private property. On the other, moral blackmail. Die Linke must be treated as a pariah as long as it does not jump through all the hoops of the establishment. 4. The arms industry A second economic front is the arms industry. For the militarists, Berlin must become a hub in the arms industry. Die Linke sees it differently. The party wants no expansion of military industry in the city, no military propaganda on the streets, and no army recruiters in schools. That is blasphemy in the church of German militarism. In its hunger for new billion-dollar contracts, the German military-industrial complex of Berlin-Brandenburg wants to create a cluster for defence and aviation. Martina Klement, Minister of Economic Affairs in Brandenburg, already threatened that her state would “continue alone if necessary” if the Berlin government blocked its plans. She points an accusing finger at Die Linke’s critical attitude towards the Rüstungsindustrie, the arms industry. That too is class struggle: will Berlin become a cog in the booming war economy, or will the city build a dam against it? 5. The federal state and the institutional veto Pressure on Die Linke comes not only from banks, real estate groups, and lobbies. Parts of the state apparatus are also intervening directly into the conflict. The decision that Die Linke must under no circumstances lead the formation of a Berlin government seems to have been decided even before the polling stations closed on September 20. A few minutes after 6 pm, with the polls barely closed, Federal Chancellor Friedrich Merz gave a speech with one clear message: he continues his course, whatever the election result. A day later, Merz announced he wants to fast-track with a federal law against the socialization of large real estate companies. His coalition had previously made agreements about this, but after Die Linke’s victory it suddenly became urgent. Germany, according to Merz, had to make it clear to international investors that private property remains safe and is not simply expropriated. That it is Merz, of all people, who brings this message is not without meaning. Before his return to politics, he chaired the supervisory board of the German branch of asset manager BlackRock from 2016 to 2020, one of the largest financial players in the world. What is at stake is Article 15 of the German Constitution.Since 1949, it has explicitly stated that land, natural resources, and means of production can be transferred to common ownership or other forms of communal economy by law, provided compensation is regulated by law. That provision has never been applied. The struggle in Berlin is precisely about whether it can be used for the first time against large real estate companies. Other statesmen also do not hold back. For example, Bavarian Minister-President Markus Söder (CSU) threatens to question financial transfers to Berlin if the city pursues too leftward a course. Bavaria is one of the richest states and a major net contributor to Germany’s system of redistribution. Bavaria is rich, Bavaria pays. And so Söder wants to dictate what Berlin votes. On Washington’s instructions, American embassies in Europe feed the witch hunt against the left, while helping the far right into the saddle. Lee en español aquí Brazil’s general election on October 4 delivered a severe warning to the left. Flávio Bolsonaro, the presidential candidate of the Liberal Party (PL), finished ahead of President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, with approximately 47% against 45%. Neither secured a majority, sending them into a runoff on October 25. More troubling than this narrow presidential gap was the advance of bolsonarismo through Congress. The election has strengthened a political movement whose ambitions extend beyond defeating Lula to reshaping the institutions that obstruct its exercise of power. The imprisonment (house arrest) and electoral exclusion of Jair Bolsonaro have not dissolved his movement. His son Flávio carries its presidential banner, while other members of the family have won seats in Congress. Michelle Bolsonaro, Jair’s wife, and Carlos Bolsonaro, his son, enter the Senate; Jair Renan, another son, and Renato, Jair’s brother, enter the Chamber of Deputies. Nepobolsonarismo is an appropriate name for this family’s expansion through the state. Yet the family is only the most visible expression of a broader project, rooted in powerful economic interests and a deeply organized reactionary politics. A presidential contest with continental stakes Lula remains the principal obstacle to the return of this project to the presidency. His defeat would affect workers and peasants throughout Brazil, but also the possibilities for regional cooperation and an independent Latin American foreign policy. Brazil’s size, industrial capacity, raw minerals (such as critical minerals), agricultural resources, and diplomatic reach give its political direction continental significance. A government that subordinates these assets to Washington’s priorities would weaken the space available to neighboring countries seeking their own paths of development. The first-round geography demonstrates both Lula’s strength and his difficulties. He retained a commanding position in the Northeast, while Flávio led across the other four regions. São Paulo’s enormous electorate provided a particularly important advantage to the right. These divisions cannot be explained by an opposition between an enlightened population and an ignorant one. They require an examination of the different conditions of work, the organization of political life, and the capacity of competing movements to interpret people’s frustrations. Lula approaches the runoff with a substantial social record. Unemployment fell to 5.3% in the quarter ending in August, the lowest rate for that period in the statistical series beginning in 2012. Such improvements matter enormously to families for whom a regular wage brings relief from daily uncertainty. But an improved national indicator does not settle the political argument. Employment can coexist with exhausting journeys, expensive housing, inadequate public services, and the insecurity of debt. The distance between statistical improvement and lived experience remains terrain on which the right operates. Bolsonarismo , or Brazil’s far right of a special type, translates these frustrations into resentment against the left, all public institutions, and vulnerable sections of society. Its promise of order offers an immediate language for fear, while concealing the economic arrangements that produce insecurity. The left cannot answer this merely by advertising government achievements. It must connect those achievements to a credible program for changing everyday life, and explain which interests prevent further advances. A worker struggling with debt needs more than reassurance that the economy is performing well. This has been the problem for Lula, whose program for the future does not seem to promise more than what has been delivered till now. The PL won 19 seats and will hold 28 in the new Senate, making it the largest party. The PT will have nine. The bolsonaristas have therefore established a formidable position, although the PL itself remains short of a majority and must bargain with other parties. Michelle Bolsonaro and Bia Kicis took both seats in the Federal District, while Carlos Bolsonaro and Carol de Toni won the two seats in Santa Catarina. These victories show the movement’s ability to organize coordinated campaigns and concentrate support behind its candidates. There were important successes for the left: Rui Costa and Jaques Wagner won in Bahia, Humberto Costa retained representation in Pernambuco, and Marília Campos won in Minas Gerais. These results preserve points of resistance within an increasingly hostile chamber. The Senate’s significance extends beyond ordinary legislation. The bolsonaristas have made their confrontation with the Supreme Federal Court a central political objective, including demands to impeach its ministers. Their strengthened representation increases their capacity to pursue that confrontation and to influence the election of the Senate president. Predictions of an automatic majority for impeachment should be treated cautiously: party affiliation, campaign declarations, and votes in a specific proceeding are different things. Nevertheless, the danger of using legislative power to intimidate the judiciary has plainly grown. This is part of a struggle over the conditions under which the far right can govern. The judicial proceedings against Jair Bolsonaro did not remove the social forces supporting him. Those forces now seek greater leverage over institutions capable of restraining them. For the left, defending democratic space must therefore accompany the struggle to expand social rights. Workers’ organizations cannot afford to lose the freedoms through which they organize, educate, and confront capital. From Campuses to Factories: On the History of the Independent Socialist Club(s)/International Socialists

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: Historical Materialism ／ 著者: Panagiotis Sotiris ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-08T06:18:03+00:00

Andrew Stone Higgins, ed., From the Free Speech Movement to the Factory Floor: A Collective History of the International Socialists (Chicago: Haymarket Books, 2026) Reviewed by David Camfield Between the mid-1960s and the early 1970s, a new revolutionary Left emerged in the USA. Thousands of young people, radicalised mainly by the Civil Rights and later Black Power movement and the movement against the US’s war in Vietnam came to the conclusion that revolution was necessary and possible. Most of these people looked to one version or another of Marxism-Leninism as the theory needed to guide the New Communist Movement they set out to build. They often saw China as the most important socialist society, a hopeful alternative to a USSR supposedly led since the death of Stalin by counterrevolutionary “revisionists”, although some in the NCM were less critical of the USSR, Cuba, and other “actually existing socialist” societies. [1] However, a sizeable minority of the new revolutionaries rejected the politics of Stalin and Mao in favour of anti-Stalinist Marxism. This most commonly took the form of some kind of Trotskyist orthodoxy. There was also a very small but dynamic heterodox offshoot that had an impact larger than its numbers would have led anyone to expect, and it is that that is the subject of From the Free Speech to the Factory Floor . While its subtitle presents it as a “collective history”, this book of over 400 pages in length is a collection of memoirs preceded by a 23-page introduction by editor Andrew Stone Higgins. [2] The supporters of this current initially identified as Independent Socialists, in continuity with the Independent Socialist League (ISL), the name taken in 1949 by what had previously been the Workers’ Party (formed in 1940). The ISL drifted rightwards under the pressure of the Cold War and dissolved in 1958, with most of its members joining the reformist Socialist Party (SP). In 1964, a few former ISL members, foremost among them Hal Draper, who had edited the ISL’s newspaper, like-minded members of the SP-affiliated Young People’s Socialist League (YPSL), and other Civil Rights movement organisers in Berkeley, California formed an Independent Socialist Club (ISC). The Berkeley ISC was committed to a socialism “both democratic and revolutionary, both humanist and working-class”, a “third choice for the world, as the alternative to both capitalism and Communism”. [3] Its members believed in the potential power of the working class to transform society and that a social revolution that would open the transition to socialism could only be accomplished by the self-emancipation of that class – the essence of what they soon came to call, using concepts presented in Draper’s 1966 pamphlet The Two Souls of Socialism, “socialism from below” politics, in contrast to the various forms of “socialism from above”. [4] The ISC did not pretend to be the nucleus of a future revolutionary socialist workers’ party. It saw the USSR and similar societies run by Communist parties as non-capitalist class societies (bureaucratic collectivism). As Higgins puts it, this group that started with two dozen members was “born and baptized in the fires of the Free Speech Movement, the first major on-campus movement of the 1960s” (p. 11). Within that successful coalition at the Berkeley campus of the University of California, the ISC was arguably “the most important single political tendency” (p. 411) thanks to its constructive, non-sectarian efforts to build the broad movement in a way that was informed by an effective strategy that flowed from its particular socialist outlook. From that point on, “[t]he ISC’s combination of militant action, rigorous study, and political clarity bolstered their reputation on campus and beyond” (p. 12). It began to attract more members in and around Berkeley, while its example encouraged a sprinkling of like-minded socialists, some of them former members of the left wing of the deeply factionalised YPSL, to create similar clubs in their locales. In 1967, these would come together in a federation, the Independent Socialist Clubs of America (ISCA). That same year saw ISC members in California and New York State make herculean efforts to get the Peace and Freedom Party (PFP), whose key demands were US withdrawal from Vietnam and support for Black liberation, on the ballot. In large part due to ISC leaders within it, the PFP was able to create an alliance with the Black Panther Party. Although ISC membership was overwhelmingly white, from its inception, members were strongly committed to the struggle against racism and influenced by the Civil Rights movement. They soon came to support Black self-organisation and Black Power (pp. 112-13). In the view of one contributor to this collection, Joel Geier, “We were as much a product of the civil rights movement as of Trotskyism. … The Black liberation movement lifted us up, liberated us from sectarianism, and facilitated our leap to the New Left” (p. 155). At the same time, ISCers were adopting “a more explicit focus on the American working class – in all its diversity – and, by extension, the labor movement” (p. 17), with a perspective that identified, celebrated, and hoped to contribute to the rising level of workers’ self-organisation visible in wildcat strikes, militant rank-and-file caucuses in unions, and other initiatives. In contrast to much of the NCM, they quickly became supporters of women’s and gay liberation, though not without difficulties (pp. 103-4; pp. 304-6). In 1969, the ISCA, growing in part from people leaving Students for a Democratic Society (SDS) as this premier organisation of the student radicalisation was torn apart by fights between rival ML tendencies, refounded as the International Socialists (IS), a name chosen in part because it was also the name of the socialism-from-below group in Britain with which the ISCA had developed links. The following year, the IS established its centre in Detroit (previously its national office had been in New York City, while its publication had been based in Berkeley). How the group was starting to change and why members decided that it should change is not discussed in Higgins’s introduction, though aspects of the shift can be gleaned from a number of chapters. “It was a sharp turn for the ISCA, which most people didn’t appreciate at the time and a lot of people still don’t appreciate” (p. 127) argues Robert “Gabe” Gabrielsky. The upheavals of 1968, above all in Vietnam, France and Czechoslovakia, provided the impetus. Many young radicals including ISCA members felt that now “Revolution was no longer confined to distant history or abstract propaganda, it was an actuality, on the world agenda, embraced by millions, possible in our lifetime” (p. 162). The IS, as Thomas Harrison recalls, began to remodel itself on what it considered Leninist lines – a centralized revolutionary organization rather than a federation of clubs, aspiring to a working-class rather than a primarily student base. “Discipline” was introduced, meaning that after a position was debated and adopted by a vote, members were forbidden to publicly organize against it. Comrades were urged to pull up stakes, move to the Midwest, and get jobs in auto factories, telephone, trucking, steel, and the Post Office. (p. 46.) The IS was starting to “regear” itself from activity on and around university campuses to workplace activity. [5] Between late 1970 and 1973, political disagreements arising from how the group was changing led to the departure of some former SDSers, the resignation of Draper and a handful of his closest cothinkers (Reorient), the expulsion of a small grouping, the Third Camp Tendency (TCT), including leading Berkeley member Joanne Landy, and the expulsion of a large minority, the Revolutionary Tendency (RT), that had come to support a sectarian version of Trotskyist orthodoxy. The IS was distinguished from other far-left groups by its socialism from below politics and its view that, as David Finkel writes, “building rank-and-file movements and working-class revolutionary socialist organization were equally important tasks, interrelated, but neither one subordinated to the other” (p. 182). Although “industrialisation” – getting a unionised job in one of the sectors designated as “priorities” – was not mandatory for members, there was strong pressure to industrialise. Through the 1970s, many IS members devoted a tremendous amount of energy to workplace organising and trying to turn the group into a “workers’ combat organisation”, with six contributors to From the Free Speech Movement to the Factory Floor describing and reflecting on their experiences. ISers either joined existing caucuses of union militants or helped create new ones, which they always treated as independent democratic workers’ groups, not IS-controlled “fronts”. Unexpectedly, they had their greatest success as freight truck drivers and at UPS, where they were part of the International Brotherhood of Teamsters. IS members were leaders in forming what became Teamsters for a Democratic Union. [6] While IS unionists were able to recruit some coworkers to the IS, few stayed long. Higgins observes that “the IS culture of deep reading, broad discussions, fierce debates, and long, numerous meetings was a hard sell to prospective members with long working hours, pressing familial obligations, and a limited amount of free time” (p. 21). With hindsight, Finkel comments that union militants who joined the IS “realized sooner than many of us that the mass working-class radicalization [which IS members believed was underway – DC] wasn’t happening. Most quietly drifted away” (p. 184). Apart from workplace organising and activity in unions, the IS had a noteworthy success with its youth group, Red Tide. The IS had attracted two groups of socialist high school students in California, one of which was publishing a newspaper, Red Tide. This became the name of the IS’s formally separate youth organisation, which presented itself as a “badass political gang” (p. 334). In 1975-6, core Red Tide members moved to Detroit. There, Red Tide initiated a campaign to free Gary Tyler, a Black teenager sentenced to death. This agitational effort turned Red Tide into a larger, majority-Black group some of whose members were also in the IS. The closing six chapters of the book are by former members of Red Tide. In late 1976, a minority of IS members announced the formation of a Left Faction (LF). This faction, aligned with the group in Britain that had just changed its name from the IS to the Socialist Workers’ Party, was expelled in early 1977 and formed the International Socialist Organization, significantly weakening the IS. [7] The following year, the IS lowered its ambitions, relaxed its expectations of discipline, and replaced its newspaper with a less-frequent magazine. In 1979, the IS was further depleted by the resignation of another group critical of the majority’s approach, the Rank and File Caucus (RFC), which formed Workers’ Power. That year, IS members launched Labor Notes as a “project… resolutely and intentionally independent , editorially, organizationally, and financially”, of the IS (p. 188). The group’s reduced membership soldiered on in the hostile political climate of the Reagan era until the 1986 merger with a group of Fourth International supporters called Socialist Unity, Workers’ Power, and other socialists that created Solidarity. [8] Although From the Free Speech Movement to the Factory Floor does not clearly present all of the basic history of the group that I have summarised here, a careful reader interested in the history of the socialist Left in the US can put together the pieces from its contents. The memories of former members evocatively convey personal experiences from the heady years between the mid-1960s and the mid-1970s when strikes surged, protests filled the streets, and riots shook the country – conditions in which hundreds of thousands of people radicalised and thousands came to consider themselves revolutionary socialists. As contributors look back half a century, they give readers a vivid sense of those exciting times, including some of what it was like to be a socialist organiser at a time when the level of class and social struggle in the USA was much higher than it is today and supporters of socialism from below were so much more optimistic about their political prospects than socialists with at least a reasonably accurate understanding of the current period can be today. Stories of university and high-school student activism, working with leaders of the Black Panthers, and organising as a socialist steelworker, autoworker, or truck driver will likely stay with readers. The inclusion of contributions by people who supported the TCT, the LF, and the RFC as well as supporters of the majorities that disagreed with each of these political minorities enriches the book by reflecting a diversity of IS perspectives (a contribution by a supporter of the RT would have furthered this [9] ). Yet, in spite of its subtitle’s promise of a “collective history”, this collection is mainly an assortment of individual memoirs along with some reflections (both critical and self-vindicating) on an organisation. The editor’s introduction does not signal the limits of memoir for the historical understanding of socialist organisations. As Alan Wald has written, this genre raises the issue of what he calls “the politics of memory”: Individuals sometimes perceive or remember facets of their lives inaccurately for psychological or emotional reasons beyond their conscious control. … sometimes the past is remembered selectively, in accord with the needs of the ideological outlook one has at a given moment or had at some significant moment in the past. The contemporary cultural historian must try to recreate a whole through the use of all available evidence – documents, publications, and correspondence – as well as a wide range of personal interviews, and not be misled by an affinity or dislike for one or another political person. [10] Not registering this does a disservice to readers who do not fully appreciate the limitations of memoir. It is not that participants’ recollections of decades ago are always unreliable, but anyone who is keen to understand the history of a political organisation needs to treat these with considerable caution. Because memoir is self-fashioned, it lacks even the element of structuring by someone else’s well-informed and thoughtful questioning that oral history interviews can introduce. Higgins’s introduction is also wanting in a number of other significant ways. It provides a much fuller overview of the ISC and ISCA years than it does of the IS from 1969 to 1977 and it is silent on the IS from the late 1970s until its end in 1986. It does not identify how the organisation began to change as it renamed itself the IS or describe some developments in the IS between 1973 and 1978 that are crucial for understanding how the organisation fared in those pivotal years of its history. Industrialisation, which Higgins mentions and which became “ de facto policy” [11] by 1973, is only one piece of the puzzle. Missing from the introduction, though not from Michael Letwin’s chapter (p. 334), is mention of how the leadership responded to the recession of 1974-75 as a historic opportunity for building a revolutionary party and so, as Milton Fisk has written, “the IS responded to the recession with a frenzied mobilization of its forces”. [12] This was accompanied with a “Bolshevisation” of the organisation that weakened internal democracy. [13] Finkel’s contribution touches on this, singling out the IS’s then National Secretary, Glenn Wolfe, for responsibility (p. 186). However, nowhere in the book is there reflection on why Wolfe’s egregious practices were accepted by most of the leadership and many members for a couple of years. [14] Also missing from the introduction is a description of how from 1978 on the IS re-evaluated what was happening in the world, acknowledged that it had failed to build a Leninist workers’ combat organisation and that a “heavy dose of voluntarism” [15] had gone along with the effort to do so, and drastically shifted its conception of members’ tasks. The split that created Workers’ Power also goes unmentioned. [16] Nor does the introduction attempt to offer even the briefest multifaceted description of the changing political culture of the ISCA/IS, something that would have been helpful for readers. Less important, there are several claims of questionable accuracy in the introduction as well as minor factual errors in a number of chapters. [17] Higgins suggests that the ISC made “[t]wo key mistakes”: not building beyond Berkeley at the start and not entering SDS (p. 21). [18] From my perspective, what stands out as the most important mistake was the adoption by the IS – like a number of previously looser socialist organisations at the time, including the British IS [19] – of a “Leninist” micro-party form of organisation in the wake of 1968. If I may be permitted an imperfect metaphor, this introduced genes into the organism that both facilitated its growth and development and began to produce mutations that later, in response to environmental changes, became increasingly damaging to its health. Reorient sensed that this organisational form was deeply flawed. The TCT warned of “voluntarism” and what they saw as the premature adoption of “national discipline” by an inexperienced and politically unclear IS, while not “disput[ing] the need for a cadre organization or a vanguard party”. [20] That said, I do not think that anyone in the IS presented a coherent better alternative to the micro-party model (organising the IS as a “Leninist organisation”). [21] This model was adopted because most members were persuaded that it responded to the need they correctly recognised for a way of organising that was more capable of enabling them to participate effectively in workers’ struggles and build the forces of socialism from below than the loose federative model of the ISCA. With the benefit of hindsight, the adoption of the micro-party model can be seen as a mistake that socialists today should not repeat, one that ultimately did more harm than good for a group of socialists who were right about so much that mattered, some of whose extraordinary efforts and genuine accomplishments are recounted in this book. Indian students and opposition parties call for national mobilization on October 10 against election commissioner

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Abdul Rahman ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-07T23:49:55+00:00

Scores of the elected opposition members of India’s Parliament were detained by security forces in the capital, New Delhi, on Tuesday, October 6, after they protested against the alleged rigging in the elections and demanded removal of the country’s Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) Gyanesh Kumar. The Leader of the Opposition Rahul Gandhi and several other MPs also claimed they were manhandled by the police during the detention and forcefully removed from the protests site. All of the detainees were released later. The protests by the MPs was a part of the ongoing nationwide mobilizations which broke out on Saturday over alleged rigging and irregularities in various elections under the incumbent CEC. The protesters are demanding his immediate removal claiming he manipulated the elections in various states to benefit the ruling party. Despite the heavyhandedness of the security forces who have refused to allow any protest gathering in the capital and repeatedly detained opposition leaders, students, and youth group protesters, people turned up on the streets of Delhi and other parts of the country for the consecutive fifth day on Wednesday . Several protesters have claimed they were beaten up during detention while some women reporters have claimed they were molested by police personnel on duty during those protests. Also on Tuesday, security forces allegedly attacked a cultural gathering organized around the issue by the left-wing Student Federation of India (SFI) in central Delhi. On Wednesday, major left and progressive student organizations such as SFI, the All India Students Association (AISA), and the Cockroach Janta Party (CJP), a movement which emerged in protests demanding reforms in the country’s education system earlier this year, announced a new joint protest beginning from October 10. Rigging in elections is murdering democracy Rahul Gandhi, who is also the leader of India’s main opposition party, the Indian National Congress (INC), claimed around 300 opposition MPs were marching from the country’s Parliament building to the office of the Election Commission of India (ECI), seeking a meeting with the CEC when the security forces stopped them and detained them. He reiterated that the ruling Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP), in collaboration with the CEC, has been responsible for destroying democracy in the country by rigging elections. He called the act “treason” and warned that none of the people responsible for “murdering democracy” in the country will be spared whether it is the Prime Minister Narendra Modi, Home Minister Amit Shah, or Gyanesh Kumar. The ultra-right-wing and Hindu-supremacist BJP has been in power since 2014, winning three national elections, including the last one in 2024. It is also ruling a majority of states in India. Speaking in a press conference in Delhi on Wednesday, Neha Bora, president of AISA and one of the leaders of the movement, claimed that the movement against the manipulations in the national elections by the CEC and the BJP will continue until the removal of Gyanesh Kumar. Over 130 million Indians have been removed from electoral rolls in the country since the ECI, the highest body responsible for conducting elections in the country, started the so-called Special Intensive Revision (SIR) last year under Gyanesh Kumar’s leadership. Opposition parties and legal experts have claimed that the SIR is illegal, as it is not mentioned in the ECI rule books and the process was started to manipulate election results to favor the ruling BJP. A recent report in one of the main English daily paper’s in the country also claimed that most of the decisions taken by Gyanesh Kumar as CEC were illegal. In the last few weeks, opposition has also alleged that the ECI and BJP collaborated to remove thousands of names, mostly Muslim voters, from the electoral rolls in various constituencies across the country. Talking to the media on Tuesday, John Britas MP from the Communist Party of India (Marxist) claimed that protests against Gyanesh Kumar are a “historical movement to save democracy” in the country. He accused Kumar, in collaboration with the BJP, of destroying the reputation of India’s electoral process and of being the “real enemy of the nation.” Britas called for his immediate removal as CEC and demanded he must be tried for his criminal activities.
