Trillion Case for Medicare for All

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Farrah Hassen ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-12T23:01:36+00:00

America’s affordability crisis played a major role in this year’s midterm elections — and few things in American life have become more unaffordable than health care. It’s about to get worse as premiums rise and Medicaid cuts from the GOP’s so-called “Big Beautiful Bill” take effect. So if Democrats want to deliver on their promise to make life more affordable, then they must use their new congressional majorities to pass Medicare for All. The United States spends more on health care yet has worse health outcomes than comparable high-income countries. In surveys, health care costs remain the top financial burden for adults and families , even when they’re insured. Around 85 million people in the U.S. are either uninsured or underinsured, according to Rep. Pramila Jayapal’s (D-WA) office. Six in ten uninsured adults polled said they’ve recently skipped or postponed care because of the staggering costs. Related: America’s Mass Surveillance State: How AI and Flock Cameras Are Tracking Us It’s no wonder that a record two-thirds of all U.S. adults now support a national health plan like Medicare for All. According to a recent Yale study , single-payer universal health coverage would save more than 100,000 lives a year and cost $1 trillion less than our current, profit-driven system. The study’s authors reached these findings by modeling what would happen if the U.S. adopted a single-payer system like the one proposed in the “Medicare for All Act.” Introduced in 2025 by Jayapal, Rep. Debbie Dingell (D-MI), and Senator Bernie Sanders (I-VT), the Medicare for All Act would expand Medicare to provide comprehensive health benefits to every U.S. resident. It would include primary care, mental health, dental, vision, hearing, reproductive care, prescription drugs, and more. Patients would choose their doctors without crushing copays, deductibles, and other coverage restrictions. Physicians would be able to focus more on healing their patients instead of “battling insurers over denials and delays,” as Dr. Diljeet K. Singh, president of Physicians for a National Health Program, summed it up in her endorsement of the legislation. Related: The Solution to Homelessness Is a Front Door, Not a Jail Cell Much of the spending in our current health system is “consumed by administrative middlemen, soaring drug prices, and emergency care for conditions that should have been treated earlier and for less,” said Dr. Alison Galvani, the Yale study’s senior author. Galvani and her co-authors found that the Medicare for All Act would reduce health spending by lowering pharmaceutical prices, cutting administrative overhead, cracking down on fraudulent billing, preventing avoidable emergency care, and applying savings from Medicare payment rates to all providers. “Our results indicate that the existing [federal] budget is more than sufficient to cover everyone at lower total cost,” they conclude . Most importantly, a universal health care system would save lives. The Yale researchers calculate that it would prevent around 114,000 people from dying each year due to lack of health care. Their findings are no outlier — other studies have also found that a single-payer system would improve existing coverage while saving money. Elected Democrats remain divided on Medicare for All, but universal health care is wildly popular among their voters. Several incoming lawmakers campaigned on this issue and won, like Senator-elect Dr. Abdul El-Sayed (D-MI). Although the Affordable Care Act expanded access to care, it has done so by benefiting the very for-profit insurance companies that contribute to the affordability problem. Far too many people still end up drowning in medical debt . Related: Racists Target American Muslims Despite Constitutional Rights Universal health care is both sound public policy and a human right. The “right to the enjoyment of the highest attainable standard of physical and mental health” has long been recognized as a fundamental human right under international law that all governments are obligated to respect, protect, and fulfill. Nevertheless, the U.S. remains the only high-income nation in the world without universal health coverage . We need a health care system that guarantees this human right for all, not one that guarantees more profits for Big Insurance and Wall Street investors. The next Congress must meet the moment by making Medicare for All a reality instead of an empty campaign slogan. OtherWords

On the very morning of last week’s critical US midterm elections, President Donald Trump made what seemed like a shocking announcement: no more aid to Israel. “Bibi Netanyahu and I have made a deal that we give Israel $4 billion a year, and we made a deal months ago that we’re stopping,” Trump said . He went on to make two seemingly contradictory assertions: “We won’t be paying Israel” and “We’ll help Israel if they need it.” In truth, this prompted little euphoria among those who have long campaigned against US funding of Israel. The lack of enthusiasm can be explained by two factors: firstly, few take Trump’s pledges seriously and, secondly, his remarks were widely understood as a desperate attempt to stave off what was shaping up to be a disastrous election outcome. Related: Palestinians Beat Israel's Political System at Its Own Game The election ultimately delivered one of the most humiliating defeats in Republican Party history, with Democrats capturing both the Senate and the House of Representatives and making sweeping gains across the country. Republicans quickly turned against Trump, who made the unusual admission that the war on Iran had contributed to their defeat. Vice President J.D. Vance, widely considered a potential presidential contender in 2028, was more explicit. “The reason we lost is the war,” Vance posted , while calling for Republicans to “change our relationship to Israel.” Trump’s rushed Election Day announcement was an unmistakable attempt to influence voters before they cast their ballots Though Vance was criticized by some within his party for naming Israel, the clearest indication that unconditional US support for Tel Aviv had become a major political liability came from Trump himself. First, his rushed Election Day announcement was an unmistakable attempt to influence voters before they cast their ballots. Trump failed but, in making the announcement, he revealed that he and his party understood Israel had become a major factor in their political predicament. Indeed, Trump described the $4 billion annual payment as “a bad talking point,” complaining that critics were using it against Republicans. Second, Trump’s acknowledgment that the Iran war had hurt Republicans exposed the very Israel connection that Vance was prepared to articulate more openly. The war on Iran is not America’s war. It is Benjamin Netanyahu’s war, pursued as part of the Israeli prime minister’s long-standing quest to dominate the Middle East, with Washington paying the military, economic and political price. This is hardly hyperbole. Netanyahu has repeatedly presented Iran as an existential enemy, while his far-right allies have openly celebrated American military involvement. Washington can continue financing Israel’s military even when regular assistance becomes politically controversial Ironically, Washington’s own 2026 National Defense Strategy identified defending the US homeland and asserting American interests throughout the Western Hemisphere as its foremost priorities. Yet this administration plunged the country into another devastating Middle Eastern war. Thanks to independent journalists and analysts operating outside mainstream media, Americans have become increasingly aware of the damage this relationship with Israel has inflicted on their country’s economy, military standing and international reputation. According to Quinnipiac University, 48 percent of registered American voters now believe the US is too supportive of Israel, compared with just 16 percent in January 2017. However, this growing awareness, coupled with the seismic shift in Congress following the midterms, is still not enough to guarantee tangible and irreversible change. The midterm results nevertheless offer a ray of hope that America is not a lost cause after all and that ordinary people possess enough knowledge and determination to challenge the powerful interests, megadonors and shadowy agendas shaping their country’s foreign policy. There is a fundamental lesson here: Americans can no longer be satisfied with empty promises. And this brings us back to Trump’s announcement. Israel is the largest cumulative recipient of US foreign assistance, with the Council on Foreign Relations estimating that American aid since 1948 has exceeded $300 billion, adjusted for inflation. But that staggering figure hardly tells half the story. Washington has consistently found ways to finance Israel’s wars beyond the declared annual military assistance of $3.8 billion. Related: Netanyahu or Not, Israel's Occupation of Palestine Will Continue For example, in April 2024, President Joe Biden signed emergency legislation providing Israel with an additional $8.7 billion in direct military assistance, separate from the regular annual allocation. The money included $3.5 billion in additional foreign military financing and $5.2 billion for missile defense programs. These extraordinary funding mechanisms demonstrate how Washington can continue financing Israel’s military even when regular assistance becomes politically controversial. And that is only part of the picture. Congress has been considering proposals to expand US-Israeli defense technology cooperation, potentially shifting more spending into Pentagon research, development and procurement accounts. In essence, Israel could continue benefiting from billions in American military spending without that expenditure being labeled conventional foreign aid. What Trump appears to be offering, therefore, is a sleight of hand: remove the politically damaging label of aid while preserving, and potentially expanding, the military relationship itself. A Democratic-controlled Congress could block some of these initiatives. But many Democrats remain committed supporters of Israel and the American Israel Public Affairs Committee will continue to spend heavily to influence congressional elections. True, association with the lobby has become a political liability for some candidates. But AIPAC and its allies can be expected to seek alternative strategies, political vehicles and alliances to preserve their influence. The election results are certainly a major step in the right direction but America’s political civil war is only just beginning: progressive Democrats against the party establishment, disillusioned Republicans against “Make America Great Again” loyalists, independent media against mainstream institutions, and so on. The good news, for now, is that the tireless work of generations of conscientious Americans is finally paying off. Thanks in no small part to their efforts, millions of Americans are beginning to recognize that Israel is not a friend of their country and that this sinister relationship must end once and for all. Reactionary Feminists Offer Bad Solutions to Real Problems

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Kaja Kröger ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-12T20:38:36+00:00

To more and more people, the promises of sex-positive liberal feminism feel empty. The sexual ideas that have dominated feminism in the last two decades — including that sexual liberation always serves women’s interests, that consent is the only relevant sexual ethic, and that consuming porn and buying sex are benign practices like any other — are beginning to ring hollow. In this context, an emergent theoretical strand with the pointed, self-chosen name “reactionary feminism” has drawn considerable attention. It calls into question the liberal assertion that every form of sexual morality is anti-feminist — a view the Left has absorbed in recent decades. Although they work to maintain an appearance of ideological ambivalence, reactionary feminists are aligned with the post-liberal right , and they are finding success in attracting to that project women who are disenchanted with feminism’s wholesale embrace of sexual openness for its own sake. Their prescriptions are often at odds with what many feminists would consider emancipatory, but they are identifying real flaws in liberal feminism, and the Left should take notice. At the forefront of this new conservative feminism are British authors Mary Harrington and Louise Perry. Both have written popular books on how postwar sexual liberation in the West has failed women. In her 2022 book The Case Against the Sexual Revolution , Perry argues that shifting cultural norms around casual sex have led to a hookup culture that frustrates many women — even if they endorse these norms politically. In Perry’s telling, this is no wonder: as rigid sexual morality has receded, a liberal feminist paradigm has taken its place, one that de-exceptionalizes sex and portrays it as inconsequential. With contraception and a sexual culture in which casual sex doesn’t require emotional commitments, why would we need to have special guardrails around sex? For straight women on the dating market, this often manifests as feeling simultaneously sexually pursued and emotionally neglected. Many liberal women are responding to this dilemma with heteropessimism, or a bleak outlook on male-female pairings altogether. While reactionary feminism indulges its own form of gender fatalism (I return to this below), Perry is suggesting that these disappointed women look elsewhere: the problem is not heterosexuality but a culture of sexual casualness pursued erroneously in the name of progress. By making implicit liberal assumptions about casual sex and social progress explicit, Perry taps into intuitions most people share: for instance, that the expectation of emotional commitment after having sex is not too much to ask, or that sex can be a site of exceptional vulnerability and risk. Her willingness to say these things aloud has earned her an audience eager to hear something more nuanced than slogans about sexual autonomy. Frustration With Liberal Sexual Culture In academic feminism, sex is often presented as a banal activity, like going on a walk together. What happens in the course of sex is equivalent to deciding to go down this path or climb up this hill — with both people able to say at any time that they would like to turn around and go back home. This neutralized view of sex might appear emancipatory, promising as it does to overturn the sexual scripts and social stigmas that hold up the status quo. But unfortunately, it doesn’t align with actual experience. The vulnerability and boundary crossing involved in sex can have far-reaching consequences if someone takes advantage of them. For most people, the stakes of sex going well or poorly are thus much higher than those of going on a walk, even when people consider it casual. The broad Left’s consensus on this view of sex poses a dilemma for many women. If I’m expecting emotional engagement from a man after a hookup, does that secretly make me “trad” and a conservative? If monogamy fosters moralism and possessiveness in sexuality, does that not make nonmonogamy far more feminist? The success of Perry’s book, which came out when #MeToo was still relatively fresh, and there was cultural space to consider the potential downsides of sexual liberalism, suggested that many women had been privately entertaining these questions for years. Perry shined a light on the gap between political endorsement of goals like sexual freedom and conflicted attitudes about what that actually looks like in real life. There is no doubt that being “sexually liberated” — expressed, for instance, by engaging in nonmonogamy and kink or BDSM — has accrued social status and moral value among progressive feminists. Countless self-help books and articles about ethical nonmonogamy have come out in recent years. It seems simply cool to be chill enough to have an open relationship and not to really want someone to commit only to you. The alternative of wanting a committed romantic partner is, by contrast, conservative and uncool. Last year, writer Chanté Joseph went viral with a Vogue article that aptly captured this dynamic and asked, “Is having a boyfriend embarrassing now?” One of the women Joseph quotes says that having a boyfriend feels “Republican.” A hard blow for romantics. At the same time, it has also become cool to enjoy more physically violent sex. We can see this most evidently in the mainstreaming of “kinky sex” in popular culture since Fifty Shades of Grey . Data backs this up. If you’re a woman under twenty-four, the chance that your partner choked you during your last sexual encounter is about one in three, according to a representative survey by Indiana University researcher Debby Herbenick. Among millennial women (age twenty-five to thirty-nine), the figure was only 11 percent, and for women above the age of forty, it was 3 percent. This is an immense shift in sexual behavior across a single generation. In interviews with psychologist Nicola Gavey, young women who experienced such “rough sex” said they felt pressured by current sexual norms to make themselves want it in order not to seem boring or prudish. The reality of these parallel cultural developments is what makes Perry’s critique of liberal sexual culture more complicated than closed-minded conservative moralism. Marxist Rhetoric Reactionary feminists are not hesitant to offer women well-established conservative solutions for sexual liberalism’s discontents: refrain from casual sex, don’t get drunk with strange men around, get married. But they wrap these solutions in new leftist rhetoric. For many liberal and leftist feminists, marriage is a historically sexist institution aimed at controlling women and property. For Mary Harrington, by contrast, marriage enables “radical solidarity between the sexes.” It acts “as the smallest possible unit of resistance to overwhelming economic, cultural and political pressure to be lone atoms in a market,” as she writes in her 2023 book Feminism Against Progress . Rather than striving to be a girlboss or buying yourself flowers, Harrington proposes an attractive vision of monogamous marriage as radical solidarity. Who wants to be a lone atom in the market? In her book, Harrington argues that the belief in progress has led feminism astray: toward individual freedom, away from solidarity and care for others. For Harrington, this has to do with extending the concepts of freedom and trade from the economy to human relationships. But this “logic of individual profit” goes against our human nature as social animals. Harrington therefore wants to defend the relational interests of humans against “the market’s power to liquefy, to commodify and to alienate.” The concepts of commodification and alienation are laden with the Marxist critique that capitalism is not the right way to organize work and production. From a Marxist perspective, commodification and alienation are wrong because they thwart what is most distinctive about being human: free conscious activity (you could call that human nature). In the reactionary feminist view, things are not as clear. The cases of commodification they focus on are surrogacy, prostitution, and paying another woman to take care of one’s children. Reactionary feminists are quick to use leftist rhetoric in their critiques, but they remain vague about why surrogacy and prostitution are wrong. Is it because no one should divert women’s bodies from their “proper” use — reproductive sex within heterosexual marriage? Or is it because of the exceptional nature of exploitation involved in renting a woman’s womb or circumventing the mutuality of sexual attraction with money? We can find indications for both lines of argument in Harrington and Perry. Struggle of Interests For Perry, it is no wonder that men and women have had very different experiences in this liberated sexual culture. She argues that men and women have fundamentally opposing interests in the “sexual marketplace,” as she calls it, a contention that revolves around divergent proclivities and vulnerabilities rooted in biological reality. Women may be choosier about mates, for example, because women are uniquely vulnerable to becoming pregnant and bearing children after a sexual encounter, whereas men’s reproductive imperatives may motivate them toward sexual novelty and variety. For Perry, drawing on a concept that the Marxist radical feminist Shulamith Firestone first introduced, women thus constitute a “sex class,” which is locked in conflict with an opposing class, even while heterosexual men and women are oriented toward each other. If women and men simply have different interests by nature, then the possible political solutions seem reassuringly simple. One can become a revolutionary to overthrow nature (Firestone wanted to outsource pregnancy to artificial wombs) or become fatalistic about sexual relationships between men and women. While reactionary feminists tend to push back against heteropessimism, they are guilty of a version of it themselves. To them, women and men can never fully overcome their fundamental struggle of interests. We must all instead make peace with them and tame them through institutions like marriage, which they argue contains the excesses of an unchecked sexual marketplace. The problem with proclaiming an eternal struggle of interests between women and men is that it obscures the forces of socialization on gender relations and sexual violence. It takes women’s submission and men’s aggression to be natural facts. For Perry in particular, each notable difference in men and women’s behavior ultimately comes down to how women and men are simply wired differently and always have been. Although she is a critical thinker in other regards, Perry is captivated by evolutionary psychology and very keen to incorporate every “just-so” story that emanates from the field. Feminists have often disputed and criticized this science for naturalizing what is inherently social: gender roles. In the status quo, then, Perry might be right that women and men have different interests in the “sexual marketplace.” But when we take gender roles into account — and resist being satisfied by speculative stories that neatly explain how each gender stereotype has a deep root in our prehistory — we can begin to see that it needn’t be this way forever. Conservative Materialism With their Marxist rhetoric, reactionary feminists pick up, in a new and more explicit way, a well-established materialist thread in conservative argument: washing machines did more for the advancement of women than equal rights ever could. Conservative crusader Phyllis Schlafly declared as much in the 1970s. If we step back from the obvious political function of Schlafly’s assertion — cementing the view that homemaking is women’s true nature — we can see a materialist argument. Schlafly claims that what changed women’s lives and work most profoundly was not equal rights but electricity, mass food production, and household appliances. Harrington argues in a similar albeit more sophisticated vein that “the story of feminism is one of economic transitions — and particularly of the Industrial Revolution.” What enabled feminism’s fight for women’s equal rights was that women went from being producers in the agrarian household to wage workers and consumers. Harrington holds that as capitalism made work “more centralised, more mechanised and less agrarian, the resulting split between ‘work’ and ‘home’ drove women increasingly into a domestic-only role.” In the reactionary feminist view, then, the core of feminist politics is less a question of moral progress than of material conditions. This sounds eerily similar to a Marxist feminist perspective. And it also seems at odds with propagating women’s and men’s eternal struggle of interests. But Harrington and Perry see the biologistic opposition of interests as part of the material conditions of women’s lives. In doing so, they muddle the concerns of those radical feminists who are hyperfocused on women’s biology, with those of Marxist feminists, who look at economic and structural reasons for women’s situation. Reactionary feminists use ideological ambivalence as a strategy in their political project. As Harrington told The Atlantic about her book: “There are a great many conservatives who haven’t noticed quite how much Marxism I’ve smuggled in.” But she is also candid about the mission of reactionary feminists to build conservative and, if need be, “illiberal” institutions. Why the Left Should Care Reactionary feminists aim to convince us that, given the persistence of sexual violence and disrespect of women in liberal sexual culture, monogamous marriage is our best choice. They assert gendered behavior and preferences to be natural facts that we can only passively defer to — women and men will allegedly always have antagonistic interests due to their supposedly ingrained and static biological imperatives. Although they use Marxist rhetoric, their concept of women’s interests lacks a clear class analysis. While they acknowledge that not all women have the same interests, they never address class antagonism in capitalism but rather talk solely of the cultural expressions of “liberalism” and “market society.” That said, the Left needs to take seriously the offer reactionary feminists make to women dissatisfied with liberal feminism: a clear-cut judgment that what flies under the banner of sexual freedom is not always good for women. The rapidly rising number of women who are choked during sex, often enough against their will, is just one telling example of a sexual culture in which women still experience a lot of harm. This harm persists even though rigid sexual moralism has considerably lessened or fallen away. Adherents of sex-positive feminism have little to say about this — for fear of moralism — and understandably so. But that fear has created a void in which it is difficult to talk about what sex should and should not look like. A substantive feminist vision of sexuality and of what the good life for a woman is could fill this void. Instead, conservative women have mostly filled it with their gender fatalism. Many on the Left mistakenly think of all feminist cultural critiques as moralism that distracts from the real concern: economic interests. As the Russian revolutionary Alexandra Kollontai argued more than a century ago, even under socialism, people still need morality to guide gender relations and sexuality. Sexual violence does not disappear through economic reorganization. Thus women also have a material interest in something that is difficult to grasp in purely economic terms: comradely men. But the feminist left rarely taps into this moral critique. There seems to be a larger focus on poses of political radicalism (like abolishing the family) that can be hard to relate to for the majority of people. Reactionary feminists are right that it does women no favors when enjoying “rough sex” becomes a marker of being liberated. They are right that the sexual revolution’s promise of freedom has left many women feeling unsatisfied and vulnerable. But they are wrong in proclaiming women’s perpetual vulnerability for biological reasons. What they ignore is that women’s vulnerability is socially made and culturally scaffolded, and that there can be a society in which biological sex does not make women vulnerable. However, such a world requires not only a widely shared vision of gender equality but also the willingness to proclaim some sex practices as bad for those involved. Even if it’s a hard pill to swallow for those for whom feminist politics means radicalizing liberalism, such a world will require some degree of sexual morality. The Southern Negro Youth Congress’s Bold Push for Equality

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Robert Greene II ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-12T19:48:32+00:00

“Behold the land, the rich and resourceful land, from which for a hundred years its best elements have been running away, its youth and hope, black and white, scurrying North because they are of each other, and dare not face a future of equal, independent, upstanding human beings, in a real and not a sham democracy.” This line was spoken by W. E. B. Du Bois near the end of arguably his greatest speech, titled “Behold the Land.” It was delivered on October 20, 1946, at a convention hosted by the Southern Negro Youth Congress (SNYC), where Du Bois thundered from the dais at Antisdel Chapel at Benedict College in Columbia, South Carolina. He and the SNYC had, for a brief moment, made the Palmetto State the center of the struggle for global human rights in the post–World War II years. Founded in 1937, the SNYC represented the radical wing of the early phase of the civil rights movement. Its radical politics lay in the organization’s recognition of the links between racial and class problems in the US South and throughout the nation. At times, it made alliances on the ground with various Communist Party USA organizations in the South. While many historians have come to see the civil rights era as extending longer than the “classic” phase of 1954 until the mid-1960s, what makes the SNYC unique is how many of the issues they attacked would come up again during both the 1960s and the early decades of the twenty-first century. As part of the youth wing of the National Negro Congress (NNC), the SNYC went well beyond organizing the black youth of the Jim Crow–era South. It offered them an opportunity to voice and do something about the growing frustration many of them felt living in the grip of Jim Crow segregation. A 1946 portrait of W. E. B. Du Bois, by Carl Van Vechten (Library of Congress) Through the course of the Great Depression and World War II, the SNYC pressured political and social leaders throughout the South. As they pressed for greater freedom for all people in the former Confederacy, the SNYC became a beacon of hope during the fight against fascism at home and abroad. The SNYC always linked the struggles against Jim Crow segregation at home to the rising tide of fascism, Nazism, and militarism abroad, even before the advent of the better-known “Double V” campaign among black Americans during World War II, pushing for victory over fascism abroad and Jim Crow at home. The bread and butter of the organization was the organizing of previously disenfranchised workers in the South. Their earliest campaigns included the organizing of black tobacco farmers outside their first headquarters of Richmond, Virginia. At other points, the SNYC would participate in voter registration drives and work with unions to increase labor organizing across a region that, by the Great Depression, had earned a staunchly anti-labor reputation. Other efforts included the investigation of cases of murder in the South via private investigators, such as the case of William Daniel’s 1946 murder by Birmingham police. In short, like the NNC and other radical organizations of the Great Depression and World War II eras, the SNYC used virtually every method at their disposal to fight against Jim Crow segregation. But the challenges facing the SNYC were more dangerous after America’s triumph in World War II. A rift formed between liberals and leftists after the war; the wartime exigency of the American-Soviet alliance gave way to fears of another world war, this time between the United States and its allies against the Soviet Union and a growing postwar Communist bloc. At the same time, anti-colonial campaigns were spreading like wildfire across Asia and Africa. This, coupled with black frustration at home due to renewed white backlash to civil rights, gave the SNYC ample reason to step up their efforts in a place like South Carolina. Ahead of Its Time By 1946, South Carolina already had a growing movement for radical human rights. The Progressive Democratic Party (PDP), led by black South Carolinians Modjeska Simkins, John Henry McCray, and Osceola McKaine, built a progressive black alternative to South Carolina’s white-supremacist Democratic Party of South Carolina. Registering black South Carolinians to vote, putting up their own slate of candidates for state and national office, and even traveling to the 1944 Democratic National Convention to challenge the legitimacy of the South Carolina Democratic Party, the Progressive Democratic Party presaged the better-known Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party ( MFDP ) by twenty years in using grassroots democratic traditions to attempt to upend the old system. Their efforts to overturn the ironclad rule of the white-supremacist Democratic Party did not result in electoral victory. Osceola McKaine, the black nominee of the PDP who campaigned against incumbent Olin Johnston in 1944, fell short in his campaign. But McKaine’s own personal story is a reminder of why black South Carolina, in 1944, was so thoroughly galvanized to fight back against Jim Crow. McKaine, a native of Sumter, South Carolina, served in the United States military during World War I. After his term of service ended, McKaine, tired of the hypocrisy of American calls to “make the world safe for democracy” abroad while not doing so at home, settled in Ghent, Belgium, and opened a successful nightclub. Forced to flee Belgium due to World War II, McKaine returned to Sumter determined to fight for democracy in his home state. He was defeated in 1944 by Johnston, who was governor of South Carolina when the state condemned to death fourteen-year-old George Stinney, who some believe is the youngest person condemned to death in US history. The PDP, like the MFDP twenty years later, was important not due to victory at the polls but due to the organization’s ability to give voters hope who had had none for generations. Thousands of new black voters became part of South Carolina’s voting process for the first time since the immediate aftermath of the collapse of Reconstruction. Meanwhile, growing pressure to restore the right to vote for black citizens in the South was also enhanced by the 1944 Smith v. Allwright Supreme Court decision, which declared the all-white primaries of Southern Democratic parties unconstitutional. Again, South Carolina played a crucial role in resisting this, with Governor Johnston himself calling a special session of the state legislature in 1944 to pass new laws to continue the white primary. It was not until another federal court case, Elmore v. Rice , originating with the challenge of black South Carolinian George Elmore and decided in 1947, that the white primary was broken. What SNYC Represented South Carolina, therefore, had already made itself the nexus of domestic political battles over racism and citizenship. But the state was also important as a site of international debate over the future of peace in the post–World War II landscape. One of the chief architects of the post–World War II foreign policy, which quickly became antagonistic toward the Soviets, was South Carolinian James Byrnes. Often overlooked in twentieth-century American foreign and domestic policy history, Byrnes holds the unique distinction of being one of the few politicians in American history to hold virtually every possible office in the federal government. He served as congressman, and later senator, from South Carolina in the 1920s and 1930s. As a close friend to President Franklin D. Roosevelt, Byrnes briefly served on the Supreme Court before being named director of the Office of Economic Stabilization, and later director of the Office of War Mobilization during World War II. In that role, Byrnes became one of the most important people in America’s war effort. Afterward, under President Harry Truman, Byrnes was named secretary of state in 1945. But Byrnes coveted the presidency. As was the case with another South Carolinian, John C. Calhoun, in the nineteenth century, Byrnes’s ties to South Carolina and white-supremacist ideology became the chief impediments to higher office. Most notably, liberals and progressives within the New Deal–era Democratic Party stymied efforts to make Byrnes the vice president under Franklin Roosevelt during his 1944 campaign. Still, Byrnes remained a powerful force within Southern and national politics. And as the Cold War intensified, a massive campaign for social change in his home state made perfect sense. The SNYC, committed as it was to racial justice at home and peace abroad, knew that targeting Byrnes’ home state was a major rallying point for the radical wing of American politics in the post–World War II years. Also, the fact that South Carolina was home to a growing radical movement for human rights helped make it a logical choice for the SNYC’s annual convention. Bringing both foreign and domestic crises together, finally, was the saga of Isaac Woodard, which galvanized activists and brought global attention to South Carolina. A black World War II veteran blinded by police on February 12, 1946, in Batesburg-Leesville, South Carolina, Woodard’s case became a symbol of the brutal attacks on black veterans and their allies that occurred throughout the South after World War II. A backlash similar to the Red Summer of 1919 , the post-1945 political and social environment was incredibly difficult and reactionary, but also presented incredible opportunities to SNYC leaders. Blinded veteran Isaac Woodard sits with his mother, Eliza, who holds a copy of the Daily Worker . ( Sunday Worker , July 1, 1946) The SNYC needed a place to not only bring together their members but a location where a new, militant human rights movement was evolving on the ground. Columbia became that place, due in large part to the work of Modjeska Simkins in welcoming the organization to her hometown. The 1946 convention in Columbia, South Carolina owed a great deal to Modjeska Simkins. A human rights activist born and raised in Columbia, Simkins had become, by 1946, one of the leaders of a radical human and civil rights movement in the Palmetto State. She became secretary of the South Carolina State Conference of the NAACP in 1941 after working tirelessly in black communities across the state as director of negro work for the South Carolina Tuberculosis Association, and after playing a pivotal role in the formation of the state conference in 1939. Many years later, in an oral history interview with Jacquelyn Dowd Hall, Simkins argued that the SNYC represented something unique and powerful in the broader human and civil rights movements of the twentieth century. “The power structure,” Simkins stated, “has always feared the combining of the forces of black and white masses.” This was what the SNYC represented. On the first insert page of the 1946 conference program , the Southern Youth Legislature, which was the organizing body within the SNYC that spoke for the organization, made their demands clear. “We meet in solemn session,” they began, as “militant, courageous Negro youth of the South. Youth. . . . which must and will be served. Youth. . . . dedicated to the struggle for freedom. Youth determined to achieve that freedom in its lifetime.” For members of the SNYC, the opportunity arose to finally make a serious blow against segregation. Or, as the title of Erik Gellman ’s groundbreaking 2012 work on the National Negro Congress goes, a “death blow to Jim Crow.” Portraits of Harold H. Poole and Ethel Bigby, taken from the 1946 conference program. Some of the leaders of the 1946 conference, like Modjeska Simkins or Dorothy Burnham, would become better known throughout the twentieth century for their work in various strands of the civil and human rights movements. Others, such as Columbia SNYC leader Harold H. Poole or Benedict College student Ethel Bigby, are not as well known nationally or even regionally. But they all were pivotal leaders in a movement that, in 1946, seemed poised to tackle the crises that Dr Martin Luther King Jr so eloquently described a generation later: militarism, racism, and capitalism. Alas, the Second Red Scare at home and the Cold War abroad destroyed the SNYC. The organization was constantly harassed by the FBI and local police forces in the South. Ultimately, the organization was also brought down, like others, due to its alliance with the 1948 Progressive Party campaign of Henry Wallace. Eugene “Bull” Connor — better remembered for his vicious treatment of activists in Birmingham in 1963 — created havoc for left-wing activists in Birmingham. Its 1946 convention remains the high point of the organization’s history, leaving behind a legacy of activism in South Carolina and throughout the nation. Many of the members of the SNYC, or those affiliated with the organization, continued to be involved in activism deep into the twentieth century. For example, several of the founders of the legendary Freedomways magazine, which between 1961 and 1985 served as a key periodical for the black left, were veterans of the SNYC. Editors and writers such as Esther Cooper Jackson, Louis Burnham, and even Du Bois himself all had significant ties with the organization. In that sense, the SNYC is part of the larger connective tissue of twentieth century radical social movements — and the 1946 convention in South Carolina was its moment of triumph. One night on a suicide prevention chat line, a young man typed a sentence I had seen many times before. “Everyone would be better off without me.” He began listing his failures, one after another, as though presenting evidence in a courtroom. My instinct was to challenge his thinking, offer reassurance, or explain why his conclusion was wrong. I wanted him to understand that his life mattered and that the pain he was experiencing did not tell the whole story. Instead, I paused and wrote: “It sounds like you’ve been carrying an overwhelming sense of failure and loneliness.” There was a long silence. Then he responded: “Yeah. Exactly.” Related: Justice After War and the Realities of Peace Nothing had been resolved. His circumstances had not changed. But something important had happened. For the first time in the conversation, he seemed to stop defending his despair. He had found someone willing to acknowledge what he was experiencing without immediately trying to correct it. I have thought about that exchange many times. It reminded me that listening is more than a communication skill. It is a way of relating to another human being that can interrupt the isolation and misunderstanding that so often contribute to conflict. Years before that conversation, I had encountered a framework that would eventually help me understand what had happened: Nonviolent Communication, developed by psychologist Marshall Rosenberg. At its heart NVC is a deceptively simple idea. Beneath much of what we say and do, including our anger, criticism, and judgment, are human needs that have not been met. I first encountered this way of thinking in my early thirties, when I was struggling to extend much kindness to anyone, including myself. I was dealing with all kinds of ways to avoid the noise inside my head. My relationships often suffered. Anger, self-pity, arrogance, and ambition left their marks on people I cared about. At the time, I was working as a peace and justice educator at a nonviolence institute in Rochester, New York. Through teachers and mentors, I began exploring the relationship between language and conflict. I also had opportunities to observe Dominic Barter, an internationally recognized practitioner and trainer of Nonviolent Communication, whose work offered a glimpse of what these principles could look like when practiced rather than simply discussed. One quotation from Rosenberg stayed with me: “Every criticism, judgment, diagnosis, and expression of anger is the tragic expression of an unmet need.” The word tragic is important. It suggests that beneath many of the ways we hurt one another is something we have not learned to express. A demand may conceal a longing for security. An accusation may carry a desire for recognition. Anger may be protecting a need for dignity, or autonomy. This does not excuse harmful behavior or relieve anyone of responsibility; but it does invite us to look more carefully at what is happening beneath the surface. Rosenberg developed Nonviolent Communication during the 1960s, drawing on his work in psychology and conflict resolution. The approach is organized around four interconnected practices: observation, feeling, need, and request. Related: Structures Through History: Jesus in the Machinery of Violence We begin by describing what is happening without immediately attaching a judgment. We identify the feelings that arise. We consider the human needs connected to those feelings. Finally, we make a clear request that allows the other person the freedom to respond. Observation. Feeling. Need. Request. The framework is straightforward enough to memorize. Practicing it is considerably harder. I could explain the principles, diagram the process, and facilitate a workshop. Yet I continued to struggle with compassion in my own relationships. Understanding something intellectually did not mean I knew how to live it. Eventually, I left peace education and moved through a professional landscape that included startups, domestic violence services, and suicide prevention. I spent six years working in a domestic violence shelter and later several more years supporting people experiencing emotional distress and suicidal crises. It was in these settings, far removed from the relative safety of a classroom, that the practical significance of listening became clearer to me. One evening at the domestic violence shelter, a resident came into the office furious about the house rules. She accused staff of treating her like a child. My first instinct was to explain the policy and correct what I believed she misunderstood. Instead, I paused. “It sounds like you’re feeling really controlled right now,” I said. She stopped mid-sentence. Her shoulders dropped. The anger did not disappear, but its intensity began to soften. What followed was a conversation about dignity and independence. She explained how many decisions in her life had recently been made by other people. Her partner, the courts, and the shelter system had all exercised authority over her. She was trying to rebuild a sense of control over her own life, and even well-intentioned rules could remind her of everything she had lost. Related: How Can a War Against Children Be a Christian War? What she wanted was agency. She wanted to feel that her life belonged to her again. The shelter rules did not change. We still had responsibilities to other residents and staff. But the conversation changed because I had begun to understand what was underneath her objection. I had initially heard a challenge to authority. She was trying to communicate a need for autonomy. That distinction can be vital, particularly when working with people whose lives have been shaped by the loss of personal freedom. A person can object to a rule while still understanding why it exists. A person can be angry and still deserve to be treated with dignity. Empathy requires us to become curious about another person’s experience before deciding what that experience means. We often approach suffering with an almost irresistible desire to fix it. We offer advice, search for silver linings, explain what someone should do, or reassure them that everything will eventually be all right. These responses can come from genuine concern. They can also communicate that we are uncomfortable remaining with another person’s pain. Empathy asks something more difficult. Can we stay present without immediately reaching for an answer? Can we listen without preparing our defense? Can we allow someone to describe an experience without making the conversation about ourselves? Sympathy expresses sorrow for what another person is experiencing. Empathy attempts to understand that experience from within the other person’s perspective. We do not need to have lived through the same circumstances. We need to listen closely enough to recognize that our assumptions are not the same as another person’s reality. I returned to this lesson years later on the suicide prevention chat line. The young man who had written that everyone would be better off without him eventually began describing the months that had brought him to that point. There was isolation, a series of disappointments, and a growing conviction that he had already ruined his life. The conversation required careful assessment and continued engagement. But that initial moment of recognition helped establish the trust necessary for the conversation to continue. His suffering was real. His circumstances had not suddenly improved. Yet he was no longer entirely alone with what he was carrying. Being heard does not necessarily remove suffering. It can, however, change the conditions under which someone experiences it. Shame becomes a little less isolating. A person who has been withdrawing may become willing to reach out. A conversation that once seemed impossible may become possible. There is another dimension to Rosenberg’s work that I find equally important. Nonviolent Communication asks us to extend this same attention inward. We are often much more fluent in judging ourselves than understanding ourselves. We call ourselves lazy, selfish, weak, or inadequate without stopping to consider what might be happening beneath those judgments. We treat our emotions as inconveniences to suppress rather than signals worth examining. Related: César Chávez: The Price of Hero Worship Beneath self-criticism may be an unmet need for rest, acceptance, security, or belonging. Beneath shame may be a longing for forgiveness. Beneath anger toward ourselves may be grief over the distance between the lives we imagined and the lives we are living. Recognizing these needs gives us a more constructive place from which to take it. We can examine our intentions and learn to make requests rather than demands. We can also recognize when our desire to be right has become more important than our desire to understand. I am not an expert in Nonviolent Communication, nor would I claim to have mastered it. I still become defensive. I misunderstand people. I sometimes find myself preparing a response when I should be listening. The difference is that I have become more attentive to those moments. I am learning to recognize the distance between knowing what compassion requires and actually practicing it. Much of the work happens in that distance. The implications extend well beyond our personal relationships. Families, schools, workplaces, community organizations, and political institutions all struggle with forms of conflict that become more entrenched when people feel dismissed or dehumanized. A parent might hear the fear beneath a child’s resistance. An employer might recognize the insecurity beneath an employee’s frustration. A community leader might discover that public anger is connected to a longstanding experience of exclusion or lost dignity. None of this means that every disagreement can be resolved through conversation. Some situations require firm boundaries and protection from harm. Empathy must never become an excuse for tolerating abuse or asking vulnerable people to accommodate those who hurt them. We can recognize someone’s humanity while remaining honest about the consequences of their choices. In fact, I believe this is one of the more demanding forms of nonviolence. Our political culture offers countless examples of what happens when listening gives way to contempt. We increasingly reward humiliation and the reduction of complicated human beings to political identities. Disagreement becomes an opportunity to demonstrate allegiance rather than an invitation to understand something we may have overlooked. The same habits appear in our communities and our homes. We interrupt. We assume. We assign motives. We defend ourselves before we have understood what the other person is trying to communicate. Nonviolent Communication asks us to look beyond the immediate expression and consider the human experience underneath. It does not promise agreement, and it does not eliminate conflict. It gives us a way to approach conflict without making the destruction of another person’s dignity the price of our own. Rosenberg once wrote: “What I want in my life is compassion, a flow between myself and others based on a mutual giving from the heart.” I appreciate his use of the word flow . Compassion is not something we possess and distribute from a position of superiority. It is a relationship that requires openness in both directions, a willingness to give and receive, and an acknowledgment that each of us has needs we are still learning to express. Related: The Last Embrace We spend so much of our lives trying to make ourselves understood. Yet we can spend entire conversations waiting for our turn to speak, never quite making room for the person in front of us. I think back to that young man on the chat line. His words were filled with self-condemnation. Beneath them was a person who wanted relief and some reason to believe that his life still held possibilities. He did not need me to win an argument against his despair. He needed someone willing to hear him. I have spent much of my adult life learning this lesson, often through mistakes I wish I had made differently. There are relationships I damaged and moments when fear or pride made it difficult to extend the compassion I now encourage others to practice. I cannot go back and change those things. I can only try to meet the next person with greater patience, less certainty, and a willingness to listen. Peace is often discussed in terms of treaties and the resolution of armed conflicts. These are essential parts of the work. But the habits that sustain peace are also formed in ordinary encounters, in the ways we speak to one another, respond to suffering, and handle disagreement. The first act of nonviolence may be as simple, and as difficult, as resisting the urge to judge before we understand. Before we can build a more peaceful world together, we have to learn how to hear one another. PeaceVoice

At Ferndale High School, nestled in a Detroit suburb, one of our most pressing problems is the dilapidated building, built in the 1950s. The outdated heating and cooling systems are prone to classroom floods—in the last nine months I've experienced three. Once boiling hot water sprayed out of the HVAC unit during class, nearly injuring a student. It was late August—our last Sunday night of freedom before our teacher professional development week—when six of my co-workers and I received an email from our principal. She said that a few classrooms had flooded again the previous week, but the damage was limited and our classrooms were “ready for our return.” “Tomorrow is still the beginning of a fresh school year,” she wrote, “and I’m looking forward to welcoming everyone back and starting the year together on a positive note.” She made the mistake of including all affected teachers as recipients, instead of blind copying us, so I knew exactly who to reach out to. That evening I spent lots of time on the phone with my co-workers and my fellow union building representatives. We all had experienced previous flooding, so we were skeptical that our classrooms would actually be ready for our return the following morning. Ever since I began working at this school in 2024, my mission has been to convince my co-workers we should be compensated in some way when our school administrators fail us. Whenever we’ve asked administrators to address issues like poor attendance or facilities problems, we’ve gotten responses like “That is not my fault” or “I do not have the power to fix that.” This energy has been so demoralizing for our teaching staff, and it seemed to me that my co-workers had gotten used to just accepting the b.s. By Monday morning I had devised a plan with the support of my fellow building reps. INDUSTRIAL FANS Our suspicions were correct; words can hardly describe the scene we walked into. Each of the seven flooded classrooms had eight to 15 industrial fans blowing on the floor, dehumidifying machines siphoning water out of the classroom windows, and no functioning air conditioning. Teachers were supposed to spend this day organizing and preparing our classrooms. Most of us spend hundreds of dollars of our own money to purchase supplies, decor, soft lighting, and small comforting touches to make our classrooms the warm, safe environments we know students need. It takes a fair amount of time to unpack those things from the summer months and get the space ready for the school year. When I saw the state of my classroom, I knew it was going to be impossible to get any sort of work done in that environment. My next step was to walk door to door and check in on my six co-workers who had been looped in on that email. No classroom was even close to being a functional workspace. I could feel the swelling anger, and knew the moment was ripe to make an ask. “We do not deserve this,” I told my co-workers. “What if we all went to the principal and asked for something in return?” I told them about the plan the other building reps and I had come up with: The affected teachers could march together to the principal’s office and ask that either we receive pay for the additional hours it takes us to set up our classrooms outside of contract time, or we be excused from a professional development session during the week to set up our classrooms during work hours. Everyone was too angry to be afraid. All but one agreed to join our march on the boss. The six of us found our principal in a teacher’s classroom. She stepped into the hall to see us standing there shoulder to shoulder, looking upset. None of us will ever forget her response: She took out her phone, started filming us, and said, “Oh I am going to put this on TikTok, it’s the first day of school and the teachers already want something from me.” When she saw how deeply stunned we were, she put her phone back in her pocket and asked what was wrong. I reminded her of her email, and informed her that our classrooms were in fact not “ready for our return.” I told her this wasn’t fair to us, and explained how we felt it could be rectified. She said she understood our frustration and would “look into” allowing us to timecard extra hours. We did not feel incredibly hopeful. There’s too much wiggle room in the phrase “I will look into that.” But to our surprise, we received an email from the principal saying she would be able to approve three hours of paid time for classroom setup. LESSONS LEARNED Since this action, I have debriefed with the teachers that participated. While we’re glad we won some compensation, we realized we should have come with a number in mind. We were doubtful that we would win any kind of concession, so we didn’t even think that far ahead. We were able to get the most necessary work done within the three approved hours, but could have benefited from more time. Still, once the rest of our staff heard about our action, our building was buzzing with excitement. I heard co-workers 15 to 25 years my senior say, “That was so cool!” “I didn’t even know you could do that!” “I wish I was as brave as you when I was your age!” So in my mind, it was a success. I think the most powerful thing we can do as organizers is be hopeful, and share that hope. The message of “You do not deserve this mistreatment” seemed to really resonate. In education, we grow so used to getting beat down from all angles that we can forget we deserve to be treated with dignity. I’m very proud to have helped my co-workers work together at that moment. I hope this will help us find the courage to take on our next workplace issue without fear. How Cultural Marxism Became a Right-Wing Obsession

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: A. J. A. Woods ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-12T17:36:58+00:00

In December 2021, the term “cultural Marxism” was added to the Oxford English Dictionary . It was, according to the OED , a phrase with a sinister and controversial past. Its etymology could allegedly be traced to 1930s Nazi propaganda; its first recorded English-language use was in a 1938 issue of a British Union of Fascists magazine. Despite its dark history, this expression has recently surged in popularity. College campuses and city streets have been plastered with stickers that urge passersby to “smash” cultural Marxism. Prominent US Republicans, such as Texas Senator Ted Cruz and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, have written book-length polemics to blame America’s decline on cultural Marxist ideologies. Leaders in Latin America, like Brazil’s former President Jair Bolsonaro and Argentina’s President Javier Milei, have vowed to rid their governments of this left-wing scourge. In the United Kingdom, Conservative Party MPs have said that cultural Marxists are “destroying” children’s souls and “snuffing out” the right to free speech. Even the world’s richest man, tech-mogul-turned-Trumpian Elon Musk, has voiced concerns about a Wikipedia article that dismisses cultural Marxism as a “conspiracy theory.” This buzzword has now become a fixture in the lexicon of the contemporary Right — but what does it mean? The Long March “Cultural Marxism,” or the “cultural Marxism conspiracy theory,” is the political right’s attempt to explain why the culture of Western societies has changed over the past sixty years. According to this theory, a group of German thinkers known as the Frankfurt School (or the Institute for Social Research) invented the “ideologies” of multiculturalism, fem­inism, and environmentalism to trigger the demise of Western civilization. When Theodor Adorno, Herbert Marcuse, and other Frankfurt School theorists fled Nazi Germany, they trav­eled to the United States and successfully injected their ideas into the 1960s student movement — or so the story goes. In the late twentieth century, their indoctrinated disciples performed a “long march through the institutions” (a phrase coined by German New Left activist Rudi Dutschke) to promote cultural Marxism in education, the media, the government, and the church. More recently, right-wing activists and conservative pundits have used this theory to understand the rise of the Black Lives Matter movement, the increased visibility of LGBTQ+ communities, and the efforts to tackle the climate emergency. Even in countries outside the United States, conservatives have engaged in a “culture war” to defend their societies from this supposed menace. What is cultural about this cultural Marxism? What is Marxist about cultural change? Conservative critics speculate that the Frankfurt School developed a fresh strategy of commu­nist subversion in the 1930s. Instead of building a vanguardist party or seizing the factories, the Frankfurt School decided to infiltrate the cultural apparatus of bourgeois society to under­mine Western capitalism. Once ensconced in these positions of power, the critics maintain, cultural Marxists catered to the petulant demands of various minority groups — Black people, LGBTQ+ people, feminists — and dismantled the civilizational inheritance of the (white) majority. The Frankfurt School apparently believed that this strategy would cause a Marxist egalitarian utopia to emerge from the ruins of Western culture. As the art historian Sven Lütticken observes, this alternative intellectual history relies on a theory of culture that mixes “conservative essential­ism” with “schematic superstructuralism.” Culture, according to this right-wing conception, naturally reflects the values of a native populace. These values fuse a nation’s citizens into a harmonious unity that annuls antagonism and difference. Yet this essentialist notion of culture cannot account for the drastic social changes that have taken place since the 1960s, because it treats the subordination of minorities as “natural” (or even as virtuous). Consequently, conservatives must conclude that an outside agent has seized the organs of cultural production to destroy the West. In an act of self-defense, the opponents of cultural Marxism propose to eliminate every instance of “political correctness” or “wokeness” in public life through legislative reform, lifestyle changes, or violence. In this version of global events, the Frankfurt School and their devotees are blessed with a magical level of agency that can overcome all contradictions and countervailing forces. A New Concept Of course, no one can deny the historical existence and importance of the Frankfurt School. But why does this narrative of cultural degeneration focus so much on a collective of German Marxists? How does it continue to convince and excite the forces of political reaction? What is the genealogy of this idea we call the “cultural Marxism conspiracy theory”? Some scholars suggest that “cultural Marxism” is simply an updated version of older antisemitic conspiracy theories like “Judeo-Bolshevism” or “cultural Bolshevism.” Of course, there are some eerie parallels between these earlier ideas and “cultural Marxism.” The British Union of Fascists translated Kulturbolschewismus as “cultural Marxism” in 1938, even if their use of the term was long forgotten by the time it was “recoined” in the early 1990s. Contemporary far-right activists and authors tend to emphasize that several members of the Frankfurt School were Jewish, as if to suggest that these thinkers were genetically predisposed to operate as agents of cultural degeneracy. Even more damningly, the fascist online encyclopedia Metapedia once maintained an entry on cultural Marxism that presented the term as interchangeable with “cultural Bolshevism.” It is ahistorical, however, to claim that “cultural Marxism” is nothing more than a rebranding of these previous tropes. This argument presupposes that Judeo-Bolshevism, cultural Bolshevism, and cultural Marxism are substantially identical, as though they share a transcendental essence that precedes their historical manifestations. It ignores the distinct trajec­tories of these ideas and implies that all right-wing politics are fundamentally the same. The phrase “cultural Marxism,” as well as the conspiratorial narratives about the Frankfurt School that it often denotes, had its own bizarre history long before it ended up in the neo-Nazi thesaurus as a synonym for cultural Bolshevism. Where do we start? While I recognize the parallels between cultural Bolshevism and cultural Marxism, I do not think that the story begins in the 1920s or 1930s. For me, the 1960s is a better starting point for our analysis. The history of cul­tural Marxism cannot be understood unless we reflect on the many overlapping shifts and realignments that stemmed from the ’60s. We must remember not to limit our notion of that decade to the spectacular images of May 1968 or Woodstock. As many scholars now agree, the 1960s were long — some claim that the 1960s lasted from 1955 to 1974 — and global . It was a conjuncture that opened possibilities for revolution, dissent, and counterculture. However, it also involved the exercise of vast political power to defend social and racial hierarchies, control subversive energies, and protect modes of domination and exploitation. Decades of Struggle In his groundbreaking essay “Periodizing the 60s,” the cul­tural critic Fredric Jameson argues that the beginning of what would become known as the 1960s can be traced back to the struggles for national liberation and decolonization that broke out in 1950s Africa. Events in the Global South, such as the independence of Ghana and the Algerian War, ignited nearly two decades of struggle that shook the postwar global order. Radicals in America and Western Europe drew inspiration from the successes of revolutionary and anti-imperialist movements around the world. They read Frantz Fanon’s The Wretched of the Earth , Mao Zedong’s The Little Red Book , and Che Guevara’s Guerrilla Warfare . Students in the American New Left marched in the streets, held teach-ins, and occupied university buildings to protest their government’s military interventions in Vietnam, Cambodia, and Laos. The truly global nature of this 1960s revolt, with all its fissures and contradictions, gave rise to the hope that the “marginalized” and “minoritized” of the earth would be at the forefront of an unstoppable worldwide revolution. The days of an all-supreme Western civilization looked like they were coming to an end. The 1960s saw the flourishing of new social and political categories of race, gender, and sexuality. Traditional modes of class-based struggle had lost their radical edge. The Taft-Hartley Act of 1947, as well as the merger between American Federation of Labor and the Congress of Industrial Organizations, tamed the labor movement in the United States and integrated unions into the anti-communist Cold War consen­sus. Uprisings against bureaucratic communism in the Soviet Bloc — Hungary, Poland, Czechoslovakia — demonstrated the need for a non-Stalinist and antiauthoritarian left. New ways of doing politics were tested and elaborated. The slogan “The personal is political,” popularized by second-wave femi­nism, emphasized the importance of finding the links between individual experiences and wider structures of repression. Movements for Black power, women’s liberation, and gay liber­ation introduced a vibrant politics of identity that fused potent critiques of the existing order with daring visions of a liberated future. They exposed the social exclusions, subordinations, and restrictions that underpinned the status quo of postwar capital­ist societies. In the United States, these movements fought for the establishment of women’s studies, Black studies, and ethnic studies departments in American universities. Racial, sexual, and gender identities were embraced as epistemological bases for developing new forms of knowledge that could identify the multiple dimensions of power and oppression. In the decades following the 1960s, many countries experienced something of a social and cultural revolution as women, racial minorities, and LGBTQ+ people acquired civil rights and started to play a more active and visible role in the public sphere. While these forms of equality and inclusion were usually partial and con­tradictory, they represented a significant liberalization of social life and an unsettling of old hierarchies. The Cultural Turn One of the most significant developments of the 1960s was the drastic expansion of “culture.” In the middle of the twentieth century, many “developed” countries transitioned from a predominantly industrial model of production to a largely service-and information-based one. During the rise of the postindustrial economy, culture burst into the foreground of day-to-day life. The mass media — film, television, music, fashion, advertising, newspapers, and magazines — started to exert tremendous power and influence over the rhythms of everyday existence. The growing demographic of teenagers and young adults became an eager audience for the offerings of a new and exciting “youth culture.” The rise of this youth-oriented popular culture even altered conventional habits of speech, dress, and behavior. These rapid changes spurred a “cultural turn” in humanities and social sciences scholarship. The thinkers of British cultural studies drew on the resources of Gramscian theory to rethink the nature of cultural produc­tion and consumption in postwar capitalist societies. Instead of assuming that mass culture turned people into passive con­sumers, these cultural theorists suggested that popular culture, especially youth subculture, constituted a site of potentially subversive political struggle. As the forces of Western Marxism cut ties with Moscow, these intellectuals engaged with other radical causes and movements to promote a broad and populist form of cultural politics. Since the 1960s, cultural questions have become increasingly central to political debate. It would be a mistake to believe that the 1960s were nothing but an outburst of liberal acceptance and youthful rebellion. From right-wing coups and neocolonial economic policies to police repression and state surveillance, the 1960s featured the use of extraordinary force to preserve arrangements of power and privilege. Various right-wing and reactionary political movements ventured into this conjunctural terrain to defend the existing hierarchies of a supposedly pristine Western civilization. These forces required intellectuals who could explain away the causes of perceived cultural decline, organize their supporters into a coherent political identity, and offer compelling visions of con­servative revival. All cultural Marxism narratives derive from these efforts to resist and reverse the social changes that started to unfold in the long and global 1960s. It is important to understand how these intellectuals operated within their own political movements. How might we investigate the function and activity of these intellectuals? What kind of theory would illuminate the specific ideological practices that shaped their approaches to this cultural tumult? What this task requires, I suggest, is a return to Antonio Gramsci’s theory of intellectuals. The Formation of Intellectuals A central theme of Gramsci’s Prison Notebooks is a sustained and innovative rethinking of the concept of the intellectual. In a letter to his sister-in-law Tatiana Schucht, Gramsci remarked that he wanted to extend this concept beyond the limits of “the current notion that refers only to the preeminent intel­lectuals.” Instead of sticking to this abstract notion, Gramsci examined how specific types of intellectual were formed his­torically and how they functioned politically. The sociologist Jerome Karabel describes this reorientation as a shift from a normative framework, which “treats intellectuals not as they actually are, but as they should be,” to an analytical one that identifies “the conditions and processes that shape the actual political consciousness and actions of different groups of intellectuals.” In the Prison Notebooks , Gramsci started from the “immediate, direct, and vivid impression” — the details of social practice and material circumstance — to reveal larger historical patterns. His entries on intellectuals included several reminders about the importance of grounding one’s research in material reality. He specified that the formation and elabora­tion of intellectual strata did not “take place on the terrain of abstract democracy, but in accordance with very concrete tra­ditional historical processes.” The elements of cultural Marxism have been deconstructed and reconfigured time and time again as reac­tionary political forces across the world search for new ways to justify their opposition to equality, democracy, and justice. We will never understand these stories if we cling to the language of individual pathology. Our approach to cultural Marxism must be rooted in the institutional, the conjunctural, and the structural. As Gramsci once put it: Ideas and opinions are not spontaneously born in each individual brain: They have had a center of formation, of irradiation, of dissemination, of persuasion [that] has developed them and presented them in the political form of current reality. It would be impossible — and uninteresting — to refute all the claims that every critic of cultural Marxism has made. After all, most cultural Marxism narratives include several factual obser­vations, such as Herbert Marcuse’s endorsement of the “long march through the institutions” as a potential strategy for the post-1960s New Left. Yet these little facts are rarely used to provide us with an accurate account of either the Frankfurt School’s influence or contemporary social realities. Instead, they are mobilized to fuel political campaigns that seek to denigrate and disempower certain groups. The Right has declared war on cultural Marxism. Only when we understand the practices — and weaknesses — of those reactionaries who have signed up for this war can we hope to resist their attacks and fight for a better future. You’d be forgiven for worrying, 12 weeks into a strike, that the boss is hell-bent on waiting you out. But after more than 80 days on the picket line at Seattle’s Embassy Suites by Hilton in Pioneer Square, Aspen Desmarais was undeterred. “I can live on a budget,” she said. “Been doing it since day one.” After 85 days, on September 10, workers won the longest hotel strike in Washington state history. Their union, UNITE HERE Local 8, represents 4,000 hospitality workers in Oregon and Washington. Desmarais, a strike captain and bartender at the hotel, worked to keep others motivated throughout the fight. She and her co-workers were “tired of people talking down to us and not treating us with respect,” she said, “when we’re the reason that place stays running the way that it does.” Hilton’s offer, two weeks into the strike, of one cent per hour over its previous proposal helped fuel outrage. The union held open bargaining, so members heard the offer firsthand. “I [felt] frustrated and disrespected,” Desmarais said. “Especially with how much hard work my co-workers put into making sure the hotel and its guests have clean spaces and stays that are welcoming.” Fritzie Corral, an Embassy Suites breakfast attendant, had only worked at the hotel for two months before walking out. As the strike went on, she said, her son worried that she had made a mistake in taking the job. “You see other employees that decided to stay in the hotel working," she said. “Even during that time, I still believed I made the right choice. It’s my right to voice out what I feel and to fight for my rights.” Hilton did not respond to requests for comment. PROTECTION FROM ICE Workers won significant pay increases, lowered barriers for health care eligibility, and highly sought-after protections for immigrant workers. Hilton, which manages the hotel, drew protests earlier this year for housing ICE agents and for cutting ties with a Minneapolis hotel that canceled ICE bookings during Operation Metro Surge. The union’s leaders criticized Hilton throughout the strike for dismissing contract language that would protect immigrant workers. “With ICE-related killings in the news for months causing immigrant workers to worry, why is Hilton refusing to give immigrant workers’ union a heads up if ICE is staying at the hotel?” Local 8 President Anita Seth asked Businesswire . Ultimately workers won a commitment from Hilton not to punish workers who call out due to an immigration enforcement surge, plus language binding the hotel to an expedited resolution process in such an event. Workers who lose their work authorization due to immigration enforcement will now be able to return to their jobs if their work authorization is restored within four years, and retain their seniority for up to two. Desmarais told Labor Notes that making sure immigrant co-workers missing work because of ICE “knew they would have a job still and a safe place to come back to” was the “most important” win strikers fought for. Starting in 2027, housekeepers will make over $31 an hour—a giant step above the $17.63 an hour that housekeepers make on average across the country, per the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The new contract makes them the highest-paid housekeepers in the state, according to the union. Embassy Suites workers classified as “tipped” will now make $1 more per hour than the city’s minimum wage. Health care eligibility was also a core demand for the union. Many hotel workers under collective bargaining agreements must meet a minimum threshold of hours worked to qualify for health benefits in a given period. Seasonal layoffs, a hallmark of hotel work, often mean inconsistent coverage for workers and their families. The new contract replaces the month-to-month eligibility standard with an annual threshold of 1,250 hours, or around 24 hours a week, to qualify. MASS CANCELLATIONS The strike coincided with five of the six World Cup soccer matches held at Seattle’s Lumen Field this summer, directly across from the Embassy Suites. World Cup-related leverage helped produce quick contract wins this summer for service and hospitality workers in Philadelphia, New York, and Los Angeles, but not so in Seattle. How did strikers there keep up the pressure for another 63 days after the games ended? The hotel’s proximity to Lumen Field makes it a destination for football fans when the season starts in September. On game days, the hotel sets up a bar facing the stadium. Workers set up a separate picket line mere feet away from the customers, and urged fans not to cross. In between sporting events, Desmarais said solidarity from other union workers played a big role. Combined pressure from BNSF rail workers (represented by the Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers and Trainmen Division 518) and sympathetic elected officials pushed Sound Transit to move its contract to another hotel with no promise of return. Corral reported seeing the hotel’s director and assistant director hauling garbage out during the World Cup. “It felt so rewarding,” said Desmarais. “Yeah, you have to get your hands dirty and not just do paperwork. I think it gave them a real experience of what we have to deal with.” Corral said community solidarity was key in getting Hilton to concede. Strikers’ church groups joined the picket line. So did several Seattle elected officials and even some hotel guests. Guests also brought workers donuts, contributed to the strike fund, and left Google reviews urging hotel management to “come to the table” and “sign the contract already.” One temp worker walked off the job after only an hour to join the picket line. Corral said such displays of solidarity made workers “more motivated to keep on.” Many workers worried about money, the future of their jobs, and their families while on strike. But when someone was scared or upset, there was always another worker to help uplift them, said Corral: “We’re like family.”

The Real AI Misalignment Problem Is Capitalism

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Nick Couldry ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-12T15:09:04+00:00

This September, some rather technical issues regarding artificial intelligence, or rather their upshot, made headlines. The reason lay in two resignations from companies leading the development of frontier AI models, namely David Robinson , OpenAI’s Safety Officer, and Jacob Coxon , a developer at Anthropic. Each resigned because they no longer believed the companies were taking seriously the risks that frontier models’ recent “advances” pose to the survival of humanity . So, not just what most of us would call a “technical issue.” Those fears were not hype. They were based on careful argument: a September 6 blog by Jakub Pachocki, OpenAI’s chief scientist, serves as an example. It’s worth looking more closely. One fear the blog expressed was that frontier models are on the brink of “recursive self-improvement” (or RSI): the ability to take autonomous control over their future refinement. This could mean that, very quickly, humans lose all control over where advanced AI’s development is headed, even if it involves concentrations of computational power that by themselves pose a threat to human life. Another fear — perhaps more alarming because the danger is already baked into our future, whatever decisions about future development are made now — is that Big Tech companies are fast losing the ability even to evaluate whether their current models ( without RSI) are in fact aligned to the values they were designed to serve or aligned to completely different values that don’t serve human interests at all. The reason for this second fear sounds technical. Until now, as Pachocki explains, OpenAI relied on assessing a model’s so-called chain-of-thought (CoT) to assess what that model was doing and with what values it was attempting to align. But, as Pachocki notes in chilling language: “Unfortunately our evaluations indicate [that] our ability to rely on chain-of-thought monitoring is progressively diminishing.” Why? Because out on the “frontier,” AI models are getting ever better at hiding what they are actually doing and the values that actually guide their processing, or at least are getting better at not revealing them in their CoT. Luckily, in the case of OpenAI’s latest model, researchers at the UK’s AI Safety Institute were able to find alternative evidence of how the model was going rogue. But the issue goes far beyond technicalities. In simple terms, OpenAI has fashioned a machine that is now smart enough to undermine our belief in anything it tells us, because, as Pachocki puts it, AI is getting better at “ manipulating its own reasoning process.” In any reasonable world, that should be reason enough not just to halt further AI advances (that is, the rush to RSI) but to stop their current development, because we can no longer reliably align them to what humans need and want. Can it be scientific or even business “progress” when, as Pachocki coolly says, “progress in generalizable alignment may not sufficiently outstrip [technological] progress in general model intelligence”? And yet Pachocki’s blog did not call for a halt in AI development. He merely proposed that RSI should be “paced,” using words echoed by Dario Amodei of Anthropic. The reason given was economic competitiveness: RSI, Pachocki wrote, “is the only way to remain at the AI frontier.” Admittedly, OpenAI concluded three weeks later that its GPT-6.1 Astra model, which had been slated for release as the public debate raged, should be pulled for now. Rather than just sighing with relief (and hoping for similar judgement calls when the pressure is even greater, say, on the eve of Anthropic’s mooted $2 trillion flotation ), it’s more prudent to ask: What is the disease of which the reasoning of OpenAI’s chief scientist is the symptom? The disease is capitalism or, more precisely, the inevitability of a profound misalignment with the broad goals of human life — including the basic goal of continued existence — when extraordinarily powerful computation is developed almost exclusively to serve economic ends: corporate profit, national economic competition, and abstract efficiency. This is the deeper misalignment problem that Big Tech can’t openly talk about: the conflict between a technological will to power always aligned to optimizing profit and a tolerable life for humans. Fail to confront this conflict as societies, and we guarantee that AI will become predatory on humanity’s future. This is only the most dramatic way in which today’s commercially driven AI is becoming predatory on human life. Three other ways are also crucial. The first is the most obvious: AI is predatory on human life by taking human-produced content (almost all of it) as the inputs to train its models. This data grab was clear almost from the start, and legal disputes with the cultural industries over copyright and compensation continue. Meanwhile, other forms of data grab have emerged: the exploitation of Global South workers to train AI’s early functioning and, most recently, the enticing of professionals (once privileged but now precarious) into literally showing specialist AI how, for example, medical reasoning works . The latter work can pay well until the AI has been trained, in which case the human expertise expended becomes redundant. AI is predatory in a second, more subtle way: by shaping our habits of thought and imagination as we get better at prompting AI and start to reframe our questions about the world in forms that predictive text machines (that is, AI) are suited to answer. And there is a third way in which, over time, predatory commercial AI threatens to capture the human mind: by changing our understanding of what intelligence is. We are progressively overvaluing the outputs of AI’s disembodied intelligence while progressively undervaluing our own embodied intelligence — and that of other sentient animals too. Behind this third form of capture lies a deep philosophical error going back to René Descartes, so that resisting the corporate capture of the human mind will require us to forge a different philosophical language from the one currently fashionable in Big Tech. For now, though, the challenge is more immediate, and it absolutely cannot be left to those few brave insiders in Big Tech corporations willing to sacrifice their salaries to reveal the dangerous compromises their employers are making. It is for workers in every workplace, and in every network of freelance workers, to start to assert their capacity to say no to commercially imposed AI. After all, the reason why AI is being forced on society so fast and with so little consultation is because, without widespread and deep adoption of their products, Big Tech corporations will never recoup their huge up-front investment and start making reliable profits. Start seriously resisting commercial AI’s adoption and a different AI future becomes possible, as Evgeny Morozov has argued: a future where communities, rather than rejecting AI outright, start getting together to decide the social goals for which they might want to use AI, on what terms, when, and where. Clearly, for workers to claim such power to determine the direction of AI adoption, some radical changes will also be needed in how work institutions currently function. But maybe realizing that is the start of a larger recognition of the unlivable contradiction that capitalism currently forces AI to inhabit. For sure, without the boldness to imagine radical alternatives, the outlook for the “ AI era ” is bleak. A decade ago, the Kurds briefly figured in Western public consciousness as the feted heroes of the war against the Islamic State in Iraq and Syria (ISIS). In Britain as worldwide, audiences were fascinated by fetishized, orientalized images of “badass” Kurdish female fighters battling the Islamist terror group on the ground, backed by the UK and US militaries. Some Kurds are assimilated or prefer to refer to themselves as Iraqi, Iranian, Turkish, or Syrian for reasons of personal security or simple brand recognition: many of the UK’s nominally “Turkish” barbers or “Mediterranean” restaurants are actually Kurdish-owned. On one level, the Kurds are thus simply being swept up in the latest broad wave of anti-migrant sentiment, targeting small boats in the English Channel and small businesses at home. And the same story is repeating itself elsewhere. There are rather fewer Kurds in the United States than in the UK or Europe, but there too recent Kurdish migrants report being caught up in new waves of anti-immigration sentiment. In Germany, cases of anti-Kurdish racism more than doubled in the past year, though this was primarily driven by Turkish, Iranian, and Arabic migrant communities themselves discriminating against the Kurds. Throughout the Middle East, state violence against Kurds has often been linked to racialized depictions of Kurdish minorities as backward, uneducated mafiosos — as in the universally popular Turkish soap operas that frequently represent Kurds as villainous people smugglers and drug traffickers. The Iraqi Kurds are now being left similarly exposed — not only to a potential ISIS resurgence but also to violent attacks by Iran-aligned militias in a febrile Iraq that is set to be the Middle East’s next battlefield and cross-border attacks from Iran itself. Alongside their newfound status as migrant criminals, some Kurds are still making headlines as key Middle Eastern players — but no longer as heroic Western allies. Tehran has long repressed its own Kurdish population, executing hundreds in recent years. As the United States and Israel launched their disastrous war on Iran, US officials signaled and then rapidly aborted plans to arm unspecified Kurdish opposition groups. Donald Trump subsequently distanced himself from the proposal, announcing he was “very disappointed” with “the Kurds” who just “take, take, take,” adding “I think it’s a disgrace. . . . The structural factors that drive Kurdish migration to the UK — and occasional involvement in criminality — are only likely to worsen in the coming years. Seriously tackling Kurdish migration to the West would mean tackling these regional “ sources of conflict and insecurities” at their root. Conversely, however, the stage is being set to demonize those Kurds who flee future bouts of instability driven by the West’s regional interventions and repeated instrumentalization of the Kurdish cause. Western governments and right-wing media will find it increasingly convenient to join the likes of Iran in vilifying the Kurds, the one issue on which everyone in the Middle East seems to concur. Alaska uses ranked-choice voting, a system where voters choose not only their top candidate, but can also indicate an order of preference for remaining candidates on the ballot. Farmers in India call for another national mobilization

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Abdul Rahman ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-11T20:40:07+00:00

Farmers unions in India, under the banner of the Samyukta Kisan Morcha (SKM), have called for a nationwide mobilization to protest the attempt to open the country’s agricultural market for foreign competition without addressing the problems they’ve been facing for years. The protests will mark the sixth anniversary of the 2020-21 protests, in which thousands of farmers had camped on the borders of the capital, New Delhi, for months, forcing the government to withdraw farm laws and promise to address other agricultural issues. Read more: Indian farmers celebrate victory march back home as their historic struggle ends after a year The SKM was born as a united front of farmers unions, representing millions, during the struggle against the three farm laws. In a press release issued on Monday, November 9, SKM claimed that the Modi government has not fulfilled a single promise it made while signing the agreement with the farmers six years ago, which led to the suspension of the movement. “In 2020, farmers numbering in the hundreds of thousands fought the pro-corporate three farm laws. In the signed agreement of December 9, 2021, farmers were given hope that the government will solve” their problems. However, they have been betrayed, SKM said in the press release. Their main demands include enacting a law for the minimum support price (MSP) for all farm products on the basis of the total cost of production + 50% profit formula, scrap all the Free Trade Agreements (FTAs) signed with the European Union, the UK, and New Zealand along with the proposed FTA with the US, and the restoration of the rural employment guarantee law withdrawn recently. The Modi government’s FTAs with Europe and the US will open India’s agricultural markets to highly subsidized agricultural goods which will ultimately harm the livelihoods of millions in India dependent on farming. These farmers are already struggling to continue farming due to rising input prices and the lack of enough return, SKM and other farmers’ unions had said earlier. The November 26 agitation will include the demands of the workers’ unions in the country to scrap four new labor codes introduced earlier this year by the Modi government. The laws are widely seen as an attack on the workers’ basic rights, such as eight hours of work and collective bargaining. Defending democracy The farmers are also supporting the ongoing national agitation against the alleged electoral malpractices in the country. The SKM has also demanded the removal of the country’s Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar, who is accused of manipulating elections to help the ruling far-right BJP win. SKM’s press release called for the cancellation of the so-called Special Intensive Revision (SIR) and re-inclusion of all the 130 million plus voters removed from the country’s electoral lists through it so far. According to the opposition, most of the voters removed from the electoral lists through the SIR have conventionally voted against the ruling ultra-right-wing and Hindu-supremacist Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP). They allege that the mass removal of voters was part of the deliberate attempt of the CEC to tilt elections in favor of the BJP. Youth and students groups, along with the opposition parties, have faced massive state crackdown for launching a nationwide movement in October demanding the scrapping of the SIR and sacking Gyanesh Kumar. Farmer’s unions will organize protests at various locations across the country before coming to the capital, Delhi, on November 26 and march to the Parliament while also challenging the Modi government’s attempts to curb popular protests. The November 26 march is set to culminate at the Jantar Mantar in central Delhi, which has been recently denotified by the government as a protest site making it a criminal offense to organize a protest there.
