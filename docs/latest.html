Far Right Gains Power

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Vijay Prashad ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-09T22:43:04+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. Brazil’s general election on 4 October delivered a severe warning to the Left. Flávio Bolsonaro, the presidential candidate of the Liberal Party (PL), finished ahead of President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, with approximately 47 per cent against 45 per cent. Neither secured a majority, sending them into a runoff on 25 October. More troubling than this narrow presidential gap was the advance of bolsonarismo through Congress. The election has strengthened a political movement whose ambitions extend beyond defeating Lula to reshaping the institutions that obstruct its exercise of power. The imprisonment (house arrest) and electoral exclusion of Jair Bolsonaro have not dissolved his movement. His son Flávio carries its presidential banner, while other members of the family have won seats in Congress. Michelle Bolsonaro, Jair’s wife, and Carlos Bolsonaro, his son, enter the Senate; Jair Renan, another son, and Renato, Jair’s brother, enter the Chamber of Deputies. Nepobolsonarismo is an appropriate name for this family’s expansion through the state. Yet the family is only the most visible expression of a broader project, rooted in powerful economic interests and a deeply organised reactionary politics. A Presidential Contest with Continental Stakes Lula remains the principal obstacle to the return of this project to the presidency. His defeat would affect workers and peasants throughout Brazil, but also the possibilities for regional cooperation and an independent Latin American foreign policy. Brazil’s size, industrial capacity, raw minerals (such as critical minerals), agricultural resources, and diplomatic reach give its political direction continental significance. A government that subordinates these assets to Washington’s priorities would weaken the space available to neighbouring countries seeking their own paths of development. Related: Netanyahu’s Empty Seats and the Business of Occupation The first-round geography demonstrates both Lula’s strength and his difficulties. He retained a commanding position in the Northeast, while Flávio led across the other four regions. São Paulo’s enormous electorate provided a particularly important advantage to the Right. These divisions cannot be explained by an opposition between an enlightened population and an ignorant one. They require an examination of the different conditions of work, the organisation of political life, and the capacity of competing movements to interpret people’s frustrations. Lula approaches the runoff with a substantial social record. Unemployment fell to 5.3 per cent in the quarter ending in August, the lowest rate for that period in the statistical series beginning in 2012. Such improvements matter enormously to families for whom a regular wage brings relief from daily uncertainty. But an improved national indicator does not settle the political argument. Employment can coexist with exhausting journeys, expensive housing, inadequate public services, and the insecurity of debt. The distance between statistical improvement and lived experience remains terrain on which the Right operates. Bolsonarismo , or Brazil’s far right of a special type, translates these frustrations into resentment against the Left, all public institutions, and vulnerable sections of society. Its promise of order offers an immediate language for fear, while concealing the economic arrangements that produce insecurity. The Left cannot answer this merely by advertising government achievements. It must connect those achievements to a credible programme for changing everyday life, and explain which interests prevent further advances. A worker struggling with debt needs more than reassurance that the economy is performing well. This has been the problem for Lula, whose programme for the future does not seem to promise more than what has been delivered till now. The Senate: A Struggle Over Institutional Power The Senate election is particularly consequential because 54 of its 81 seats were contested. Senators serve eight-year terms, so the results will shape national politics well beyond the next presidential administration. The PL won 19 seats and will hold 28 in the new Senate, making it the largest party. The PT will have nine. The bolsonaristas have therefore established a formidable position, although the PL itself remains short of a majority and must bargain with other parties. Michelle Bolsonaro and Bia Kicis took both seats in the Federal District, while Carlos Bolsonaro and Carol de Toni won the two seats in Santa Catarina. These victories show the movement’s ability to organise coordinated campaigns and concentrate support behind its candidates. There were important successes for the Left: Rui Costa and Jaques Wagner won in Bahia, Humberto Costa retained representation in Pernambuco, and Marília Campos won in Minas Gerais. These results preserve points of resistance within an increasingly hostile chamber. The Senate’s significance extends beyond ordinary legislation. The bolsonaristas have made their confrontation with the Supreme Federal Court a central political objective, including demands to impeach its ministers. Their strengthened representation increases their capacity to pursue that confrontation and to influence the election of the Senate president. Predictions of an automatic majority for impeachment should be treated cautiously: party affiliation, campaign declarations, and votes in a specific proceeding are different things. Nevertheless, the danger of using legislative power to intimidate the judiciary has plainly grown. This is part of a struggle over the conditions under which the far right can govern. The judicial proceedings against Jair Bolsonaro did not remove the social forces supporting him. Those forces now seek greater leverage over institutions capable of restraining them. For the Left, defending democratic space must therefore accompany the struggle to expand social rights. Workers’ organisations cannot afford to lose the freedoms through which they organise, educate, and confront capital. Related: The Donroe Doctrine Comes for Greenland Federal Deputies: the Right Advances, the Left Finds Openings All 513 seats in the Chamber of Deputies were up for election this year. The PL secured 121, consolidating its position as the largest party in this chamber as well as in the Senate. The federation of the Workers’ Party, Communist Party of Brazil, and Green Party won 88 seats, with the PT itself taking 70. The federation’s growth is significant, but it leaves Lula’s allies far from a majority. Parties of the Right hold 303 seats, while the broader parliamentary Left holds 124. The outcome strengthens the far right of a special type within a legislature already dominated by conservative interests. It also weakens some established brokers of parliamentary power, such as the centrão , the collection of parties that trades legislative support for influence over appointments and public resources, although it remains indispensable for majority building. What bargaining will take place within the centrão will now take place alongside a larger and more assertive PL. If Lula wins in the second round, he will confront substantial congressional resistance, but if Flávio wins, the same Congress could facilitate a reactionary programme. Yet the election also brought important representatives of a fighting Left into the Chamber. Jones Manoel, the communist historian elected through PSOL in Pernambuco, received 249,077 votes and finished first in Recife. Elias Jabbour, the communist geographer, was elected for the PCdoB in Rio de Janeiro. Their victories suggest an audience for political education and socialist arguments, even amid the Right’s advance. They offer resources for rebuilding confidence among people who have grown impatient with the narrowing ambitions of parliamentary politics. Jones Manoel has already offered to assist Lula’s runoff campaign, including its digital communication, while proposing stronger attention to demands such as free public transport. This is a useful example of political clarity: criticism of the government’s limitations can coexist with active mobilisation against the far right of a special type. Such unity does not require workers to abandon their demands. It becomes stronger when those demands supply the campaign’s energy and direction. Related: U.S.-China Relations at a Crossroads as Xi Jinping Heads to Washington The Task Before 25 October The runoff cannot be won through negotiations among party leaders alone. It requires organised activity in workplaces, neighbourhoods, universities, and rural communities, with a programme people can recognise in their own lives. Transport, wages, housing, debt, public healthcare, and the time consumed by work must become the substance of a democratic campaign. Defence of sovereignty becomes tangible when it is connected to the ability to use Brazil’s wealth for the needs of its people. The angry tide has advanced, but it has not carried away the millions who still seek another Brazil. The new communist deputies, the surviving mass organisations (especially the landless workers of the MST), and Lula’s enormous electoral base provide foundations for resistance. The immediate task is to defeat Flávio Bolsonaro. The longer task is to build a popular power strong enough to prevent the oligarchy from deciding, election after election, how little change the majority will be allowed. In the 19th century, Belgian trade unionists from the glass industry (the FGTB represents, among other sectors, workers in the glass industry) fled Belgium because they were being persecuted by the anti-worker governments of the time. Descent Into Fascism

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Sharon Kyle, Dick Price, Henry A. Giroux ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-09T21:17:16+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. What happens when cruelty becomes a governing philosophy? When political leaders celebrate violence, billionaires wield extraordinary influence over government, and institutions supposedly designed to defend democracy become instruments of repression? These questions were at the center of our recent conversation with political theorist Henry Giroux, whose latest LA Progressive essay, "The Politics of the Abyss: MAGA, Moral Rot, and the Struggle for the Future," examines what he considers the accelerating collapse of America's democratic and moral foundations. Giroux, who holds the McMaster University Chair for Scholarship in the Public Interest, offered an unsparing assessment of contemporary American politics. But our conversation went considerably beyond Donald Trump and MAGA. It explored how the United States arrived at this moment—and whether Americans can imagine and construct a different future. Henry Giroux: Descent into Fascism (30:56) The Collapse of Conscience Giroux began by discussing Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and his embrace of military power, violence, and the spectacle of punishment. For Giroux, the issue extends beyond individual incompetence or corruption. Hegseth represents a political culture that increasingly celebrates aggression, domination, and authoritarian masculinity. Giroux described the underlying problem as "the collapse of conscience." This moral deterioration, he argued, is reflected not only in the celebration of violence but in the treatment of entire populations as disposable. He distinguished between disposability , in which certain people are denied the resources necessary to live dignified lives, and disappearance , in which people are effectively erased from public recognition, legal protection, and historical memory. Immigration enforcement, attacks on due process, and the normalization of state violence exemplify this larger transformation. Related: The Politics of the Abyss: MAGA, Moral Rot, and the Struggle for the Future But Sharon raised a question that shifted our discussion from political leadership to the American public itself: What does it say about the population that these developments can occur so openly? Giroux's answer pointed toward the institutions that shape public consciousness. How Americans Learned to Accept Authoritarianism "People learn to be racist," Giroux observed. They also learn to embrace ignorance, distrust intellectuals, and accept domination. That learning doesn't occur in a vacuum. Schools, universities, television, social media, and other cultural institutions help shape what people consider normal, legitimate, or desirable. Giroux argued that decades of neoliberalism have encouraged Americans to understand themselves primarily as isolated individuals competing for personal advantage. Under this ideology, self-interest becomes the organizing principle of society, empathy becomes a liability, and solidarity is displaced by competition. "We live in a country that should be appropriately called gangster capitalism," he declared. And, in his formulation, gangster capitalism produces a population increasingly accustomed to its values. The Powell Memo and the Counterrevolution Against the 1960s One of the most revealing exchanges occurred when Sharon raised the 1971 Powell Memo, written by corporate attorney Lewis Powell shortly before his appointment to the Supreme Court. She situated the memorandum within a much longer American history of racial domination, arguing that the reaction against the civil rights, women's liberation, and antiwar movements represented an effort to restore forms of power those movements had begun to challenge. Giroux agreed but added an important distinction. "The Powell memo was the first shot in a counter-revolution," he said. Related: What I Learned Along the Way: Memory, Education,and the Struggle for the Future Its significance, he explained, was that corporate conservatives recognized that maintaining their power required more than government repression. They needed to control ideas. They needed think tanks, influence over universities, and institutions capable of shaping public understanding. The struggles of the 1960s had demonstrated that education could do more than prepare people for employment. It could encourage critical thinking, challenge hierarchy, expand democratic participation, and help previously excluded communities demand power. Conservative elites recognized that possibility as a threat. For Giroux, the subsequent decades witnessed the expansion of a coordinated cultural and political project that increasingly transformed education from a potential instrument of liberation into an instrument of conformity. Today, with artificial intelligence and powerful digital platforms, the reach of those institutions extends into nearly every corner of American life. Will Electing Democrats Be Enough? We asked Giroux whether the United States could experience the kind of fundamental transformation that seemed possible during the 1960s. After all, even if Democrats regain control of Congress or the presidency, wouldn't something much deeper have to change? Giroux was emphatic. "The Democrats may win Congress. But it doesn't mean much really if ... the Schumers of the Democratic Party control Congress," he argued. His criticism extended beyond electoral tactics to the party's relationship with capitalism, militarism, and concentrated wealth. He identified Bernie Sanders, Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, and younger political movements as signs that important changes may be underway. Related: From Monsters to Machinery: How Fascism Is Made Particularly encouraging, he said, is the growing willingness of young Americans to consider socialism and reject the anticommunist rhetoric that shaped earlier generations. The possibilities for transformation exist, he suggested, but they cannot be reduced to replacing one set of elected officials with another. Hope Requires Political Action Perhaps Giroux's most important observation concerned hope. Sharon noted that many Americans, particularly those historically excluded from meaningful democratic participation, never fully accepted the claim that the United States was a democracy in the first place. Giroux acknowledged that history but warned against abandoning hope altogether. "I don't see how agency can exist without hope and hope can exist without agency," he said. Without the capacity to imagine a different future, people risk accepting the existing political order as inevitable. He pointed to youth movements internationally as evidence that younger generations are beginning to challenge not merely individual policies but the political and economic arrangements that produce inequality. For Giroux, democratic struggle requires more than outrage and resistance. It requires the imagination to envision something fundamentally different. Our conversation ended on a personal note. Giroux expressed his hope that he might someday return to the United States and participate in gatherings such as our recent Left Coast Forum. For now, he said, he is afraid to cross the border. That admission underscored the seriousness of his warnings. Related: Jason Arday and the Machinery of Racialized Disposability Yet his central message was not resignation. It was that education, political imagination, and collective struggle remain indispensable to the construction of a more democratic future. The question isn't simply whether Americans can defeat Trump. It's whether we can build a society that no longer produces the conditions that made Trumpism possible. Online Pornography Created the Model for Platform Capitalism

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Patricia Nilsson ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-09T19:05:44+00:00

Online pornography is often seen as a corner of the web that doesn’t bear much analysis. And yet it can hardly be ignored. Firstly, that’s because of the sheer size of the adult-content industry — a sector that in itself is worth around $97 billion annually worldwide and that, at peak times, commands 10 percent of all internet traffic. But more than that, a discussion of its inner workings sheds light on the mechanisms that govern the special kind of attention economy and platform capitalism that we are all wrapped up in. In Guilty Pleasure: The Pornification of the Internet, the Economy, and Everything Else , the journalist Patricia Nilsson discusses porn’s place at the heart of today’s digital ecosystem. As the Financial Times journalist explains, many of the technologies meant to hold and monetize our attention were pioneered by porn sites, and we can even say that digital life in general operates according to the same logic of endless titillation. For Nilsson, “online culture runs on the onanistic logic of porn.” In this new creator economy, everyone is forced to perform a heightened and often openly sexualized version of themselves. In an interview for Jacobin , she spoke to Bartolomeo Sala about what effects this is having on relationships, users, and creators themselves. Bartolomeo Sala You begin Guilty Pleasure by discussing the arguments for and against pornography originating in debates dating back to the 1970s. You relay the criticism, shared by both second-wave feminists and conservatives, that porn should be censored, as it demeans women (“porn is the theory, rape is the practice”) and undermines social cohesion. On the other hand, you discuss the experience of sex workers and porn professionals who value it for its emancipatory, transgressive potential. However, you continue by showing how this moral debate fails to consider the specific digital ecosystem in which porn is produced, disseminated, and consumed. Why do you think these old attitudes are no longer sufficient, and what do they miss about the current political economy of the porn industry? Patricia Nilsson That’s a big question. I’ll say two main things. Firstly, if we talk about porn from the perspective of a traditional feminist critique, whether we should be for or against — I think I write at some point in the book that, to this day, we have not resolved what sex actually means to women. That’s the elephant in the room. I find what someone like Ellen Willis has to say very interesting: that women should be formulating their own sexual desires and fantasies and taking part in the world as equal beings. But the reality is that it’s still a world dominated by male experiences. As to whether these debates aren’t really relevant nowadays, I think we’re so focused on sex that we have failed to form a better understanding of what pornography means today. Why is it so shameful, so taboo, to be a sex worker? The idea that you’re selling yourself? What does it mean to be a sex worker in this digital economy, where we have a paradigm of platform labor where we’re all more or less encouraged to monetize our image and to vie for attention online? A lot of these aspects you could see from sex workers, especially cam workers more than thirty years ago: thinking about your online persona and building a virtual self to attract attention and sell a fantasy other people can consume. That is the core of platform labor today. So I think that a lot of the conversations we’re having today about sex work, especially pornography, miss the point that we have a virtual economy that’s now completely dependent on this type of self-exploitation, which goes way beyond the purely pornographic. Bartolomeo Sala The central idea of Guilty Pleasure is that porn, far from “a trivial sideshow,” is a key feature of the platform capitalism that rules every aspect of our lives. Many of the technologies that keep us glued to our devices, and that track, monitor, and analyze each and every one of our behaviors, were pioneered by porn sites. Today most tube sites such as Pornhub and Youporn are owned by a handful of monopolists backed by big finance who, together with payment processors Visa and Mastercard, are ultimately the arbiters of what can or cannot be shown online. Can you tell us a bit more about how the architecture of the attention economy is a product of online porn? Patricia Nilsson When I say that the logic of porn shaped the business model of the internet, I mean that some of the first people who started making money online were making money in pornography. I remember speaking to this guy, who made a lot of money in the ’90s, who told me, “I never wanted to be a pornographer. I just wanted to make money on the internet. Pornography just happened to be the way.” A lot of these early sites were the first ones to play with how to attract huge amounts of people online — to get them to stay for as long as possible and return as often as possible — and how to make money from their attention. Take things like affiliate marketing: when you host a link on your website, and then if someone clicks on it and goes somewhere else, you get paid for it. Fabian Thylmann — the German dude who is known for inventing tube sites’ business model by bringing Wall Street money into it — didn’t build tube sites. What he did was build one of the first affiliate marketing softwares, which essentially enabled tube sites to track people’s behaviors while on the platform. Or, if you look at camming, it pioneered live streaming. It was the place where people first got to experiment with such things as pay-per-minute models, taking people into private rooms, and using prepaid tokens for tipping. These are all driving the business model of platforms like Twitch and TikTok and attracting more and more people into this virtual economy. But camming also brought a completely extraordinary notion of the online persona: the idea that we can be whoever we want to be or whoever the person on the other side of the screen wants us to be. This doesn’t apply just to ourselves but also to how normal it has become to consume others, to consume the image of others. By that I don’t only mean watching porn or following your favorite influencer on Instagram. Everything has become this form of consumption. This is how we date; this is how we follow our friends. I live in Germany now. I used to live in the UK. I know what’s happening in many of my friends’ lives because I’m consuming their feeds. So that’s what I mean when I say that the logic of porn has seeped through the internet and influenced everything. Five years after the fall of the Berlin Wall, the communist historian Eric Hobsbawm published his best-selling history of the twentieth century, The Age of Extremes . The book presented a man coming to terms with the fate of the political project to which he had committed his adult life while trying to understand the twentieth century as a historical epoch. Though famous for its pessimism, the book, like the work of many left-wing authors in the 1990s, was also an opportunity to rethink what the project of the Left might become after the collapse of communism. In its final pages, Hobsbawm reflected darkly on the future. The “titanic economic and techno-scientific” development of capitalism, he wrote, had unleashed forces capable of destroying “the material foundations of human life” while eroding the social bonds of the past, including the ones capitalism itself depends upon. “Our world risks both explosion and implosion. It must change.” Humanity could not survive the third millennium by prolonging the past or the present, he warned, and “the price of failure, that is to say, the alternative to a changed society, is darkness.” Almost twenty years later, in 2011, mass protests erupted across the world. The Arab Spring overthrew dictatorships in Egypt and Tunisia, while the uprising in Syria descended into civil war. Elsewhere, Occupy Wall Street emerged in the United States, and the Indignados movement occupied the streets and squares of Spain. I was among those participating in these demonstrations and at the time was reading The Age of Extremes with my classmates. The book made a lasting impression on how we thought about both the past and the possibilities of the future. It was not only the history Hobsbawm was telling but the feeling that we were somehow living through the same story again. For a left that had struggled to articulate a vision of the future since the collapse of communism, the Indignados movement became a moment of political imagination, when a different future seemed possible. For a new generation of politically committed historians, the struggles of Eric Hobsbawm — a communist who had lived in Berlin when the Nazis came to power, been in Paris at the height of the Popular Front in 1936, and traveled to Spain during the civil war — could be inspiring. I wasn’t alone. In 2018, the historian Emile Chabal traveled to Delhi where he encountered a group of left-wing students who admired Hobsbawm’s work. They encouraged him to search for the hashtag #Hobsbawm. To his surprise, he found numerous social media posts featuring the historian’s books: typically an open laptop flanked by a copy of The Age of Extremes accompanied by hashtags about the challenges of student life. But as Chabal asks, what can the life of Hobsbawm — the historical figure rather than the myth — teach us about the prospects of the Left today? A Historian With a Historical Life Few twentieth-century historians have generated as much biographical interest as Eric Hobsbawm. Born in Alexandria in 1917, raised in Vienna and Berlin, and arriving in Britain shortly after Adolf Hitler’s seizure of power, Hobsbawm joined the Communist Party of Great Britain in 1936 and remained a member until its dissolution in 1991. He also became one of the most influential historians of the twentieth century, moving from the relatively confined world of British Marxist historiography to an extraordinary international readership. Emile Chabal’s The Age of Hobsbawm therefore enters crowded territory. Yet his new biography provides an excellent opportunity to revisit the life of a committed communist through the wider history of twentieth-century communism and to ask what Hobsbawm might teach us at another moment of profound uncertainty for the international Left. Chabal is less interested in discovering an unknown Hobsbawm than in explaining the making of the historian and political intellectual. His is fundamentally an intellectual biography. Rather than separating Hobsbawm’s historical writing from his communism, journalism, friendships, travels, and political interventions, Chabal reconstructs the environments in which Hobsbawm’s ideas were produced. The result is something larger than a biography: a history of twentieth-century intellectual life viewed through an individual who moved between communism and academia, Britain and continental Europe, specialist scholarship and mass publishing. As the historian Alberto De Bernardi has argued, antifascism needs to be reconsidered as a central component of European political culture, one that shaped political identities through the second half of the twentieth century. Hobsbawm belonged to what might be called the “generation of the October Revolution”: those who lived through the rise of Nazism, the antifascist response, and World War II. These experiences forged a powerful political commitment that, in Hobsbawm’s case, proved impossible to break even after the communist crisis of 1956. If 1945–48 marked the high point of communism’s appeal in Europe, the following decades witnessed its gradual decline and eventual collapse in 1989–91. Hobsbawm lived through all of it without renouncing communism. His generation was defeated and had to adapt to a world shorn of the project to which so many of them had dedicated themselves. Few intellectuals experienced these transformations with the same degree of proximity or persistence. Fewer still attempted, as Hobsbawm did, to interpret these upheavals historically while simultaneously participating in the debates that animated them. His political identity may sometimes have appeared understated, but the extraordinary reach of his historical writing ensured that his engagement was far from marginal. While Hobsbawm’s formative years and the crisis of 1956 are among the best-known aspects of his biography, less attention has been devoted to Hobsbawm during the crucial middle and later decades of the Cold War. How did a committed partisan of the October Revolution navigate the successive downturns of communism, and what might his responses teach us about the prospects of the Left? Each major crisis — in 1956, ’68, and ’89 — prompted Hobsbawm to reconsider fundamental Marxist concepts such as revolution, class, and political organization. His responses were shaped by a combination of personal experience, scholarly research, and interactions with international political movements, particularly those in Latin America. A Communist in Retreat? Until 1956, Hobsbawm remained a loyal communist, even an open Stalinist. Chabal’s biography argues that the crisis of 1956 transformed him into a semidetached communist. He interprets Hobsbawm’s subsequent turn toward primitive rebels, bandits, and millenarian movements partly as an intellectual retreat. This is a persuasive interpretation. Yet a closer examination also reveals a Hobsbawm attempting to reconstruct a theory of revolutionary agency at a moment when old certainties had collapsed and the search for alternative models of political transformation increasingly led beyond Europe’s borders. After 1956, the problem of who makes history, under what conditions, and through which forms of organization became increasingly central to his thinking. This inquiry took him beyond the industrial heartlands of Britain to the rural peripheries of Spain and Italy and, eventually, to Latin America. Between the early 1950s and the late 1960s, these travels became a means of thinking through some of the dilemmas exposed by 1956. In Andalusian villages, in the hills of Calabria, and later in the Andes and the Cuban countryside, he encountered agrarian worlds that appeared to exist outside conventional categories of modern politics, inhabited by millenarians, rebellious peasants, and bandits whose forms of resistance often predated the structures of modern class politics. These encounters culminated in Primitive Rebels (1959) and Bandits (1969). His travels to Cuba, Peru, Chile, and Colombia exposed him to forms of political struggle that reshaped his understanding of Marxism. The so-called Third World became not simply an object of European theory but a generator of concepts and political strategies. The Cuban Revolution, Andean guerrilla movements, and the nationalist experiment of Juan Velasco Alvarado in Peru suggested that radical political transformation might emerge from unexpected sources (armies, states, and rural insurgencies) rather than exclusively from the industrial working class. Such encounters broadened the geographical and conceptual horizons of European Marxism. They also fed back into Hobsbawm’s reflections on the politics of his own continent. The global circulation of ideas that characterized the postwar left is often narrated as a one-way movement from metropole to periphery, but Hobsbawm’s trajectory reveals a more complex picture: Latin American thought and political experience helped transform European debates about revolution, development, and political strategy. In this sense, Latin American revolutionary movements contributed to what Christoph Kalter has termed the “decolonization of the imagination” within the European left. Hobsbawm and ’68 The second great crisis, 1968, represented by the events in Paris and Prague, exposed a widening gulf between Communist parties and the new social movements transforming political culture. It revealed tensions long present within Western Marxism: between cultural critique and political strategy, democratic aspirations and party orthodoxy, and working-class traditions and new political identities. For Hobsbawm, it raised a central question: What was the future of socialism in advanced industrial societies in which the traditional working class was changing, fragmenting, or losing its political coherence? His answer evolved through his deepening contacts with the Communist parties and intellectual cultures of Italy, France, and Spain, the emergence of Eurocommunist strategies, and political events ranging from the Prague Spring of 1968 to Augusto Pinochet’s coup in Chile in 1973. They informed his important contribution to British politics in the late 1970s and ’80s, when he applied decades of intellectual and political reflection to the specific conditions of the United Kingdom. His landmark 1978 essay, “The Forward March of Labour Halted?,” argued that the working class was undergoing a profound transformation, that new social groups were emerging, and that the labor movement had failed to provide adequate strategic leadership. It sparked fierce debate. Chabal critically reconstructs how Hobsbawm remained skeptical of, and at times openly hostile toward, aspects of the legacy of the New Left and postmodernism. One revealing example is a letter reproduced by Chabal in which Hobsbawm responded to a student who proposed reconstructing a historical narrative in which gender, race, sexuality, and subjectivity would be central rather than adjuncts to a “grand narrative.” Hobsbawm rejected the proposed approach as neither his “kind of history” nor, in his view, Karl Marx’s, and regarded the project as symptomatic of the retreat of the Left. Hobsbawm’s attitude toward the New Left had two important and contradictory consequences. It left him poorly equipped to understand some of the new political forces that emerged after the 1960s, including feminism, identity politics, new social movements, cultural politics, and some forms of anti-colonial and postcolonial politics. It narrowed his political analysis, but in some respects it also strengthened it. Less attached to the revolutionary optimism associated with parts of the New Left, Hobsbawm became one of the first major Marxist intellectuals in Britain to confront directly the decline of traditional industrial working-class politics. By the 1970s, he was increasingly concerned not with proclaiming revolutionary possibilities but with identifying the social forces and political alliances capable of producing meaningful change. This suggests that Hobsbawm’s analysis of the relationship between Britain, the labor movement and the working class was shaped neither by British exceptionalism nor solely by the history of the British left. Rather, it emerged from global Cold War experiences and, in particular, from the history of international communism and Marxism during the critical years between 1956 and 1978. The End of the Cold War Finally came the collapse of communism in 1989–91 and the emergence of the post–Cold War world, an episode that, for Hobsbawm, brought the “short twentieth century” to an end. For some, the fall of the Eastern Bloc signaled the triumph of liberal capitalism and the exhaustion of ideological alternatives. For Hobsbawm, however, it marked the disappearance of the historical horizon that had structured the twentieth century. The collapse of the communist project did not vindicate capitalism in his eyes. Rather, it exposed the fragility of a world order deprived of a credible vision of collective emancipation. The post-1989 decades were characterized by what he and others perceived as a profound fragmentation of political and social life: the resurgence of nationalism, the assertion of American military power, and the deepening individualism of neoliberal societies. For Hobsbawm, the collapse of communism was therefore not simply a geopolitical event. It represented the disappearance of the world that had shaped his political and intellectual imagination since his youth. Many welcomed 1989 as a moment of liberation; Hobsbawm experienced it as the closing of a horizon. The moment echoed earlier defeats that weighed heavily upon the Left’s collective memory, but it carried a deeper sense of finality. He faced fundamental questions: What remained of Marxism when the political project historically associated with it collapsed? How should a historian write in an age that appeared to have lost its belief in alternative futures? Could history itself function as a form of political intervention when political horizons had narrowed? Confronting the end of the project that had animated his generation, he turned increasingly to history itself as a mode of political engagement. The collapse of communism did not silence him; it compelled him to find a different voice, one that combined melancholy with realism and realism with a persistent belief that historical understanding could still serve the cause of human emancipation. The Hobsbawm who emerged after 1989 was continuous with the earlier historian of rebels, revolutionaries, and Eurocommunists, but his task had changed. If the twentieth century had been defined by competing visions of the future, the world after communism demanded something different: an explanation of history in the absence of the very future that had once animated it. His biography reveals that his greatest political strength was not his loyalty to a particular communist doctrine but his capacity to rethink socialist strategy after defeat. Yet it also shows the danger of letting loyalty to inherited categories blind the Left to new agents of change. What, then, can Hobsbawm’s life tell us about the future of the Left? Hobsbawm belonged to a political world that has disappeared: the world of mass communist parties, powerful trade unions, organized industrial working classes, and a socialist project that, however compromised by the realities of Soviet power, appeared to millions of people to offer an alternative future. Yet this distance from our own political circumstances is precisely what makes his trajectory worth revisiting. Hobsbawm’s political life was defined not simply by commitment but by a succession of defeats that forced him repeatedly to ask what remained possible when inherited expectations no longer corresponded to political reality. Wisdom in Defeat There is much in Hobsbawm’s answers that cannot simply be recovered for the present. His commitment to broad alliances could also become excessively cautious, privileging electoral viability over political mobilization. And his lifelong loyalty to communism sometimes prevented him from confronting fully the failures of a political tradition to which he remained emotionally attached. Yet Hobsbawm also understood something that remains indispensable: political strategy cannot be constructed from desire alone. The Left has to begin with the world as it exists, identifying the social forces capable of changing it rather than assuming that the agents, organizations, and strategies inherited from an earlier period will somehow return. This was the enduring insight behind “The Forward March of Labour Halted?” The industrial working class that had sustained the European left was being transformed. The task was therefore not to abandon class politics but to understand class anew and to construct alliances capable of turning fragmented social interests into a political project. This problem has hardly disappeared. The twenty-first-century left confronts a working class that is larger globally but transformed in its composition, fragmented across services, logistics, care, technology, and precarious employment. It confronts climate change, migration, and racial inequality alongside the older questions of exploitation and economic power. At the same time, the crisis of the neoliberal order has not automatically benefited the Left. Across much of the world, the far right has proved remarkably effective at transforming economic insecurity, political alienation, and anger at established institutions into nationalist and authoritarian politics. The contemporary challenge is therefore not simply to demonstrate that capitalism is in crisis. Hobsbawm knew better than most that capitalism could survive crises. The harder task is to build the social forces, organizations, and political imagination capable of turning crisis into progressive transformation. Here Hobsbawm’s relationship with the New Left offers perhaps the most productive lesson. His skepticism toward its politics simultaneously narrowed and strengthened his analysis. He failed to appreciate the extent to which feminism, anti-racism, and new forms of political subjectivity were permanently transforming the meaning of emancipation. But his insistence on class, organization, strategy, and the construction of political majorities identified a weakness that continues to trouble parts of the Left. A viable socialist politics must be capable of incorporating the expanded understanding of emancipation produced after 1968 while recovering Hobsbawm’s insistence that political movements ultimately have to organize social interests into durable forms of collective power. This brings us back to 2011. For those of us who occupied squares during the Indignados movement, as for those who participated in Occupy Wall Street and the movements that followed the Arab Spring, those months seemed briefly to reopen a future that had been declared closed after 1989. The years that followed demonstrated both the possibilities and the limitations of that political moment. Protest could recover political imagination, but imagination was not the same thing as power. The movements that emerged from the crisis of 2008 succeeded in changing political language, creating new organizations, and returning inequality, class, and capitalism to public debate. But they also encountered the old problems that had preoccupied Hobsbawm: organization, social alliances, political strategy, and the difficult transformation of popular mobilization into durable power. This is why returning to Hobsbawm now is more useful than simply celebrating him as the great historian of a vanished left. The value of his life lies neither in nostalgia for twentieth-century communism nor in finding a political blueprint among his writings. It lies instead in his persistent willingness to ask difficult questions after defeat. Who are the agents of political transformation when the old working class changes? How can movements with different interests be brought into a common political project? How should the Left respond when its inherited organizations cease to command loyalty? And, most importantly, how can political defeat become an opportunity for strategic reconstruction rather than permanent retreat? There is an irony here. Hobsbawm ended The Age of Extremes warning that humanity could not enter the third millennium by simply prolonging the past or the present. More than thirty years later, the warning appears remarkably contemporary. The climate crisis, deepening inequality, geopolitical instability, and the resurgence of the radical right make the future no less uncertain than it appeared to Hobsbawm after 1989. But the history reconstructed by Chabal also suggests that uncertainty need not mean political paralysis. Hobsbawm’s twentieth century was punctuated by moments when apparently settled political worlds suddenly collapsed, and new possibilities emerged from their ruins. Black Liberation Is Central to Everyone's Liberation One of Turner's most consequential arguments concerned the foundational role of Black liberation in American struggles for justice. Turner challenged progressives who believe that economic inequality can be addressed independently of the country's racial hierarchy. She argued that the Black freedom struggle helped establish the conditions under which women, immigrants, LGBTQ communities, and other marginalized groups could advance their own demands for equality. Turner's keynote offered a challenge consistent with that mission: Building a more just society requires more than resisting injustice. It requires confronting the structures that produce inequality, developing shared goals, and organizing the power necessary to reconstruct them.

Abdul El-Sayed Isn’t Backing Down on Medicare for All

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Matt Bruenig ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-09T15:10:44+00:00

The discourse around Abdul El-Sayed’s position on Medicare for All is mostly confused. Reading most of the reporting on the matter would lead one to believe that El-Sayed initially supported Medicare for All but now merely supports a “public option.” This is not really true under conventional meanings of the phrase “public option.” El-Sayed seems to be inviting this understanding for rhetorical effect, but all he has really done is drop his opposition to a type of duplicative insurance that nobody would ever want to buy. Conventional Public Options In the health insurance context, the phrase “public option” has typically referred to two types of arrangements: The government creates a health insurance plan and attempts to sell it in the employer and individual markets. People become insured under the government plan when their employer switches them onto it or when they select it in an individual health exchange. When people become insured in this way, they cease to pay premiums to private insurance companies and instead pay them to the government. This is how the LA Care Health Plan works. The government provides free health insurance to people. It then allows private insurers to compete with the free health insurance plan. If people select a private plan, they cease to be covered by the government plan and become covered by the private plan. The government pays their premium. This is how Medicare Advantage works. The key to these conventional public options is that individuals have to choose between the public plan and a private plan. They are not covered by both. Medicare for All Medicare for All proposals do not work like this. Instead, every individual is enrolled in the public plan for free. Unlike under the second type of public option above, individuals are not permitted to opt out of this plan and select a private plan that the government pays for. Typically, Medicare for All proposals have allowed individuals to buy supplemental private insurance for things not covered by the public plan but not to buy duplicative private insurance that covers the same things the public plan covers. All El-Sayed did in his Medicare for All “flip-flop” was say that, unlike typical Medicare for All proposals, he favors a version that would not ban duplicative private insurance. This modification allows him to say things like “I will not ban private insurance” and “people can still keep their private insurance plans.” But in reality, virtually nobody is going to buy duplicative insurance when they already receive the same tax-funded coverage for free. According to KFF, the average premium for an employer-sponsored family plan now stands at $27,000 per year. In the world El-Sayed is proposing, these families would receive this same sort of coverage from the government, and employers would no longer be required to provide private health insurance to their workers. In this scenario, employers are not going to put $27,000 of their labor budget toward a private insurance plan that duplicates coverage their employees already have. Nor would their employees want them to: they’d much prefer to have the cash, especially given that they are now paying taxes into the public health insurance system. Even a superrich individual who is obsessed with insuring against health risks is not going to spend $27,000 a year to insure things that the government already insures. It would make far more sense for them to simply accumulate that money in a bank account so they can self-pay for concierge care. Depending on how generous the Medicare for All plan is, some people might opt for supplemental private insurance that covers things Medicare for All does not. But this has always been allowed in Medicare for All proposals. And these would not be anything like the private insurance that currently exists. Even insurance plans that partially duplicate the Medicare for All package, if they are offered at all, would not look anything like the private insurance plans that currently exist. So from my vantage point, El-Sayed is basically playing games here, for better or worse. Formally permitting duplicative insurance while making it uneconomical for anyone to buy or sell it unlocks certain rhetoric but does not really change anything about the reality of the proposal. Anwar Shaikh’s Analysis of Capitalism Is Vital for the Left

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Howard Botwinick ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-09T14:08:37+00:00

Most economists assume that when workers with similar skills earn very different pay, something must be blocking normal competition. But for Howard Botwinick, capitalist competition itself creates those gaps. His book Persistent Inequalities: Wage Disparity under Capitalist Competition , first published in 1993 and reissued in 2017, shows how the everyday workings of capitalism split the working class into better- and worse-paid groups — no monopoly power required. Botwinick came to economics from organizing. Early on, he helped workers win a union at an electronics plant, only to watch the company shut the plant down rather than deal with them. Looking for a better way to understand what he was up against, he went to the New School for Social Research to study with the economist Anwar Shaikh. Shaikh’s ideas about capitalist competition radically departed from the consensus, shared by many economists on the Left, that giant corporations had largely escaped it. For Botwinick, this meant that if workers organized the most efficient firms in an industry, the ones whose costs set prices for everyone else, they could raise wages across the whole industry. It was, in other words, good news. Botwinick spoke with Stephen Maher about his intellectual origins, what he learned from Shaikh, what the industrial unions of the 1930s got right, his objections to the idea of a Global North labor aristocracy, and the enduring potential of classwide solidarity. Stephen Maher You were involved in labor politics before and alongside your academic work. How did you get interested in labor issues, and how did your experiences shape the kinds of economic questions you wanted to answer? Howard Botwinick As a college student in Madison, Wisconsin, I got interested in labor issues by studying Capital and US labor history. After graduating, my first labor organizing job was in a newly built electronics assembly plant that made cable TV boxes — the kind everyone had sitting on their televisions. The workforce was predominantly women, wages were very low, and the assembly line work was very stressful. I had just finished a degree in neoclassical economics, which basically painted the world as a perfect one where workers are paid exactly what they deserve. And if they aren’t treated properly, they are free to leave for a better job somewhere else. In reality, these women were trapped in these jobs because there were very few job opportunities. They were being paid less than a livable wage, and the way they were being treated was ruthless. So I was helping to organize a union there with the United Electrical Workers (UE). After a year, we won the organizing drive, and we began negotiating our first contract. But three months later, they shut the entire plant down. It was a labor-intensive industry, and the plant was basically a corrugated-steel shell on a concrete slab. Rather than deal with the union, they simply went back to Illinois. It was very cheap for them to move, and that was that. Eventually, almost all electronics work moved overseas for similar reasons. Although the wages were already very low in the US electronics industry, they were a hell of a lot lower in developing countries. That taught me a lot about the importance of certain structural determinants in various industries that can either facilitate or hamper union organization. I also started thinking about capital mobility and how you become an effective organizer in a world like that. After several years of ineffective labor organizing on my own in five different workplaces, I decided there must be a better way for me to help the labor movement. I started thinking about going to graduate school in order to deepen my understanding of Marxist political economy. I wasn’t inspired by the contemporary work I was reading until I came across an article by Anwar Shaikh called “An Introduction to the History of Crisis Theories,” which was in a crisis reader put out by the Union for Radical Political Economics. I read it avidly, and I finally said: “There is a guy I can learn a lot from.” So I applied to the New School. Stephen Maher What did you find in Anwar Shaikh’s work that was missing from other approaches to Marxist political economy? Howard Botwinick Shaikh impressed me as someone who took Karl Marx’s method very seriously. He was poring over all three volumes of Capital , Theories of Surplus Value , and the history of economic thought. He took the process of inquiry that Marx went through very seriously. As I began doing my own work on the labor market, I was impressed by that methodology. How do you carefully work your way down from the abstract level of the first volume of Capital to the most concrete levels where you are dealing with things like ongoing capitalist competition between and within various industries, differential wage and profit rates, the intertwining of capital and labor mobility, and finally, the effect that unions can potentially have on wages? Those are very concrete questions. You have to be ready to arrive at the concrete properly; otherwise you are going to misunderstand a lot of what is going on. Unfortunately, I saw many radical economists making two common mistakes. Some radicals would go from Marx’s abstract arguments in Volume I — or what they thought were his arguments — and then see that the real world was much more complex. They would then dogmatically insist that Marx was a genius, and that those concrete complexities don’t really matter. Other political economists would make the jump to the concrete and say: “There is nothing here in Volume I of Capital . Marx doesn’t talk about wage differentials, monopoly power, effective demand, the role of the state, and all this stuff that is important to contemporary capitalist economies. There is nothing useful there in Marx.” And so, they jump to the concrete. And who is mucking around in the concrete? Neoclassical economists. Because they skipped over a great deal of the journey Marx took, this second group is tempted to incorporate things from John Maynard Keynes and even from more conservative elements of neoclassical theory, like the theory of perfect and imperfect competition. It ends up an eclectic mess. It is almost worse than useless, because you are taming Marx’s argument with neoclassical theory and not giving Marx the chance to speak through his own analysis and really test what his propositions are. The other thing that was very impressive about Shaikh was that he felt that, once you had taken that careful journey and were ready to arrive at the concrete, you then had to test Marx’s theories. You have to test whether they really were the most powerful explanations for what is going on in the modern capitalist economy. And if not, you would need to find a way to incorporate other schools of thought or new dynamics that Marx was not able to consider. And so, Shaikh initially spent several years with one of his grad students, Ahmet Tonak, developing a way to translate Keynesian income categories into Marxist value categories so that things like the rate of surplus value and the rate of profit could be accurately formulated, measured, and tracked over time. Of course, after many more years of work, Shaikh eventually did carefully incorporate selected insights from the classical and Keynesian schools in his recent magnum opus, Capitalism: Competition, Conflict, Crises . But he did this in a highly systematic way, largely retaining the critical importance of Marx’s arguments in Capital. Stephen Maher Shaikh’s idea of “real competition” was central to your work. How does it differ from the monopoly capital view? Howard Botwinick Before going to the New School, I had read Vladimir Lenin and all of the monopoly capital literature, and I was used to thinking in terms of monopoly capitalism. The things Shaikh was saying about Marx’s analysis of competition flew in the face of what I had learned, and I was skeptical at first. But I eventually reread Capital with different eyes, and I saw that Shaikh was right. Marx’s approach to capitalist competition was actually very different. Paul Sweezy and many other radical economists assumed that when Marx was talking about capitalist competition, he was talking about something like perfect competition with tiny price-taking firms. Then, with the merger movements after Marx died, they argued that we now have much bigger firms and Marx’s analysis of competition is no longer relevant. But Marx was never talking about tiny price-taking firms. After all, Marx was writing well into the Industrial Revolution in the 1870s. He made a big deal about large-scale investments with significant levels of fixed capital. He was never talking about the mythical kinds of firms that exist in neoclassical theory. Instead, he was asking the following: How do these large firms, with large amounts of fixed capital and a good deal of uncertainty, effectively compete? No one has perfect information. They are taking a chance, making investments, and trying to get the most profit they can in order to grow and ultimately survive. How do they do this? His analysis of capitalist competition is about that real world. How do these very sluggish, large capitals fight with one another over market share? As Shaikh often notes, the metaphor that Marx generally uses is that capitalist competition is like a war. You try to kill your enemy; you steal market share from them, reduce them, and damage them so that you can grow and expand. You need to expand because a large-scale enterprise needs an expansive scale of production to take advantage of economies of scale. So you must expand, and you are going to come into conflict with other capitals that are doing the same thing. The larger you are, the more vicious the battle is. There is no notion in Marx that, as firms get bigger and more powerful, competition lessens. Stephen Maher How did this approach change your understanding of labor market segmentation and working-class differentiation? Howard Botwinick I realized: Wow, you do not need a modern theory of labor market segmentation and a new stage of capitalism to understand not only how capitalism brutally exploits the working class en masse but also how different segments of the working class are thrown — in Marx’s words — from pillar to post, from one industry to another. If you do a careful reading of Capital , you see that capitalist competition is constantly differentiating the working class because it is also constantly creating differential profit rates between and within industries. And this places very different downward pressures on wage rates. And then you have the reserve army of desperate workers trying to find a job at any wage they can. So even the most inefficient firms and even dying industries can still find workers who are desperate enough to take jobs with substandard wages and conditions. In sum, Marx’s analysis in Capital lays the basis for a complex structure of differentiation within the working class even as the relentless deskilling dynamics of technical change work to lower the overall levels of skill for the working class. It is a far more complex analysis than labor market segmentationists and others had assumed about Marx when they quoted from the Communist Manifesto . From their superficial reading of Marx, they claimed that he primarily described the creation of a homogeneous working class that would famously become capitalism’s gravediggers. But since workers didn’t rise to the occasion and the segmentationists discovered significant and persistent inequality within the working class, they argued that we had now entered an entirely new period of heterogeneity and segmentation. In fact, Marx’s argument is much richer than that, and there is no need to posit a new stage of capitalism. Wage differentials among workers of similar skill have been around for a very long time. The segmentationists also argued that the modern economy was a dual economy with a monopoly core sector and a competitive periphery. In the monopoly core that was organized by the Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO) in the 1930s (steel, auto, rubber tire, and so on), workers were supposedly able to raise their wages because those firms had monopoly power. The workers raised their wages, and the firms simply raised their prices. By the same token, firms in the so-called competitive sector lacked monopoly power and could not pass higher wages on through higher prices. There was therefore very little workers could do to improve their wages and working conditions in this very large sector of the economy called the competitive periphery. Well, I worked in the competitive periphery. I saw some lousy unions that were not doing much, but I also saw some good unions doing a great deal to improve wages and working conditions. That showed me immediately that there was much more room for workers to improve their situation than the segmentationists were suggesting. My argument, based on Marx and on the rich detail of capitalist competition, taught me to pay far more attention to structures and technical conditions and to worry less about monopoly power. Once you start thinking that way, you find many other positive possibilities for labor to affect wages and working conditions. For example, many workers in service sectors like health care are highly organizable because capital cannot leave the areas where services are being delivered. They are a captive audience for unions, especially if they involve fairly large workplaces like hospitals. Stephen Maher What strategic conclusions follow from Shaikh’s theory of regulating capitals for unions operating under intense competitive pressure? Howard Botwinick One of the things that I got from Shaikh’s analysis was this notion that every industry has what he calls regulating capitals. I think Shaikh has a strong argument that the logic of this is just a further concretization of Marx’s notion of competitive prices of production. Regulating capitals basically set the standard for those competitive prices of production. Each industry has firms with production facilities that embody the state-of-the-art, most efficient standards possible and are generally accessible to other capitals. This is not due to a rent situation or a monopoly over some scarce resource. These regulating capitals effectively act as the competitive standard for the industry. My argument about wages is that, in the 1930s, the CIO unions confronted those regulating capitals — the most efficient capitals in the industry — and said: You now have to deal with us, and your conditions of production and competitive costs are going to change. We are going to organize all the regulating capitals in this industry. We are raising the cost of hiring labor, and we are raising other costs because we are going to improve our working conditions and slow production down. You have to deal with that. The only way workers can do this is by organizing the entire industry. Then they raise the industry standards, and relative prices will eventually adjust under the new conditions of competition. Monopoly power has little to do with it. Classwide solidarity is the only thing workers have to stand up against the relentless pressures of capitalist competition. If you go down the road of labor-management cooperation, you undermine that solidarity, and you cannot win the battle. You have to take wages and working conditions out of competition, and there are things workers can do to accomplish that. You have to know your stuff. You have to understand how capitalist competition operates between and within industries. Then you can work out how to turn it against capital. Suppose you have a powerful firm under competitive pressure. Workers can respond by taking wages and working conditions out of competition. That is essentially what the CIO did so powerfully in the 1930s. Workers said to capital: You can compete through new technology, advertising, and better-quality products, but you are not going to compete on our backs. You are not going to compete by destroying our health, forcing us to work at unsafe speeds, or paying us wages we cannot live on. That is off the table; it is outside the new dynamics of competition that we are creating. If you do not give us reasonable wages and working conditions, we are going to raise the costs of doing business as usual. We are going to work to rule and organize strikes or slowdowns, and your profits will be seriously squeezed. That is how you turn competition against capital: recognize that employers are under the gun and impose costs on them. And ultimately, if all the regulating capitals are effectively organized, relative prices will adjust to these new costs of doing business. To do this, however, you need militant democratic unions where the entire workforce and the surrounding communities are involved and ready to act. You cannot sit around a board meeting talking about labor-management cooperation from a position of weakness. You have to be strong and able to impose costs on capital, and you probably have to go on strike periodically in order to make your strike threat real. Then you can enter negotiations with the weapons you need. Stephen Maher How does the falling rate of profit change the horizon of class struggle? Howard Botwinick That is not a short-run question of strategy or tactics, which is what I was just discussing. But if Marx is right about the falling-rate-of-profit scenario — that capitalism passes through roughly thirty- to forty-year periods of growth followed by a serious profitability crisis and a prolonged downturn — it has broader implications. And this is something else that I learned from Shaikh. During periods of growth, if workers are effectively organized and do not exceed certain limits — for instance, if they do not push wages up faster than productivity growth — there is room for real wages to rise. But that struggle becomes harder and more constrained when the profitability crisis arrives. Accumulation slows, the reserve army grows, and everything workers had to fight for before requires an even greater battle. You hope that, during the period of growth, the labor movement has acquired sufficient organizational strength — and even better, it has organized its own political party that clearly represents its interests. Then, when the crisis comes, workers can say: “What kind of system is this? We have to fight constantly for a reasonable wage and to be treated like human beings even in the good times. Now we face massive unemployment and terrible destruction everywhere simply because the system is not profitable enough. It is time for a new system.” Movements in profitability affect what is possible in different periods. But ultimately, if Marx is right that a crisis arrives every other generation, workers who fought hard and made important gains for themselves and their children will face losing many of those gains during the profitability crisis. That is a powerful argument that we have to move beyond the system. We cannot keep struggling only for reforms. And we haven’t even touched on how capitalism is relentlessly destroying our climate. Stephen Maher Many have claimed that workers in the Global North constitute a “labor aristocracy.” What are the implications of Shaikh’s notion of regulating capitals for this conception? Howard Botwinick From the post–World War II period until the 1970s, CIO unions had successfully fought to push up wages in highly capital-intensive industries in the North and Midwest. They were partly able to do this because those capital-intensive industries like steel and auto were difficult to relocate to cheaper labor in the US South. But as that wage differential between the North and South continued to grow, and those Northern plants continued to age, eventually capital moved part of its operations to the South when it needed to build new plants. Capital mobility was slower, but eventually it took place. Now, is there any world where we would have argued that those low-wage workers in the South allowed Northern workers in those CIO industries to become a labor aristocracy that was indirectly exploiting those Southern workers? Quite the contrary — the CIO knew it would eventually be in deep trouble if it did not organize those workers in the South. In fact, many would argue that the lack of union organization in the South became one of the key causes of the post–World War II decline of the US labor movement. It is not terribly different when you think internationally. When most of the regulating capitals were located in the United States, organized workers were able to raise their wages because those regulating capitals were able to incorporate higher wages in their competitive cost structures. However, those potential low-wage workers in developing countries were quietly setting long-term limits for how high wages in the US could go. And as wage differentials continued to grow, capitals increasingly moved to less developed countries in order to take advantage of those lower wages. It happened first in the labor-intensive industries because they were less costly to move. Moreover, in these industries like textiles and shoes, wage costs were a much higher share of total costs, so there was far more to gain by relocating. But eventually, as the wage differentials grew across the border, even capital-intensive industries began to move: first to the US South and then on to even lower wages in Mexico, China, Vietnam, and Malaysia. There is no point here where one could rationally argue that low wages in the US South or across the developing world allowed US workers to achieve higher wages at the expense of low-wage workers elsewhere. Downward wage pressure on workers in the first world is continually intensified by capital’s growing access to lower-wage labor elsewhere. There is no superexploitation and certainly no “labor aristocracy” that includes large segments of first-world workers. In fact, if you do not organize all the regulating capitals across the globe — if you do not organize the entire global industry — your ability to raise wages is greatly constrained. Marx’s injunction “Workers of the world, unite!” is more powerful than ever under today’s increasing levels of globalization and multinational corporations. Stephen Maher Critics often argue that attention to capitalism’s laws of motion leaves too little room for human agency. How do you respond, and what does your analysis imply for racial and gender inequality? Howard Botwinick I would argue that you do not necessarily lose the space for human agency. Rather, you will understand far better how workers’ struggles must ultimately interact with very real dynamics that will make their presence felt whether you like it or not. Whether you ignore them or not, they are going to come down on your head — competitive forces, declining rates of profit, and so on. If you are not aware of and tracking these things, you will not have an effective strategy for dealing with what happens when you press against those limits. In each industry, you have to pay attention to each firm’s profitability, the national location of the regulating capitals, and the particular dynamics of competitive pricing. That allows you to develop an effective strategy for both union intervention and public policy. Without understanding those things, you can claim to be entirely concerned with human agency, but you will be very ineffective and limited in what you can do. Ironically, my more determinate analysis of competitive wage determination actually indicates that workers have far more power to improve their circumstances in far more industries than the segmentationists ever suggested. Very briefly, regarding issues of racial and gender inequality, it is useful to think about my work in two stages. The analysis in my book largely abstracts from race and gender, but it nevertheless shows how capitalism creates not equality but inequality throughout the working class. The combined dynamics of technical change, capitalist competition, and the reserve army of labor result in highly unequal slots within the labor market that have nothing to do with the skill or quality of individual workers. Once we understand this, we can then move on to the question of the discriminatory assignment of a disproportionate number of people of color and women to many of these low-paying and often highly unstable jobs. This would then require a detailed analysis of the historical development of the different components of the reserve army of labor, paying particular attention to how various forms of discrimination and subordination have critically shaped the conditions of entry of both women and people of color into the capitalist labor force. And here, these institutional and historical dynamics may vary within limits in different capitalist countries. However, the bottom line here is that if you fundamentally want to create a more equal society, you better have an understanding of the political economy of capitalism. There is no way around it. A political movement that recognizes these deep systemic dynamics of inequality will be able to develop a richer understanding of why these inequalities continue to occur, and we will be far more effective in uniting all of the different segments of the working class. Pedro Sánchez Makes His Last Gamble

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Eoghan Gilmartin ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-09T13:12:28+00:00

Less than two weeks after a wave of housing protests broke out across Spain, on Monday center-left Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez called an early general election. Upending the country’s political agenda in just days, the protests created an opportunity for an embattled Sánchez to go to the polls on more favorable terms. He is now framing the election as a choice between his defense of the social majority and right-wing parties aligned with speculators and vulture funds. This was a remarkable turn of events, especially as Sánchez’s government had been in the firing line of housing protests just days before. The eviction of eighty-seven-year-old Maricarmen Abascal from her Madrid apartment on September 23 had ignited the largest protests Sánchez has faced from his left flank during over eight years in office. Tens of thousands marched through Madrid three days later, before the Sindicato de Inquilinas (the city’s tenants’ union) set up a sprawling protest camp in the iconic Puerta del Sol square, with similar encampments then springing up in other cities — echoing the Indignados occupations of fifteen years before. Protesters demanded to know “Where is the progressive government?” and tenants’ union spokesperson Valeria Racu warned: “Either rentierism ends, or the government falls.” They were right to be angry: rental prices in Spain have increased 87 percent in the last decade, nearly three times as fast as wages. Yet by last Friday, attention had shifted to Congress, where Sánchez’s minority coalition government looked to rush through a raft of measures to offer tenants necessary but temporary relief, such as a two-year extension on all expiring leases and stronger eviction protections up through 2030. The package also included changes aimed at plugging some of the holes of the existing rent control legislation. With the tenants’ union calling on lawmakers to support the measures, Sánchez laid down his marker, claiming the vote was “the moment of truth” to see whose side parties were actually on. As the right-wing Catalan nationalist Junts cast their swing vote to block the measures, Sánchez had his opening. Having been on the back foot over his management of the Ceuta migrant crisis since the summer and trailing in the polls, he is betting that in a snap election, his Socialist Party (PSOE) can rally a broad progressive electorate amid a moment of intense social mobilization. But fighting his sixth general election in eleven years, he will need to pull off his most impressive comeback yet to ensure a hard-right coalition is not installed by Christmas. Housing or Migration Indeed, the latest polling from El País (conducted prior to the elections being called) shows the conservative Partido Popular (PP) and far-right Vox on a combined 50 percent of the vote compared to 36 percent for the broad-left bloc, with the remaining votes largely going to regional parties and a further two percent for the fringe alt-right Se Acabó La Fiesta (“The Party Is Over”). Vox looks likely to be the major beneficiary of the November 29 election, jumping from 12.4 percent of the vote in 2023 to 18.4 in the El País poll. In recent months, this far-right party has continued to gain ground against a backdrop that, until the housing protests, had been dominated by the fallout from the mass influx of migrants to Ceuta this summer. The dramatic images, late this July, of an estimated 72,000 people streaming in from Morocco to Spain’s north African exclave were immediately seized upon by Vox and its media allies as they imposed a narrative of invasion and national betrayal across the broader right-wing bloc. In this respect, the election campaign looks set to largely play out around two competing frames: an anti-migrant narrative seeking to stoke further moral panic over Ceuta, and a class-based one targeting landlordism and housing speculation. Vox leader Santiago Abascal set out on Monday the three conditions for a right-wing coalition government with the PP, insisting it accept its policies of “national priority,” i.e., giving Spaniards preferential access to public services and welfare benefits, deregulation, and forced “remigration.” Furthermore, with at least ten thousand migrants still in Ceuta and the tiny exclave’s resources under considerable strain, the unresolved crisis remains a flash point the far right is likely to further exploit during the campaign. Yet with the housing protests reaching new heights over the weekend, and with Spain’s two main trade unions agreeing to a one-day general strike for early November in support of their demands, the Right may struggle to shift the focus away from housing in the weeks before polling day. For Sánchez and his left-wing coalition partner Sumar, however, turning this opening into electoral support requires winning back voters demoralized by their grueling second term in office, which has failed to deliver the kind of substantive social policies seen in its first term. The coalition government’s dependence on parties outside the Left for its parliamentary majority since 2023 has blocked much of its reform agenda, and PSOE corruption scandals have only compounded voter frustration. Meanwhile, its principled stands against the genocide in Gaza and the US-Israeli war on Iran have provided only intermittent political momentum. As a social democratic politician whose instincts were honed through a near decade-long battle to hold off Podemos’s left-populist challenge, Sánchez is the center-left leader in Europe most attuned to the need to represent and mobilize rather than simply retreat behind claims of responsible governance. In his address to announce the early election, he framed his decision as a bid for “a much broader, progressive majority with which to bring the elites to heel. . . . and continue transforming Spain.” Sánchez deployed this type of anti-elite rhetoric earlier this year against what he described as “the techno oligarchs” and relished online confrontations with Elon Musk. Now it is being extended to also confront the vulture funds and large landlords. Sánchez is adept at projecting moral conviction, no matter the contradictions of his own record. The test now is whether he can convince broadly left-wing voters that naming these enemies signals a new willingness to push through reforms that challenge their power. Even in the coalition’s first term, when it had a more favorable majority, his party operated as a constant break on his partners’ demands for comprehensive rent controls. A Broad Front? In this respect, the housing protests should, in theory, represent a particular opportunity for the radical left. Yet after six years as junior coalition partner to PSOE, it is bearing the brunt of progressive voters’ disillusionment. Unlike in the 2023 election, when Sumar’s Deputy Prime Minister Yolanda Díaz could run on her record of pro-worker labor reforms and cost-of-living measures, the radical left has very little to show for having remained at cabinet for the last three years. Now, eight weeks out from polling day, the radical left lacks both a clear leader and an agreed electoral formula that can bring together Sumar, Podemos, and various regional left-wing parties after years of debilitating factional conflict. As before the 2023 elections, it faces a race against the clock to negotiate a viable arrangement before the October 16 deadline. The candidate with the broadest appeal across the rival factions, Minister of Social Rights Pablo Bustinduy, has repeatedly ruled himself out, including in an interview just last weekend in El País . Choosing another Sumar figure already in government, such as Minister of Health Mónica García or Minister of Culture Ernest Urtasun, risks tying the new alliance too closely to Sánchez and the coalition’s record, making it harder to offer disillusioned left-wing voters a distinct political alternative. In this respect, a ticket led by former Barcelona mayor Ada Colau could offer a different profile, combining her roots in housing activism and evident political charisma to reengage the Left’s base. Colau has made her candidacy dependent on the negotiation of a “truly broad” left front that would also include Podemos. Yet securing joint lists remains difficult, particularly in the capital, where Más Madrid (one of the leading forces in the old Sumar alliance) has been the most resistant to both an agreement with Podemos and its proposal for open primaries. Given the tight time frame, organizing such primaries looks difficult. On Wednesday, Más Madrid publicly backed Colau as the national candidate for the first time, but as a quid pro quo in which it insisted it get the first four spots on the Madrid list, which would essentially exclude Podemos from securing representation there. Even as these negotiations seem stuck, a last-minute move by the popular Catalan left-nationalist Gabriel Rufián cannot be ruled out as an alternative to Colau topping the list. One of the few left-wing politicians able to break through with younger voters and compete with the Right on social media platforms like TikTok, he is once again flirting with the idea of breaking with his party Esquerra Republicana to front the Left’s list. That would be quite the leap for a figure who first became a household name nearly a decade ago as a leading voice of the 2017 Catalan independence referendum — underscoring just how chaotic the Spanish left’s position is as it seeks to put together a viable candidacy. In polling, Sumar is currently on 5.6 percent and Podemos 2.7 — down from their combined 12.3 percent vote in 2023. Any hope of an upset, again holding off the right-wing bloc, would require a surge back to these past scores, and quickly. The stakes of overcoming those divisions are clear. As political strategist Iván Redondo argues , Sánchez has “no chance whatsoever of forming a government” if the combined PP-Vox vote exceeds 47 percent, whereas pushing it a little below that would reopen the possibility of a progressive majority perhaps again reliant on hardly-left-wing regional parties. This is a daunting task even as the housing movement has given the Left a chance to fight the election on favorable ground. The right-wing bloc’s lead is currently double what it was in 2023, when the left-wing coalition parties pulled off a major upset to deny the PP-Vox duo the chance to govern. Without a dramatic shift in the coming weeks, Spain’s run as an outlier in Europe looks set to end. Su hijo Flávio lleva su estandarte presidencial, mientras que otros miembros de la familia han obtenido escaños en el Congreso. Michelle Bolsonaro, esposa de Jair, y Carlos Bolsonaro, su hijo, ingresan al Senado; Jair Renan, otro de sus hijos, y Renato, hermano de Jair, ingresan a la Cámara de Diputados. Nepobolsonarismo es un nombre apropiado para la expansión de esta familia a través del Estado. Una contienda presidencial con implicaciones continentales Lula sigue siendo el principal obstáculo para el regreso de este proyecto a la presidencia. Su derrota afectaría a los trabajadores y campesinos de todo Brasil, pero también a las posibilidades de cooperación regional y de una política exterior latinoamericana independiente. El tamaño de Brasil, su capacidad industrial, sus minerales en bruto (como los minerales críticos), sus recursos agrícolas y su alcance diplomático otorgan a su orientación política una importancia continental. Un gobierno que subordine estos activos a las prioridades de Washington debilitaría el espacio disponible para los países vecinos que buscan sus propios caminos de desarrollo. La distribución geográfica de la primera vuelta pone de manifiesto tanto la fortaleza de Lula como sus dificultades. Mantuvo una posición dominante en el Noreste, mientras que Flávio lideró en las otras cuatro regiones. El enorme electorado de São Paulo proporcionó una ventaja particularmente importante a la derecha. Estas divisiones no pueden explicarse mediante una oposición entre una población ilustrada y otra ignorante. Requieren un análisis de las diferentes condiciones laborales, la organización de la vida política y la capacidad de los movimientos rivales para interpretar las frustraciones de la gente. Lula llega a la segunda vuelta con un historial social sustancial. El desempleo cayó al 5,3% en el trimestre que finalizó en agosto, la tasa más baja para ese período en la serie estadística que comienza en 2012. Estas mejoras son de enorme importancia para las familias para quienes un salario regular supone un alivio frente a la incertidumbre cotidiana. Sin embargo, un indicador nacional mejorado no zanja la discusión política. La brecha entre la mejora estadística y la experiencia vivida sigue siendo un terreno en el que opera la derecha. Un trabajador que lucha contra las deudas necesita algo más que la seguridad de que la economía va bien. Voters or capital: Who rules Berlin?

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Peter Mertens ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-08T20:09:53+00:00

On September 20, 2026, Die Linke achieved a historic result. With 25.7% of the vote, the party became the largest in the Berlin parliament for the first time. The traditional parties of power (the CDU, the SPD, and the Greens) are left standing. A left-wing government with Elif Eralp as governing mayor is, in theory, within reach. The reason for this success is no mystery. It is substance. During the campaign, thousands of Die Linke volunteers went out into the neighborhoods. Door to door. Building to building. Up one stairwell, down the next. They came not with shiny brochures, but with one fundamental question: what keeps people awake at night? For hundreds of thousands of Berliners, the answer is painfully clear: rent. The envelope that lands on the doormat every month. The knowledge that an ever-larger share of their wages flows straight into the bank account of some anonymous slumlord. Die Linke wants to make rents affordable, break the power of listed real estate giants, and finally implement the outcome of the referendum. In that 2021 referendum, more than one million Berliners voted for the socialization of large housing stock. Berliners consider no other party as competent on housing. The figures speak for themselves: after the election, 37% of all Berliners say Die Linke has the best solutions for affordable housing. The establishment strikes back: the great reversal of the debate Die Linke won on material issues: housing, rent, ownership. But within no time, the press and the talk shows have all but stopped talking about any of it. Suddenly the political debate is dominated by accusations of antisemitism. By stories about the Rote Hilfe, a left-wing solidarity organization that provides legal support to activists and prisoners. By alleged connections to criminal “clans.” By the suggestion that parts of the party lie outside the acceptable democratic framework. Suddenly, the burning question in the media is no longer how to lower rents, but whether Die Linke is “even fit to govern at all.” It is the great reversal of the debate. Center stage no longer belongs to the unaffordable rents, the power of real estate corporations, and the housing crisis, but to the legitimacy of the party that won precisely on those issues. Meanwhile, the federal establishment works hard behind the scenes to make any possible socialization of real estate giants, the so-called Vergesellschaftung, legally impossible. Yet Article 15 of the German Constitution makes such socialization possible in principle. What we see here is an organized counterattack from established economic and political power blocs. They open multiple fronts simultaneously – economic, legal, political, and ideological. Let us take a closer look at the nine forces that have now mobilized against Die Linke in Berlin. 1. Real estate capital The most direct clash is with real estate capital. Die Linke wants to expropriate profit-driven housing companies that own more than three thousand units each. That concerns a total of over 200,000 housing units in the city. Among them is Europe’s largest real estate company Vonovia, which alone owns 138,000 units in Berlin. On September 21, the morning after the election, BFW Berlin-Brandenburg, the lobby club of private project developers, pulls the emergency brake. The real estate group warns of “a step backward in housing policy.” Expropriation would slow new construction. Private capital would flee. The BBU, the association of housing companies in Berlin-Brandenburg, also springs into action. In Handelsblatt, Welt, Die Zeit, and Tagesspiegel it warns of “massive investment risks.” In short, as soon as its property and profit model is even slightly threatened, the real estate lobby pulls out all the stops and lets the organ roar. Here you see class struggle in its most material form. Die Linke wants to shift power from large real estate owners to tenants and public ownership. The real estate sector wants to preserve the existing regime of private property, rental income, and investment returns. What is at stake is concrete: who owns the apartments, who controls rental income, where does public money go, and who may decide politically about it? 2. Financial capital Where there is real estate, there are banks. On that same September 21, Die Deutsche Kreditwirtschaft, the powerful umbrella organization of the German banking sector, issues an official warning. Socialization of housing would deter private capital. It would make real estate financing more difficult. It would drive risk premiums sky-high. It would – for hypocrisy knows no bounds – “reduce funds for education and social policy,” as if the German banking sector had ever been guided by such concerns in past decades. Financial markets also react immediately. Vonovia’s stock price takes a hit and falls 1.7% against the trend, to its lowest level since June 2023. Deutsche Wohnen takes a beating too. Why? Because for these guys, housing in Berlin are not places where people live, love, and raise children. For big capital, housing units are investment vehicles and collateral for gigantic mountains of debt. If you remove those homes from the private market, you hit not only the slumlord but also the wealth manager, the banker, and the speculator behind the entire parasitic ecosystem of Frankfurt. That too is class struggle. 3. The Berlin business establishment Traditional Berlin capital had already dug in before the election. The four major employer organizations – the Chamber of Commerce (IHK Berlin), the Handwerkskammer, the VBKI, and the UVB – closed ranks in their so-called Wahlarena der Berliner Wirtschaft, a joint election forum where the main parties had to defend their economic programs before organized Berlin business. Their lobbying does not stop on election day. After Die Linke’s victory, VBKI chairman Harald Christ mounts his warhorse. His message? “Anyone who wants to expropriate private property in this city puts our future at stake.” The VBKI is one of the most elite clubs of Berlin businessmen, of the sort that lives more on returns from property and the labour of others than on their own labour. According to Harald Christ, who himself holds no elected public mandate, a party “that does not clearly distance itself from antisemitism” should not govern either. Here you see how the two lines of attack of the establishment merge perfectly. On one hand, defence of sacred private property. On the other, moral blackmail. Die Linke must be treated as a pariah as long as it does not jump through all the hoops of the establishment. 4. The arms industry A second economic front is the arms industry. For the militarists, Berlin must become a hub in the arms industry. Die Linke sees it differently. The party wants no expansion of military industry in the city, no military propaganda on the streets, and no army recruiters in schools. That is blasphemy in the church of German militarism. In its hunger for new billion-dollar contracts, the German military-industrial complex of Berlin-Brandenburg wants to create a cluster for defence and aviation. Martina Klement, Minister of Economic Affairs in Brandenburg, already threatened that her state would “continue alone if necessary” if the Berlin government blocked its plans. She points an accusing finger at Die Linke’s critical attitude towards the Rüstungsindustrie, the arms industry. That too is class struggle: will Berlin become a cog in the booming war economy, or will the city build a dam against it? 5. The federal state and the institutional veto Pressure on Die Linke comes not only from banks, real estate groups, and lobbies. Parts of the state apparatus are also intervening directly into the conflict. The decision that Die Linke must under no circumstances lead the formation of a Berlin government seems to have been decided even before the polling stations closed on September 20. A few minutes after 6 pm, with the polls barely closed, Federal Chancellor Friedrich Merz gave a speech with one clear message: he continues his course, whatever the election result. A day later, Merz announced he wants to fast-track with a federal law against the socialization of large real estate companies. His coalition had previously made agreements about this, but after Die Linke’s victory it suddenly became urgent. Germany, according to Merz, had to make it clear to international investors that private property remains safe and is not simply expropriated. That it is Merz, of all people, who brings this message is not without meaning. Before his return to politics, he chaired the supervisory board of the German branch of asset manager BlackRock from 2016 to 2020, one of the largest financial players in the world. What is at stake is Article 15 of the German Constitution.Since 1949, it has explicitly stated that land, natural resources, and means of production can be transferred to common ownership or other forms of communal economy by law, provided compensation is regulated by law. That provision has never been applied. The struggle in Berlin is precisely about whether it can be used for the first time against large real estate companies. Other statesmen also do not hold back. For example, Bavarian Minister-President Markus Söder (CSU) threatens to question financial transfers to Berlin if the city pursues too leftward a course. Bavaria is one of the richest states and a major net contributor to Germany’s system of redistribution. Bavaria is rich, Bavaria pays. And so Söder wants to dictate what Berlin votes. A dangerous Lega-Nord-ization of German federalism: the rich region buying off democracy in the poorer region. Individual ministers also intervene via their official function. Federal Minister of Economic Affairs Katherina Reiche used her ministry’s Instagram account to attack Die Linke over expropriations, antisemitism, and alleged ties to organized crime. Die Linke has taken legal action against this, invoking the duty of neutrality that binds state institutions. Even the judiciary has been drawn into the debate. Berlin Attorney General Margarete Koppers warned, amidst the controversy over alleged ‘clan’ contacts around Die Linke, that criminal networks deliberately seek out politicians. She did not accuse Die Linke directly, but her intervention gave additional institutional weight to the image built around the election winner. Merz and his Vice Chancellor and Finance Minister Lars Klingbeil (SPD) know well what is at stake: if socialization of large real estate companies succeeds in Berlin, it could also raise expectations elsewhere – in the automotive industry, energy sector, or other strategic sectors. It is precisely this precedent that must be stopped. An electoral victory for Die Linke must under no circumstances grow into an infringement of the existing order of property, let alone into an example that sparks other social struggles. 6. The police and security apparatus A sixth pressure line comes from the police apparatus. After the election, certain police unions in Berlin warned that a government led by Die Linke could weaken the police by restricting its powers, its surveillance, and its capacity for repression. The Gewerkschaft der Polizei (GdP) went further and warned SPD and Greens that “the security of this city is at stake.” It also suggested officers might leave Berlin to work in other German states. That threat quickly gained a practical dimension. CDU-led governments in Baden-Württemberg and Brandenburg openly invited Berlin police officers to transfer and explicitly linked that recruitment campaign to the prospect of a government led by Die Linke. That is organized pressure and blackmail from certain police unions and conservative state governments: accept our vision of police and security, or we will drain your force dry. 7. The Israeli Embassy And then there is the elephant in the room: the campaign around antisemitism. On September 26, ARD, the major German public broadcasting corporation, found that the controversy over antisemitism had by then “overshadowed all other topics since the election victory.” Ambassadors are normally not supposed to interfere directly in the host country’s domestic political decision-making. That did not prevent Israeli Ambassador Ron Prosor from warning after Die Linke’s victory of an “immediate existential threat” to Jewish life in Berlin. Prosor, a Netanyahu loyalist and critic of the International Criminal Court, accuses parts of Die Linke of “unrestrained antisemitism” and hostility toward Israel. Virtually simultaneously, Josef Schuster, Chairman of the Central Council of Jews in Germany, addressed the SPD and the Greens directly: anyone who helps Die Linke into the Rote Rathaus, the Berlin City Hall, is, in his view, putting social cohesion and Jewish life at risk. Within the SPD and the Greens, that Israeli pressure finds a willing ear. SPD politician Martin Hikel calls cooperation impossible with a party that, in his words, “tolerates antisemitic statements.” Antisemitism and the defence of Israel have now become central topics in initial talks between Die Linke, the SPD, and the Greens. In this way, the Israeli intervention weighs directly on the political climate in which a new Berlin government is being negotiated. Behind that lies a broader strategic entanglement. Despite the genocide in Gaza, Germany and Israel continue expanding their military-industrial cooperation. Israeli arms producer Elbit Systems signed new strategic cooperation agreements this year with companies including TKMS and Diehl Defence, among other things for naval weapons systems and drones for the Bundeswehr. Elbit wants to build up to eight new facilities in Germany over the coming years. That makes the struggle over Israel material as well. It’s not just about Gaza, apartheid, and Israeli settler colonialism, but also about increasingly close military and industrial interconnection. In such a context, the label “antisemitism” takes on particularly political charge when used against forces opposing the Israeli government, German rearmament, or that growing military-industrial cooperation. 8. ELNET: the international lobby network Beyond the Israeli embassy lies a much broader lobby infrastructure. What stands out is how the theme of antisemitism repeatedly comes to centre stage whenever a consistent left force achieves electoral breakthroughs. In Great Britain, three major newspapers declared jointly in 2018 that a government under Jeremy Corbyn would constitute an “existential threat” to Jewish life. Corbyn had to be politically delegitimized because he broke with the consensus on Israel, openly supported the Palestinian cause, and simultaneously defended a socio-economic program that collided head-on with British establishment. The accusation of antisemitism thus became a weapon in a broader struggle to declare him unfit for governmental power. The same pattern surfaced in France. In 2024, Yonathan Arfi, chairman of the umbrella organization of Jewish institutions in France (CRIF), declared that “La France Insoumise (LFI) has no place in government.” Again the accusation was: “antisemitism.” In reality, it was about left criticism of the genocide in Gaza. For recent French municipal elections, French security service Viginum exposed a massive Israeli disinformation campaign run by the shadowy Blackcore. With fake sites, false profiles, fake news, and AI bots, LFI candidates were systematically smeared. In Berlin, attention focuses mainly on ELNET Deutschland, a lobby organization that seeks to strengthen Israeli-European ties. The money for this little party comes mainly from the US. The German office received over one million euros from ELNET US in a single year. The United States Finally, there is American interference. The United States has made no secret in its official National Security Strategy that it actively supports far-right and nationalist forces in Europe – such as the Rassemblement National in France or the AfD in Germany. For Trump, such Europe offers a double advantage: allies who stand closer to Washington, but simultaneously an EU that remains internally divided and less able to act as an independent international power. American heavyweights like Elon Musk, Donald Trump, JD Vance, Marco Rubio, and former ambassador Richard Grenell have intervened openly in German politics in recent years, typically in ways giving AfD more legitimacy and political space. When AfD scored in Saxony-Anhalt, Trump, Musk, and Grenell were in the front row to cheer. Trump publicly celebrated the result, Musk wrote “Gut gemacht!” (Well done!), and Grenell spoke of “a great victory and a new day in Germany.” That was barely two weeks before the Berlin election. Meanwhile, the American embassy in Germany set aside approximately $400,000 this summer for German civil society organizations, among other things for projects around “countering antisemitism.” They use diplomatic funds to determine domestic political agendas. On Washington’s instructions, American embassies in Europe feed the witch hunt against the left, while helping the far right into the saddle. A desperate move Add all these forces together (banks, real estate companies, employers, arms industry, judiciary, federal government, embassies, and international capital) and you see what really happens in Berlin: different factions of the ruling class defend their own interests but find each other as soon as existing property and power relations are touched. The first front is material. Real estate capital defends its rents and land ownership, banks their credits and collateral, investors their returns, the arms industry its billion-dollar contracts and state orders. Their interests differ, but on one point they converge: Die Linke must not undermine the grip of private capital on housing, investments, and public funds. The second front is political and ideological. As soon as those material interests come under pressure, the debate shifts to antisemitism, extremism, Rote Hilfe, and alleged clan connections. The goal then is no longer only to block concrete reforms. Die Linke itself must be pushed outside the boundaries of the politically acceptable. But let’s be honest: that smear campaign is nothing but a desperate move. The elites play dirty because they lack arguments. No one can look Berliners in the face and claim €1,500 per month for a shack is a good thing. The housing crisis is not an extreme position, but daily reality for the absolute majority of the population, including many voters of the SPD, the Greens, or the right. Because the establishment cannot win that debate, it tries to impose another one. The smoke bombs go off one after another: “antisemitism!” “clans!” “Islamists!” “Rote Hilfe!” The less of an answer there is to the social question, the louder the noise about Die Linke’s supposed unfitness to govern at all. The real extremism in this world is not a party that organizes tenants. The real extremism is a society in which millions of people are driven out of their city because a handful of vultures demand their returns. The real extremism is a system that reduces housing to speculation and war to a profit model. Die Linke won on the fundamental question: whose city is Berlin? Who sets the rules, who controls the bricks, and whom should the city serve? The answer from Berliners was clear. The intensity of the counterattack shows how well the other side understands what is at stake. Nobody should fear that polarization. It is simply the noise of class struggle: economic powers defending their property and capital, state powers trying to guard the boundaries within which an election result may change something. The scent of panic hangs in the corridors of power. There is no reason to turn defensive or to retreat. There is all the more reason to rely on the people and to mobilize. Because in the end, that is where the decisive question lies: who truly forms the majority in Berlin, and who holds the power to stand in its way?
