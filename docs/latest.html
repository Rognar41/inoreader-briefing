The Odyssey Is a Thrilling, Reactionary Lament

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Grant Morgan ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-15T14:19:35+00:00

The Odyssey , Christopher Nolan’s cinematic retelling of Homer’s epic poem, has taken the country by storm. While on vacation back home in southern Illinois, I visited a Dolby theater with my family to experience it myself — and was not disappointed. The film delivered spectacular visuals, clever effects, and breathtaking sound design. Dozens of scenes had my heart racing as massive jumps in decibels shook the walls around me and the seat beneath me. When the Cyclops was stabbed in the eye, its screams of agony bounced off the walls of the cave and landed directly in our theater, creating a real sense of terror. When Circe transformed the soldiers into pigs, the sound of crunching bone leaped into each aisle and made my skin crawl. The acting was superb, as was Nolan’s ability to instill energy and suspense into a story that has been told, retold, and parodied hundreds of times. At a moment of widespread oversaturation and unoriginality, Nolan’s passionate retelling inspired and captivated. It was by no means perfect, but as a rendering of the poem’s narrative, warnings, and symbols, it excelled. I was entertained; my family was entertained; and my mother, a former mythology teacher, was so entertained that she saw it twice. Just as fascinating has been watching critics and commentators interpret the film’s message about norms and social bonds. Invoked throughout the film, “Zeus’s Law” serves as the Bronze Age’s Golden Rule, meant to instill mutual respect between individuals and society. Hospitality, tradition, and empathy are all held in high regard, with institutions and individuals alike called on to care for guests and their needs. Hosts are to be kind and courteous, as are guests; palaces are to provide moral direction and some of society’s bounty to the underclasses; and strangers and beggars are to be treated with care, since any one of them might be a god in disguise who dare not be angered. From very early in the film, it becomes clear that Nolan is critiquing our current moment from a small-‘c’ conservative perspective. The historian Bret Devereaux put this argument well in a review when he described Nolan’s understanding of Zeus’s Law and civilizational structure as almost Burkean in outlook. Nolan views civilizational collapse, in Ancient Greece and in our own period, as driven by a breakdown of morality, mercy, decorum, and social bonds. Vice — greed, hedonism, violence, sadism — becomes the new id driving human pursuits, creating a society where collective well-being is destroyed by warped individual selfishness. We see this conservative vision play out in Troy. Odysseus’s Trojan Horse at once breaks Zeus’s Law and unleashes the ungodly destruction of Troy and its people. Greek soldiers pouring through the city’s gates, with Agamemnon in command, devolve into wild, debauched creatures hell-bent on destruction. Women and children are killed, raped, and enslaved; men are slaughtered by the hundreds; sacred statues and temples are desecrated and innocent priests cut down. With social bonds and morals broken, humanity is launched into a world where anarchy, power, and brutality govern one’s relationship — or lack thereof — to society’s structures and obligations. Odysseus realizes as much in Athena’s temple. It is, after all, his own men who behead children and priestesses, who set fires that engulf homes and shops, who desecrate both gods and mortals by violating a sacred law meant to be upheld by all. Amid the jubilant celebrations and toasts, Odysseus begins to feel dread, humiliation, and trauma. He is hailed as a hero, a tactician who brought unequivocal victory to his people. But internally, his mind begins to suffer. He has broken the tenets of a Burkean social order in which obligation and moral standing come before individual interest. Who was he, just one man, to break an order that had held for untold generations? The trip through Hades — or hell — serves as a reckoning with the collapse of the traditional order. Soldiers who perished at Troy are not welcomed as heroes in the afterlife but buried under ash and torment. Veterans who made it home are not greeted as returning victors; they become beggars or thieves in a world where mercy and empathy are in steep decline. Much like the Reign of Terror that followed the French Revolution, the break with the traditions of old is presented as begetting something far more barbaric. Our inherited rights, bequeathed to us by the dead and upheld for generations through moral and cultural obligation, are abandoned and burned, leaving behind a decayed society. Cities and ports are laid to waste as the breakdown in norms, hospitality, and trade spreads anarchy and ruin. The dreaded “sea people,” really just fellow Greeks fleeing collapse and war, are said to be bringing the apocalypse. Terror is everywhere, and no rule is infallible. Nolan’s Burkean interpretation of The Odyssey , with its emphasis on traditional moral orders and social obligations, casts our present as a moment of ideological and social rupture. At the end of history, politicians devoid of decorum, respectability, and empathy turn toward a politics of cruel avarice and bitterness. We are all now the people of Ithaca, watching our social order collapse into immorality and war. Borders have been militarized against the world’s various “sea peoples”; entire swaths of the earth have been turned into zones of cruelty, violence, and inhospitality; the law has been weaponized to attack norms, individuals, and institutions and to ensure personal enrichment; international arbitration and coordination have been supplanted by a brazen imperial appetite for conquest. Whatever ideals once kept our liberal order in check now seem dead and buried, cast aside by a generation that has traded tradition and national obligation for blood, wealth, and power. The powerful vision animating Nolan’s Odyssey no doubt resonates with Americans who feel our current moment is unprecedented and devoid of reason. Walls are being erected, gates closed, and cosmopolitanism thrown to the wolves in favor of retrenchment and new forms of nationalism. Supply-Side Liberalism as Zeus’s Law For liberals especially, this Burkean interpretation ironically makes a lot of sense. After all, many of the traditions and norms we have come to cherish are vestiges of American liberalism. Mid-century liberalism delivered virtually all the laws, rulings, and norms around privacy, speech, property, and equality that have come to be viewed as fixed and permanent. Liberalism itself has become a kind of tradition. Our economic logic fits this mold too, with deregulation, tax incentives, and supply-side growth serving as cornerstones of liberalism’s modern orthodoxy. For well over fifty years, America’s social order has bequeathed a slate of rights and obligations without fundamentally breaking from the New Deal’s administrative machinery. The role of labor has of course diminished since its prominence in the 1940s, but even this was not much of a disruption. After World War II, labor and working-class militancy were gradually converted into just another interest group alongside activists, nonprofits, nongovernmental organizations (NGOs), and businesses. Unions still played some role in the liberal electoral coalition, but they were no longer where the party’s energy was felt. In this analogy, Zeus’s Law is our own liberal social order. Our law, put simply, was a social bond carrying certain expectations and promises toward both capitalism and the individual. Individual rights and social freedoms — privacy, abortion, contraception, freedom of religion, the de jure end of segregation — were to be protected and removed from political contention. The American dream, or the idea of social mobility more generally, was to be at least hypothetically available to all who were willing to work hard, innovate, and educate themselves in the burgeoning fields of the future. As for state and local governments, the supply-side investments and grants that for decades had flowed from federal coffers into regional business groups, communities, and projects would continue apace. Supply-siders would drive the direction of federal money, delivering an economy that was not planned but not entirely private either. From the 1970s onward, this moral and political order offered everyone a supply-side liberal contract that supposedly guaranteed the right to a safe and secure life. Departing from more communal and class-based visions, it was a new way of seeing your fellow citizens — a way of spotting a potential Zeus in every passerby. Every person was to be bequeathed a fair playing field, with government there to help business and consumer, entrepreneur and professional alike, get ahead and enjoy the bounties of individual striving and growth. Nolan witnessed this order firsthand in his childhood in the UK and the United States. Growing up in London and spending summers in Evanston, Illinois, he experienced these promises in urban centers defined by finance, real estate, tech, and above all steady growth. At the start of his career, in the 1990s and early 2000s, this bond and its obligations were at their most admired: it was an era of faith in markets, faith in limited government, and faith in the power of individuals to educate themselves, invest, and soar. Political battles, when they broke into national consciousness, never verged on outright weaponization or mass civil unrest. Government shutdowns came and went; Bush v. Gore caused controversy, but the country moved forward with the ostensible president-elect; the dot-com bubble briefly roiled markets and 401(k)s, then disappeared from the headlines. The 9/11 attacks jolted the country into patriotic fervor and sent soldiers overseas, but this wasn’t exactly new in American politics. As for stark differences between the parties, both sides of the aisle were happy to support supply-side policies, vote for the Iraq War, and drag their feet on social issues — except, of course, crime. Hard crackdowns in the legal system — broken-windows policing, three-strikes laws, harsher sentencing — ensued, as did punitive drug policies that swelled the country’s prison population. Long-standing entitlements were ended and replaced with tax credits, temporary assistance, and a growing emphasis on work requirements and individual merit. The law did little to protect working-class women, who were now expected to join the labor market, earn less than their male counterparts, and still care for their children with dwindling assistance. Nor did the safety net address the regional inequalities and diseases of despair spreading through the rusting Northeast and Midwest. Nolan’s analogy between Zeus’s Law and our own social order is appealing, especially to those who see our period as marked by a sudden collapse in norms and sanity. When the Trojan Horse is shown burning alongside Troy, it renders the viewer speechless — contemplating the film’s meaning in their own lives, in a moment when unprecedented political behavior and environmental crisis alike seem to signal divine damnation. But despite the stunning imagery and direction, Nolan’s analogy ultimately falls flat, because our current moment is one of continuation, not divergence. Put another way, Nolan sees a moral rupture that never took place. America was not blown off course by some foundational violation of contract or bond. Deregulation, market-oriented models, and attacks on labor were underway as early as the 1940s and 1950s. Brent Cebul’s Illusions of Progress makes clear just how dominant supply-side liberalism and business-friendly policies were within our order all along. It was, after all, the American West and South — bastions of conservatism and reaction — that benefited most from federal investment delivered through the mechanisms of federalism and public-private partnerships. Even in the heyday of the New Deal state, government intervention in the economy was delivered through existing channels that often bypassed entire populations and regions. Rather than relying on public institutions or state-owned assets, as in a social democratic state, the American government became the funder and insurer of the market, with a weak presence on the ground. Instead of directly building things or providing services, it worked through tax cuts, inducements, outsourcing, and subsidies. Government, business, and philanthropy became joined at the hip in a triangular system that channeled federal dollars to private organizations. The mid-century male breadwinner benefited, as did business and the ever growing nonprofit sector. This, in essence, was as far as our welfare state was willing to go. And even at its strongest, labor militancy and class consciousness were never the ideological or material underpinnings of this structure. They might have generated different political outcomes, but sadly they never did. So while stagflation and neoliberalism battered labor and remade the country’s economy from industrial to service-oriented, these events never broke us away from the triangular supply-side bond that still drives the country today. This is where Nolan’s analogy fails: We never had a sack of Troy. We never broke Zeus’s Law. And that is precisely the problem with our current moment. The Odyssey frames our moment as one in which morals and bonds have been broken. But in reality, our social order has continued unabated, with supply-side liberalism chugging along despite growing polarization, inequality, and political upheaval. Sure, social questions have grown more contentious, especially around abortion and LGBTQ rights, but just as in the 1980s and 1990s, they have never been the fulcrum of our order. Broken-windows policing disproportionately targeted working-class and African American communities, harming core constituencies of the liberal coalition. Welfare reform overwhelmingly hurt single mothers, working-class people, and the poor — all groups liberalism purported to fight for. Democrats infamously dragged their feet on gay marriage and abortion protections, only vehemently defending the latter once Roe v. Wade was overturned. Continuation, Not Aberration The core bond of supply-side liberalism — its promise of wealth, unfettered markets, and market autonomy — has yet to be broken. Donald Trump has derailed certain commitments, like free trade and the GOP’s ostensible anti-statism. But those commitments could always be shifted to protect the broader bond and its spoils. Despite historic tariffs and administrative curtailment, no paradigm-ending event has occurred. Ours is still a triangular system of big government, big business, and big philanthropy. Federal dollars still flow to states, communities, businesses, and nonprofits and still enable the basic functions of a modern state. This structure has not changed. Nolan’s suggestion seems to be that Trumpism is the cause of our rupture — that a lack of respect for decorum, institutional legitimacy, and morality has generated this crisis; that the open embrace of nationalism and cruelty is driving us toward greater inequality and violence. Crucially, we are meant to believe that we have become something fundamentally different, that we have broken from the old order and mutated away from what we used to be and stand for. Surely, the thinking goes, we were not always just imperial operators serving an American capitalism that benefits the few and disrespects everyone else. But capitalist America has always been thus. During the era of consensus, Washington bombed nation after nation and killed untold numbers of innocent people and fomented coups and civil wars to defend banana companies and financial institutions. American politicians, in thrall to the domestic capitalist class, deregulated business, privatized the welfare state, and protected elites’ right to exploitation and to untaxed wealth. At the height of neoliberal triumphalism, the United States invaded Iraq and Afghanistan, then caused a global financial crisis that pushed millions to the brink of poverty and starvation. When was the supposed rupture? Trump’s antics have been historic, but as many have argued, he is no aberration. Plenty of Republicans, especially in the base, held Trumpian beliefs well before 2016 — and those beliefs won’t suddenly dissipate when the man himself leaves office. No rupture in America’s social order has occurred. Troy has not been sacked, and Zeus’s Law has not been broken, because no fundamental part of America’s social bond has been severed. The supply-side, business-friendly state that emerged from the consolidation of the New Deal is still kicking, its fundamental logic still in place. The Odyssey is an entertaining film, but its conservative critique of American society is simply wrong. We are still living in the Bronze Age. Society has not collapsed, revolution has not occurred, and no alternative has appeared. We are living, instead, at the point in an order when purpose seems exhausted, promises weak, and the future tenuous. Much like Odysseus, we all now find ourselves adrift at sea, hoping to make our way back to a mythical, happy home that never existed in the first place.

Trump Gets Real About US Dominance of Latin America

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Andre Pagliarini ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-15T13:49:09+00:00

In 1914, addressing the University of Buenos Aires, President Theodore Roosevelt called the Monroe Doctrine a policy “which the United States promulgated, partly as a matter of policy in its own interest, partly as a matter of policy in the interest of all the republics of the New World.” Earlier this week, the State Department posted a five-minute video to its official account on Twitter tracing a straight line from James Monroe’s 1823 message to Congress through the Spanish-American War, the Roosevelt Corollary, and the Cuban Missile Crisis, arriving finally at the abduction of Nicolás Maduro from Caracas this past January. The video presents Donald Trump as the doctrine’s “latest defender.”Dispensing with two centuries of diplomatic euphemism, the video concludes that “American dominance in the Western Hemisphere will never be questioned again.” Rather than a defense of hemispheric independence that just so happens to benefit the United States, the Monroe Doctrine today is presented openly as a tool of American dominance.This is not entirely surprising — the pretense of goodwill has never been central to the MAGA project. But for most of its history, the Monroe Doctrine required a certain interpretive work to be understood as an instrument of US hegemony. Defenders could point to its origins as a warning against European recolonization, a solidaristic shield extended over shaky, newly independent republics. Critics had to trace how the shield became a cudgel.Henry M. Brackenridge, who led a special mission to Brazil in 1817 at President James Monroe’s behest, captured the ambiguous sensibility of US officials. “As an American, I cannot but feel a kind of pride in looking forward to the lofty destinies of this new world,” he wrote, concluding, however, that “when we consider the vast capacities and resources of Brazil, it is not visionary to say, that this empire is destined to be our rival.” All suspicion and no grace or gesture at understanding, the Trump administration is clearly being moved by the darkest historical impulses of the United States toward Latin America.On the day after the State Department tweet, Celso Amorim — chief foreign policy adviser to Brazilian president Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and former foreign minister of Latin America’s largest nation — told Brazil’s Congress that the video calls into question the sovereignty of every country in Latin America. He noted that Brazil goes unmentioned but insisted this hardly matters. A declaration of hemispheric dominance does not need to name targets if it claims them all.Amorim then told lawmakers what enforcement could look like. Washington’s recent designation of Brazilian criminal factions as terrorist organizations, he warned, cannot be read apart from this context. Elastic interpretations of the terrorist and narcoterrorist labels have already justified military operations elsewhere.Ironically, the government of Dom Pedro I was the first in South America to publicly endorse the Monroe Doctrine. The young emperor had the US president’s declaration printed in Portuguese and circulated as welcome news. Brazil, which had earned its independence only two years prior and remained anxious to assert itself in the region and beyond, read the doctrine as a promise that the New World would be left to govern itself. José Silvestre Rebello, Brazil’s first envoy to Washington, invoked exactly this logic when he pressed John Quincy Adams for recognition, which came on May 26, 1824, following the adoption of Brazil’s first constitution. Brazil’s independence was just and constitutional, Rebello argued, and Brazil should therefore be free to follow its own path.That was the bargain as the most optimistic nineteenth-century Latin Americans understood it. The hemisphere would be closed to European empire, and the American nations would determine their own destinies. What the region got instead is well documented. Theodore Roosevelt’s 1904 corollary converted a warning to Europe into a license for the United States to police its neighbors. The doctrine underwrote occupations in the Caribbean and Central America, gunboat diplomacy, dollar diplomacy, and, during the Cold War, support for coups and dictatorships from Guatemala to Chile to Brazil itself.Rep. Delia Ramirez, whose parents fled Guatemala during the US-backed slaughter there, responded to the State Department’s video by calling it cheap imperialist propaganda for an approach that has delivered “instability, deep poverty, extreme migration, and colonialism throughout Latin America and the Caribbean.”Last November, the administration’s National Security Strategy announced a “Trump Corollary” to the Monroe Doctrine, declaring that the United States would deny non-hemispheric competitors — above all China — any foothold in the Americas and treat the region’s strategic assets as matters of American security. The first months of 2026 showed what that means in practice. Maduro was seized in a US military operation the administration called Operation Absolute Resolve. Cuba, meanwhile, has been subjected to an expanding sanctions regime.“What we’re trying to teach them is there are no escape valves,” Rubio told Axios.In a recent interview, Amorim called the Venezuela operation a violation of international law in its oldest sense, the law of nations, and observed that its only recent parallel is the fate of Saddam Hussein. He was careful to say he was not defending Maduro. That is precisely the point. One need not defend any particular government to grasp that a world in which Washington abducts hemispheric heads of state is a world in which no government’s sovereignty is secure.With the bilateral relationship at its lowest point in decades — perhaps its historical nadir — Brazil has particular reasons to fret about the implications of this message from Washington. Brazil has never sought a break from the United States. It endorsed the Monroe Doctrine before any of its neighbors. It has fought alongside the United States, traded with it, and cooperated with administrations of both parties. What Brazil has sought, consistently, is something more valuable and apparently harder to grant than friendship: recognition of its right to pursue its own interests as Brazilians define them, even when doing so inconveniences Washington.Lula trades with China because China buys what Brazil sells. He talks to Moscow and Tehran because Brazil believes conflict ends through mediation rather than escalation. Despite the Trump administration’s implications, none of this constitutes alignment with US enemies. It constitutes the foreign policy of a large, self-governing nation that declines to live inside anyone’s sphere of influence.That refusal is the real target of the video. Amorim has argued that multipolarity is degenerating into something cruder: a carve-up of the world into spheres of influence, with the great powers rhetorically condemning one another’s land grabs while respecting them in practice. Asked what Brazil would do if the United States invaded, Brazil’s defense minister said recently that a helpless Brazil “would have to open the gate.” For his part, Amorim has concluded that Brazil cannot match the military power of the United States, China, or Russia, and should not try, but it can make aggression costly. Hence his call for defense spending approaching 2 percent of GDP and for a national strategy around critical minerals, where Brazil already ranks second in the world in rare earth mineral reserves. A country announces that hemispheric dominance is its birthright; its largest neighbors start pricing submarines. This is how the Trump administration has the hemisphere thinking.The deeper problem is the one many Latin Americans identified in 1823. A doctrine that closes the hemisphere to outside empires is only as good as the self-restraint of the power announcing it. Simón Bolívar realized this early on. Brazilian diplomats saw it by the 1820s, when Rebello, no republican, left Washington convinced that the United States favored its own interests over any abstract principle.The doctrine’s defenders have always insisted that it protects the Americas. Its history shows something narrower: it protects American primacy. The State Department’s video has now discarded the distinction. The United States has stopped pretending that the Monroe Doctrine was about anything other than its own freedom of action. Latin America clocked this two centuries ago. Washington is only now catching up with its own record.Brazilians will go to the polls in October under the shadow of capricious tariffs imposed for political gain, terrorist designations that could license intervention, and a superpower openly proclaiming dominance over their hemisphere. If they conclude that sovereignty is on the ballot, they will be reading the moment correctly.

Karl Marx’s Vision of the Good Society

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Jan Kandiyali ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-15T12:49:58+00:00

This argument has two aims. One is to develop an interpretation of Karl Marx’s vision of the good society that is more social than the prevailing interpretation. The second is to show that this interpretation represents an appealing account of the good society, one that offers a compelling alternative to prevailing views of the just society in contemporary political philosophy. These two aims are related. Marx’s vision of the good society is rarely taken seriously in contemporary political philosophy. It is often thought to be subject to devastating objections. However, these objections rest on an individualistic interpretation of Marx’s vision, popularized by Jon Elster and G. A. Cohen. By providing a more social interpretation, I show that the objections facing Marx’s position are not decisive, and that he has a more attractive account of the good society than we are usually presented with. The Mutualist Interpretation In what follows, I present an interpretation of Marx’s vision of the good society that is based on the following three claims: A good society is one that enables its members to flourish. Self-realization in work is a vital component of human flourishing. Self-realization in work consists in realizing oneself (exercising, developing, and manifesting one’s essential human powers) through providing others with the goods and services they need to pursue their flourishing (a vital component of which is their self-realization). Points 1 and 2 are commonplace in the literature on Marx. Even commentators who argue that Marx’s writings do not contain a moral critique of capitalism agree that Marx had a commitment to human flourishing that underpins his condemnation of capitalism and endorsement of communism. And most commentators agree that Marx saw self-realization in work as a vital component of human flourishing. The primary contribution of this argument consists in its interpretation of 3, Marx’s view of self-realization in work. Drawing especially on the concluding passage of Marx’s 1844 text the “Comments on James Mill, Éléments D’économie Politique ” in which Marx describes people as “producing as human beings,” I develop an interpretation of Marx’s view of self-realization in work that emphasizes its social and other-regarding character. According to this interpretation, people do not realize themselves merely by exercising and developing their essential powers; they realize themselves by exercising and developing those powers in work that simultaneously provides others with the goods and services they need to flourish. Since people realize themselves by promoting the flourishing and self-realization of others, I call this “the mutual self-realization interpretation of Marx’s vision of the good society,” or the “mutualist interpretation” for short. In developing the mutualist interpretation, I hope to challenge and ultimately supplant the prevailing interpretation of Marx’s vision of the good society, which has been articulated by Jon Elster and G. A. Cohen . This interpretation is influential: when Anglophone political philosophers criticize and dismiss Marx’s views on the good society, they tend to rely on Elster’s and Cohen’s interpretative work. By providing an alternative interpretation, my aim is to undermine the foundation upon which many of these criticisms are based and reestablish Marxism as a live option for contemporary political philosophy. Human Flourishing Elster and Cohen agree that the good society for Marx is one that promotes human flourishing, and that self-realization in work is a vital component of that flourishing. However, they interpret self-realization in work in a different, more individualistic way. Specifically, on their interpretation, self-realization in work consists in the full and free development of the powers of the individual and does not have to involve satisfying the needs of others. On this view, other people are necessary for self-realization, since we need others’ goods and services to pursue our self-realization. But they are only necessary contingently, as a mere means for our individual goals (the full and free development of our powers). They are not necessary constitutively, as a core component of our self-realization. I call the Elster–Cohen interpretation “the parallel self-realization interpretation of Marx’s vision of the good society” or the “parallelist interpretation” for short. Like the concept of parallel play in infants, people in the parallelist interpretation achieve self-realization alongside others: the interpretation is social in this sense. However, it is only social in a very weak sense, because doing things for others is not a constitutive component of the individual’s self-realization. It follows that if people could get what they need to pursue their self-realization without producing for each other — suppose God were to rain manna from heaven — nothing would be lost. By contrast, on the mutualist interpretation, self-realization in work constitutively involves realizing oneself through providing others with the goods and services they need to pursue their flourishing. If God were to rain manna from heaven, self-realization in work would be undermined. I show how the mutualist interpretation provides a very different, and, to my mind, significantly more appealing, account of the good society. More precisely, where the parallelist interpretation commits Marx to a starkly individualistic vision of the good society, which involves an abolition of the division of labor, limitless abundance, and a society in which working for others is eliminated altogether, the mutualist interpretation commits Marx to a solidaristic vision of the good society, which involves a radical reorganization (but not abolition) of the division of labor, modest (but not limitless) abundance, and a position that defends the centrality of working for others to human flourishing. Where the parallelist interpretation leaves Marx vulnerable to serious objections, the mutualist interpretation provides the basis for an appealing view of how we might live together. The Good Society In Karl Marx’s Theory of History: A Defense , Cohen says the following about the interpretation of Marx that he develops in that book: The presentation respects two constraints: on the one hand, what Marx wrote, and, on the other, those standards of clarity and rigor which distinguish twentieth-century analytical philosophy. The aim is to construct a tenable theory of history which is in broad accord with what Marx said on the subject. Thus, Cohen’s aim is not simply exegesis but to reconstruct a “tenable” version of Marx’s theory. As Cohen says, such a view must be in “broad accord” with the texts; otherwise, it would not be Marx’s theory of history. Yet since Marx was a “restless and creative thinker who produced many ideas in many directions,” decisions must be made about which of those ideas to develop, and in what direction. Cohen’s interpretation of Marx’s theory of history does just that, defending a technologically determinist theory of history that is in “broad accord” with the texts, though perhaps not with everything that Marx wrote on the subject. I follow Cohen’s lead. I aim to develop a tenable account of the good society that is consistent with Marx’s pronouncements on the subject. I argue that the mutualist interpretation is better than the parallelist interpretation according to these criteria, criteria that Cohen and Elster themselves accept. The mutualist interpretation is more consistent with the texts and more tenable as an account of the good society than the parallelist interpretation. Although I criticize two of the founding figures of analytical Marxism, I do not, unlike Vanessa Wills’s recent book on Marx and ethics, make a critique of analytical Marxism as such. I argue that the parallelist interpretation is not the best interpretation of Marx’s view. I do not argue that analytical Marxism — understood as a variety of Marxism that is distinguished “from certain classical forms of Marxism by its openness to analytical methods, its critical attitude to certain substantive Marxist claims, its acknowledgement of its own normative commitments, and its assertion of the need for socialist design” — is defective as such, perhaps on the grounds that an analytical approach is inconsistent with Marxism’s dialectical methodology. While Elster and Cohen get things wrong, I do not think the flaws are inherent in the analytical approach they take. On the contrary, I am convinced that analytical Marxism’s emphasis on precision, conceptual clarity, detailed argumentation, openness to non-Marxist thought, and “commitment without reverence” to Marx’s texts makes it an especially valuable approach to this subject. I hope to vindicate this judgment and help revive analytical Marxism as a research program.

OPINION: Fighting for Puerto Rican Independence is Local Work, Especially in Massachusetts

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Working Mass: The Massachusetts DSA Labor Outlet ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-15T09:00:58+00:00

By: Dom S LAWRENCE, LOWELL, LYNN – As the national co-chair of the DSA’s Puerto Rico Working Group, I often hear the question from other organization members: “Why should a socialist here care about the territorial status of Puerto Rico?” My answer: “As an American citizen, it is your Congress who holds absolute control over the colony of Puerto Rico.” Massachusetts is not merely a sympathetic bystander to Puerto Rico’s struggle. We are the fifth-largest hub of the Puerto Rican diaspora in the United States. Our Commonwealth is home to over 330,000 Boricuas, and within the jurisdiction of Boston DSA, we hold an immense, concentrated organizing power. Amazon Is Spending Big Against NYC’s Delivery Protection Act

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Alex N. Press ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-14T19:08:11+00:00

When Latrice Johnson learned that her brother had died, she was delivering Amazon packages in New York City. The next day, an Amazon manager gave her contact information for a grief counselor. Johnson called and spoke with the counselor, only to get another call telling her she wasn’t eligible for the service. She didn’t work for Amazon.Johnson delivered Amazon packages for two years out of DBK4 in Queens. Unable to afford more time off after her brother’s death, Johnson went back to delivering packages. As she testified before New York City Council, she sometimes cried as she made her stops.On Thursday afternoon, Johnson was outside the gates of New York City Hall with the Teamsters, pushing for legislation meant to do away with that distinction. The Delivery Protection Act (DPA), introduced by City Council Member Tiffany Cabán, would require delivery companies to directly employ the drivers and warehouse workers doing their core work, as well as set licensing, safety, and training requirements for last-mile facilities. Three days earlier, Mayor Zohran Mamdani endorsed the bill. More than thirty council members are signed on as cosponsors, though Speaker Julie Menin, who supported an earlier version, has not yet backed the latest version.“Corporations like Amazon build billion-dollar business models by insulating themselves from accountability through a system of exploitative subcontracting,” Mamdani said in announcing his support for the legislation. “As last-mile delivery centers have exploded across New York, so too have traffic accidents and worker injuries. The Delivery Protection Act is commonsense regulation that protects delivery workers, safeguards the communities where these facilities operate and ensures that the corporations benefiting from workers’ labor are responsible for the consequences of their business practices.”Latrice Johnson, a former delivery driver for one of Amazon’s Delivery Service Partners, speaks in front of New York City Hall in support of the Delivery Protection Act. (Courtesy ALIGN)Amazon delivery drivers wear Amazon uniforms, drive Amazon-branded vans, and deliver Amazon packages. But the names on their paychecks belong to smaller contractors, known as Delivery Service Partners (DSPs). That distinction has become central to the Teamsters’ attempts to organize Amazon drivers. The union has gone to the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB), arguing that Amazon exercises enough control over drivers to be considered a “joint employer” — and is thus required to bargain with them. Amazon maintains that the DSPs are independent businesses.Amazon has spent more than $5 million to try to defeat the legislation. Between February and mid-May, Amazon made five payments totaling $5,001,377 to the Five Borough Jobs Campaign, a business lobbying coalition affiliated with the Real Estate Board of New York. From March through June, Five Borough Jobs reported spending $4.7 million under the name New York Delivers.New York Delivers has put the money into television and social media ads as well as lobbying, often featuring DSP owners and workers who say the bill threatens their businesses and jobs. One Facebook ad drew more than a million views over several days in May. The campaign also says the DPA would cost the average New York household $664 a year. That estimate comes from AKRF, a consulting firm Five Borough Jobs paid $52,500.The stakes for Amazon go well beyond New York. The DSP program is central to its delivery operation: Amazon says it has created hundreds of thousands of driving jobs through thousands of contractors around the world. The arrangement puts that workforce at a legal remove from Amazon, allowing the company to disclaim responsibility for wages, injuries, and other basic conditions of employment.It has also proved a formidable obstacle to unionization. When DSP drivers organize, Amazon can argue that it is not their employer and has no obligation to bargain with them — precisely the question workers organizing with the Teamsters are now fighting over at the NLRB.Cabán, who has been working on the legislation for more than four years, pointed to the spending when I asked her outside city hall why Amazon was fighting the bill so aggressively.“It’s a record,” she said. “They’ve never spent so much money lobbying for or against the bill in New York State.”Julie Su, Mamdani’s deputy mayor for economic justice and the former acting US secretary of labor under President Joe Biden, called Amazon’s arrangement “a business model built on getting all the benefits of labor while disclaiming any responsibility for it.”“It is an end run around a century of labor protections that have been fought for and won,” she said, a means “for the company at the top, Amazon, to say, ‘We get to dictate all the terms.’”“We get to say what you deliver, when you deliver, how fast you deliver, and what you wear when you deliver it,” she said, describing the company’s position. “But when something happens to you, you don’t get paid what you’re supposed to get paid; you get injured on the job, which is very commonplace, they take no responsibility for it.”Johnson, the DBK4 driver, and her coworkers later began organizing with the Teamsters. Last year, Amazon terminated its relationship with the DSP that employed them, leaving more than one hundred drivers without jobs. Johnson and the Teamsters say Amazon terminated the contract in retaliation for their organizing; Amazon has denied retaliating against workers for union activity.New York City Council Member Tiffany Cabán speaks outside city hall in support of the Delivery Protection Act. (Courtesy ALIGN)“I was laid off last year, back in August, with the rest of my coworkers,” Johnson told me. “The whole DSP was laid off, over one hundred drivers, and we know that it was retaliation for trying to organize the workplace.”“It definitely proves that Amazon has that power to just get rid of us,” she said.Johnson said direct employment would also make a difference when drivers get hurt. “Amazon can back away and say we don’t have anything to do with it,” she said. “That worker who got hurt has no help and is out of work with no pay.” For a driver, she added, “I just bust my ass working and delivering all these packages for all these months or years, and I get nothing.”“Isn’t it crazy in this day and age, that you’re working for Amazon, but Amazon says you’re not working for them?” Vinnie Perrone, president of Teamsters Local 804, told me. Having the drivers become direct employees, he said, “would be a tremendous help.”Local 804 represents United Parcel Service (UPS) workers, whose jobs offer Perrone a point of comparison. “They don’t want a unionized workforce. It’s very simple,” he said. “If UPS could avoid a unionized workforce, they would do it in a heartbeat.” But for unionized UPS members, he said, “a fair day’s work encompasses your salary and your benefits — and Amazon doesn’t want to pay.”Amazon says the more than forty DSPs operating in New York City are independent small businesses, employing more than five thousand people whose jobs would be threatened by the bill. The company argues that direct employment could raise delivery costs and put the contractors themselves out of business. Christine Chan, whose Harlem-based Highgate Logistics employs about 175 people delivering Amazon packages by cargo bike, told the New York Times, “You’re betting that Amazon is going to employ these people directly. They are, Su said, “completely under the thumb of Amazon.” If a DSP wants to give drivers time to rest because of the heat, she said, “Amazon tells them no, and even has the power to cut them off.”Amazon has also threatened to move delivery facilities outside the city. They need to be as close to the door as possible.” Moving further away, he said, would mean spending more on gas and tolls, and taking longer to make deliveries.Cabán pointed to the workers outside city hall. “It’s a lot of black and brown workers just trying to provide for their families,” she said. “They want to go to work and not come home hurt, and they want to go to work and know that they have their job the next day.” New Yorkers are on pace to receive roughly a billion packages this year, around three million a day.“A person has to deliver them,” she said. “And if Amazon doesn’t want to do it, certainly somebody else will. Mamdani, she said, was “taking a very strong stand. We’re going to put the full force of this administration behind getting this bill passed.”“I just want Amazon to take accountability,” said Johnson, the former DBK4 driver. “Without us, there would be no Amazon. So respect your workers.”

Learning From the Byron Campaign for Congress

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Midwestern Socialist -- Chicago DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-14T16:51:03+00:00

Over the last few months, it has been extremely exciting and gratifying to see the way members of the Chicago Democratic Socialists of America turned out to try to get our comrade Byron Sigcho-Lopez on the ballot to run for Congress. Whether motivated by anger at the comically underhanded attempts by Chuy and Patty Garcia to deny voters a choice in who represents them in Congress, a respect and admiration for Byron’s willingness to fight for the working class of the city, or both, our members ran a field campaign that gathered almost 13,000 signatures (65% of all signatures collected by the campaign) as part of the effort to get Byron on the ballot. I am not writing this to pretend that those efforts did not ultimately fall short, nor do I think it is helpful to try to pretend that we don’t all feel a little sad, frustrated, or demotivated about the way things ended. But we are socialists. Workplace organizing doesn’t stop when you run into a wall. Our brothers, sisters, and siblings of United Auto Workers Local 42 working for Volkswagen in Chattanooga, Tennessee lost two National Labor Relations Board elections before winning their union in 2024, and Lula da Silva ran for president in Brazil three times before finally breaking through in 2002. So we know we are in good company. With that being said, we should take a second and look at what we did do. Accomplishments -We turned out 167 of our members and 203 people in total to our canvasses. -We had 56 members turn out to three or more canvases. -We added 12 new people who are trained to be canvass leads. -We made connections on the southwest side with organizations like the Pilsen Defense Alliance and MORENA Chicago. Comrades who had never worked on an election campaign were able to talk to thousands and thousands of working-class Chicagoans about socialism, abolishing ICE, Medicare for All, and a more just, anti-imperialist foreign policy. Members of our South Side branch who had not been active in the chapter started leading canvasses, coming to meetings, and integrating themselves more into the chapter. Our West Cook branch ran a whole field operation of their own, building capacity to run campaigns in the west suburbs themselves. The Democracy Working Group, the Environmental Justice Working Group, the Membership Engagement Committee, and the Executive Committee all turned out members to canvasses they co-sponsored. Comrades in the Chicago Teachers Union organized members in their workplaces to turnout to canvas for Byron. What I was most excited about was the way we showed up across geographical lines. Every branch sponsored multiple canvasses. We organized car pools to assist comrades in getting down to neighborhoods from West Lawn to Pilsen. The branch that had the most members turn out for Byron was the farthest away: the North Side Red Line branch. Their efforts in making sure that comrades were linked to people that could give them rides helped turn out members, and serves as a model we should try to replicate as much as possible going forward. In this campaign, we were able to answer the question of whether or not our membership is willing to travel to electorally organize with a resounding yes. With that being said, Byron did not get on the ballot. The Democratic Party, the Chuy Garcia machine, and Illinois election laws (designed by the very people that benefit from them) conspired to use a rigged system to keep Byron from being able to run to keep their self-appointed heirs from having to face a real challenge. This is an important thing to remember; the people who benefit from the status quo, the people who serve the interests of capital, are not going to give up their advantages without a fight. They will fight dirty, and they will be ruthless in the protection of their interests and their offices. But we should not despair! Losses are not truly losses if we identify lessons to be learned from them and incorporate those lessons into actions we take in the future. Lessons Learned Structural Barriers for Independent Ballot Line The first of those lessons, in my opinion, is that in Illinois, with the way current laws are set up, barring extreme circumstances (like what happened in the 4th congressional district), we should focus on contesting the Democratic primary. It has been said over and over in Byron’s campaign that Patty Garcia only needed to get 900 signatures to be on the ballot, whereas Byron needed to get roughly 10,800. In addition, there is a maximum number of signatures one can gather. Bryon was only allowed to submit 18,700 signatures, which negates a major defense against signature challenges. A candidate trying to run as an independent or with a not-currently-established third party cannot even begin circulating petitions until after the primary is held, and must wait until the end of May to turn them in. The Democratic party is going to be lying in wait for them there, as we have seen. They will unleash an army of lawyers and political operatives to comb through signatures, objecting to anything they find whether in good faith or not. If a candidate survives that, they will start their campaign on the back foot, with even less time to organize the kind of field campaign necessary to win. We spent so much time simply trying to get the signatures necessary to get on the ballot that we were not able to do the kinds of things we would have been able to do if we had our candidate already on the ballot. We were not able to construct a list of likely Byron voters, or start the process of doing the persuasive canvassing that is needed to build on that list. Having to start that process in June against a candidate who has been able to do those things since January was always going to be a very difficult challenge. This state of affairs is not only frustrating, but also anti-democratic. The system is rigged against candidates trying to run outside of the two-party system. But we are socialists. Many of us are Marxists. We do not view the world through an idealistic lens, but a materialist one. These are the laws as they currently exist, and we should fight to change them. At the same time, we must both acknowledge that they are a barrier, and that comrades across the country running in partisan races have been able to elect avowed socialists to both federal and state offices by snatching the Democratic Party’s ballot line away from them, taking these struggles out of the hands of party lawyers and paid hacks and placing them in the hands of the electorate. Of course, these issues will come up even if we run on a Democratic ballot line. Again, the capitalist class and their officeholders will not hesitate to utilize anything they can to put roadblocks in our way. But if we look at the numbers needed to both get on the ballot andd win a primary, the struggle becomes easier. A candidate would have only needed to get around 700 signatures to get on the ballot to run in the Democratic primary in 2026. This is in contrast to needing over 10,000 to run as a third-party or independent candidate. The smaller number allows for a better chance of being able to survive any potential challenges, if only by being able to double, triple, or quadruple the number of signatures needed, a strategy denied to independent candidacies by a signature cap. In addition to that, the win number becomes much smaller. In the last competitive Democratic primary in the 4th Congressional District, Chuy Garcia defeated local goblin Ray Lopez, winning 30,443 votes to RayLo’s 13,286. What I saw floated around for Byron’s win number (what it would take to win the race), what was in the packet to go off to National for a potential endorsement, was roughly 70,000 votes. In almost any case yet seen, the winner of a Democratic primary goes on to win the general election in Chicago. Illinois has laws to prevent losers of primaries from running in general elections. From a purely strategic perspective, barring blatant corruption like we have seen this year, it makes far more sense to contest partisan races on the Democratic ballot line. From an ideological standpoint, I do not think that Darializa Avila Chevalier or Chris Rabb are going to fight for socialism or working people any less because they entered Congress through a Democratic Party ballot line. American laws do not allow for the type of disciplining of politicians that many comrades would like. If every DSA socialist in office was elected on the Socialist Party line, we could not recall them. We could expel them from the party, but they could contest our ballot line in the next election. Simply put, if we choose to push forward with a complete breach with an elected official, no matter what vehicle they were elected through, we still have to raise up a challenger and defeat them on Election Day. In effect, the Democratic Party gets to choose which independents make it on the ballot; they allow rightists, centrists, and weak leftist challengers, but can effectively block socialist and progressive candidates like Byron and Mayra who have a real shot. Given these handicaps, without election reform, I can’t imagine the race where it would be worth running Chicago DSA-endorsed candidates as independents when the Democratic Party ballot line is contestable. The challenges are vast and the benefits relatively small. With that being said, there are certainly things we could have done to make it easy for ourselves. To begin with, we could have been curing (the process of checking signatures to make sure they are registered voters at the address they listed) petition sheets as they were gathered. While this was somewhat out of our hands as a chapter, if we had been curing as we went from the beginning, I believe we would have been able to identify the unregistered voters or misregistered voters we had on our petitions as we were getting them. We would have had a running count and known exactly where we were. I would suggest, going forward, that in any campaign we run, we should have a dedicated group of people going over signatures in between canvasses. Looking through the amount of signatures, I believe that we could have done better in really highlighting to our canvassers the need to make sure that people signing the petition were registered voters in the district. I think part of this was caused by not having as many Spanish-speaking canvassers as we needed, and part of this was caused by a desire to get as many signatures as we could. We probably canvassed in Pilsen for longer than we needed to, and moving to the west of the district where there were more English speakers would have helped with this issue. There was a feeling that ‘when in doubt, have them sign’ that probably should have been tempered with a caution about making sure our signatures were registered voters in the district. Having more trainings independent of the canvasses would have been very helpful in drilling home that we needed to make sure that the voter was registered at the given address. I don’t think going door-to-door to get signatures would have solved the problem as much as it would have created a different one. Simply put, when we went door-to-door, we were just not getting enough signatures for us to have been able to meet our target. In many instances, it was very common for a team of two to come back from a shift with fewer than ten signatures. Where records exist, the average number of signatures gathered from a door-to-door canvass team was six. It’s true that really training people on talking to everyone they came into contact with in a neighborhood would have certainly added to that number, but I don’t think it would have been enough to really put a dent in that difference. There is a formula one can use to figure out how many voter attempts needed to be made to reach the goal you are seeking. For our purposes, we will say the goal is 11,000 signatures. The formula is 11,000, divided by the percentage of people who answer the door, divided by the percentage of people you talk to who will sign the petition. So we would say 11,000, divided by an 18% contact rate, divided by a 75% success rate would mean to get our goal we would have needed to knock 97,777 doors. To place that number in perspective, during the entirety of Chris Rabb’s campaign for Congress in Philadelphia, they made 140,000 voter attempts. That is not to say we used VAN (the voter management software used to do door-to-door canvassing) effectively. It would have been a great tool to check signatures against voter registration tools, as was seen when signatures were challenged. Campaign Operations In addition, there are things we can improve upon that are independent of the specifics of the campaign. We did not do the best job of collecting data. Often, canvassers went out and there was no data collected beyond who showed up and how many signatures were collected. We did not always remember to have people sign in. While this did not prevent us from accomplishing what we set out to do, it does mean that our analysis of what we did is less than perfect. Our communications during the course of the petitioning was uneven. In my opinion, in future campaigns, it will be essential to have regular meetings with the Communications Committee to discuss what we want to say and how we want to say it, and have a schedule of regular communications about the campaigns we are doing and the issues we want to highlight as we go forward. Our comms should be able to inform the wider public about what we are fighting for, and should make electoral organizing seem fun. Member Engagement and Cohesion This brings me to the last item I think we can improve on going forward. We really struggled to add any sense of social cohesion. Ideally, participating in the struggle binds comrades together. We are more likely to spend three or four hours on a weekend canvassing if we are doing it with people that we feel we have a bond with. Some of that bonding is forged in the work, but many of our sister chapters that have been successful in this type of organizing have regular social debriefs after canvasses and other events where comrades can not only talk about the work, but also get to know each other better. It’s certainly true that some of the issues we had with pulling those off had to do with the nature of the campaign. Comrades who were coming from the Northside probably were less inclined to want to stick around facing a 45 minute to an hour commute to get home from the canvass launch. In city council elections that will no doubt be more dispersed across the city, we will probably find comrades more willing to hang out with others afterwards. With that said, however, we will need to work on making these kinds of things more accessible and ‘built-in’ to future campaigns, as I think they will help quite a bit with getting comrades to come back to more canvasses and events. Looking Ahead Any campaign we do as a chapter is going to have more than one vector of determining whether or not it was a success. We failed to get Byron on the ballot, but we actively campaigned in parts of the city we did not have much of a presence in, we rebuilt a cadre of the membership excited and willing to work in electoral organizing, and we identified a core of people that are willing to step into leadership roles in future campaigns we will work on. We should be proud of what we accomplished this spring. We should be excited about what lies ahead for us as a chapter! Chicago DSA has a cadre of veteran organizers with a wealth of knowledge, we have newer members who are excited to talk to people about socialism and are learning how to become more effective organizers, and we have a plan for 2027, informed by the lessons we have learned over the last eight years. As we have seen across the country, nothing energizes people about DSA like electoral victories. If we continue to work, execute our plans, and learn from the inevitable mistakes we will make along the way, there will come a time where the next spike in DSA membership will come from Chicago. Colombia and Israel’s Cynical Disaster Diplomacy

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Gabe Levine-Drizin ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-14T16:10:36+00:00

On the morning of August 10, a powerful 7.4 magnitude earthquake struck western Colombia, leaving nearly three hundred people dead, four thousand injured, and potentially thousands more missing by the latest count. The crisis created an early test for new far-right President Abelardo de la Espriella, who was inaugurated days before. A few hours after the quake, as Colombians still lay under the rubble of destroyed buildings, the government published a long statement recognizing Israeli sovereignty over the Golan Heights, a Syrian territory illegally occupied by the country since 1967. The statement, which triggered a diplomatic crisis with the Arab states, placed Colombia alongside the United States as the only other country in the world to recognize Israel’s claim over the territory. While the Colombian government was rightfully panned at home and abroad for the timing of its announcement, it was not incidental. Although de la Espriella’s desire to deepen ties with Israel predates the earthquake, the new administration’s moves since the disaster demonstrate that it plans on using the crisis to justify its larger rapprochement with the Middle Eastern country. This dynamic just played out in Venezuela, where Israel’s provision of humanitarian aid in the wake of deadly earthquakes paved the way for the reestablishment of consular relations between the former foes. The closer alignment between Colombia and Israel is especially difficult to swallow given the former country’s recent role as a leader of the global Palestinian solidarity movement under President Gustavo Petro, de la Espriella’s predecessor. Nevertheless, it’s part of a wider regional trend in which far-right governments brandish their allegiance to Israel or immediately make overtures to its leaders upon taking power. Disaster Diplomacy The August 10 earthquake caused intense damage in the departments of Chocó (the epicenter), Valle del Cauca, Risaralda, Quindío, and Caldas. The power went out, buildings collapsed, and the human toll continues to climb . The government, scrambling to deal with the chaos, mobilized state resources and relied on local communities themselves for much of the immediate disaster relief. The first seventy-two hours are regarded as essential in earthquake response to preserving the life of trapped survivors, yet it took a whole three days for the state to formally accept international rescue teams and, when it finally did, it limited itself to collaborating only with right-wing allies — the United States, Ecuador, El Salvador, and Israel. While the last could come as a surprise, this was a familiar role for the Israelis to play in the region. For decades , the country has provided humanitarian aid and rescue teams in the aftermath of natural disasters. In recent years, the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) and the Israeli Forum for International Aid (IsraAID) have deployed to Peru , Mexico , and Haiti following earthquakes as well as Brazil after its 2019 dam collapse . This aid functions as a form of disaster diplomacy that seeks to launder Israel’s image on the international stage even as it simultaneously wields humanitarian aid in Gaza as a weapon of war against Palestinians. Through these means, the Israeli state seeks to improve diplomatic relations with countries whose governments may at one time have been more critical. In neighboring Venezuela, the provision of Israeli aid in the wake of devastating twin earthquakes set the stage for their reestablishment of consular ties. As Simón Rodríguez recently reported in NACLA , Israel’s rescue mission was explicitly framed by both sides as a way to reinitiate diplomatic ties that had been severed under former Venezuelan leader Hugo Chávez. Early on, Interim President Delcy Rodríguez expressed her hope that the aid would “open new paths and doors” with “countries with which we do not have diplomatic relations”; Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, meanwhile, thanked the Israeli rescue team for “rebuilding ruins and relations.” In Venezuela, the same humanitarian actors taking part in the mission played an active role in justifying and later perpetuating Israel’s ongoing genocide in Gaza. As Rodríguez notes , ZAKA, an NGO founded in the 1990s by a former member of an ultraorthodox terrorist organization , was on the ground shortly after the quakes. The organization, which specializes in emergency response and rescue operations in Israel and abroad, was a prime source for many of the myths that the US media used to justify genocide in response to the October 7 attacks, including the narrative of beheaded babies . Yosef Garmon, its Latin America director, once published a video of himself speaking Spanish from the ruins of Gaza, appealing to Christian Zionists for their support; in Caracas, he met with university students, whom he filmed awkwardly joining him in a chant of “Am Yisrael Chai” (“The people of Israel live”). ZAKA praised the announcement that Israel and Venezuela were formally restoring consular relations. It also took its share of the credit , pointing to its mission as an essential part of the process. The Venezuela Playbook in Colombia Already in the country, having arrived in Bogotá hours before, Garmon was giving interviews to Israeli television from Colombia shortly after the quake struck on Monday morning. Days earlier, he had been in the city of Cali, where he had once served as rabbi , for the presidential inauguration of Abelardo de la Espriella. De la Espriella has made a rapprochement with Israel a priority. Prior to his inauguration, he had announced his intention to move Colombia’s embassy to Jerusalem and suspend the planned opening of an embassy in Ramallah, in addition to meeting with Israeli Foreign Minister Gideon Sa’ar. He also announced the creation of a bilateral economic commission and a formal visit to the country by a Colombian delegation in October. Under the cover of earthquake response, Colombia has taken its support for Israel to new levels, including in a statement recognizing Israeli sovereignty over the Golan Heights. More substantively, the government took advantage of the natural disaster to revoke two decrees that had banned the export of Colombian coal to Israel. The mandated five-day window to comment on the proposed legislation began the day after the earthquake, when attention was directed elsewhere. These actions have been warmly received by the Israeli government. On Tuesday, the day after the earthquake, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu posted a video thanking Colombia for its recognition of Israel’s claim to the Golan Heights, saying that just as the “people are with the Golan Heights,” today the “people are with Colombia.” The day after, following a request from de la Espriella, Netanyahu ordered the dispatch of a formal humanitarian mission. Israel was one of the few countries granted permission to send a rescue team alongside the United States, Ecuador, and El Salvador. While the government has denied that any ideological considerations are at play — claiming instead that these missions were the only ones that “comply with international protocols” — it is clear that they were only willing to accept desperately needed assistance from their right-wing allies. The government’s rejection of an elite search and rescue team from Mexico, which yesterday morning President Claudia Sheinbaum disclosed was refused entry into the country, made the politicization of aid clear. Israel’s formal aid mission will complement the work of ZAKA, which is already on the ground in affected cities like Cali and Pereira. As they did in Venezuela , Israelis are already sharing stories with the media about the positive reception they are receiving from Colombian citizens. One Israeli businessman who had also attended de la Espriella’s inauguration spoke of Colombians approaching him and asking for his blessings in the aftermath of the earthquakes. It was “the opposite of hatred,” he said. Right-wing Colombian politicians are also publicly praising Israeli organizations like ZAKA, highlighting their technological marvels and the essential contribution they will make to the recovery efforts. Israeli aid may very well be an essential component of saving lives in a situation that remains dangerous; people remain trapped under buildings, and the window for finding survivors among the rubble has all but closed. Likewise, for many Colombians, the belief in Israel’s technological prowess is likely apolitical, a response to an increasingly dire situation. Nevertheless, the government’s rejection of foreign rescue missions like the Mexican brigadistas — a force with far more experience in post-earthquake operations — will surely lead to unnecessary death. The Colombian government’s attention to Israeli matters has stood in stark contrast to the coordination of its own rescue efforts. A Regional Trend Under Gustavo Petro, Colombia was a global leader in fighting to hold Israel accountable for its genocidal crimes. Petro severed diplomatic ties and banned the export of coal to Israel. His outspoken advocacy for the Palestinian cause also placed him firmly in the crosshairs of the Trump administration, which at one point revoked his US visa for his criticisms. Though he went further than any leader in the region, Petro was not alone in criticizing Israel’s genocide. At the time, the leaders of Bolivia , Chile , and Honduras did the same, often joining forces in organizations like the Hague Group, dedicated to finding ways to hold Israel to account. Latin America was at the forefront of the Palestinian solidarity movement: in addition to its outspoken leaders, students led solidarity encampments , trade unions launched innovative campaigns to boycott Israeli goods, and indigenous groups saw their struggles mirrored in the plight of the Palestinians. Nevertheless, in the last year, a series of right-wing electoral victories have drastically changed the picture, as these new leaders quickly walked back any pro-Palestinian policies. In the past year, newly elected presidents like Rodrigo Paz of Bolivia, the far-right José Antonio Kast of Chile , and Nasry Asfura of Honduras (who happens to be of Palestinian descent) reopened or actively strengthened ties with Israel since taking office. A year earlier, with the Israeli government increasingly isolated on the global stage, it turned its attention to Latin America, and its efforts seem to be paying off. At the end of last year, Israeli Foreign Minister Gideon Sa’ar declared that 2026 would be the “year of Latin America” for Israel. He has since worked to make that dream a reality, meeting with allied foreign ministers and visiting the region in person. In the cases of Venezuela and Colombia, natural disaster relief has provided the Israeli state with a vehicle to connect to local populations directly. It has also enabled these governments to legitimize their rapprochements with a state that is widely denounced by the majority of the population. Foreign aid missions have long been understood as an extension of diplomacy. Yet aid driven primarily by political rather than humanitarian objectives — exemplified by the Colombian administration’s rejection of rescue missions from nonaligned countries and Israel’s framing of humanitarian relief as a vehicle to achieve geopolitical goals and secure access to resources — only reflects right-wing leaders’ aversion to diplomatic cooperation in favor of transactionalism.

Help Us Break the Two-Party Stranglehold, James Carville

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Ben Burgis ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-14T14:11:56+00:00

With his bald head, glasses, and Cajun accent, political consultant James Carville was long one of the most iconic representatives of the Democratic Party establishment. In the 1990s, he was often seen as a kind of consigliere to President Bill Clinton. In the 2000s, he was everywhere on cable television offering up his characteristic mix of colorful zingers and boilerplate liberal politics. Now he says he’s considering leaving the Democrats altogether. In multiple interviews, he’s raged against the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) and left-wing influencer Hasan Piker . In an appearance on Fox Business earlier this month, he expressed amazement at “this idea that we’re going to seize the means of production” and urged viewers to “just go look at the DSA platform.” ( I agree .) At one point, he said: I am not going to be in the same party with Hasan Piker, I can tell you that right now. If he becomes a force in the Democratic Party, I’m outta here. I have no intention of ever being in the same political party with that guy. As the segment continued, he said that the “two-party system” might be under “tremendous stress” in the coming months, and that he wasn’t sure what would happen. The one thing he could say “for sure,” though, is that he and Hasan Piker won’t be in the same party. Sadly, this is almost certainly hot air. But if he does turn out to be serious, and he and other centrists start a third party, that would be an excellent thing for American democracy. Ways to “Leave” the Democratic Party When Carville talks about leaving the Democratic Party, his meaning is not entirely clear. For one thing, the Democrats and Republicans aren’t membership organizations like European or Latin American political parties. If you’re a congressman or a senator, you can be a member of the Democratic caucus within one of those institutions, but technically there’s nothing for a citizen like Carville to either join or leave. Instead, “the Democratic Party” is a nebulous concept that vaguely refers to many things — the Democratic caucuses in various elected bodies, the Democratic “brand,” the population of Americans who vote in Democratic primaries or consider themselves Democrats, and so on. Most of all, though, it refers to the Democratic ballot line . America’s half-democratic election system is designed to make it extremely difficult for any candidate who isn’t on either the Democratic or the Republican ballot line to meaningfully compete in a general election. The deck is stacked against third-party candidates on every level, from signature gathering requirements to participation in debates. Additionally, we have what’s called a “first-past-the-post” voting system, where voters can’t rank candidates. Instead of a runoff when no candidate clears 50 percent, whoever wins a plurality is simply declared the winner. This creates strong incentives for voters to vote not for their ideological first choice but simply for the least-bad candidate with a chance of winning. This in turn creates collective action problems that make it extremely difficult to build up a new party to a level where it can win. That’s why the overwhelming majority of the socialist left in the United States has concluded that trying to form a ballot-line third party is a nonstarter under current conditions. The deck is stacked in many ways against insurgents in major-party primaries, but realistically it’s stacked much more against third-party candidates . If we want to actually elect socialists to office rather than simply run symbolic protest candidacies, the only way to do that is to challenge the Democratic establishment for the Democratic ballot line in races where there’s a strong socialist contender. What a Fuller Democracy Might Look Like In the short term, this has been an effective strategy. As of now, it looks like several new socialists will be arriving in Congress after this fall’s general election, a fact that has caused a cascade of rolling meltdowns among centrist commentators and politicians. In the long run, as Carville says, the two-party system will be under increasing stress. Sooner or later, divorce may be inevitable. If Carville “leaves the Democratic Party” in the sense that he starts rhetorically calling himself an independent but nothing else changes, and he still regularly weighs in on Democratic contests, that means nothing. If he leaves to become a Republican, an annoying presence will be removed from those contests (and it’s unlikely that the GOP will benefit much from his presence). But the least likely possibility indicated by his comment about the two-party system is the most interesting. What if Carville convinces some nontrivial subset of his centrist friends to leave and start a third party with its own ballot line? If nothing else changes, the dynamics described above will destroy the new centrist party just as they’ve crushed every attempt to form a third party on the Left. In order to get a different result, at a bare minimum, Carville and his friends would have to push for reforms like ranked-choice voting that would remove many of these hurdles. The good news for them is that, as they fight for election reforms, they can tap into networks of deep-pocketed corporate and individual donors to which the Left has no access. Who knows? Perhaps they can even pull it off. If so, they would be doing the Left a tremendous favor. In 2016 and 2020, polls often showed that Bernie Sanders would cruise to victory over Donald Trump if he reached the general election, but he never did. If Carville and whomever he brought with him truly were able to reform our system to the point where a centrist third party was viable, then the Left would be spared the choice between fighting an uphill battle over a ballot line ultimately controlled by our ideological enemies and fighting a much steeper uphill battle against the basic dynamics of American elections. Instead, there could be one ballot line controlled by socialists, another controlled by centrists, and voters could face a real choice without the distorting effect of “spoiler” worries. It won’t be easy, but if Carville can figure it out, the Left will owe him a debt of gratitude.

AI data centers in India’s Visakhapatnam city face popular resistance

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Abdul Rahman ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-14T21:07:31+00:00

In Visakhapatnam or Vizag, a city on the south-western coast of India, a movement against large-scale AI data centers is growing, as more people in the community are mobilizing against the projects due to possible impacts on their lives and the local environment. People in Vizag have already organized several protests and public meetings to raise awareness about the projects, comparing their possible economic benefits to the costs involved, such as the drying up of their water resources, pollution, and rising temperatures. Last month on July 19, scores of local people and activists took to the streets in the city to oppose the data centers and demand they be withdrawn. Protestors claim they will harm their access to essential resources, such as water, electricity, and clean air, once fully operational. Security forces arrested leaders of the left parties and other activists who went to express solidarity with the people facing displacement and scarcity of water due to the upcoming nearby data center in Tarluvada in the city on August 6. Police also disrupted an awareness campaign launched by the activists for the visitors of the Vizag beach last Sunday, August 9. The activists organized under Stop Vizag Data Centers, the Human Rights Forum, and other such groups have organized several conventions and public meetings both in-person and through social media nonetheless. As part of their efforts, the organizers have launched a petition demanding an immediate halt to work on the already sanctioned projects and greater popular involvement and transparency in the matter. Despite the lack of broader public awareness around AI data centers in the country, the refusal of mainstream media to talk about them, and the government’s active attempts to silence all forms of resistance, around 16,000 people have signed the petition already, within a month of its launch. Environment impact and living conditions The Andhra Pradesh government has already approved several AI data centers totaling a capacity of 6.5 GW in the state mostly around the Vizag city. A large number of these data centers are being built around ecologically sensitive water bodies and hills surrounding the city. The Google-Adani data center is one such project with an overall capacity of 1 GW split into three different sites, with the largest being a 512 MW data center at Adavivaram-Mudasarlova water reservoir. Activists claim the Mudasarlova Reservoir, which is 120 meters away from the data center, is already drying because its catchment area was vandalized, trees were felled, and land was cleared even before the government gave them the necessary environmental clearance. The clearance was provided within days last month, however, leading to more trees being felled. The government also declared different approval processes with other companies to construct their own data centers are underway. Reliance Industries and Sify Infinite Spaces are seeking to build data centers ranging between 500 MW to 1.5 GW capacity. The data center boom in Andhra Pradesh comes as the government of India is promoting policies to incentivize data center construction, seeking to capitalize on the widespread rejection they are facing in Europe and North America as an opportunity to make India a hub for such investments. The government of India has given a 20-year tax exemption to all such investments and completely removed the need for environment clearances for most of the projects. Some of the proposed data centers, such as the one by Amazon in Thane, Maharashtra, which is also facing popular resistance, have been classified as “essential services” by the state government meaning it will get priority over common people in the supply of water and electricity during shortages. In Vizag, most of these projects are located near the hills surrounding the city and would lead to both deforestation and hill cuttings fundamentally changing the local environment. “Mudasarlova is a critical drinking water source for the city and construction at this site threatens natural water flows into the reservoir,” the petition has claimed further saying “large-scale hill cutting, deforestation and intensive construction threaten the city’s water security, biodiversity and environmental stability.” There are also concerns about various forms of hazards created by these data centers including the noise pollution and rising temperatures due to constant use of large processors. Demands of prioritizing people over profit The government however, continues to downplay the possible impact with state’s chief minister Chandrababu Naidu recently calling the campaign against the data centers as “fake.” He also tried to mislead the people by claiming that three new data centers approved recently with over 2.5 GW capacity in the state would only need 3.5 TMC water without acknowledging the fact that this would be half of all the water consumed by over 4 million residents of the Vizag city. The petitioners also claim that, once built, 6.5 GW AI data centers would consume as much electricity as the entire state of Andhra Pradesh, with around 54 million people, consumes today. The petition claims that the “enormous demand for land, water and power [these projects make] risks overwhelming regional infrastructure and accelerating ecological degradation on an unprecedented scale.” The petitioners demand all environmentally destructive activities related to the projects such as hill cutting, land clearing and excavations must be halted immediately along with public disclosures of all relevant information related. They also push for a comprehensive cumulative environmental impact assessment of all proposed data center projects in and around Vizag and “protection of reservoir catchments, forested hills, wetlands, coastal ecosystems and other ecologically sensitive areas from resource-insensitive industrial development.”

On August 12, US Secretary of War Pete Hegseth visited Panama , where he attended the Coalition of the Americas Against Cartels (A3C). In addition to the United States, member countries include Argentina, Bolivia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, El Salvador, Guyana, Honduras, Panama, Paraguay, Chile, Guatemala, Peru, and the Dominican Republic ( and, more recently, Colombia, following the change in government ), all of which have declared their political and economic alignment with Washington. Thus, for a few days, Panama became the hub for planning and meetings among the new international cooperation initiative led by the Trump administration, which has stated that its primary goal is to combat organized crime and cartels. Following the start of Trump’s second term, the Panamanian government under José Raúl Mulino has aligned itself with Washington’s stance, even going so far as to distance itself from agreements with China regarding the Panama Canal, which Trump himself has threatened to “take back.” In this regard, Hegseth, on his second trip as Secretary, said : “I look forward to strengthening our partnership to keep the Panama Canal secure and disrupt cartel operations throughout the region.” However, not all Panamanians welcomed Hegseth’s second visit with open arms. We do not want bases, enclaves, or facilities that could use our territory as a springboard for aggression against other peoples and nations. Nor do we accept that decisions made by others turn Panama, its people, or the Canal into military targets in conflicts that are not our own. Our security does not lie in militarization or in subordinating ourselves to the geopolitical interests of a superpower,” FRENADESO stated. In the statement, FRENADESO also said: “[We must] defend our sovereignty, neutrality, independence, and self-determination, and maintain relations of respect and cooperation with all the peoples of the world. Panama, a land of peace and sovereignty! Neither a platform for aggression nor a military target! Respect for the self-determination of peoples!” New attacks announced During his visit to Panama, Hegseth reported that his government plans to launch “ground” attacks against “drug traffickers and terrorists” with the approval of the member governments of Shield of the Americas. This type of operation has already been seen in Ecuador, when US forces claimed to have carried out an attack on Ecuadorian soil alongside the Ecuadorian armed forces. Furthermore, it is important to recall that the United States has already carried out several attacks in the Caribbean Sea and the Pacific Ocean, where it has sunk over 60 vessels allegedly carrying drugs, resulting in the deaths of over 200 people. Hegseth warned that attacks could occur “anywhere” drug trafficking groups operate. He stated that US forces hope to deploy, alongside their allies, “an operational capability like the one you saw in the attacks on drug boats [in the Caribbean and the Pacific]… We are working with Ecuador, Colombia, and other partners to take the fight to DTOs (designated terrorist organizations) on land.” In addition, Hegseth stated that the attacks will be carried out precisely to avoid a judicial process and take direct action: “We’re not going to put them through a judicial process where we know they’ll be released. The press has reported that, for now, Hegseth has reached agreements with Colombia and Ecuador. Regarding the former, Colombia’s Minister of Defense requested the deployment of US troops on Colombian territory. “Through the newly inaugurated president… Colombia has already requested that the War Department join Colombia in its fight against narco-terrorism, authorizing joint military operations to destroy terrorists and terrorist networks,” said the Pentagon chief . According to the Ecuadorian Ministry of Defense , the agreements with Ecuador – though still confidential – aim to establish a framework for the participation of US forces in the South American country. In this way, the United States plans to expand its presence in Latin America not only through diplomatic and economic channels, but also through military means. The effects that this new series of US military operations will have on the region remain to be seen, and it will be a wait marked by the smell of gunpowder and a great deal of controversy.
