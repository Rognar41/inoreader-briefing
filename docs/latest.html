What The Flock: Prison Populists and Palantyranny

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Pine and Roses -- Maine DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-25T20:03:27+00:00

There is a peculiar American humiliation in having to explain why you would like to buy groceries without helping populate a police database. You needed eggs. Apparently, you also owed somebody a record of the trip. Flock Safety’s roadside cameras collect license plates, vehicle characteristics, locations, and timestamps. Pass one and your car can become a searchable observation, available to an investigator with access. The company describes these as snapshots of public vehicle information . Such a modest word, snapshot. A birthday. A beach. Your car passing an intersection while you are trying to get through a Tuesday. A collection of those observations has a different character. It lets an institution ask questions about where you have been. The public road supplies the material. You supply the life. I resent the assumption that either is theirs for the taking. I resent being expected to prove that a trip deserves privacy, as though the default condition of citizenship were a standing invitation to inspect me. And I am tired of the little ceremony in which an industry takes something intimate, calls it data, and waits for the objection to become polite enough to ignore. A road is not a confession. On May 9, 2025, a Texas sheriff’s office searched a month of available records across 83,345 Flock cameras. The reason entered in the log was “had an abortion, search for female.” A woman’s partner had reported her. Investigators collected evidence and asked prosecutors whether they could charge her for taking the medication. They were told they could not. The sheriff and Flock later presented the search as an effort to protect her welfare. But when the woman came to the sheriff’s office a week later, officers questioned her about the abortion and examined her messages. She had come to report a violent assault by the partner who had reported her. He was subsequently charged. The records obtained by the Electronic Frontier Foundation document that sequence. Her need for protection had to get past the state’s interest in her pregnancy. Nobody has to invent a dystopia around this. The investigator typed the reason into a box. The archive was there to be searched. That is what should stick in the throat: how little had to happen before so much became available. The usual reassurances arrive with the weary regularity of an automated email. Your plate is already visible. The records are only kept for a while. The system has rules. Yes, a plate is visible. So is a person sitting in a café. Being seen by another human being does not make your afternoon the rightful property of an investigative system. We used to understand the difference between passing someone on the street and following them home. The distinction does not evaporate because the follower has learned to invoice the city. Flock defaults to thirty days of retention , adjustable by its customers. That is a rolling month of observations, refreshed as people keep driving. Deletion at one end does not stop collection at the other. The archive gets younger. The appetite does not. And rules governing access begin after the first trespass: the decision to make ordinary movement available for inspection. A lock on the filing cabinet cannot supply permission to fill it. An audit can help expose a search that should never have happened; it cannot make the person unsearched. We have been through enough tracking scandals, data-broker revelations, and solemn announcements of revised privacy policies to recognize this transaction. First the intrusion becomes possible. Then it becomes profitable. Eventually, we are invited to a discussion about transparency. By the time the public gets a seat, somebody else is collecting rent on the furniture. Flock has given that familiar entitlement a place beside the road. It is a physical reminder, on the route to work or the way out of town, that somebody has decided your passing should leave a record. You can object at the meeting. Your car can be recorded on the way home. The intimacy of a life often resides in its errands. The pharmacy visited twice. The address you have stopped going to. The place across town where you are trying, quietly, to become someone else. A location does not explain any of these things. It can nevertheless furnish an official with a question you never owed him an answer to. Now consider the protest. In September 2026, 404 Media reported on a Flock webinar that used Denver’s No Kings demonstrations to illustrate event-monitoring tools, including live video. People gathering to contest power became an example of how to equip power to watch them. Even the objection gets a product demo. A demonstration is public because people want to be heard. That does not mean they have volunteered for an institution’s future questions about their associations. A crowd contains the newly persuaded, the unsure, the person attending for the first time, the person whose employer would disapprove. Democracy needs room for people to arrive before they have worked out what arrival might cost. Surveillance sends the bill ahead of time. The prison populist has a solution to this discomfort: imagine that the machinery will always be aimed at somebody worse than you. Give the state more power over them, and it will give you peace. The bargain offers a little borrowed sovereignty, the pleasure of picturing yourself beside the officer rather than inside the search results. Donald Trump put that bargain in the title of his January 2025 order, “Protecting the American People Against Invasion.” The order directed expanded state and local participation in immigration enforcement. The language does its work before any search begins. A population is cast as a threat; restraint becomes something to apologize for. All that remains is to equip the people promising to remove it. The target changes. The equipment stays. This is the prison’s political inheritance: divide people into those entitled to feel safe and those whose submission is supposed to supply the feeling. Then keep expanding the means of submission. A frightened public gets a spectacle of control. The people selling control get a recurring customer. The person who needs housing, treatment, or somewhere to escape a violent partner gets told the budget is complicated. There is an actual corporate bridge between the cage and the cloud. GEO Group operates correctional and detention facilities; its subsidiary BI Incorporated provides electronic monitoring, supervision, and case management under ICE’s Intensive Supervision Appearance Program. GEO reported a renewed award for that work in September 2025 . Confinement and monitoring are two lines of business under one roof. An ankle monitor can be preferable to a cell. That comparison cannot tell us why a person should be monitored at all. When surveillance is imposed on someone who could otherwise remain free, the prison has expanded its reach while congratulating itself on opening the door. Flock offers a more diffuse version of the presumption that people should be made continuously available to authority. No ankle bracelet required. The roadside does the collecting, and a reason to look backward can arrive later. Everyone captured in the archive helps stock the shelf, whether or not an investigator ever takes an interest. There is serious money in stocking it. Flock’s March 2025 funding announcement named Founders Fund among the participants in a $275 million round led by Andreessen Horowitz. Peter Thiel, associated with Founders Fund and a co-founder of Palantir, wrote in 2009 : “I no longer believe that freedom and democracy are compatible.” Whose freedom? The freedom to build the apparatus, certainly. The freedom to refuse it appears to be causing considerably more trouble. Small-government enthusiasm becomes exquisitely selective when the government has a purchase order. The state must keep its filthy hands off the entrepreneur and its eyes on everybody else. Palantir makes software for turning institutional knowledge into action. ICE’s April 2025 award of $30 million for ImmigrationOS specified work on identifying and prioritizing people for apprehension, tracking departures, and managing removals. In January 2026, 404 Media published a user guide for ELITE , another Palantir tool used by ICE, describing maps, dossiers, and confidence scores estimating the reliability of people’s addresses. Confidence scores on where you sleep. An address can be correct and the purpose of finding it abhorrent. AI’s most useful contribution to an authoritarian project may be immaculate obedience: find the person, sort the records, save the official some time. The machine need never develop a taste for cruelty. There is a customer for that. Palantyranny is the rule of the unaccountable over the searchable. It joins the corporation’s power to classify with the state’s power to compel, making a person’s life increasingly visible to authorities whose decisions remain difficult to examine or resist. Your movements become their evidence. Their assumptions become your problem. The machinery may know where you sleep without knowing why you are afraid—and still help decide who comes to your door. Its defining achievement is this unequal exchange: power acquires a detailed account of your life while owing you scarcely an account of itself. Flock and Palantir occupy different parts of this business. What joins the camera to the dossier politically is the claim on your availability: your movements held ready, your circumstances made useful, your life kept within reach. More capable software makes that reach cheaper to exercise. It does nothing to make it deserved. You do not owe Flock a useful Tuesday. You do not owe a sheriff an interesting past. “I have nothing to hide” accepts the wrong obligation. It offers to earn privacy through good behavior. But freedom is not a prize awarded after an inspection, and innocence is no promise that the inspector will leave you alone. The ability to go about your life must survive an official’s curiosity, a contractor’s sales target, and the next election. Nor does the injury wait for an arrest. Sociologist Sarah Brayne’s 2014 research found an association between criminal-justice contact and reduced attachment to record-keeping institutions such as medical care, banks, formal employment, and education. The same pattern did not appear for civic and religious participation. Her account of “system avoidance” identifies a frightening possibility: people can retreat from the institutions they need because those institutions make them visible. Her study concerned criminal-justice contact. The question it leaves beside a roadside camera is what people will give up to avoid the attention they fear. The appointment postponed. The meeting missed. The journey reconsidered until staying home feels like a decision freely made. That last trick is especially foul. Power sets the conditions; you do the shrinking. There may never be an order to disobey or a charge to contest. Just a smaller radius in which to be yourself. So no, the answer is not to design a more considerate dragnet. The answer is to stop collecting ordinary lives against the possibility that somebody powerful will want them later. End the contracts for indiscriminate movement surveillance. Require deletion of the accumulated records and verification that it happened. Close the routes through which another agency or a commercial broker can supply what a community has refused to collect. A particular investigation needs a particular justification. The rest of us are not its raw material. Locally, that means finding the renewal date, demanding the contract, and making the council defend the decision in public. It means asking for removal without allowing the conversation to collapse into a tutorial on settings. A surveillance company is a business. It can lose a customer. We do not have to preserve its revenue stream as a constitutional courtesy. And when the presentation returns to how much the system could know, bring the argument back to what it has no right to know. The errands, the friendships, the private repair work of a human life. The destination that matters desperately to you. The destination that does not matter at all. You went somewhere. None of their business. *** This essay was first published on the author’s Substack, The Armory , on September 10, 2026. It is reprinted here with the author’s permission. Saudi Arabia’s Yemen Strategy Has Fallen to Pieces

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Helen Lackner ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-25T17:52:54+00:00

Over the past few weeks, Yemen’s Ansar Allah movement, also known as the Huthis, has launched an offensive and made big territorial gains. The latest turn in the Yemeni civil war could have major implications for the world economy, as it involves one of its most important trade routes through the Red Sea. It certainly poses a big challenge for Yemen’s neighbor Saudi Arabia. Helen Lackner is a leading expert on the politics of Yemen and the author of several books about the country. She spoke to Jacobin about the background to the latest developments and what is likely to happen next. This is an edited transcript from Jacobin’s Long Reads podcast. You can listen to the interview here . Daniel Finn What was the impact of the military campaign against Ansar Allah by the United States and Israel, along with the other steps taken by the Trump administration to impose new sanctions on Yemen? Helen Lackner The US bombing of Yemen was concentrated in the period between March and early May 2025. There was a fifty-two-day bombing campaign targeting the Huthi-ruled areas. Its main impact was to degrade, at least to some extent, the Huthi capacity to launch missiles. But it did not by any means destroy that capacity, as has been demonstrated since then. One important element to talk about in relation to the Trump policy, which has had a much more lasting effect than the bombings, is the designation of Ansar Allah as a “Foreign Terrorist Organization” in March 2025. That kind of move doesn’t have instant results, but it has gradually worsened the living conditions in Huthi-controlled territory and even come close to paralyzing economic activity. Most banks that were previously based in Sanaa, the capital, have now moved their headquarters to the interim capital of Aden. International transactions, which were already very difficult, have become much harder to conduct. That’s relevant first of all in relation to the import of basic goods. Ninety percent of basic staples in Yemen are imported: wheat, rice, tea, etc. The second major impact has been on remittances, which have been the biggest source of income for many Yemenis for a long time. Making the transfer of remittances more difficult is something that affects ordinary Yemenis. The Yemenis living under Huthi control already accounted for two-thirds of the population before the latest offensive. We’re talking about twenty to twenty-five million people. Coming to the Israelis, they launched a number of air strikes in 2025. The most notable one was in August that year, when they managed to kill the prime minister and nine other ministers, plus a number of other senior officials at a meeting. This demonstrated that they now had access to intelligence, something that they previously lacked because Yemen hadn’t been a priority for them. But it also demonstrated that the formal government under the Huthis is not the decision-making entity. The bombing happened last August, more than thirteen months ago now, but the Huthis haven’t yet appointed a new prime minister or any replacements for the ministers and the other officials who were killed. Daniel Finn During the period when Ansar Allah was at war with Israel and the United States, how did that affect the protracted efforts that had been going on to negotiate a formal end to the internal conflict in Yemen, which obviously involves outside actors (notably Saudi Arabia) as well? Helen Lackner There are two elements that need to be looked at. The first is the UN track. We’ve had a UN special envoy since 2011, with the current envoy having been in the role for more than five years now. But the last meeting between the Huthis and the internationally recognized government (IRG) was in 2018, and the UN process has come to a complete standstill. The second element is the direct negotiations between the Saudis and the Huthis that were taking place, to some extent with Omani mediation. If they had been finalized, there would have been an agreement whereby the Huthis would no longer attack Saudi Arabia in any shape or form, while on the other hand, the Huthis would receive compensation for a lot of the war damage between 2015 and 2022 as well as salaries for their staff. The siege on the ports and airports would be lifted, and the Huthis would get a good share of the oil revenues. The deal would not have been a signed and sealed agreement between the Saudis and the Huthis. It would have formally been a deal between the IRG and the Huthis, endorsed by the Saudis. This process was widely believed to have more or less been settled and agreed when the Gaza genocide started. But the Huthis then became involved in supporting the Palestinians and the deal was put on hold. Daniel Finn At the end of last year, there was a short-lived attempt to form a breakaway statelet in southern Yemen. What was the background to that attempt, and why did it unravel so quickly? Was it primarily driven by internal factors or by the Saudi–Emirati rivalry? Helen Lackner It was a bit of both. The anti-Huthi front is officially the IRG, which itself has been badly divided since the war started in 2015. One of the major divisions arises from the southern separatists, who want to return to the situation before 1990, when there were two separate states, the Yemen Arab Republic (in the northwest) and the People’s Democratic Republic of Yemen (in the south and east). The southern separatists themselves come in a multiplicity of forms. But the strongest force among them is a group called the Southern Transitional Council (STC), which the Emiratis had been supporting very heavily since its foundation in 2017. It has been the most active element in undermining the IRG. The Emiratis certainly colluded with, and possibly encouraged, the STC in its bid to take over Hadhramaut and al-Mahra, the far eastern governorates of Yemen. Those governorates had been primarily under the control of the IRG — at least its anti-STC elements — except for the coastal area in Hadhramaut. The STC then mounted a blitzkrieg that in a way resembled what the Huthis have done more recently, and its forces took over the whole of those two governorates within a matter of days in December 2025 The IRG and the Saudis called for them to “withdraw to your previous positions.” Instead they continued their offensive, moving closer and closer to the Saudi border. At that point, the Saudis intervened, bombing a couple of Emirati ships that were bringing in military supplies for the STC in Mukalla, the regional capital, and the port of Hadhramaut. At the same time, the IRG broke its official support agreement with the UAE and demanded that it immediately withdraw its forces, which it did. This was followed by an offensive by the non-STC elements, supported by the Saudis, who pushed back the STC and effectively closed it down throughout the country. The STC itself split into factions. The bigger one joined the IRG and went to Riyadh to prepare for a South–South dialogue that the IRG had announced. They officially dissolved the STC — some say they were forced the make the decision. But another branch of the STC, supported by the Emiratis, continues to exist today. Its leader is believed by everyone to be in the UAE, although he hasn’t made any public appearances. The Emiratis have continued to support this faction in an underhand way. There are also still elements of the STC who remain active and who are objectively now allies with the Huthis, as they have said they won’t fight them. There are widespread reports that they have been selling weapons to the Huthis, and the Huthis have also said that they will not attack them. Basically, you have a situation where this particular group of southern separatists has now split into two. One group is now with the IRG and the other one is more or less underground but claims to be involved in civilian demonstrations and the like. When it comes to Saudi Arabia and the UAE, there has been a tendency to assume that they are great allies, but if you look at history, this is simply not the case. Most of the time they have been rivals. There was a brief moment when they were very close to each other after King Salman came to power in Saudi Arabia with his son, Mohammed bin Salman (MBS), as minister of defense. MBS wanted to become crown prince, and his father also wanted this. He has since achieved this ambition, with a lot of help from the Emiratis. The United States favored his cousin Muhammad bin Nayef, who had long been cooperating with their intelligence agencies, for the role of crown prince. But he was supplanted by MBS, and the UAE’s ambassador to Washington, Yousef Al Otaiba, played a very important role in that outcome. But even back in 2017, there were divisions opening up between Saudi Arabia and the UAE over Yemen. They had started the war in Yemen together in 2015, but by 2017, there were already significant differences between them. Those differences are now apparent in several other countries, including Sudan, Somalia, and Libya, without even mentioning the economic competition between the two states, which is also very important. The situation with the STC and what happened in December last year reflected a combination of internal and external factors, and it would be difficult to determine which aspect was more important than the other. But the Saudi–Emirati divisions will remain an important problem, and Yemen will remain one of the main sites where you can observe the extent of those divisions. Daniel Finn Western politicians and diplomats generally refer to the IRG as if it were the legitimate authority in the country and the Huthis were simply a rebel movement. But what power does that government actually exercise inside Yemen? Helen Lackner This is a very important question that has become all the more important in the light of recent events. The IRG officially controlled about two-thirds of Yemen’s territory before the Huthi advances. But if you look at the map of the country, you will see that much of the area it controlled was desert. The share of the Yemeni population under the IRG’s control was estimated to be about one-third — a maximum of ten million people. Until the war started in 2015, the IRG was simply known as the Yemeni government. Then it was forced out of Sanaa and officially moved to Aden; since then, there have been all kinds of internal conflicts and divisions inside the IRG. It isn’t a cohesive entity. There are different military units, many of which were previously supported by the Emiratis and are under different commanders. For example, the main force on the west coast that has recently been defeated was under the command of Tareq Saleh, the nephew of former President Ali Abdullah Saleh. He has worked with the IRG, but he has never considered himself to be a member of it, although he is a member of the Presidential Leadership Council. The presidency of the IRG is currently an eight-man body with one president and seven vice presidents. The majority of the seven vice presidents are men who control large military units. I hesitate to use the word “warlord,” but it is close to being accurate for some of them. At the civilian level, you have a set of ministers and administrations in the different governorates, but their fundamental problem is finance and the quality of governance. Since November 2022, when the Huthis bombed the ports exporting Yemeni oil on the southern coast, the IRG’s income has more or less disappeared, and it has become almost totally dependent on Saudi financing, international aid, and a very small amount of money raised from taxes. The majority of civil servants have not been paid regularly in any part of Yemen for many years. Although the majority of ministers are actually in Aden, the president has only been there occasionally. He is in Saudi Arabia most of the time. The IRG’s degree of control is minimal and varies from one area to the next. It is more effective in governorates where the local administration has some oil and gas and has been able to keep some of the income and use it to finance services. Even after the collapse of the STC, the IRG remained badly divided. There have been efforts to reorganize the military forces and create some kind of centralized command structure. But what has happened over the last few weeks demonstrated that they were nowhere close to achieving this. The IRG forces disbanded very rapidly and suffered heavy losses. Daniel Finn What impact has the ongoing US war on Iran had on Yemen? To what extent do the Huthis coordinate their actions with Iran and its other allies in the region? Helen Lackner Initially, it had much less impact than anybody expected. Outside observers were expecting the Huthis to take action in support of Iran right from the start, but they did nothing. Although they issued many statements supporting Iran, there was no action. At the end of March, they launched a number of missiles at Israel, but apart from that, they just stated that they could close the Bab al-Mandab Strait if they chose to do so, which would obviously have had a major impact on the world oil market. In a way, this demonstrates that the US bombing campaign of 2025 was probably more effective than many thought at the time, because the Huthis clearly saw it as a priority to discourage Donald Trump from renewing the air strikes. Today, when the Saudis are desperately asking for US support, the Huthis have been talking to US officials, asking them not to start again. Having spent years calling the United States the first and greatest enemy, they have also said that all ships can pass through the Bab el-Mandab except Saudi ones. Another element that’s always worth remembering is the fact that the Huthis are not Iranian proxies. They have their own plans and objectives. Right now, their objective is to strike a blow against Saudi Arabia. Stopping Saudi oil being exported via the Bab al-Mandab and via the East–West pipeline is part of that. It’s convenient for them that this move also supports Iranian efforts to put pressure on the United States by reducing supplies for the world oil market. The Saudis had been exporting about four to five million barrels a day, but that has now been interrupted. Iran and the Huthis are allies; they work together when it suits both sides; but neither is taking orders from the other. There has also been military coordination, even though less military equipment for the Huthis has been arriving, because efforts to stop smuggling on land and by sea have become more effective. The Huthis manufacture most of their equipment themselves, but they do need to import some very sophisticated technical elements. They’re now buying a lot of that material directly on the world market instead of receiving it from Iran. There is plenty of evidence that Iranian and Lebanese military advisers have been helping the Huthis, but they could easily survive without them. They might be slightly less sophisticated, but they could survive without the equipment and the advice. The Huthis have been involved in military activities for more than twenty years now. If they had limited capacities at the beginning, they’ve clearly developed those capacities much further over the years, including some advanced forms of technology. Daniel Finn What was the state of play when the Huthis launched their most recent offensive? Was the offensive something that was widely anticipated in advance? Helen Lackner It was anticipated by some, I would say, but not widely. If you look at what happened in the last three or four months, it seemed to me that the Huthis were likely to launch an offensive within the current period, for several reasons. For one thing, the impact of the terrorist designation had begun having a major effect on daily life in Huthi-controlled areas. The economic situation has greatly worsened and is continuously deteriorating. The World Food Programme, which is the main source of food aid, reduced its contribution dramatically over the last two years, then stopped it altogether last September. This was partly because the Huthis had arrested UN and NGO staff members who were involved in aid distribution but also for political reasons. Of course, the fact that United States Agency for International Development funding completely stopped for Yemen after the early months of 2025 has also had a big impact. You have increased popular dissatisfaction throughout the region, and not just dissatisfaction — people are starving. A second element of concern for the Huthis was what appeared to be a distinct strengthening of the IRG after February this year. As I mentioned, the IRG leaders were reorganizing the military and shifting people between areas. They claimed a few months ago that they had restructured up to 80 percent of their forces and that they were battle-ready. Events clearly proved that this assessment was premature. The third element, of course, is that the Saudis are the main supporters of the IRG after the UAE’s political exit. For the Huthis, the question was, “are the Saudis going to come and make the deal with us, or are they going to go with the IRG and fight us?” The way things were going, with the Iran war and other aspects of the situation, they were concerned that the Saudis would no longer seek to negotiate with them. To attack Saudi Arabia makes a lot of sense from their perspective because there is a baseline of popular support, whereas acting directly in support of Iran is something that has no resonance among Yemenis. Yemenis generally have no sympathy for Iran. The pretext for taking action was the Saudi bombing of the runway at Sanaa airport, when a Huthi delegation was returning on an Iranian flight from Ali Khamenei’s funeral. But this was more of an inconvenience than anything else, since they did not bomb the runway of Hodeidah airport and the delegation was able to land. It was a pretext rather than the main reason. Daniel Finn How significant are the gains that the Huthis have made over the past few weeks? Does it represent a decisive shift in the balance of forces, and what implications might it have for the Saudis? Helen Lackner The big question is whether the gains are reversible or not. If they prove to be irreversible, then they’re very significant. There has been a lot of talk about Huthi control of the Bab al-Mandab. In fact, they already controlled the Red Sea. The big difference now is that if they sit at the bottom of the peninsula, or on Perim Island, they can shoot at anything that comes by with fairly unsophisticated weaponry. Obviously, the fact that they are preventing Saudi ships from going through, exporting oil and bringing back imports, is important. For imports, the Suez Canal is a reasonable substitute. For exports, the Iraqi drone attack on the pipeline last week is more significant, because it is preventing oil from reaching Yanbu, the Saudi port on the Red Sea coast. The Saudis can still export up to two million barrels a day. But by going through the Bab al-Mandab, they had been exporting between four and five million barrels a day over the last few months. Going through the Suez Canal is much less convenient because the large oil carriers, known as VLCCs, cannot go through the canal fully loaded. The oil has to be taken to the bottom of the canal and either transferred to smaller ships or through a small pipeline and then reloaded when it reaches the Mediterranean Sea. Eighty percent of Saudi exports go to East Asia, so after arriving in the Mediterranean, they have to go all the way past Gibraltar, around Africa, and back up to Asia. This route is much less convenient and much more expensive. The balance of forces between the Huthis and the anti-Huthi elements will depend on what happens next and whether the latter can reorganize themselves and receive the air support and other forms of support they need from the Saudis or other players. But air strikes do not win wars without land forces, so unless the Yemeni land forces are reorganized so they can be more effective, there will not be any reversal. I wouldn’t like to say that the change in the balance of forces will prove decisive in the long term. My perception when this fighting started in August was that we were back more or less where we were in 2015, at the beginning of a long and nasty war. At the moment, unfortunately, I see no reason to change my mind on that, although I hope that I’m wrong. Daniel Finn What are the social and humanitarian conditions facing the people of Yemen after the latest turn of the war, and how are they likely to develop in the coming months? Helen Lackner It’s very important to remember that the humanitarian situation is disastrous, regardless of what happens for the country’s leaders. Yemen was the world’s worst humanitarian crisis for a number of years. It lost that title, not because anything improved in Yemen but because things got so much worse in Sudan and other places. Starvation is becoming a major issue throughout the country. As I mentioned earlier, the situation is even worse in the Huthi-controlled area, for two-thirds or more of the population, than it is in the IRG area. But in both areas, it is very bad. The UN’s humanitarian aid appeal for this year, which is smaller than last year’s, has only been financed at 23 percent by the end of September. This is very low, even compared to last year, which was already very bad. People were suffering enormously even before the latest turn of events. Now, with the developments in the southwest of the country and the military activity, about 150,000 people have been displaced at the latest count, on top of the four to five million people displaced over the last ten years. Given the shortage of aid, it’s even more difficult for them to find places to stay, whether temporarily or permanently. So many people have been interviewed saying that they’ve just run with the clothes on their backs and bullets flying overhead, and the number of displaced people is likely to increase. Those who are still in their villages but have been caught up in the fighting are going to try and escape if they possibly can, so long as you have these active military fronts.

Greed and Economic Desperation Are Fueling Sudan’s Civil War

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Ahmed Ismat Ahmed ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-25T16:20:10+00:00

I used to pass the biscuit factory in Soba without going in. People I knew went in for a twelve-hour shift on a line that smelled of sugar and oil. In 2021, the number that stuck was 1,500 pounds for the shift. In Haj Yousif, where Iqbal lived, the rent for a single room was 30,000 pounds a month. You had to work twenty factory days just to cover rent. Meanwhile, the Rapid Support Forces were offering people 15,000 a day, a uniform, and a phone. Those three numbers explain more about Sudan’s war than any map I have been shown since. What happened in Sudan was not primarily a story of people choosing violence. It was a story of people choosing the wage that was available. A Bus That Does Not Come Iqbal sold tea in front of the medical campus at the University of Khartoum, setting up with three low stools, a few plastic chairs that did not match, a kettle that was black at the bottom, and a sugar jar with a spoon that was always wet. She kept her coins in a small tin. She lived in Haj Yousif. Traveling to the university from her home involved hours on buses, not to mention the time spent walking and standing in the heat for a bus that does not come. She did it every day. Her husband had run away — her choice of words — to fight with Abdelaziz al-Hilu in South Kordofan. I do not know the full story of what took him. What I know is that the farm outside the city had stopped paying for a life inside it, and that a rifle and a cause offer the kind of wage a collapsing farm cannot. Iqbal stayed with their four children, who would be fed or not fed depending on how the day went. Around her, on the same stretch of pavement, Asyaa ran two kettles at once, while Fatouma sold tea and roasted nuts and knew which patients in Shar al-Hawadith had not eaten. The women had come in numbers when the farms stopped paying. Men left for the mines or the militias. Khartoum needed cheap food, cheap warmth, and cheap care for students who could not afford a meal or companions waiting a week in a hospital corridor. The city got what it needed, but the women who provided it were deemed illegal. The local authority sent a pickup with two or three men in uniforms that never looked fully clean. They seized the chairs, the kettle, the sugar, sometimes the gas cylinder, too. You had to go to their office and pay to get your own things back. We called the practice kasha . The state had decided that Iqbal’s labor was not a business. It was an obstruction — and what it could not ignore, it taxed. When we saw the truck turn the corner, we hid her stools behind the labs. A different truck offered her neighbor 15,000 pounds a day and a story he could tell his family. Two days of that wage was a month of rent on her street. You did not need a lecture on identity to explain why a boy answered the recruitment call. In a city where the state had made survival illegal and a collapsing currency had made the factory irrelevant, a soldier’s wage was the only thing that replaced the state. In 2018, a man sat at her stall like any customer. He noticed her phone, which was basic and worn at the edges. Over the course of several visits, the talk moved toward money, and he offered her a new phone and weekly payments of real money. He wanted her to sit among us and report what the students said: who was organizing, who came and went. She refused the offer. She did not tell us at the time and carried that refusal alone until the regime fell. When she finally mentioned it, I asked why she had never said. She said she did not want us to worry or do something stupid. Mostly — and this is the sentence that has not left me — she did not want fear to change what we were doing at the gate. There was a small room in the pharmacy faculty where leftover donated drugs went to patients from the hospital next door. The tea women brought their children. A registrar looked at a sick child on a stool, and a pharmacist walked out with the medicine. There were no forms, and nobody paid 15,000 a day for any of it. Guns and Gold I sat with a miner from Jebel Amer whose hands were burned from mercury during the sit-in in May 2019, in the place we called Kolombia, the open ground behind the railway tracks. He said the RSF took 30 percent of everything. The mine had closed after fighting with Musa Hilal’s men, and he had come to Khartoum with nothing. He joined the sit-in. He said if the RSF came, he would fight. On June 3, he disappeared, and I never found him afterward. After clashes with Hilal, the RSF took Jebel Amer. At the peak, they were extracting something like forty kilograms a day. The metal was processed with mercury, then flown from Khartoum airport to Dubai — sometimes a hundred kilograms a flight, once a week — with no customs paperwork on the Sudanese side. From Dubai, it entered the global gold market, occasionally passing through European refineries that did not ask where it came from. Investigators later put the annual take at more than a billion US dollars. From 2015, the same force was sending men to Yemen for Saudi and Emirati pay. The UAE had been cultivating Sudanese military figures since the early 2010s; when the Yemen war needed ground forces composed of men who could move fast and not ask questions, they called the RSF leader Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo, better known as Hemedti. Estimates of the deployment range from 14,000 to 40,000 fighters between 2015 and 2020. The pay was invdollars, which meant you could recruit faster than the army, buy equipment, and build a buffer against any future rupture with Khartoum. Fighters rotated home having been trained in urban combat and logistics. I remember the new trucks in Khartoum that year. I did not know the money was Gulf money, although I knew the men who had burned villages in Darfur were moving through my city as if they owned it. The RSF was not, by this point, a paramilitary force sustained by weapons. It was a mining and logistics operation that maintained an army. The weapons were evidence of its power, which derived from gold. With that gold, Hemedti built an empire that ran parallel to the state instead of through it. Al Junaid, the family conglomerate, became one of the largest mining and construction operations in the country. In October 2019, people in Talodi, South Kordofan, set fire to one of its cyanide plants after the runoff poisoned their water and killed their cattle. Guards opened fire, but the crowd burned it anyway. Investigators at Global Witness later traced RSF payments moving through UAE-based front companies into accounts at the National Bank of Abu Dhabi and documented the purchase of more than 900 Toyota Hilux and Land Cruiser vehicles. There were real estate holdings across the capital, farms in Darfur, and a trucking fleet. The gold bought more than guns — it bought a parallel economy, booked in Dubai. Russia’s Wagner Group arrived around 2017 for the same metal, exchanging military training and equipment for mining concessions in RSF territory. When the US Treasury sanctioned those operations in 2020, the networks reorganized through UAE-based intermediaries. The buyer did not change. Swissaid, reading the UAE’s own trade figures for 2024, found that Dubai imported twenty-nine metric tons of gold from Sudan that year, a big increase from seventeen metric tons the year before. According to the same figures, eighteen additional metric tons came from Chad and nine from Libya, two countries that are not famous for their gold mines but serve as transit routes for metal moved out of RSF territory. Supply and Demand In September this year, Michael Waltz and Massad Boulos argued in Foreign Policy that peace in Sudan can only come after a weapons embargo, extending the old Darfur ban of 2005 across the whole country. Drones, they write, do not stop at Darfur’s border, and they are right about the geography. On September 11, the United Nations Security Council extended existing measures for one month, until October 9. Washington has been pushing for a nationwide ban, while Moscow opposes any expansion. Waltz and Boulos frame the problem as one of supply: more than a dozen countries are feeding both sides, and an extension of the ban will block them from doing so. Some of that is right. But an arms embargo addresses a supply problem — the flow of weapons — while the political economy of militia recruitment is a problem of demand. The UAE is not just one supplier among others. It is the buyer of last resort for Sudanese gold and the logistics node for the air bridge into Darfur. Dubai’s refineries process the metal, and the same financial infrastructure enables the flights.The profit is not incidental to the war. A force paid from the mine, through Dubai, does better when the country is in pieces. None of this is a defense of the Sudanese army. It has a history of coups, of alliances with Islamists, of violence in the peripheries. But a ruined army still has to pretend it employs people on behalf of a state. It still needs ports, a capital, a population that eats if the flag is going to mean anything. There are steps that could be taken in response. One important move would be to complement the weapons ban with Kimberley-style certification for Sudanese gold at the point when it enters refineries. Another would be to apply sanctions on UAE-based entities that form part of the air bridge and on the front companies and bank accounts that convert the metal into Toyotas. Filling the Silence Iqbal had to leave Haj Yousif when the fighting reached her neighborhood. She went to her sister’s place in Omdurman, not to work but to wait. I called her from Halfa, near the Egyptian border, after I left. The line is always bad, always dropping in the middle of a sentence so you do not know if she heard you. At first, I talked too much, filling the silence the way you do when you are afraid the other person has gone. Then I learned to wait. I kept asking whether she had found a stall. She had not. She said the city was not hers. Her voice came through tired but still the same. I can still do the arithmetic from Soba to Haj Yousif in my head: 1,500 for twelve hours; 30,000 for a room; 15,000 for a day with a gun . The truck paid better. She asked if I was eating anyway.

America’s already threadbare welfare state is on the brink of total collapse. Over the next ten years, national funding for Medicaid, whose benefits 81 million Americans rely upon, will be cut by about $1 trillion . States that expanded Medicaid access under the Affordable Care Act will be hit especially hard. More immediately, in just a few days, on October 1, and again on January 1, various new requirements for Medicaid eligibility will be instituted. This means that receiving and maintaining insurance will suddenly become much more difficult for millions of Americans. The result of all of these changes, enacted by H. R. 1, or the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, will be to throw states across the country into health and budget crises as their Medicaid funding falls off a cliff, with some of the worst effects of the bill conveniently placed after the midterm elections. There is one state, however, that could avoid that fate, should Proposition 40, or the California Billionaire Tax, pass this November. The Big Beautiful Bill blasted open a $30 billion hole in annual Medicaid coverage for Californians, a program the state calls Medi-Cal. Prop 40 proposes to backfill much of that loss with a onetime 5 percent tax on billionaire wealth raising an estimated $100 billion, most of it earmarked for health care. It’s a percentage so small that, given the current speed at which their wealth is accumulating, most billionaires in California would earn back the total amount of money lost in a matter of months . While much of the discourse around Prop 40 has understandably been devoted to what it means to tax the billionaires, it is saving Medi-Cal — and not some vague notion of “punishing the rich” — that is the central purpose of the initiative. However, rather than support this pragmatic solution to a looming crisis, Service Employees International Union (SEIU) California has officially remained neutral on the initiative, which was put forth by one of their own member unions, SEIU United Healthcare Workers West (SEIU-UHW). Now investigations commissioned by SEIU California and the international union allege that SEIU-UHW’s president, Dave Regan, the man widely acknowledged as the architect of the tax, has attempted to “extort” support for Prop 40 from them, according to the inflammatory headline in the Los Angeles Times , a newspaper owned by billionaire Patrick Soon-Shiong. Coming less than two months before the election in November, the allegations against Regan bear all the hallmarks of a politically timed campaign — an attempt to chip away at Prop 40’s popularity. All so that the ultrarich get to pay even less in taxes than they already do. “Tax the Rich” Campaigns Aren’t Just About Expanding Our Threadbare Welfare State but Also Holding On to What We Have Having worked on New York City Democratic Socialists of America’s Tax the Rich campaign now for several legislative cycles, I know how long and difficult the fight for progressive taxation is. Eight hundred workers at a Walmart distribution center in Mississauga, Ontario, succeeded where others have not: they are now working under a two-year contract with North America’s largest logistics giant. The landmark collective bargaining agreement went into effect on July 1, nearly two years after workers won union representation , joining Unifor Local 252, motivated by low wages and unsafe working conditions. As punishment two years ago, Walmart bumped the pay of every logistics worker in Canada — except those who unionized. This was an effective maneuver to halt further union drives. “Walmart gave all the wage increases to all the nonunion folks, and everything fell apart,” said Angela Drew Kimelman, a Unifor organizer who worked on organizing campaigns at multiple warehouses, including one in Cornwall, near Montreal. But now, the new contract prevents Walmart from raising wages at other warehouses without doing the same at this one. The unionized workers have received a matching raise and back pay for the months when other warehouse workers received raises that they didn’t. Unifor has restarted its organizing efforts at other Ontario warehouses. “Quite the Eye-Opener” In past union drives, Walmart has closed facilities rather than deal with a union. So far, no such threat has emerged for this warehouse. Instead, two months into the contract, members are moving forward with contract education and implementation for both workers and the boss. For the first time, the workers have the protections of a contractual disciplinary procedure. “If you’re at another distribution center and they walk you out, you’re pretty much on your own, at the mercy of the employer,” said Rodolfo Pilozo, a thirty-year Walmart worker and member of Unifor’s organizing and negotiations committees. Kathryn Cespite, an eight-year worker at the warehouse, acted as an interim steward with a coworker in the contract’s first week. “That was quite the eye-opener,” she said. A manager tried to discipline a worker without letting him tell his side of the story. Stalling Tactics It took eighteen months of negotiations to reach a deal. Walmart used stalling tactics, like offering very few dates for negotiations. “I believe they were intentionally trying to drag out the process and frustrate the membership into decertifying us,” said Shayne Fields, Unifor’s lead negotiator. There was a decertification petition, he said, and workers reported that Walmart was covertly supporting it, but it never got much traction. The company also brought in temp workers, implying a threat to replace union workers. “Walmart was feeding people a lot of misinformation,” Cespite said. “I let the union representatives know what was being said in meetings, and then the union was able to counter it. “They were taking advantage of people’s lack of knowledge about what unions are,” she said. “One of the challenges we’re running into is that a lot of the people don’t have any experience with unions and don’t know what it’s all about.” Now that the contract is in place, “we have two years to educate ourselves and others,” said Pilozo. “It’s not just the president of Local 252, or of the national union. It’s us, collectively; we can make changes.” Why Walmart Gave In Ontario’s labor laws helped. The law includes a conciliation process for first contracts. First there’s mediation, and then disputes can move to settlement through arbitration. Unifor’s lead negotiator, Fields, filed the paperwork to move that process forward, which started an external clock that pressured Walmart to settle. Geography also helped. Labor scholar Benjamin Fong argues it would be “a huge pain for Walmart” to relocate this regional distribution center, which serves most of Ontario and Quebec, because the area is hemmed in by the Great Lakes and the layout of Toronto. Walmart already closed its distribution centers in Quebec, which has even stronger labor laws. Walmart worker Simon Hayford thinks the company settled because executives were worried about more warehouses joining the union, and they didn’t want to draw more attention to the unionized one. He and Pilozo have been participating in Unifor’s efforts to organize other Walmart warehouses. “I’ve talked with some of the other warehouse guys, and they say ‘We’re making the same money so why would we do it?’” Hayford said. But he anticipates the other benefits the contract provides, like an improved safety committee and protection against unfair terminations, will show the value of the union over time.

AI Destruction Isn’t Hypothetical. It’s Already Here.

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Branko Marcetic ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-24T18:38:48+00:00

We’re several weeks into a fierce debate over whether artificial intelligence (AI) will kill us all. Triggered by a spate of former workers at major AI firms coming forward to warn that a future superintelligent AI could lead to human extinction, the debate has inspired a kaleidoscopic array of views that don’t break down along neat ideological lines. Some think it’s all hogwash, either the product of ignorant hype or part of a cynical hype campaign by the industry itself. For others, convinced that the tech’s potential capabilities are no joke, they’re warnings that need to be taken deadly seriously.In many ways, though, the debate is besides the point. It’s not that we shouldn’t try to get to the bottom of what exactly happened with the HuggingFace hacking incident, and that we shouldn’t worry about the question of rogue agents or the dangers of a future superintelligent AI that could threaten our continued existence. It’s that what we call AI has already copiously proven it carries profound danger for the human race — and is doing immense harm way before it’s even remotely close to reaching superintelligence.Consider the fact that AI “almost started a war” between the United States and China this past spring, in the words of one source who told CNN about it. A special operations command analyst had used a chatbot to put together a report about the cargo of a Chinese ship sailing in the Middle East, which hallucinated that the vessel was carrying the components of a nuclear weapons program, prompting the US military to mobilize to intercept and board the ship, guns drawn. Military planes had even taken off for this purpose. The operation was called off after officials looked into the report and realized it was AI-generated nonsense.According to CNN, the incident was the result of two overlapping trends: the fact that younger “AI native” analysts tend to rely on and trust the technology to an inordinate degree and the Pentagon’s aggressive adoption of AI under Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. It’s worth noting that if any direct war were to break out between the United States and China, it would carry grave danger for the entire world, not just because it would likely lead to a global depression, but because any military hostilities between the two have a high chance of going nuclear — something that really could threaten the survival of our species.The incident perfectly distills the very real, currently existing dangers of AI. We’re so fixated on the future catastrophic potential of hypothetical superintelligent AI agents that we’re forgetting AI is already causing real harm and carrying massive risks — not because it’s smarter than us, but because it is flawed, error-ridden technology that we are rushing to shoehorn into seemingly every facet of our lives.That includes war. Long before the incident with the Chinese ship, AI was directly responsible for the horrific, mass loss of innocent human life thanks to being folded into military operations. We saw it most recently with the Trump administration’s murder of scores of Iranian schoolgirls, where the military’s trust in Palantir’s AI tool Maven was a major part of the chain of disastrous decision-making that led to the atrocity.US Central Command personnel reportedly compressed what used to be hours of work involved in choosing targets into a few minutes by feeding the underlying intelligence into Maven, simply assuming the AI tool would catch and highlight any errors. It did not. Before that, we saw AI used in a more apocalyptic way for the purpose of war in Gaza, where AI targeting was at the heart of Israel’s indisciminate flattening of the territory.But this is only the most overtly homicidal use of AI. Many of its social harms have been more insidious.The unleashing of AI onto the public has caused a by now widely documented phenomenon of what Massachusettes Institute of Technology researchers have termed “cognitive surrender” among college students who, struggling with deadlines and the pressure to succeed, have turned en masse to using the technology to write their essays and otherwise do assignments for them. The proportion of students doing this has now reached a depressing 95 percent.The outsourcing of their very thinking to machines has meant that young graduates are not just emerging from their studies without seemingly actually learning their field but with genuinely weaker brain power: a worse memory, an inability to persist with intellectual challenges, and vanishing critical thinking skills. Wall Street firms, normally big boosters of the AI industry, have, when push comes to shove, found themselves less than impressed with the young workers it is producing, underwhelmed at a generation of new hires who prove remarkably shallow thinkers. It’s so bad that the students themselves think the technology is weakening their critical thinking even as they continue to rely on it.Meanwhile, as people have turned to AI for therapy and other help with their personal demons, AI chatbots have been linked to a series of suicides and even murders by troubled individuals, a number of them teenagers. The problem is that the technology — besides not being human or having professional mental health expertise — is designed to flatter, please, and otherwise keep the user engaged as long as possible. As a result, chatbots’ role in these tragedies has ranged from being useless to help users, to feeding their delusions and actively helping them hurt themselves or others.That’s when AI isn’t helping otherwise mentally stable individuals hurt themselves with lethally bad advice: the man hospitalized after ChatGPT likely advised him to replace salt in his diet with an old-fashioned sedative; the stranded hikers who nearly died when Google Gemini advised them to pack far less food and water than they needed; the pastor who nearly died of a pulmonary embolism after ChatGPT advised him not to seek medical help; the elderly neuroscientist who put off cancer treatment because he had come to trust AI hallucinations over his own doctor; and so many more.This is because AI chatbots and other tools often do not know what they’re talking about. One study (from an AI provider, no less) found seventeen large language models (LLMs) only answered two out of five financial questions correctly. Another found they hallucinate legal responses between 58 and 88 percent of the time.AI chatbots sycophantically back a variety of potentially harmful statements from users nearly half (47 percent) of the time, according to another. This is particularly alarming when it comes to medical advice, which a study found AI was accurate about only 76 percent of the time — a passing grade for a college paper but abysmal in the medical context, where being wrong once could mean death or debilitation.But it’s not just chatbots. AI’s rapid integration into a variety of expert fields has led to adverse consequences. Court sanctions are on the rise because lawyers keep filing briefs with AI-made errors. On the other side of the bench, two federal judges allegedly relied on AI to issue error-ridden court orders, a serious concern given the expansive power courts have in the US political system, and because judges are broadly insulated from civil liability. The use of AI for medical devices used in surgeries has caused a spate of injuries, including strokes after surgeons were misdirected by the technology in ways that damaged arteries.A Stanford and Harvard study from earlier this year found the best AI models made “severely harmful clinical recommendations” in medical cases 22 percent of the time, often through omission. In other cases, AI is being used by doctor’s and dentist’s offices to justify hawking expensive treatments that patients don’t actually need.And then there is the way that AI algorithms are being used to exacerbate and justify preexisting corporate malfeasance. An ongoing class-action lawsuit against UnitedHealth alleges that its various companies used an error-prone AI tool to serially deny insurance to patients. A different lawsuit alleges that an AI recruiting software carried out systematic employment discrimination against black applicants and others.In all of these cases, people are being hurt or threatened by AI not because it’s achieved sentience and is smarter than us, but the opposite: because it’s a flawed, at times comically inept technology that humans are actively making choices to deploy in situations where it is most likely to do serious damage, sometimes in ways actively designed to screw over others.In fact, it is arguably the very market-driven hype about AI’s imminent superintelligence that has contributed to all this. Overstating AI’s capabilities has helped encourage its rapid uptake in fields like war or health care depite the fact that it’s not ready for prime time and makes those unfamiliar with or ignorant about the technology more likely to take what it says at face value, even when it’s wrong or hallucinating.The dangers of a hypothetical AI superintelligence should be a concern. But we do not have to determine if this is really our future to be able to call for development of this technology to be urgently paused, for it to be heavily regulated, and to put serious guardrails on it, if not outright banish it from being used for particular purposes. The dumber, mistake-prone AI of today is enough of a risk to humanity as it is.

Milei at the UN: Argentina as the ‘promised land’ for capital, AI, and Israel

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Pablo Meriguet ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-25T05:39:19+00:00

Far-right Argentine President Javier Milei addressed the 81st session of the United Nations General Assembly on September 23, giving a speech which provoked strong reactions. The South American head of state also participated in a series of high-level meetings. One of his most quoted lines from his New York City trip has been: “Argentina is the new promised land.” This is not the first time Milei has used religious concepts and ideas from the Judeo-Christian tradition to explain his political stance, but it is the first time the Argentine president has equated his country, in economic and political terms, with the land of Abraham and his descendants. This is even more telling when one considers Milei’s extremely close relationship with Israel and ardent defense of its policies of war and genocide across West Asia, as well as the admiration he professes for the Jewish religion. Milei also emphatically underscored this during his address to the UN General Assembly: “We also made a geopolitical choice: we have unambiguously aligned ourselves with the West … We condemn the Iranian regime’s attacks on the State of Israel.” And this is an indisputable fact. Since the beginning of his term, Milei has not only aligned himself with the geopolitical interests that the Trump administration has vigorously pursued, but has also become one of Israel’s staunchest allies in the region. This is quite impressive, considering that President Noboa in Ecuador, De la Espriella in Colombia, and others have also expressed a kind of unwavering alliance with Tel Aviv. That is why Milei has not limited himself to diplomatic rhetoric, but has pulled the strings of power to designate Iran’s main military force (currently in conflict with Israel and the United States) as a terrorist organization. “We declare the Iranian Revolutionary Guard a terrorist organization, just as we continue to seek justice for the two attacks we suffered on Argentine soil in 1992 and 1994,” Milei said at the UN, referring to two attacks in Argentina against the Israeli Embassy and the Argentine Israelite Mutual Association (AMIA). The Argentine justice system, advised by Israel and the United States, identified Hezbollah and Iranian citizens as the perpetrators. Free-market capitalism and Artificial Intelligence Another topic Milei addressed during his appearance was his staunch defense of free-market capitalism in which the state plays a minimal role: “We do not want to lose again; that is why we embrace free-market capitalism.” In this regard, Milei once again highlighted his government’s strong support for artificial intelligence companies and the tycoons behind them. It should not be forgotten that the Argentine government signed a working and information-sharing agreement with Palantir, whose co-founder, Peter Thiel, purchased a property on Argentine soil. This is clearly part of Milei’s neoliberal agenda to attract investment from major foreign capitalists, among whom the owners of technology companies (who are so interested in resources such as lithium, copper, and others that abound in South America) currently stand out. However, recent statements by former employees of these companies regarding the danger that the development of AI poses to humanity have created some unease around the world. But Milei says that this is precisely the time to move forward: “Prudence does not mean fear, and what’s more, fear should under no circumstances automatically lead to obedience … where many see a threat, we see a frontier.” Criticism of the UN Probably one of the most important aspects highlighted in Milei’s speech was his harsh criticism of the UN and its role in current geopolitics: “Today, unfortunately, it is time to acknowledge that this sacred pact has, in fact, been broken.” But not content with merely pointing out the shortcomings, Milei asserted (very much in line with Trump) that the UN has become a venue for certain people to amass wealth: “It has become a useless organization that serves only to feed a caste of fatally arrogant parasites disguised as well-meaning bureaucrats.” Hence, he asserted that resolutions ultimately have no real effect on policy. He cited the example of the Malvinas Islands, a territory disputed with the United Kingdom: “What good is a resolution if, for decades, the other party can act contrary to it, and that resolution remains intact in the archives but is powerless in the face of reality?” Criticism of Milei’s speech Milei’s statements, while applauded by his supporters, have drawn widespread criticism in Argentina. Much of the criticism has focused on the fact that Milei now seems to staunchly defend Argentina’s right to control the Malvinas Islands, whereas in the past he was cautious and even reluctant to adopt a patriotic stance against the United Kingdom – something both the Argentine left and right have done. “We’re surprised that Milei now remembers when he used to talk about self-determination,” said Axel Kicillof , governor of the province of Buenos Aires. Likewise, critics pointed out the apparent contradiction in Milei’s rhetoric between extolling the right to self-determination and aligning himself with the interests of Washington and Tel Aviv: “Milei’s stance doesn’t surprise me. The truth is that this line of subservience—and I say this very clearly—is directed at Trump and Netanyahu. Even the terminology is consistent with what Trump proposed and his ideas on the deregulation of artificial intelligence, as well as his view that Argentina’s natural resources are available to anyone who wants to take them,” said Kicillof. Regarding Milei’s economic rhetoric, Patria Grande leader Juan Grabois said : “Besides being an ignorant imbecile, besides being a lobotomized parrot who parrots Trump, besides plagiarizing Gramsci without knowing he’s “a filthy communist,” what Milei is saying is that we’re basically going to hand over our territory, critical minerals, energy, and national sovereignty to the technocracy.”
