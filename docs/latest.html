Workers Pay the Price

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Mark Kreidler ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-21T21:57:04+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. His professional life chose him. At first, Julio Godinez said, the move was obvious. His parents, living in his hometown of Hanford, California, were getting to the point where they both needed regular care, particularly his mother, who was battling cancer. Godinez, who’d been working at a nearby car dealership, was the person best suited to provide it. That was almost 20 years ago. Godinez’s mother died in 2011. By the time his father died eight years later, Godinez had come to realize, after years of doing the work, that caring for others in their own homes, which allowed those people to age in place and avoid institutional settings like nursing homes, was his calling. “If it’s a woman I’m taking care of, I see my mom. If it’s a man, I see my dad,” Godinez, 54, said recently. “Each one, they become like family to me. I love what I do.” Godinez speaks glowingly of the two men for whom he currently provides care. But there’s a reason that it’s two clients and not one: Godinez cannot cover even the modest costs of his life in Hanford without, essentially, a second job. Working more than 60 hours a week, Godinez averages about $3,750 in net income each month. Even a cursory run through his monthly bills either taps out that total entirely or leaves Godinez actively in the red. With virtually no savings, he said he could not afford an emergency expense like a repair bill, and he is currently foregoing health insurance because of the cost. Asked if he is close to living on the edge, Godinez replied simply, “I’m on it. I’m already there.” He is not alone. Godinez, employed through California’s In Home Supportive Services program (IHSS), is one of more than 4.6 million such professional home care providers across the country. It is an employment figure that the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) expects to rise dramatically over the next several years, as the nation’s population continues to skew older. California’s home health care program is by far the largest in the U.S., followed by New York, Texas, Pennsylvania and Massachusetts. But although the need for these caregivers is constantly growing, the wages in most states do not reflect that value. According to the BLS, the median wage — half earn more, half less — is less than $17 per hour. Godinez occupies an unusual space within California’s labyrinth of health care. He is technically employed by his own patients; his paychecks come from the state; but his wage is set locally, by Kings County officials. Today, that rate is $17.50 per hour, barely above the state’s minimum wage of $16.90 . Even by the standards of a job classification that does not pay well, it’s on the lower end among California’s 58 counties. That reality not only pushed Godinez to take on a second client, but pushed him toward activism, which he said he hadn’t previously considered. He’s now a member of the bargaining team for the union that represents him and other IHSS workers in negotiations with the county. “I still want to do this work,” Godinez said. “It needs to be valued.” Julio Godinez's Biggest Expenses California created its In Home Supportive Services model more than 50 years ago , right after the passage of federal legislation allowed states to establish such public assistance programs. All 50 states, plus the District of Columbia, offer some form of paid home health care. Its premise was and remains straightforward: Caring for elderly or disabled people in their own homes not only brings those people a greater level of comfort and security, but it’s a relative bargain when set against the state and county expense of placing them in institutional settings like nursing homes. A state report in 2021 put the savings at between $22,000 and $153,000 per person each year. With an estimated 875,000 IHSS recipients each month, roughly half of whom would qualify for long-term institutional care , that is a potential savings of tens of millions of dollars per year. As a Medi-Cal benefit, IHSS is funded by federal, state and county dollars. It is designed to assist people with limited incomes who are blind, disabled, medically ill or over 65. By most estimates, about three quarters of California’s IHSS caregivers are family members — the same way Julio Godinez started. But after years of struggling — and often failing — to make ends meet financially, Godinez earlier this year added a second client, a 61-year old stroke survivor. He visits that man most days for a few hours a day, about 25 hours per week, to cook, clean and drive him to appointments. Godinez is thus bumping up against an IHSS mandated limit of 66 hours per week for any caregiver who works with two or more clients. Even so, he often finds himself juggling bills, deciding which payment to delay. In a typical month, Godinez said his net pay lands somewhere between $3,500 and $4,000 once taxes and other withholdings are deducted. (His 40 hours as a live-in caregiver are not subject to state or federal income tax.) It is gone almost before he can count it. Although he stays overnight at his primary client’s home, Godinez has his own small house in Hanford, which he said he was able to buy from a longtime family friend years ago at a price so low that his monthly mortgage (including home insurance) is only $1,000. Still, it needs to be paid — and the house needs to be constantly running, especially with Godinez’s dog there most of the time. His utility, energy and internet bills add another $400 to $450, depending upon the time of year. Godinez’s monthly balance sheet doesn’t leave room for something like an appliance or plumbing repair. As an IHSS provider who needs to drive both of his clients to appointments or errands on a near-daily basis, Godinez said he felt the need to invest in a quality car, a 2025 Nissan Pathfinder SUV. As a purchase rather than a lease, that monthly cost is also $1,000, plus $350 for insurance and $250 to $300 for gas. (IHSS providers are generally not reimbursed for the miles they drive for clients.) His groceries come to $250 or so. Godinez said he rarely eats out, although his clients occasionally take him for a meal as a way of saying thanks. As a result of years of low-wage work, Godinez also carries small credit debt on three cards, which amount to $250 to $300 in monthly payments. And he spends about $50 a month to pay off a back debt to Southern California Edison after having skipped his energy payment several months ago. Those payments will last about another year. Those costs alone suck up almost everything Godinez earns between the two jobs — and until he added the 25 hours from the second client, he had been falling further behind every month. Even with both clients, Godinez said he visits the Kings Community Action Organization’s food bank about once a month. Related: Who Really Pays for California Politics? Follow the $5.8 Billion And there’s something missing: health insurance. Godinez’s relatively low earnings were nevertheless too much to allow him to qualify for Medi-Cal, and when Covered California’s policy offerings skyrocketed in cost after enhanced federal tax credits ended last December, he found himself searching for an affordable alternative — without success. “That’s another thing I’m still looking for,” Godinez said. “But [insurers] are asking for almost 400 bucks a month plus deductible, and right now, I just don’t have that.” What to do? Godinez is considering adding a third job. How that would happen within the structure of his existing 66-hour work week, he said, is hard to see right now. Godinez’s experience is not unlike those of many of the estimated 3,500 IHSS caregivers in Kings County. In a survey of its members last fall shared with Capital & Main, the Service Employees International Union Local 2015 found that more than 70% of its IHSS members said they worked a second job at least some of the time in order to make ends meet. Forty-five percent of the survey’s respondents said they sometimes or always have trouble paying their rent or mortgage. More than half said they relied on Medi-Cal themselves for their health care coverage. (Disclosure: SEIU is a financial supporter of Capital & Main.) Godinez said he joined SEIU more than a decade ago, while still caring for his father, but his activity has ratcheted up in recent years as the hourly wage in Kings County continued to fall further behind the cost of living. “I know what caregivers go through, and they need a voice,” Godinez said. “I just want to advocate for them so that we all get what we deserve.” Related: Billionaires vs. Labor: The Battle Over California’s Proposition 40 According to the union, its last negotiated wage increase with Kings County took effect in August of 2022, and that contract expired in 2025. The union and county officials have held occasional talks this summer, but with no progress. Kings County public information officer Matthew Boyett said the county has not made a wage offer, and had no details on when an offer might come. When asked what sort of wage would ease his financial crunch, Godinez paused for a moment, then replied, “If we had, man, $25 an hour, that would be great.” In fact, the MIT Living Wage Calculator estimates that a person in Godinez’s exact position — one adult, no children — would need to earn $24.16 an hour in 2026 dollars, working fulltime, to meet basic needs while living in Kings County. The road from $17.50 to $25 will clearly be a long one, Godinez said — years in the making. The caregiver said he’s on board for that ride. “Even with not getting paid what we should, I honestly still find it a blessing to work for these clients,” Godinez said. “It’s what I know. I’m going to keep on doing it and fighting for it.”

PROPS AND MEASURES = endorsed by CA DSA Proposition 1: YES Borrow $11.25 billion for housing Prop. 1 would allow the state to sell $11.25 billion in bonds to fund various affordable housing programs. Of that total amount, $1.25 billion would go toward veteran housing, while the rest would fund rental homes, homeownership programs, farmworker housing, affordable student housing, tribal housing and more. The bond is expected to help fund up to 40,000 multifamily rental units, while also helping another 40,000 households buy a home. Proposition 2: YES Raise limit on rainy day fund deposits This constitutional amendment would allow the state to deposit up to 20% of its general fund tax revenue in the state’s reserves, up from the current ceiling of 10%. The deposits would not count toward the voter-approved Gann Limit, which caps the amount state lawmakers can spend each year and requires excess funds to be returned to the people. It also authorizes spending some tax revenue to pay down California’s $20 billion federal unemployment insurance debt. Proposition 3: YES Make income taxes on higher earners permanent to fund education and healthcare The tax, first enacted in 2012, is set to expire in 2031. Under the measure, income taxes for top earners range from 10.3% (for individual income over $360,000) to 12.3% (for individual income over $721,000), with graduated rates in between. Eighty-nine percent of the money goes to K-12 public schools and 11% goes to community colleges, with any excess going to public healthcare. The tax typically raises $5 billion to $15 billion a year. Proposition 4: YES Allow public financing of political campaigns (From CA DSA) Prop 4 repeals California’s ban on state/local public campaign-finance systems and allows governments to establish programs for qualifying candidates who accept spending limits. It doesn’t itself create a statewide public-financing program. Electoral politics systematically privileges people who can raise money from wealthy donors, corporations, and professional political networks. In cities and states across the country, public financing of elections has allowed socialists and working people to compete on something at least marginally closer to a level playing field. Many socialist wins across the country, like Zohran Mamdani in New York City, would not have been possible without public financing. This is a clear positive step for working people, and can reduce candidates’ dependence on wealthy private donors and allow working class mass membership organizations like our own to participate in larger and more expensive elections. Prop 4 isn’t a silver bullet: it’s only enabling legislation, not the final reform. Winning Prop 4 creates space for socialists and reformers to fight locally/statewide over what strong public-finance systems actually look like. Public financing also does not eliminate capitalist political power, independent expenditures, lobbying, or wealthy ownership of media, or even come close to leveling the playing field. It is, however, an important and necessary step forward for working class political power. Proposition 5: YES Delay replacing recalled state officials Under current law, after choosing whether to recall an elected official, voters are asked who should replace them. This constitutional amendment, placed on the ballot by state lawmakers, would delay the election of a successor until a separate election. The recalled officer would be allowed to run in that election. If the governor is recalled, the lieutenant governor would take over their duties until a new governor is elected. Fiscal Impact: Net fiscal effect unknown. In the event of a recall, savings or costs of millions of dollars to administer the election depending on the office recalled. Proposition 37: No Recommendation Create low-cost mortgages for homebuyers California’s Housing Finance Agency would be able (but not required) to borrow up to $25 billion to help would-be homebuyers purchase newly constructed houses and condos. These loans could provide up to 17% of a home’s purchase price, leaving buyers with a cash down payment of 3% if paired with a typical mortgage — $24,000 for an $800,000 home. Homebuyers would repay the loan in monthly installments to private lenders. Anyone earning up to twice the area’s median income would be eligible. Private lenders, not taxpayers, would be on the hook if homebuyers default. Proposition 38: NO Borrow $8.4 billion for immunology research Proposition 38 would create an $8.4 billion bond largely to fund research into fighting cancer, heart disease and Alzheimer’s, plus other diseases that can be treated through immunotherapies. Half the money would go to a research institute affiliated with the University of California. The other half would be distributed to select public universities and nonprofits. Drugs created through this research would have to be discounted at 20% for Californians and 10% of any revenue from the drugs would go back to the state general fund. Philanthropist and medical inventor Gary Michelson put this measure on the ballot. Proposition 39: NO Require identification for voting This initiative would require voters to show government-issued identification at the polls and order election officials to verify that all registered voters are U.S. citizens. Voters who cast a mail-in ballot would need to give the last four digits of a government ID, such as a driver’s license number. California currently requires residents to provide an identification card and Social Security number to register to vote, but does not request it at polling places. It was placed on the ballot by conservative groups. Proposition 40: YES Apply one-time tax to billionaires to fund healthcare and education Proposition 40 would require the estimated 200 Californians whose net worth exceeded $1 billion at the start of this year to pay a one-time 5% tax. Service Employees International Union-United Healthcare Workers West, the group that put the measure on the ballot, projects that it would raise roughly $100 billion over five years. That money would be earmarked primarily for healthcare, with some reserved for food assistance and education. If propositions 41 or 42 earn more votes than Proposition 40, this measure would be nullified. Proposition 41: NO Require audits of new tax programs This proposition would make it harder to pass new special taxes by requiring the state auditor to review any special tax proposal before it goes to voters. The auditor would assess whether the program that would receive funding could cut spending by at least 10% annually. Any new special tax approved by voters would count toward California’s spending limit, potentially triggering automatic refunds that reduce funding for other state programs. If this proposition receives more votes than Proposition 40 (a wealth tax on billionaires), Proposition 40 is nullified, even if Proposition 40 also passes. It was placed on the ballot by opponents of the proposed billionaire tax. Proposition 42: NO Ban new taxes on personal property Proposition 42 would make it much harder to create a wealth tax in California by prohibiting new taxes on the mere ownership of assets such as investment accounts, business interests and personal property — assets that are typically only taxed when they are sold or generate income. Like Proposition 41, which also attempts to block Proposition 40 (a wealth tax on billionaires), Proposition 42 would nullify Proposition 40 if it receives more votes, even if the billionaire tax also passes. It was placed on the ballot by opponents of the proposed billionaire tax. Proposition 43: NO Raise vote threshold for special tax measures This would make it harder to impose or increase local taxes by raising the threshold for local citizen-driven special tax ballot initiatives to pass from a simple majority to two-thirds. The two-thirds threshold already applies to taxes proposed by local governments but not by individuals. The measure reflects a deal between state lawmakers and the anti-tax Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association to keep a more restrictive tax proposal off the ballot. Proposition 44: YES Require more clinic spending on patient care Proposition 44 would require community clinics that serve low-income patients to spend 90% of their annual revenue on patient care. The measure directs the attorney general to publish guidance on what spending qualifies and allows the state to fine clinics that don’t spend the full 90%. The labor union Service Employees International Union-United Healthcare Workers West put it on the ballot after pushing a similar bill last year that failed. Clinics failed to keep the measure off the ballot with a lawsuit earlier this year. Proposition 45: NO Speed up environmental reviews for housing and infrastructure Proposition 45 would limit reviews under the state’s landmark environmental law, the California Environmental Quality Act. Placed on the ballot by the California Chamber of Commerce, the measure aims to help developers advance projects they designate as “essential” more quickly and easily. It would impose a 365-day limit on environmental reviews for those projects, including infrastructure like roads and bridges, housing, schools, broadband and wildfire mitigation. It would also require courts to rule within 270 days on lawsuits related to the environmental review law. Mississippi's Black Majority

The future of the nation is in the South, the former Confederacy and ground zero of America’s civil rights struggles. We need only look to a place like Mississippi to understand the mess that is this settler colonial project called America, with its unaddressed and seemingly intractable internal contradictions. Mississippi has shown us the promise of Black power snuffed out by white supremacy, unjust laws and the Klan. Today, Mississippi is an example of how Black people are once again so close to cracking the code and freeing ourselves and the nation in the process. The first time was during Reconstruction following the Civil War, when Black folks were a majority of the population, and had over 200 elected officials, including statewide offices, two U.S. senators, a lieutenant governor and secretary of state. A new state constitution provided for basic rights, including voting rights, and reforms such as public education. Then, the federal troops left and took with them their protection of Black people, leaving the white power structure to their own devices and allowing them to take back the Magnolia state. Related: The War on Black Educators The 1890 Mississippi convention - which had only one Black delegate, Isaiah Montgomery - was designed to enact a new state constitution to legalize voter suppression and strip Black people of their rights. Specifically, the new constitution included a poll tax and literacy test to wipe out Black power in the majority Black state, and was the death knell for Black power in that state. Black folks in Mississippi have not recovered to this day. In a state of contradictions, the blackest state in the Union - about 40% - is among the reddest and most conservative states. Despite the gains of the civil rights movement and the legislation that it brought, there is now an effort in Mississippi to deprive Black voters of their voting rights through redistricting and other means. And the state has not had a statewide Black elected official since 1890. Welcome to America. America is a place where they force you to fight for your basic rights, time and time again, and Mississippi is a prime example of this. Known for its Jim Crow-era lynchings and brutal violence - from the lynching of Emmett Till and those three civil rights workers, James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner, to the assassination of Medgar Evers and Vernon Dahmer and others - Mississippi has been a hot mess for quite some time. W hen the credits began to roll at the screening I attended of You Can See Everything , Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim’s buzzy new documentary, it was like waking up from a dream. I walked away from those three hours — which felt like quite a bit less, despite the long sequences free of dialogue — with my opinion of Elizabeth Holmes unchanged: namely, that she is guilty of everything for which she is currently serving a prison sentence and a great deal more besides, the least of which is her consistently unnerving affect. That said, for those three hours, it was a great deal of fun imagining, along with Fielder and Oppenheim, that there might be something else, something more to her. Holmes and her partner, Billy Evans, present the project as an act of “radical transparency,” though anyone familiar with her case is likely to immediately read this as reputation laundering. It turns out, though, that she’s in safe hands with Fielder and Oppenheim. Their interrogation of the facts of her case is minimal. Anyone wishing for a “gotcha” moment in which Holmes is forced to confront the enormity of her deceptions will leave You Can See Everything disappointed. Not only does she double down on the capabilities of Theranos’s technology, but she goes even further, claiming that she has never lied — not just about Theranos but in her entire life . The best Fielder can get from her is that it is possible that she might in the past have told a white lie on the order of untruthfully claiming to be feeling unwell to get out of attending an acquaintance’s party. But she “can’t remember” a single instance. For a thorough accounting of her bigger lies — of which, naturally, she also has no recollection — one can consult investigative reporting, John Carreyrou’s Bad Blood , court transcripts, a podcast , or a streaming miniseries adapted from that podcast (and thus lightly fictionalized). What audiences will get from You Can See Everything will not add much of substance to that story. Rather, the movie will tell a more self-contained tale in which Fielder interrogates what it is that he sees in those weeks before Holmes’s incarceration. Initially guarded and skeptical, Fielder gradually seems to undergo something resembling Freudian transference. He subjects Elizabeth Holmes to his particular, somewhat paranoiac method of close reading, which — those familiar with his shows Nathan for You and The Rehearsal will not be surprised to learn — involves meticulous reenactments in which he invests the smallest of details (a gesture, a word) with outsize significance. He arguably begins to mirror Holmes herself in this respect: there is a curious resonance between the two, and I find it as suspicious in the former as I do the latter. That is, if Holmes seems to put on an utterly unconvincing babe-in-the-woods act in the face of all of the forces arrayed against her, Fielder, in going so far out of his way to give Holmes the benefit of the doubt, displays — or performs — an almost total credulity that, while it certainly serves a narrative purpose, lacks verisimilitude. Simply put, it is hard to believe that he believes her, however much it adds to the complexity and interest of the film to — for a time — suspend that disbelief. Exhibit A is the sequence in which Fielder shows Holmes the famous Abbott and Costello routine “Who’s on First?” Holmes appears not to understand the basic situation underlying the frenetic dialogue: that there is a baseball player named “Who.” While she feigns amusement, she is unable to explain why. Evans seizes upon this supposed lack of understanding in order to claim that Holmes just understands the world differently than the rest of us. This is highly tendentious, of course: what an ordinary (maybe even neurotypical?) person — say, the sort of normie who might be called to serve on a jury — understands as “lying” could look quite different to the masters of the universe. Art House Methods for the Megaplex In any case, You Can See Everything is a terrifically entertaining entry in the canon of documentary filmmaking for the age of the uncertainty principle, in which the film knows, and is at a basic level interested in, the fact that the act of observation is an act upon the object of observation. One is seeing people who are constantly, acutely aware that they are being seen, and they try their best to be seen a certain way. This calls the documentary genre into question, and radically so — which is no innovation on Fielder and Oppenheim’s part, but it is notable that they managed to pull it out of art house cinema and onto commercial screens, and that it is currently causing about as much ink to flow as any recent blockbuster has. Given the directors’ previous work — Oppenheim’s Some Kind of Heaven (2020), Ren Faire (2024), and Primetime (2026), and Fielder’s television projects Nathan for You (2013–18), The Rehearsal (2022–present), and The Curse (2023–24), his collaboration with Benny Safdie — it is unsurprising that the film resists an explicit political interpretation. But the portrayal of the Holmes–Evans couple is an almost caricatural embodiment of the milieu out of which they emerged, one whose influence over our debased present is difficult to overstate. Consider the evolving representation of Evans, an MIT graduate who in the beginning of the film seems a culturally liberal sort, conversant in therapy/wellness speak — it’s he who utters the words “radical transparency.” This stretch of You Can See Everything was filmed, appropriately, in coastal Southern California, where the couple spends the majority of their time together before Holmes departs to serve her sentence. Even there on the beach, however, Evans’s performances of empathy are often comically inept, a violent rage just barely concealed beneath an “I hear what you’re saying. . . . ” Another tense moment sees Billy somewhat forcefully wrest a bowl of cracked eggs from Elizabeth, who is presumably beating them too gently. Is Billy in fact a controlling partner? And wouldn’t that be a little convenient, particularly given that Holmes’s defense of her own conduct relies in part on being prey to another controlling man: Ramesh “Sunny” Balwani, her former lover and chief operating officer of Theranos? Just before Holmes’s prison sentence begins and midway through the film, the family relocates from California to Texas to be near the minimum-security facility where she’ll be incarcerated. Evans is now in Austin. As has been known to happen for other tech entrepreneurs making the move from California to Texas, his physical displacement is accompanied by an explicit embrace of hypermasculinity and extremely online right-wing politics. His Austin home decor is minimal, though there are a number of pet reptiles crawling about, and a framed motivational poster of Winston Churchill adorns the wall above his desk. The Billy of the latter half of You Can See Everything is suddenly a gun-toting Charlie Kirk admirer with “vengeance in [his] heart.” He would also like for you to know that he’s a very talented lover — a boast that’s less surprising after an earlier shot in which Billy has seemingly invited the camera for a close-up of his penis. In the late sequences, Billy’s aggression is on full display, as when he berates Amanda Seyfried, as much on the late Kirk’s behalf as on Holmes’s. Or when he attempts to shut down Fielder’s line of questioning with one of his and Holmes’s collaborators, who is in the process of acknowledging, if only by implication, that the original version of the Theranos blood-testing device did not in fact work. The Hubris of the Wannabe Tech Overlords That the film exists at all is itself a monument to the hubris of Holmes, whose adoption of the tech industry’s modus operandi of empty mythology as marketing allowed her to get as far as she did. It must have been as stupefying as it was exhilarating the first go-round. Why wouldn’t she think it could work once more? Alas, pathological liars who never completed their Stanford degrees seem to be about a dime a dozen . Regarding Elizabeth Holmes, insofar as you can see everything, it’s because there’s not a whole lot there. But Fielder and Oppenheim’s considerable achievement lies in their ability to elevate such material into a genuinely compelling work of art. Go see it with someone; you’ll be talking about it for days. Medieval Egypt Was No Stranger to Class War

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Nicholas Liu ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-21T17:56:00+00:00

On Roda Island in the Nile stands a stone column nineteen cubits (thirty-four feet) tall and nearly 1,200 years old. It is called the Nilometer . Before the construction of the Aswan Dam, an official would measure how high the river rose and from that reading determine the fate of the country’s annual harvest. By the eleventh century and continuing until the Ottoman Turkish conquest of 1517, the rulers of Egypt had made a tradition of exorbitant pageantry whenever the waterline reached sixteen cubits: the harvest would be reaped, the cosmic and communal order confirmed, the people fed and placated, and the land tax collected. But not all Nilometer news was good news. If, by around the end of August, the water still had not reached sixteen cubits, the festivities and the land tax would be canceled, and the people would know that hard times lay ahead. Conversely, a continued rise to eighteen or nineteen cubits would mean the Nile was flooding and could take thousands of homes and people with it. Some believed that such disasters were cosmic warnings against society’s sins, and that the ruling class — military cliques, government officials, and private landowners — was to blame for those sins. But God’s punishment did not fall only on the guilty. “Fear a trial which will not strike those who have wronged among you exclusively,” reads a verse in Al-Anfal, the eighth chapter of the Qur’an. Tyranny and corruption ( fasad ) were seen as a rot that spread through the entire communal order, as with the People of Lot before their destruction. Divine wrath was an elemental force, not a paper writ delivered to particular individuals. Popular protest was different, as the ruling classes were sometimes reminded. It could be just as sudden, furious, and overwhelming, but it was precise in its targets, and its moral and religious justification was plain to all. The wealthiest members of society often profited most during these periods of heightened suffering. Merchants and nobles would hoard grain even in good times to anticipate a future shortage, but most forms of state discipline targeted only bakers and millers of the lower middle class, or what medievalists often call the “ conjunctural poor ” — those who fluctuated between subsistence and poverty as economic conditions shifted. Al-Musabbihi, a chronicler and official under the Fatimid dynasty (969–1171), lived through the famine of 1024–25 and wrote of grain shipments diverted to the palace, the sight of bodies buried without ritual washing or shrouds, and starving people reduced to eating grass. The threat of popular violence put the palace on edge. On January 16, 1025, the caliph, making a procession through Cairo, was confronted by shouting: “Hunger, oh Commander of the Faithful, hunger! Neither your father nor your grandfather did such a thing to us.” Hunger spread from the civilian population to the black infantry of the caliph’s army, a corps composed of Sudanese slave soldiers. Soon both were rummaging for grain wherever they could find it, including in government warehouses opened by sympathetic or terrified officials. That famine set a pattern that would hold for more than five hundred years. When the river failed, the people who controlled the grain found ways to profit, and the people who went hungry found ways to make them pay, or at least fear. Not Merely Acts of God The Fatimid regime was dismantled in 1171 by the Kurdish warlord Saladin, whose Ayyubid dynasty was in turn overthrown in 1250 by its own Mamluks — Turkic and Circassian slave soldiers who subsequently established a military-patronage state that controlled Egypt, Palestine, and Syria until 1517. Despite the perennial hum of coups and infighting around the sultan’s throne, 250 years of relative administrative continuity lent the regime enough time and resources to build towering institutions of religious welfare and political legitimacy, patronize a literary renaissance, and organize Egypt as the focal point of Mediterranean and Indian Ocean commerce. Nevertheless, the limited safety net and trade money were parts of a system far more extractive than the Fatimid state had ever been. Mamluk captains, trying to control a potentially restive population and needing money to keep buying new Mamluks from the slave markets of Crimea, parceled out iqta (revenue assignments) among themselves and hoarded supplies to an unprecedented degree. The traditional lines between military and civilian roles blurred as some captains in turn leased their iqtas to non-Mamluk administrators, while they themselves lobbied the sultan for lucrative administrative positions, like that of muhtasib , the office responsible for policing the markets. Compounding disasters then made a bad situation far worse: the black death of the mid-fourteenth century wiped out the rural workforce, dikes and canals silted up (which meant the Nile had to rise higher for a sufficient harvest), and the effects of the Little Ice Age made the river itself far more erratic. Matters came to a head in 1403 during the rule of the teenage Sultan Faraj ibn Barquq, when the Nile once again failed to rise just after the Turco-Mongol warlord Timur had ravaged Mamluk Syria and another wave of the black death approached. Mamluk amirs , or commanders, responded by fighting each other and squeezing their tenants even harder to keep their own ledgers balanced. Faraj and his government, unable to materially solve any problems or admit to their own failures, resorted to massive public prayers for rain and crackdowns on supposed expressions of moral decay like alcohol, prostitution, and religious minorities who flouted sumptuary laws. Adam Sabra writes in Poverty and Charity in Medieval Islam: Mamluk Egypt, 1250–1517 that during the 1403–4 crisis, chroniclers made no mention of any state-sanctioned relief efforts, though individual sultans and amirs were typically more generous with their alms whenever they themselves fell sick, hoping God would reward their charity with a cure. The sense of insecurity and trauma brought by calamity and misrule may have encouraged Mamluk-era historians to increasingly supplement their record of events with commentary on the conditions of the wider city and common people. Al-Maqrizi (1364–1442), the most prolific among them, opined that the Nile disasters were not merely acts of God or unlucky natural patterns but a product of three human causes: a corrupt government lubricated with bribery, extreme rent-seeking and hoarding behavior by the moneyed and landowning classes, and the reckless policy of flooding the market with low-value, inflationary copper coins. “Whoever reflects on this occurrence from its beginning to its end and knows its origin and development realizes that what has befallen the population is caused solely by the malfeasance of the leaders and rulers and their negligence with regard to the public interest,” he wrote in his treatise Helping the Community by Examining the Causes of Its Distress ( Ighathat al-ummah bi-kashf al-ghummah ). “Indeed, wherever the causes are known, it is easy for the knowledgeable person to remedy all conditions, whether significant or insignificant. I seek God’s help in facing both difficult and easy matters; indeed ‘He tells the truth and He shows the way.’” The people in the streets had reached the same conclusion on their own. And when the powerful pushed too far, they pushed back. Prayers Against the Sultan Al-Maqrizi’s chronicles record the constant protests that emerged in reaction to state depredation. Throughout the Middle Ages and even more frequently during the Mamluk period, ordinary people channeled their frustration through tax evasion, smuggling, blocking the sultan’s or governor’s processions, collective petitioning (shouted or in writing), and, in extreme cases, violence. Those activities often came hand in hand, with petition typically following demonstration. Protest had no realistic chance of overturning state power (nor was that its purpose) but could apply sufficient pressure on authorities hoping to maintain their government positions and protect their property from physical damage. In pursuit of material reward and social correction, crowds applied violence systematically, indicating that they had a clear sense of a moral economy and the wrongness of transgressing it. One favored tradition of anti-profiteering protesters was finding the local muhtasib they deemed responsible and stoning him. The Mamluk-era historian Ibn Taghribirdi recorded how in 1435, a crowd in Cairo sent the muhtasib fleeing into the Citadel for protection. Another example of this occurred in 1487, when, amid rising grain prices, the muhtasib of Damascus was found to be mixing his government duties with his private involvement in the grain trade. Shams al-Din ibn Tulun recorded that an organized crowd found him patrolling the market and proceeded to unload its displeasure on him. These two officials may have suffered a nasty shock and some injuries, but they were fortunate compared to the governor and deputy governor of Damietta in 1417, who, as al-Maqrizi records, were “arrested” by the crowd, humiliated, and then struck dead. The elite response to such protests was varied. Amina Elbendary writes in Crowds and Sultans: Urban Protest in Late Medieval Egypt and Syria of the fluidity of such crowds and indeed of social categories in general. The protesters, consisting not just of the “structural/permanent poor” but also artisans, shopkeepers, and lower-level clerics, often sought out members of the clerical establishment ( ulama ), or even a muhtasib they considered to be morally upright, to intercede with the government on their behalf. In the case of the 1417 incident at Damietta, the crowd brought some of their local religious judges to register a case against the same governor they were about to lynch. These actions sometimes elicited refusal or even more punitive taxes. Other times, the protest and subsequent petition secured localized, temporary alleviation rather than structural reforms. But the broader tradition of protesting economic predation was understood by both rulers and the people as a constant threat or leverage that punished anyone who overstepped the line. Muslims are instructed by the Qur’an and hadith to enjoin good and forbid wrong — a commandment that extends beyond personal survival and piety to the proactive pursuit of social justice. Innocent victims of misfortune could be seen as casualties of elite misconduct or, if they bore their suffering patiently, even as martyrs. But a society that accepted unjust rulers and did not rise up against them was itself complicit. Both sides, then, took their case to God. In a crisis, the ruling class organized mass public prayers for rain. Protesters often shouted “God is great” before a demonstration, and chroniclers described such moments by writing that “prayers against the sultan [or governor] increased.” The Mamluk regime did not survive the collapse of its trade revenue, driven by Portuguese naval ambitions, and the Ottoman advance into the Levant and Egypt. Qansuh al-Ghuri, the penultimate Mamluk sultan, regarded by chroniclers as the most avaricious among the rulers of that period, supposedly died of a stress- and battle-induced stroke on the plains of Marj Dabiq in front of his Qur’an readers. A change in regime, however, did not keep people off the streets whenever greed or misrule threatened their livelihoods. In 1591, not long after the Cairenes “prayed against” him, Awis Pasha, the city’s governor, died of a stroke. Mike Wallace, Gotham’s Chronicler

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Joel Feingold ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-21T16:52:47+00:00

Mike Wallace once handed me a matchbook, the kind of paper trinket that served like ticker-tape as a currency of the twentieth-century city: given out at corner stores and newsstands, boxing matches and cafeterias, free-flowing from cigarette and candy booths on subway platforms. These flammable paper memes were often used for advertising: “Get yer alterations hemmed right here!” “Smoke Nat Shermans.” “Join the Army!” “Have a refreshing PAPAYA drink and a frank.” You could strike a match on an electioneering pitch or on a slogan: “Vote Jimmy Walker and good old Tammany!” “Boycott the Nazis and German goods.” “Look for the union label!” “Don’t buy where you can’t work!” You might strike your match on the matchbook from the nightclub you went to last Saturday night, or get a light walking home from the train at the end of the day, tired but relieved, as the next shift jammed the streets and the subway. Even the commanding heights had their own matchboxes and matchbooks, monogrammed like: Paul D. Cravath, Partner: Cravath, Swaine & Moore, ACQUISITIONS & TRUSTS. Mike’s matchbook — all caps in an emphatic mid-century corporate-recruiting Helvetica, embossed on a cardboard square flint the hue of a big yellow taxi — said: EARN BIG MONEY! BECOME A HISTORIAN And on the reverse: SUBSCRIBE TO THE RADICAL HISTORY REVIEW I doubled over in laughter; this was a great sight gag, I thought. I also felt in that split second that I understood what Wallace, the legendary historian of New York City who died July 4, was trying to tell me about his life’s work. An anti-Nazi matchbook cover ( Ephemeral New York ) Writing a new history of the world in one city was also a project to find, underneath the pavement, the beach: Coney Island’s beach to be precise, that real-life proletarian paradise, the garden at the edge of the workers’ city where you went to get lucky, or try your luck, or when you were down on your luck: the place you could always go if your luck ran out, to make yourself new again with everyone else there on the boardwalk and the beach. From this Coney Island of the mind, as Wallace wrote in the his Gotham book series’ rare mystic note in the first volume’s introduction, the city appeared all at once as a “mountain valley filled with a turquoise lake, in the middle of which floated a thousand-petaled lotus flower.” That flower, I argue, is the class-struggle- in-uno-plures tradition (“in one, many”), defiant and improbably jutting out of the Manhattan schist, face-to-face with the silk-stocking districts and their eugenic worldview. In uno plures (“in one, many”) was a Mike Wallace coinage, a core concept in both Greater Gotham and Gotham at War — and in some ways the main character of the Gotham series, which you can read as a genesis story of the gorgeous mosaic and the city’s built environment and creed. Reversing the melting-down imagery of the American ruling class’s national motto “ e pluribus unum ” — instead of the commanding heights’ vision of “out of many, one” — working-class Gotham has long insisted that wholeness only exists where there’s infinite diversity, that radical pluralism is the only possible unity. A photograph of Coney Island, unknown date. (Bain News Service / Library of Congress) This tradition originated from and belongs to New York’s working class and its organizations, and, by association, to all the workers of the world. As the International Ladies’ Garment Workers Union [ILGWU] Local #22 May Day float put it in 1934: “The Power of Labor Lives in Organization and Militancy: Workers of 32 Nationalities United.” Their banner envisioned the dressmakers’ union as a force “Fighting for One Cause — The Cause of Labor.” This tradition has echoed down the ages — the source of the city’s social democracy. I heard it this spring, when an eighty-year-old Chinese American woman named Yu Story rallied her neighbors to beat back the city’s attempts to demolish and privatize their public housing on 26th Street, in the once-Irish working-class blocks along the west side docks, now a working-class neighborhood of people from all over the world, in spite of the masters of the universe. Ms Story said, “We are people. . . . We are the hero.” That class-struggle- in-uno-plures tradition — the flower — is exactly what E. B. White captured in ink in the New Yorker on April 14, 1945, at the moment it was co-opted and tamed from above, becoming the liberal common sense of postwar American politics. In this starry-eyed Victory in Europe Day context, as the world powers wondered where the United Nations would be headquartered, White acknowledged the debt this new emerging ideology owed to New York’s working class — to Gotham — because here was a sort of world state in miniature. Here dwell the world’s emigrants under one law, and the law is: Thou shalt not push thy neighbor around. By some curious divinity which in him lies, Man, in this experiment of mixed races and mixed creeds, has turned out more good than bad, more right than wrong, more kind than cruel, and more sinned against than sinning. This is the world’s hope and its chance. This is the apotheosis of the class-struggle- in-uno-plures tradition forged by workers and immigrants on the streets of New York, now taken up and toned down by the ruling classes as an ideal for the governance of the whole world, from the place that model originated (and where the victory over fascism and the new model of imperial free trade would be anchored): of course the United Nations had to be headquartered in New York. A reminder of this idealist variation on the class-struggle- in-uno-plures tradition and its more militant source is desperately needed in New York and in the world today: now the flower confronts perhaps the ugliest and most brutal expression of the social Darwinism and habitual dog-eat-dog cruelty of the rich in nearly a century. The Gotham project set out to deliver history to the reader as something alive, something we are all a part of, something that can be acted on, something that we can change . The matchboxes they gave out everywhere in this town used to say, “Strike anywhere.” Write the Whole City Down I worked as Mike’s researcher for almost fifteen years, from the fall of 2011, the season of Occupy Wall Street (when Mike would look the other way if we’d skip a seminar or two to march on the banks) to the fall of 2025, just before the general strike in Minnesota’s Twin Cities began last winter. One generation gave way again to the next. We were drawn together by a shared commitment to finding a usable past and a shared conviction that the only way to do this was to write the whole city down. While I assembled two of Wallace’s 2,500-source bibliographies — and as I wrote thousands of comments on drafts of Greater Gotham and Gotham at War — the months became years, then a decade and a half. What began as an apprenticeship became a search for the whole of human experience in the city. Only Mike’s droll, cheerful, and humanistic Marxism could really capture it all, that unique Wallace vision of history. In uno plures:“ in one, many.” Here also was the Gotham project’s research design and historical method from the very beginning, the necessary result of synthesizing all the new history from below — as Wallace and Edwin G. Burrows set out to do nearly fifty years ago, when they began to write the city’s history anew “from the Beginning of the World,” as its first biographer Washington Irving had envisioned in 1809. Gotham would be a new coming together of women’s history, working-class history, black and native history, immigration history, the history of sexuality, the history of mass culture, and the collision of a million different folkways transformed by the tenements — arraying all that new history from below against the new history from above: the history of capitalism. This was not your old man’s “synthesis” — not the simple additive method Wallace learned from his teacher Richard Hofstadter. But perhaps Wallace turned Hofstadter on his head. “Social Darwinism” was, of course, a Richard Hofstadter coinage: one of several enduring ideas that he has passed down to us. Hofstadter’s classic wartime dissertation Social Darwinism charted the origins and trajectory of a homegrown American fascist ideology with roots deep in the American experience: “A people long familiar with Indian warfare on the frontier and the pro-slavery arguments of Southern politicians and publicists had been thoroughly grounded in notions of racial superiority.” Summing up the origins and trajectory of this ideology, Hofstadter quoted a particularly bloodthirsty 1845 American expansionist: “The Mexican race now see in the fate of the aborigines of the north, their own inevitable destiny. They must amalgamate or be lost in the superior vigor of the Anglo-Saxon race, or they must utterly perish.” Wallace, for his part, did not fail to trace the origins of a “Manifest Destiny” ideology of American conquest to New York boosters, but followed his teacher’s 1944 aside: “Yet it is possible to take another view. . . . ” Mike also showed us that Gotham and its people fought back, in how some New Yorkers have always demanded an end to imperialism, pulling for the victory of the oppressed in homelands all over the hemisphere and in every corner of the world. Gotham at War records that ever “since Francisco de Miranda’s 1806 expedition sailed from the East River in its doomed attempt to liberate South America from Spanish rule, New York had been the premier base of operations for rebel Latin Americans. Once the Wars of Independence triumphed in the 1820s, leaving Cuba and Puerto Rico as Spain’s only hemispheric possessions, an array of exiles, from Félix Varela to José Martí” made Gotham not just the seat of American expansionism and settler colonialism, but also held out the promise that New York could be the tomb of imperialism. Certainly, in the city from a very early date, visions of racial conquest and extermination were anathema to a creed emerging from the streets. As Mary Louise Booth wrote in her 1859 History of the City of New York , Gotham was already “blended with all the races of the earth; and if it be true, as one of our most eminent philosophers asserts, that a mixture of many materials makes the best mortar, there is no reason to regret it.” This was decidedly not the consensus in many layers of New York society in the middle of the nineteenth century. As Wallace and Hofstadter argued together in 1970 in American Violence , the brutal downtown nativism of Bill the Butcher — who cracked heads and stole ballots at the polling places of immigrant workers on Park Row and the Bowery in the early 1850s — was just muscle for the millionaires uptown. “A high proportion of our violent actions,” Hofstadter wrote in his introduction to the sources Wallace excavated and arranged, “has thus come from the top dogs or the middle dogs. Such has been the character of most mob and vigilante movements. This may help explain why so little of it has been used against state authority, and why in turn it has been so easily and indulgently forgotten.” Overlaid by the capitalist ethos of domination by the strong, premised on natural hierarchies and scientific racism, this peculiar American vision was crystalized in the Gilded Age maxim attributed to (but never actually uttered by) the New York robber baron Jay Gould: “I can hire one half of the working class to kill the other half.” As the nineteenth century gave way to the crisis of imperialism and World War I, this elitist current in America came to espouse a politics of panic over “threatened Anglo-Saxon power throughout the world.” In a United States “submerged by a flood of non-Anglo-Saxon immigrants,” the white men of America could assert “fitness only through a national reawakening.” In some ways, Hofstadter suggests, American social-Darwinist ideology and practice was a model for European fascism. Picking this thread up where his teacher left off, Wallace shows us in Gotham at War that Jim Crow segregation and American race law (the one-drop rule and otherwise) directly inspired Adolf Hitler. The Führer taunts us from the pages of Gotham at War that the United States was “the first to draw practical political consequences from the differentiation of races.. . . . Through its immigration law, America has inhibited the unwelcome influence of such races as it has been unable to tolerate within its midst.” The children of the new immigrants roared back from City College, where the young radicals of the 1930s wore pins that said: “I am a guttersnipe. I fight fascism.” Among the most famous false prophets of this early American fascism was the Upper East Side’s own Madison Grant. The pseudoscientist, professional racist, and prominent advocate of immigration restriction lived in a mansion at 960 Fifth Avenue between 77th and 78th Streets, overlooking Central Park and the Metropolitan Museum of Art. In these sumptuous digs in 1934, Grant gratefully received a letter from Hitler hailing The Passing of the Great Race as “my Bible.” Where Hofstadter was often (and with age, increasingly) preoccupied by the dark currents in American life — social Darwinism, the paranoid style, anti-intellectualism — it took Wallace and the revolution in history from below to find what his teacher had shown only glimmers of: solidarity and pluralism as the working class’s antidote and answer. In 1943 and 1944, as the war shuddered into its nightmare end, and as the question of the postwar order hung in the air, the young Hofstadter looked back a generation, to the Progressive historian Herbert Croly’s injunction: We must overcome the “traditional American ‘mixture of optimism, fatalism, and conservatism’ in favor of a more positive attempt to realize the national potential.” Sneaking in his own youthful Marxism the best he could, the twenty-seven-year-old Hofstadter quoted a dissident scholar’s 1906 anti-eugenic salute to “the proletariat, the working class, the hewers of wood and drawers of water” — and concluded “that the physical well-being of men is a result of their social organization and not vice versa.” As Angela Davis — who lived on the corner of St Marks and Kingston in Crown Heights, Brooklyn, as a teenager, having fled Alabama in the middle of the 1950s to attend high school in Gotham — would put it years later: “One cannot be free alone. Freedom is collective.” New York Will Never Have Another Mike Wallace Mike made a point never to preach, often even avoiding arguments about the meaning of events in the sweep of history. The pointillist, humanistic method would show the truth. But Mike was a radical, active in the movement to remake American history from below in a struggle with the city’s ruling class. As Hofstadter’s research assistant, Wallace had the keys to several buildings on the Columbia University campus — and while his teacher veered from a wry and erudite consensus liberalism into bitter attacks on the “book burners” and ruffians of the New Left, it was Mike who quietly opened the doors to these buildings so that his comrades in the black and antiwar student movements could occupy them in April and May of 1968, in solidarity with the people of Harlem and the people of Vietnam. Wallace maintained these commitments throughout his life. As a junior scholar at the City University of New York’s John Jay College, he coauthored the Newt Davidson Collective’s 1974 Crisis at CUNY , immediately recognizing in the “fiscal crisis” an attack on the social democratic victories of generations of working-class youth movements in the city’s public university system. The bankers’ abolition of CUNY’s free tuition — a policy that had stood since City College’s inception in 1847 — was realized by 1976, as were the first limitations on the open admissions policy that guaranteed every graduate of a New York City high school a spot in a CUNY college: a victory secured by black and Puerto Rican student radicals in the City College sit-ins of 1969 and only completely eliminated by Rudy Giuliani’s mayoral administration in 1999. Mike’s instant recognition of the rise of neoliberalism in Gotham helped shape his colleague David Harvey’s view that the first salvos of a coordinated attack on working-class power and social democracy were waged simultaneously in Abraham Beame’s New York and Augusto Pinochet’s Chile. In 1973 — the last time the Knicks won the NBA championship — CUNY was free and open to every New Yorker. The Knicks have won again, and it is high time to retake that higher-education victory too. As a professor, Wallace reveled in the atmosphere of John Jay College of Criminal Justice, teaching working-class kids and first-year cops in rough-and-tumble night school sessions. He told me he usually got along with the rookie cops and the kids because, unlike the ivory-tower liberals, they often shared a materialist understanding of the city and the world (“follow the money”) and most also shared the streets of New York as a first teacher. Mike was born in Brooklyn in 1942 and came up in Fresh Meadows, Queens; he spent a spell as a child in San Francisco (where his uncle Al was a Latin jazz and swing bandleader); and he graduated from high school in Great Neck, Long Island. Wallace’s standard assignment for a final paper was to interview the oldest New Yorker you knew about their life coming up in the big city — showing students that we are all a part of history, and that we can all write it too. A generation of City University and Wallace-trained scholars continue the work in his wake: Emily Brooks ( Gotham’s War Within a War: Policing and the Birth of Law-and-Order Liberalism in World War II–Era New York City ); Michelle Chen (among many other works, “Radical Defense: The American Committee for the Protection of the Foreign Born and the Movement for Deportation Resistance and Immigrants’ Rights,” in Journal of Migration History ); Andy Battle (“New York 2140: Climate Change and Gentrification in Tomorrow’s Ruined New Yorks,” in Capitalism Nature Socialism ; “On the Auction Block: The Garment Industry and the Deindustrialization of New York City,” in International Labor and Working-Class History ); and Katie Uva (“1964 and the State of the City,” in World’s Fairs in the Cold War ), among many others. New York will never have another teacher or partisan like Mike. Everyone who has ever lived here, or traveled here, or dreams of this place inherits the life work of the city’s singer and the keeper of its memory. Mike Wallace died on July 4, 2026. Whenever someone speaks a word or takes a step anywhere in the five boroughs, we weave a new thread in the story he wrote down, for all of us to keep forever. With the People in the City On the East Side, where Le Corbusier’s United Nations complex was built over the ruins of slaughterhouses on land long owned by the Astors, New York became the headquarters of the world (if not ever truly the capital of the world). The United Nations headquarters in Midtown Manhattan (Jakub Hałun / CC BY-SA 4.0 ) This was one of the many ways that Wallace thought he’d conclude Gotham at War . Seeing parallels in the postwar corporate economy — the skyscraper headquarters of multinational capital towering over Midtown, the canyons of brick and concrete rippling out from Wall Street — the sleek glass and steel tower of the General Assembly of the United Nations (at 405 East 42nd Street and First Avenue) reflected a world order orbiting New York City, in the city’s image, and in its model of consolidated corporate capital. Gotham presumed only to be the central and highest node in the organizational chart of a worldwide network of offices. And like the corporate boardrooms atop the multinationals’ skyscrapers, the world powers convened a security council, where the real power from above was. Here then are the architectonics of the whole Gotham trilogy, the big argument now assuming full form. The city came into being as a colonial periphery, then it was a model and metonym for the nation, now it was the headquarters of the postwar world made in its image: pluralistic, multilateralist, free trade, linked to the center by Wall Street finance, the city itself governed as a social democracy with a restive and powerful working class but an even more powerful ruling class: they’re about to be the masters of the universe. For all of the explanatory power of Mike’s (usually) unstated argument, Gotham as the song of the city is at most revealing when it is on the ground, with the people, in the city. Wallace’s prelapsarian image of the Bronx — before the development wave at the turn of the twentieth century that turned it into an instant garden city — is gorgeous social history, a landscape of “Romani tinkers, scissor grinders, fishermen with shad from the North River,” when Morrisania and Melrose were chiefly German. Villagers worked in the local breweries.. . . . They worshiped at local Lutheran and Catholic churches and shopped and amused themselves along Courtlandt Avenue — known as ‘Dutch [Deutsch] Broadway’ for its German saloons, beer halls, and gymnastic and singing societies. Meanwhile: Highbridgeville, an Irish settlement since the building of the Croton Aqueduct, afforded work for railroad section hands, stevedores along the Harlem River docks, and gardeners. Wallace had an eye for old, deep histories that would generally go unremarked, little jetties of liberation in the larger streams of municipal history. Like the Union American Methodist Episcopal Church of Staten Island, founded by free black workers as early as 1801, anchor of a black community in Stapleton for the last 225 years. The Stapelton Union American Methodist Episcopal Church of Staten Island (Jim Henderson / CC BY-SA 4.0 ) The welter of working-class life in Gotham produced solidarity and pluralism — not out of neighborly love or multicultural idealism but out of sheer necessity. Wallace, in rare argumentative interludes in Greater Gotham , makes it plain. For workers in the twentieth-century city: Forging alliances with other ethnic groups was a practical necessity if they were to build a collective base of resistance to exploitation. . . . there had always been strenuous inter-ethnic competition over jobs, housing, and access to public resources, a jostling exacerbated by economic crisis, ethno-racial-religious tensions, and repercussions from overseas conflicts. Confronted with these obstacles, workers in New York sought to overcome them, by learning each other’s languages, celebrating together on birthdays, at baby showers, and at weddings, and organizing the labor movement side by side. Picket lines and Labor Day parades were routinely multi-ethnic affairs, marchers hoisting signs and banners in multiple languages. Industrial unionists were particularly determined to bridge ethnic fault lines: there were, as Big Bill Haywood put it, “no foreigners in the working class.” This is to say: the United Nations already existed on the streets of Gotham — and in that and other ways, New York was a model for, metonym of, and capital of the emerging postwar order. If We Ever Finish It Mike’s last story is the madcap tale of the real estate deal that brought the United Nations to town in 1946. This evokes nothing so much as Washington Irving’s original Gotham — a city of ridiculous “bulbous-bottomed burghers” spreading “forth over the land until they covered the actual site of this venerable city.” In literal landownership and in spirit, Wallace’s last tale about the Astors and the slaughterhouses and the land that became the United Nations harkens all the way back to old John Jacob Astor himself: “If I could live all over again, I would buy every square inch of Manhattan.” There’s an important question here about the “capital of the world” argument that Mike leaves to us. What about New York as the center of the postwar world was historical necessity amid the longue durée? A 1901 view of the Manhattan skyline, captured from Brooklyn (Irving Underhill) What about the big architectonics — class struggle in uno plures ; what Samuel Stein has called New York’s “real estate state;” the city’s place in the military-industrial complex; Wall Street; the diasporic links New York already had to the rest of the world — made the contingent possible, sealed the deal? How then can we account for the very contingent, uncertain, touch-and-go way it came about? There’s something to be said here on New York and the “real estate state” — its longue durée in Gotham. All I know is, as O. Henry once said, “It’ll be a great place if they ever finish it.” As Mike once wrote, at the end of Greater Gotham : So far, so good. Profits Are Soaring. Workers’ Share of Income Is Sinking.

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Conor Lynch ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-21T15:53:26+00:00

Outside periods of recession, the public mood has rarely been as downbeat about the economy as it is today, with consumer confidence at a historic low and polls recording a deep pessimism about where things are headed. Against this backdrop, political and business leaders are touting an economic “golden age,” pointing to solid growth , stable unemployment, record profits , and a stock market that seems to only go up. The vibes on Main Street are gloomy, but on Wall Street they are nearly euphoric. One way to understand this growing disconnect between everyday people and elites is to examine how today’s celebrated gains are distributed across the economy compared with earlier periods. To do that, economists typically look at the national income and how much of it goes to workers — the “labor share” — versus the portion that flows to capital in profits and rents. (Together labor and profits make up roughly two-thirds of gross domestic income; the remaining third is absorbed by rents, taxes, and a mix of other smaller categories.) The US Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) tracks the labor share of the nonfarm business sector (the private economy minus agriculture) with quarterly data going back to 1947. For most of the twentieth century, that share was remarkably stable: it rose during recessions, as business income fell, and it declined as companies regained profitability, yet it never dropped below 60 percent and averaged around 63 percent. Labor’s consistent capture of about two-thirds of the national income was, in the words of John Maynard Keynes, “one of the most surprising, yet best-established, facts in the whole range of economic statistics.” This historical pattern began to shift around the turn of the century. After peaking at 64 percent during the 2001 recession, the labor share began a long slide, falling below 60 percent for the first time in 2004 and staying there ever since. More recently, this long-term decline has accelerated. In September, the BLS announced that the labor share had dropped to its lowest level on record for the fourth quarter in a row. The labor share now stands at 52.8 percent, more than ten points below its twentieth-century average. The downward trajectory coincides with rising profits across corporate America . As a share of gross domestic income, corporate profits are now the highest they’ve been since the early 1950s — at a time when hourly wage growth has cooled to a five-year low and is now well behind inflation. Economists have offered many explanations for this trend. In a 2021 paper , Gene M. Grossman and Ezra Oberfield reviewed the vast literature on the subject and compiled the most commonly cited culprits, including technological change and automation, globalization and neoliberal trade policies, deindustrialization, increasing market concentration, the decline of unions, and more. Beyond these structural causes, others have pointed to shifts in how some business owners and executives report their income. According to research by economists Matthew Smith, Danny Yagan, Owen Zidar, and Eric Zwick, roughly a third of the decline in the corporate sector’s labor share between 1978 and 2017 can be attributed to changes in how business owners pay themselves and how this income is recorded in the national accounts. They largely attribute this change to the Tax Reform Act of 1986, which encouraged firms to organize as “pass-throughs” and their owners to take income as profits rather than wages. Among pass-through firms owned by the top 1 percent, the owners’ share of value added rose from 37 percent to 48 percent between 2001 and 2014, a gain that came at the expense of their employees. The different theories of labor’s declining share are often framed as rival explanations. In practice, they’re pieces of the same political-economic shift. The neoliberal turn of the late 1970s ushered in a new era of shareholder capitalism, which made maximizing shareholder returns the organizing goal of the corporation. To deliver those returns, America’s business leaders waged a broad assault on the labor movement, using every lever available to curb worker power. Technology, globalization, and union busting all operated through the same channel toward that end. This reality has led some economists to put forward a more “unified explanation,” one that identifies the decades-long erosion of worker power as the root cause of labor’s dwindling share. The Hidden Wedge Dramatic as it is, the labor share decline understates how much income has moved away from ordinary workers over the last forty years, because it tells us nothing about the vast inequalities hidden within the labor share itself. According to research by economists at the Economic Policy Institute (EPI), nearly 80 percent of the productivity-pay gap — the widening gulf between productivity growth and pay gains for nonsupervisory workers — has been driven by inequalities within the labor share, with the shift toward capital accounting for the remaining 20 percent. Before 1979, wage growth for the vast majority of workers tracked closely with productivity gains and with the growing compensation of high-income earners. Since then, most of the gains from productivity have flowed to top earners and shareholders. EPI’s analysis shows that the bottom 90 percent of workers captured nearly 70 percent of all earnings in 1979, while the top 1 percent of earners took in a little over 7 percent. By 2021, the share of the bottom 90 percent had fallen by more than eleven points to 58.6 percent, while the top 1 percent of earners had doubled their share to nearly 15 percent — the highest point since the data became available in 1937. This trend was even more pronounced for the top 0.1 percent, whose share of earnings more than tripled, from 1.6 percent to 5.9 percent. Subtract the top 1 percent’s earnings, and the labor share falls well below its current historic low, by a much wider margin than in the past. In an email, EPI Chief Economist Josh Bivens told Jacobin that the fall in the labor share and the growing inequality within it both stem from excess employer power in the labor market and decades of wage suppression. “Basically, I think a set of policies aimed at wage suppression have successfully slowed wage growth for the vast majority of workers,” Bivens said, “and this left a large and growing ‘excess’ wedge of income that went to much more privileged actors in the economy.” Before 2000, most of this excess wedge showed up as rapid pay growth at the top of the income scale; during that period, as Bivens put it, “there was not a huge shift in the labor/profit shares.” Instead, top executives like General Electric CEO Jack Welch, poster boy of the shareholder revolution and ruthless union-buster , saw their pay explode. Between 1978 and 2000, the CEO-to-worker pay ratio at America’s top companies ballooned from under 25-to-1 to nearly 400-to-1. Since the turn of the century, the excess has flowed more directly from labor to capital. “Post-2000, you see less of a wedge in pay growth between the very top and typical workers (still a big one, just not as big), but you start to see a noticeable rise in the profit share,” Bivens said. Part of this reflects changes in how business owners and executives report their income. The post-2000 shift away from labor coincided with a time when “a bunch of tax policy and regulatory policy changes made it more advantageous for super-privileged actors (corporate executives mostly) to report their income in forms that don’t show up in the labor share but do show up in the profit share.” But reclassification explains only part of the shift. Profits also claimed a genuinely larger share of what the economy produced, boosting stock prices along with them. Whether it flowed to executives or shareholders, this redistribution of income has been a driving force behind the rapid growth of both income and wealth inequality over the last half century. As Bivens and EPI President Heidi Shierholz point out in a recent essay , estimates suggest that “anywhere from 40 percent to nearly 100 percent” of the nominal gains in the stock market since 1989 can be attributed to capital’s growing share of the national income. The implication, they write, is clear: “The wage suppression of recent decades is not just by far the biggest driver of the rise in income inequality; it is also by far the biggest driver of the rise in wealth inequality.” Labor and the AI Boom The recent acceleration in the labor share’s decline has unfolded alongside an explosion in corporate profits and the strongest stock market bull run since the dot-com era. Yet these surging profits and asset prices have been driven less by genuine productivity gains than by financial speculation and a series of market shocks — from the supply-chain disruptions of the pandemic years to the recent spike in energy costs triggered by the Iran war. Apart from these disaster-driven profits , the stock market boom has been powered by a handful of superstar companies concentrated in the tech sector. The top ten companies listed on the S&P 500 now make up roughly 40 percent of the index’s total market cap and generate over a third of its earnings, buoyed by the debt-fueled artificial intelligence boom. These record profits are unlikely to last. If the current AI boom goes bust, as a growing number of market watchers now believe it will , earnings will plummet, and the profit share will decline relative to labor. But workers, not shareholders, invariably bear the brunt of recessions. Labor currently has little to gain and much to lose from the breakneck development of AI, especially if it proves as disruptive to jobs as many economists and AI boosters predict. Yet workers have even less to gain from a recession triggered by the collapse of the AI bubble, which would create ideal conditions for capital to further discipline labor — this time with some of the shiny new AI tools developed over the last several years. The path to avoiding these outcomes and reversing the long-term decline of the labor share lies in a revitalized labor movement. As long as labor is weak and fragmented, workers will continue to see their share of the economic pie contract as billionaires and CEOs reap the rewards of the new AI economy. The recently reelected president of the United Auto Workers (UAW), Shawn Fain, wasn’t being hyperbolic this June when he described the development of AI and the push for mass automation as a “profound threat and challenge to our way of life.” The compounding threats that workers now face have, in turn, increased public support and sympathy for labor unions. In September, Gallup’s annual survey on the labor movement showed that more than seven in ten Americans now approve of unions — the highest level since the 1960s — along with a record-high plurality of 47 percent who say that unions should have more influence. Fain is among a cohort of new labor leaders who have pushed for a bolder and more combative labor movement willing to take the fight to capital. The UAW’s successful “stand-up strike” against the Big Three automakers in 2023 was part of a new wave of labor action in the post-pandemic years. New organizing efforts have also helped to slowly reverse the long-term decline in unionization, with data from the BLS showing the number of workers represented by unions rising to a sixteen-year high in 2025. Whether the labor movement can sustain its current momentum is far from certain. What is clear is that its strength and influence will need to grow substantially in the years ahead if it is to meaningfully shape the emerging AI economy and shift the balance of power back toward workers. Until then, the economic gains touted by political and industry elites will almost certainly continue flowing straight to the top. Isabella Weber on How to Defeat the Far Right

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Isabella Weber ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-21T13:56:39+00:00

The economist Isabella Weber is best known for her work on what is often called “seller’s inflation,” the ability of firms with market power to hike prices and boost profits during an emergency. During and after the Covid-19 pandemic, Weber was one of the loudest voices criticizing those who treated inflation purely as a product of too much demand, earning the censure of establishment economists in the process. Six years on, her arguments have been largely vindicated. It is clear that while constraints to supply did cause some prices to spike, the resulting cost shocks coordinated corporate pricing that spread inflation across the whole economy. In her new book, Anti-Fascist Economics: In Defense of Affordability, Dignity, and Democracy , she turns to an equally pressing political issue: What is needed to stem the rising tide of far-right extremism? Her answer is an alternative economic playbook, building on her earlier work, which puts affordability of essentials and dignity at its center. Weber spoke to Jacobin about what an economic system that prioritizes ordinary people’s material needs over corporate profits would look like, and how it could contribute to halting the march of what some have called fascism in its tracks. Bartolomeo Sala In your new book Anti-Fascist Economics , you build a powerful case for a new “economics for common people” as the only antidote to the threat of a resurgent “end-times fascism.” You spend the central part of the book showing how Trump 2.0 was ultimately a consequence of high inflation that shot up the prices of essentials and ate into people’s standards of living. However, you open the book by showing the current threat to democracy is not the result of one mismanaged crisis. Rather, it is the inevitable end point of fifty years of neoliberalism and a system which puts profits above everything else. How is it that neoliberalism — with its dogmatic insistence on what you call “profit fundamentalism” and limited view of freedom as submission to the impersonal rule of the market — almost necessarily leads to fascism? Isabella Weber Just one qualification before we start. I wouldn’t say neoliberalism necessarily leads to fascism. I think fascism is a very complex historical phenomenon that has multiple drivers, and the economy is one of them. Nevertheless, I think it’s helpful to see that there is a kind of convergence between the logics of the two, which is not to say that they are the same, but that, at bottom, they are both forms of social Darwinism. In a neoliberal world, there is this idea that everyone’s worth has to be established on the market, that the market creates a meritocratic hierarchy. If you are rich and are on top of said hierarchy, it is because you have strong abilities, you are superior to others, you have achieved more. Conversely, if you are poor or if you are dependent on social transfers, then this is basically a result of your lack of abilities. Now, in this sense, neoliberalism is a very hierarchical, competition-based way of seeing the world. Under fascism, we don’t have a hierarchy that is established through the market, but a “natural” one. Indeed, the whole idea of fascism is that the decadence and despair of the present are the result of interventions against this natural hierarchy, and the job of fascism is to restore a mythical past where this natural hierarchy is again allowed to play out. This natural hierarchy in the fascist case explicitly deems some populations dispensable. You see this very clearly with climate change, where if we let climate change happen, then some populations will die, or the energy crisis, where if we let the energy prices explode, then some people’s livelihoods may be destroyed. From the neoliberal perspective, this is an unfortunate collateral damage. Unfortunately, the market produces outcomes where some people’s livelihoods are destroyed. What the fascists do is to embrace this Malthusian logic that is baked into neoliberal thinking. They see it as a cleansing mechanism where some of these surplus populations are being cleaned out, and thus more space is made for the supreme race. Bartolomeo Sala So fascism is a kind of last resort for neoliberalism? Isabella Weber So far I have talked about neoliberalism as a system of thought. However, neoliberalism in practice, as a regime to organize our economy, has brought about spectacular wealth concentration, especially corporate concentration. Just to give you a statistic, in 1930, the top 0.1 percent of firms, in terms of their asset ownership, owned 47 percent of assets in the United States. In 2013, the top 0.1 percent owned 88 percent of the assets. Now, the fact that neoliberalism brings about this concentration of power and conceals this process, thereby opening the door for the possibility for the fascists to embrace it, is one side of the coin. The other side of some people getting extremely rich is, of course, many people living enormously precarious lives and being constantly at the mercy of the next market turmoil. To be sure, economic insecurity is not the same as poverty, and often it’s those who have something to lose who are most receptive to the far right’s promises. It’s a lack of alternatives — which is programmatic under neoliberalism — and the powerlessness that comes with it that makes the fascist promise of restoring the self-worth of people whose lives have been degraded over decades by returning to a mythical past of racial purity appealing. Bartolomeo Sala The place where this inability to take common people’s material needs seriously manifested itself in full force and ultimately proved fatal is the idea of “vibecession,” that is, the idea that the US economy was booming post-Covid and that people who felt otherwise were just wrong. Many of the arguments you made five years ago — that we were going through a crisis of affordability driven by corporations treating shocks and bottlenecks as an excuse to jack up prices — have become mainstream. However, at the time, the debate around high inflation was divided between those who advocated for austerity and those who suggested simply waiting for the storm to pass. Ultimately, this softer approach won out, but it did little to combat the effects of the ballooning cost of living. What did it get wrong about what you at the time called a case of “seller-induced inflation”? What could have been done differently? And why was Joe Biden’s unwillingness to be more bullish with big business and go after their profits ultimately the cause of the Democrats’ downfall? Isabella Weber Isabella Weber On the subject of who won the debate in terms of policy response: it is true that Team Wait-and-See prevailed in terms of fiscal policy, but we did get aggressive interest rate hikes eventually, which was the prescription of Team Austerity. That the fiscal side kept going strong partially offset monetary austerity. Anyway, sorry to be such a professor . . . Bartolomeo Sala That’s what we are here for! Isabella Weber Okay, going back to the subject of inflation, I think we need to realize what it is that happened in this crisis. First, we had a pandemic, the first global pandemic in a century. People were either locked down at home or working on the front lines exposed to the virus. Then the economy restarted. However, due to all the dislocation that the shutdowns had created, suddenly there were lots of shortages. These shortages created a pretext for price hikes, but the way this played out depended on the sector: in the case of energy, there was a collapse in demand during the lockdowns. Oil companies then shut down some of their production capacity. When demand came back on, they were in no rush to bring capacity back up. This mismatch sent oil prices upward, and the Russian attack on Ukraine made them explode. The question then is: How does the rest of the corporate world react to an increase in energy prices? Do they absorb this price increase, or do they pass it on in ways that protect their margins or maybe even increase their margin? What we saw is that the corporate sector as a whole managed to claim record profits right in the middle of this crisis. Now, this is an economic question. As a heterodox economist, my inclination is always to point to the structures, and my research shows how emergencies have opened windows of opportunity for price hikes. However, it’s also a question of morality and how we organize our society and economy. Capitalism is always immoral, but when, in a situation like this — where people have been fighting for their survival and the world has lived in fear over the threat of the virus — the reaction of the corporate sector is to jack up prices in ways that produce record profits, then this brings the immorality of capitalism out into plain sight. That’s why people were very angry about what myself and others called “greedflation.” Then there is the question of lived reality. People keep going to work every day and see the price of essentials, from food to energy to housing to transportation, become more expensive. This forces them to impossible choices between buying another used car because the old one broke and paying rent. This creates an enormous sense of insecurity and anxiety. If you just go, “Okay, let’s wait for this to pass,” that’s just another demonstration of the inability of the government to act. This is the contradiction that I start the book with. Under neoliberalism, governments say democracy is the greatest of all political systems, but when it comes to people’s basic material needs, unfortunately there seems to be nothing they can do. That’s already a contradiction in normal times, but even more so in crises, because people’s trust in democracy is then undermined in fundamental ways. Bartolomeo Sala And it’s not that these crises are one-offs, if anything, they will become ever more frequent. Isabella Weber We have seen it in polls time and again. As inflation progressed, more and more people started to blame corporations for high prices and believed the government should intervene and impose price controls. As you say, this is not ancient history, and these shocks are not random. We are in a global crisis of disintegration of our natural environments and geopolitical order that makes future, frequent shocks inevitable. There is so much more to antifascist economics than targeted price controls for essentials. However, in a time of crisis like ours, the question of price stabilization for the things people cannot do without is ultimately a question of whether the government is going to intervene to secure people’s access to affordable living conditions, even if doing so means going against the interests of powerful corporations or those who insist that the market is the only arbiter and so expose their constituencies to deeply immoral outcomes. Bartolomeo Sala As you write in your chapter “Capital Won’t Save Democracy,” capitalists and fascists are natural allies today just as they were in the 1930s — not just because the latter line the pockets of the former when stripping the state for parts, but because fascists are the best bulwark for their monopolies. Indeed, the two share a common enemy, “democratic states . . . when they regulate business activities in the common interest, protect the environment, or uphold labor rights.” Can you tell us why the unholy alliance between Davos Men and strongmen like Donald Trump isn’t an anomaly but a natural development? Isabella Weber First of all, one of the things that really struck me about being in Davos in 2025 during the inauguration of Trump was that large parts of the business community appeared to me almost more like followers than leaders. Many of them didn’t donate to Trump, but the moment the power shifted, they went with Trump. They are profit fundamentalists and, as such, they will always go with power. Now, to your question of what the democratic state does here, I actually found Javier Milei’s Davos speech very telling. He is the president of a country, of course, but he insists that all the problems that the West has are a result of feminist wokeness, which again goes back to this fascist idea that there are natural hierarchies and that if you are trying to intervene, you are messing up with the natural order. His diagnosis of the decline of the West is simply, “if only free enterprise had prevailed in its pure form, then we would not have had this aberration.” That’s I think how someone like Milei comes to embody the convergence between neoliberalism and fascism that we talked about in the beginning. As to what’s actually happening in terms of who globalization is working for, the first China shock was really an outsourcing shock. China was one part of the story, but the other was multinational companies headquartered in the West that were perfectly happy to throw a significant share of the working class in Europe and the United States under the bus. The second China shock is a very different beast, which is why the name is somewhat misleading. This shock is one where the almost universal dominance of Western corporations is suddenly under threat, as Chinese companies approaching the technological frontier in key industries start to pose a serious challenge to a world hierarchy in the global division of labor that dates back to the nineteenth century. What’s interesting is that the moment that exposure to markets, free capital flows, and open trade flows no longer is univocally to the benefit of Western-headquartered companies, the allegiances to these liberal ideals start flipping and instead an extreme anti-China “by any means necessary” stance ends up becoming mainstream. What I’m arguing is that this is important context that we need to acknowledge when we look at how globalization of the neoliberal type is being destroyed and is being replaced with tariff blitzes and geopolitical maneuvering that bring the whole toolbox of the might of states into the game of fortifying a certain global division of labor. Bartolomeo Sala If all that is possible is a zero-sum game between states and corporations, the best way to ride out the crisis is to invite it and hope to win, so at least in this zero-sum world you are the one calling the shots. Isabella Weber Exactly, it’s a zero-sum competition, which is now being fought with different means in a way, but it’s still a zero-sum competition. Bartolomeo Sala Could you talk a bit explicitly about how you understand antifascist economics? Isabella Weber An important inspiration is Karl Polanyi, who wanted to write a book, “Masterplan for the Common Man,” as an antifascist economics. For him, when there is a crisis that threatens the existential foundation of people’s lives, what he calls countermovements emerge. Fascism is a countermovement to fortify a profit fundamentalist order with state violence. What I am arguing is for a countermovement against today’s “end-times fascism” — to use Naomi Klein and Astra Taylor’s term — one that secures the material foundations of people’s livelihoods as the precondition for a dignified life as its overarching goal. The starting point for this is, very concretely, essentials for meeting people’s needs and for production systems, because obviously, to fulfil those needs, we need to have certain production structures in place. That’s the core. Then there are affordability policies that keep all of this together — things like rent freezes, a basket of price-controlled goods, free public transportation, or public ownership of energy and affordable utilities — policies that bring immediate relief. However, in order to sustain a price intervention, you need to change the structures of provisioning in these essential sectors. And that, ultimately, is a question of ownership. Given that we are in this global crisis of livelihoods, it’s not enough to bring down the price of gasoline and thereby render life more affordable to people who drive their cars, and meanwhile we burn through ever more oil and let the planet burn. We need to use those measures that stabilize prices to buy time for the structural change that can get us into a situation where our use of natural resources becomes compatible with the continued existence of human lives on planet Earth, which might sound a bit big, but I think that’s really what is at stake here. Bartolomeo Sala How can this economic outlook, that starts with people’s essential needs, work hand in hand with combating climate change and responding to anxieties about globalization? Isabella Weber Polanyi has this idea of land, labor, and money being essentials. I am basically broadening this category by saying that all the things that our life depends on are essentials, because for all of these things, it’s a lie that they can just work like the market for plastic toys or chewing gum. If the rent is too high, it’s not like you just reduce your demand. You need shelter and a place to live. And for a dignified life, you also need to be able to live where your friends are and where your family is. You cannot just be moved around like a fungible atom from one place to the next because rent went up. For food, it’s even more straightforward. You can’t say, the price of food went up, I’m not going to eat. The question then becomes: How do you organize each of these sectors in ways to achieve short-run affordability and to change the structure of provisioning in service of, among other goals, climate stabilization? What I’m arguing for in the case of agriculture are buffer stock systems, ideally multilateral, where you have a public stockholding entity that buys when the prices collapse because there’s a bumper harvest and sells when there’s a supply shock that constrains supply and prices spike. Now, the effect of such an entity would not only be to stabilize prices but also to change the dynamic in a market where right now four global food commodity trading companies control 70 to 90 percent of global food commodity trading. If you have such public stockholding systems, you basically build a massive procurement operation, because you start to buy grain and other durable commodities on a large scale. This allows you to set conditionalities: you only buy certain types of grain that are climate resilient; you buy types of grain that have been produced in a certain, environmentally friendly way. You then could combine it — indeed you should combine it — with a procurement and industrial policy for green fertilizers. By building out this public capacity, you also build out a lever that you can use to change the ways in which you organize food production in a sector — agriculture — that is one of the most difficult to decarbonize. By contrast, if you go in with carbon prices, in a context of already high inflation where food prices are up by more than 30 percent since 2019 like in the United States, you just make things worse. The same principle applies to the housing crisis. If you implement rent freezes, then the next step is to create more public housing, which, in a city like Berlin, could include socialization of large-scale corporate landlords and real estate companies that already control significant shares of your housing supply. If you take this housing stock over and put it in public hands, you have a public option that has a significant impact on what is happening in the housing market as a whole. You need to build more, of course, but you can make sure it’s done in green ways. Same for energy. And then, when you take all three together and add other essentials like transportation, you really are getting into the territory where you are tackling significant shares of the resource use and emissions in your economy. Bartolomeo Sala Brilliant. We didn’t really get into the subject of global trade and how an agenda of this kind would remove the trigger for renewed competition and imperialism, which is a shame. Not sure if you have any final thoughts to add? Isabella Weber So maybe to wrap up: the idea is to put a floor under people’s livelihoods by guaranteeing affordable livelihoods in such a way that, over time, you change the ways they are provisioned. But you also put a ceiling on extreme forms of wealth and corporate power. This means of course taxing the ultrarich, antitrust laws that are willing to break up large corporations, but also public options and ownership. Because when you create public opinions, you create public power that can act as a counterpoint. Now, if you look at this from a macroeconomic perspective, then what I am proposing also is a regime for “inflation preparedness,” because when shocks hit, you would not let the prices of energy and food commodities shoot up and create these knock-on effects across the economy, and then react with interest rate hikes that not only throw people out of work and treat their livelihoods as collateral damage but also make the investments that are needed to change the structures of your economy more expensive and out of reach. Inflation preparedness, as I’ve described it, de-emphasizes the blunt tool of monetary austerity. This means that you are freeing up space for things like a full employment policy, where if you invest in these essential areas, you would also be creating useful and decent jobs. And by having this form of public job creation, you would actually also again create public competition to private employers by offering the kind of labor conditions that are necessary for a dignified life. The right to a good and meaningful job becomes a real possibility. So, in that sense, if you put all of this together, it actually becomes a coherent program, also macroeconomically speaking. And then in terms of the international dimension, what I’m arguing is that affordability is also a peace question. We see this very clearly with the Iran war. Working toward peace is actually working toward sustained affordability. Now, in terms of the international trade, what I’m arguing is to move beyond the dichotomy between nationalist protectionism and free-trade liberalism, and to have forms of managed trade that are organized in the interest of commodity-producing nations in the Global South, which are extremely vulnerable to commodity price swings, but also in the interest of working people around the world. The Green Party Will Regain Momentum if It Keeps Its Nerve

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Daniel Finn ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-21T13:56:39+00:00

On October 8, the Green Party fell short in a parliamentary by-election for the London constituency of Holborn and St Pancras that had been triggered by the resignation of Keir Starmer. The by-election was always going to be a long shot for the Green leader Zack Polanski, who stood as the party’s candidate. The result, which saw Labour retain the seat, was broadly what you would have expected from the current state of national opinion polls. That hasn’t stopped much of the British media from proclaiming the outcome to have been a game-changing disaster for the Greens and Polanski in particular. Some prominent Green politicians have also launched a push to reverse the party’s left turn under Polanski and guide it back into their political comfort zone. A motion that Green members passed at their recent party conference about Israel and Zionism has become a rallying point for Polanski’s critics. If the advocates of a more conventional and unthreatening approach to politics win out, the gains that the Greens have made over the last year will prove to be ephemeral. If the party can hold its collective nerve, there will be new opportunities to win support as the contradictions of Andy Burnham’s political platform reveal themselves in practice. From Starmer to Burnham Zack Polanski became the leader of the English and Welsh Greens in August 2025 with resounding support from party members for his “eco-populist” agenda. At the time, Polanski’s leadership campaign was one of two projects that sought to capitalize on disillusionment with the Labour Party. The new Green leader declared his willingness to cooperate with the new left-wing party that Jeremy Corbyn and his allies were planning to launch in the next few months. In the best-case scenario for the British left, the parallel projects would have proved to be complementary. But there was a huge gap between the objective potential of the situation and the subjective readiness of Your Party’s founding team to exploit it. A faction fight between rival camps grouped around Corbyn and Zarah Sultana dominated the first months of the new party’s existence, while the enthusiasm of the previous summer gradually drained away. During a period of unprecedented volatility in British politics, Your Party proved to be much less than the sum of its parts. While all of this was happening, Polanski and his allies pressed ahead with their own project, and they made some very significant political gains at Labour’s expense during the first half of this year. In February, Hannah Spencer delivered the first ever Green by-election victory in the Manchester constituency of Gorton and Denton after a three-way contest with Labour and Reform, the hard-right party led by Nigel Farage. Labour sources explicitly stated that they would have preferred Reform to win the seat, because Spencer’s breakthrough showed that progressive voters no longer felt obliged to support Labour. Popular anger at the genocide in Gaza and Starmer’s endorsement of Israeli war crimes was an important factor behind disillusionment with Labour. Polanski and the Greens explicitly charged Israel with genocide and strongly opposed the ban on Palestine Action that resulted in hundreds of peaceful protesters being arrested and charged with terrorism. The fact that Polanski himself was Jewish merely added to the fury of his critics, who had grown accustomed to branding the Palestine solidarity movement as a putrid cesspool of antisemitism. The local elections in May represented another big step forward for Polanski’s party, which now overtook Labour in terms of projected vote share . For every English vote that Labour lost to Reform, it lost nearly four to the Greens. The Greens in Scotland have a separate organization with its own leadership team, but Polanski joined them on the campaign trail before they achieved their highest ever vote for the Scottish Parliament. The election results prompted a dramatic change of approach from the Labour Party, at least in terms of style and presentation. Under Starmer’s leadership, Labour treated left-wing voters with undisguised contempt and sought to compete with Reform on its chosen political ground. After Starmer’s meltdown, Labour turned to the Manchester mayor, Andy Burnham, as his replacement. Burnham was the most high-profile Labour politician who was not tainted by association with the Starmer project. He brought with him a new kind of rhetoric that contrasted sharply with the familiar tropes of Starmerism. Starmer had praised Margaret Thatcher for “setting loose our natural entrepreneurialism.” Burnham now declared his belief that Britain had spent “forty years on the wrong path” since Thatcher’s neoliberal Great Leap Forward. He promised that his government would be a “circuit breaker,” working to establish “a new political model and a new economic model.” There was also a clear discursive shift in relation to Palestine, with Burnham’s Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband taking the lead. His comments at this year’s Labour conference reflected the pressure of the solidarity movement, including its impact on the field of electoral politics: We call out ethnic cleansing by the settler terrorists in the West Bank. We call out the evidence of war crimes in Gaza. We call out Israel’s illegal occupation of Palestine. And we match words with actions. We will ban the import of goods from illegal settlements. We will disrupt the supply lines of British finance, British construction, and British services for illegal settlements. Surrendering on Day One If we look beyond the rhetoric, the substance of Burnham’s new regime doesn’t measure up to his sales pitch. When he announced his first cabinet, he reappointed ministers like Pat McFadden, an influential figure who wants to roll back what is left of Britain’s welfare state and shares Tony Blair’s conviction that Labour should abandon its goal of phasing out fossil fuels by 2050. John McTernan, an unapologetic Blairite who nonetheless seems to recognize that Britain has changed radically since 1997, urged Burnham to defy a critical chorus by nominating Ed Miliband as his chancellor: “The journey of ending neoliberalism starts with one appointment.” For McTernan, shirking this “easy choice” would mean “surrendering, on day one, to economic orthodoxy and conceding to those who oppose your fundamental agenda.” Burnham disregarded his advice and surrendered to orthodoxy by selecting John Healey instead. There is also a big gap between language and policy when it comes to Israel. Miliband has ruled out applying any sanctions to Israel as a whole and condemned the Boycott, Divestment, and Sanctions movement. But the “illegal settlements” are in no way external to the Israeli state. The overwhelming consensus of the Israeli political class defines those settlements as an integral part of Israel itself. Moreover, the war crimes Miliband correctly identifies in Gaza were obviously not the work of “settler terrorists.” It was the regular armed forces that turned Gaza into a charnel house. Since Miliband delivered his conference speech, the British government has capitulated to Israel over the order to evacuate the British consulate in East Jerusalem. According to the reporting of Peter Oborne, this act of surrender was a victory for Burnham’s national security adviser, Jonathan Powell, who successfully imposed his line on the foreign secretary. The selection of ministers and advisers who are deeply committed to the economic status quo and Britain’s traditional role in world affairs gives us a much better sense of what Burnham’s project is going to deliver than his speechmaking. But we have to recognize that most voters won’t be paying close attention to the details of his appointments. For now, his language about “the break we are making with the flawed thinking of the last forty years” clearly does appeal to a section of Labour’s target electorate. Even if Burnham was deadly serious about his stated goals, it would not be possible to reverse four decades of economic policy in the space of a few months. Many people will be inclined to wait and see if he can deliver on his promises, especially when Labour still seems to offer the best chance of blocking a government led by Reform. In the month of the Gorton and Denton by-election, the average polling gap between Labour and the Greens was less than 5 percent. By September, it was almost 15 percent. In itself, this is enough to explain the outcome in Holborn and St Pancras. If Polanski had run for the constituency when Starmer was still the Labour leader, he would probably have won — but in that scenario, of course, there never would have been a by-election in the first place. To some extent, Polanski and the Greens have been victims of their own success. By forcing Labour to hold out the promise of a genuinely transformative agenda, they have inadvertently reduced the political space available to them, at least temporarily. Under these circumstances, the best course of action would be to remain patient, avoid panicking, and look for opportunities to expose the gap between Burnham’s rhetoric and the substance of his policies. If it were up to Polanski alone, or the members who voted for him, this would surely have been the main line of argument from the Greens as they sought to move on from the by-election defeat. But the result in Holborn and St Pancras supplied an opening for Green politicians who never wanted the party to challenge Labour from the left in the first place. Eco-centrism It would be a mistake to speak about the right wing of the Green Party as if it were merely a smaller version of Labour’s dominant faction, since the two organizations have very different relationships with the British state and the capitalist class. As things stand, there is no Green counterpart of Peter Mandelson, who was passing on information about the Labour government’s economic plans to his friend Jeffrey Epstein while serving as a cabinet minister. Nor is there a Green version of Tom Watson, Labour’s deputy leader under Corbyn, who has recently taken up a job as a lobbyist for Palantir. At the same time, there are clear ideological differences within the Green Party that were on display during the 2025 leadership campaign. Polanski defeated a joint ticket comprising two of the Green MPs elected the previous year, Adrian Ramsay and Ellie Chowns. A New Statesman profile described Ramsay and Chowns, without any hint of malice, as “eco-centrists.” Ramsay claimed that Polanski’s approach would alienate more potential supporters than it gained, while Chowns insisted that the Greens were “way past those kinds of old ideas of politics being binary, left and right.” There is no reason to think that the experience of the last year has prompted them to rethink their basic political outlook. When Ramsay spoke recently about the need for his party to establish “a broad base of support,” it was more than a platitude. He would like the Greens to position themselves as an ecological version of the Liberal Democrats instead of competing aggressively with Labour for urban left-wing voters. This is a recipe for being all things to all people and nothing much to anyone in particular. It is important to note here that Ramsay and Chowns are Westminster MPs, while Polanski and the two Green deputy leaders elected alongside him are not (Polanski is a member of the London Assembly; Mothin Ali and Rachel Millward are local councilors). No matter how democratic a party constitution may be, elected representatives still occupy the key positions of influence, with MPs at the top of the hierarchy. There was a spectacular increase in party membership after Polanski took the reins, from 66,000 in September 2025 to 215,000 by the following spring. However, as the examples of Labour and the Scottish National Party from the last decade show, an influx of new members will not change a party unless there is a wider transformation of its program and personnel. Nice to Be Nice Caroline Lucas, who was the first and only Green MP between 2010 and 2024, endorsed the unsuccessful leadership ticket of Ramsay and Chowns. The Green motion does not say that Palestinians should or must take up arms against the Israeli occupation forces — it merely defends their right to do so if they consider it to be the wisest course of action. Green politicians such as Caroline Lucas objected to defining Zionism as a racist ideology, but their arguments were unconvincing and inconsistent. One day in 2024, Justin Laudadio found his cat, Chicken, writhing on the floor of his home in Feasterville, Pennsylvania, apparently in the midst of a seizure. Though he was diagnosed with acromegaly, a hormonal disorder caused by a benign pituitary gland tumor, it never became entirely clear what caused the seizure. Justin and Airy Laudadio’s cat Chicken. Veterinary care in the United States has grown much more advanced in recent years; Chicken, for example, has his own cardiologist. It’s also become much more expensive, partly because of the costs of more sophisticated medicine. At the same time, vet bills have climbed as the industry has become increasingly consolidated, with more clinics and emergency hospitals now owned by corporate groups and private equity firms. The consequences of these rising costs vary widely depending on pet owners’ ability to pay. Some can afford human-level care for their pets. Others may be forced to take on debt, forgo treatment or even surrender or euthanize their animals. “The biggest struggle in access to care is certainly the cost of it,” said Donald Consla, a veterinarian at Animal Friends, a Pittsburgh animal shelter with a low-cost veterinary clinic. Since 2017, veterinary prices have increased about 67%, much faster than overall consumer prices, which rose about 38% during the same time period, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. “Veterinary medicine has had a big transformation in the last decades,” said Jennifer Quammen, president of the American Veterinary Medical Association. Pet owners now have access to technologies such as MRI and ultrasound that “at one point [were] found in human hospitals only,” she added. “That equipment certainly comes with a cost.” Higher operating expenses, pharmaceutical prices and general inflation are also contributing to bigger bills, Quammen said. Pet owners may be responding: Vet visits have declined over the past year even as industry revenues increased, according to the veterinary association. “We are seeing people delay routine care, preventive care,” Quammen said. The low-cost Pittsburgh clinic has seen greater demand for its services in recent years, Consla said, as more owners sought out affordable vaccinations and wellness exams. While it’s hard to compare the business model of a low-cost clinic with that of a typical private practice, Consla said, he has seen some extraordinary prices quoted by other veterinarians. “Some of the estimates people come in with are truly wild,” he said. National Veterinary Associates, owned by the investment firm JAB Holding Co., has more than 1,000 veterinary hospitals in the United States and Canada. During a high-level visit to Tehran earlier this week, Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov expressed his country’s official position demanding a restoration of the pre-war situation in the Persian Gulf region and guarantees that there will be no return to war against Iran in the future. Lavrov also claimed that his country was ready to assist in “searching for mutually acceptable solutions to resolve” the “artificial” crisis created by “unprovoked US-Israeli aggression” against Iran. Later on Wednesday, Russian President Vladimir Putin repeated the offer of mediation during the visit of Steve Witkoff, the White House’s special envoy to the Middle East, to Moscow. Lavrov, who visited Iran on Monday, October 18, also reiterated the Russian position that Iran’s responses to US aggression was justified and legitimate and claimed his country has provided whatever political and economic assistance it could to help it face the repercussions of the unlawful US-Israeli war. Lavrov’s statement could be seen as a rebuttal to US President Donald Trump, who appreciated Russia’s supposed neutral role in Iran during the eight-month-long war. Trump claimed it was a “positive” development that Russia did nothing to help and was even “laying off Iran” as the US is hitting Iranians economically and militarily. Trump was forced to withdraw some of its sanctions on Russian energy exports, imposed from 2024, in his bid to secure an energy deal earlier this month. Trump’s move is largely seen as an attempt to lower the impact of the rising gas prices on the prospects of Republican candidates before the upcoming midterm elections. Lavrov, however, cited the Russian veto in the UN against the US attempts to justify its military aggression against Iran, providing humanitarian aid including medicine, other medical supplies and food during the war also claiming that such aid is continued to be “delivered regularly.” Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian also appreciated the Russian help during the war and expressed his country’s desire to further deepen cooperation between the two countries. Iran and Russia signed and implemented a 20-year Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Treaty last year. The treaty has provisions of close coordination on issues related to economy, geo-politics, and security. Lavrov pointed out the growing bilateral trade between the two countries in the last two years, despite the war and sanctions they are both facing. According to him, in the first eight months of the present year, the bilateral trade between Russia and Iran grew 10% in comparison to the same period last year. Lavrov also informed that Russian scientists who were withdrawn from Iran’s Bushehr nuclear power plant during the war have started returning gradually. Russia and Iran are closer than ever Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi claimed that bilateral relations and cooperation between Russia and Iran has never been as good as it was today. While addressing a joint press conference with Lavrov in Tehran on Monday, Araghchi reiterated that both the countries maintain close cooperation and consultation on all regional issues, including Iranian relations with the US. He claimed that the two countries agree that the instability and disruption in the Persian Gulf and the Strait of Hormuz is a result of unprovoked US-Israeli aggression and that they share the vision of a diplomatic solution to all outstanding conflicts. Talking about their mutual understanding about regional issues, such as Lebanon and Palestine, Araghchi claimed that both Russia and Iran agree the “genocide of the Palestinian people and the Israeli regime’s dangerous expansionism in the region must stop.” Lavrov alleged that some countries are trying to push the Palestinian issue to the background, which should not be allowed. He stated that both Iran and Russia would push for the implementation of all relevant UN resolutions because it is “crucial to intensify efforts to resolve the Palestinian issue.” Lavrov also demanded an end to all Israeli hostilities in Lebanon and peace in Yemen, demanding the full implementation of all relevant UN resolutions and previous understandings. Lavrov rejected US threats of secondary sanctions over its relationship with Iran, claiming “despite the challenging situation … Russia has no intention to abandon the implementation of economic and investment agreements with Iran.” As a part of its ongoing war, the Trump administration had announced the complete economic isolation of Iran in August, threatening secondary sanctions against all the countries who still maintained economic relations with it. Only a handful of countries, such as the UAE, have announced compliance with the US attempts to economically isolate Iran so far.

On the opening day of its National Smallholder Farmers’ Conference and 31st Annual General Meeting (AGM), held from September 29 to October 1, MVIWATA, Tanzania’s national network of smallholder farmers’ groups, set aside time during a program on land, seed, and markets to state solidarity with the peoples of Palestine and Cuba, with the ambassadors of both countries present in the hall. “We, the members of MVIWATA, who have convened in Morogoro for our 31st Annual General Meeting, wish to give a brief statement in support of the people of Palestine and the Cuban people,” read Theodora Pius, MVIWATA’s head of program, opening the statement. The organization said it was “extremely angered by the ongoing genocide of the Palestinian people” and “frustrated to see an open silence on war crimes that happen every day against the people of Palestine by the State of Israel and its allies.” It said its solidarity was with all of Palestine, “not only in the Gaza Strip.” The statement then turned to Cuba, denouncing more than 60 years of US-imposed blockade, it said, imposed because Cubans exercised their freedom to choose their own political system, and went on to articulate what the blockade now means: “no electricity, no fuel, no medicines, no food.” Despite this, the statement said that the Cuban people have continued to send their expertise (doctors above all) to support life in other countries. The Cuban Ambassador, Yordenis Despaigne Vera, addresses delegates attending the National Smallholder Farmers’ Conference and the 31st Annual General Meeting of MVIWATA. The Cuban Ambassador, Yordenis Despaigne Vera , thanked the smallholder farmers and described the current stage of the blockade as an energy war. United Nations agencies reinforce this characterization. By May, the Cuban people were living with daily blackouts of up to twenty hours , and more than 100,000 patients, including 11,000 children, were waiting for operations delayed by power cuts and shortages. Cuba’s own health ministry recorded infant mortality rising from 7.1 to 9.9 deaths per thousand live births between 2024 and 2025. The ambassador called the United States program “a silent genocide” and said Tanzania’s government and people had stood with Cuba throughout. A week earlier, at the UN General Assembly, Tanzania’s Vice-President had reaffirmed the country’s “unwavering solidarity with Cuba” against unilateral sanctions. Last October, the Assembly voted 165 to 7 , with 12 abstentions, to demand an end to the blockade; Tanzania voted in favor. The Palestinian ambassador, Salam Abu Sharar , addressed the room of farmers. Land, he said, is close to the hearts of the Palestinian people and of MVIWATA alike, and the question of Palestine “is not about religion; it is about land.” In a hall where the first working session opened with the words “land is life, land is not a commodity,” he spoke the delegates’ own language: the struggle of Tanzania’s peasants and the national liberation of Palestine are one struggle. The Palestinian ambassador to Tanzania, Salam Abu Sharar with MVIWATA Chairperson Apollo Chamwela. Photo: MVIWATA MVIWATA has been here before. Its AGM in Njombe in December 2023 condemned Israel’s genocide in Palestine. In July 2025, it hosted the youth of La Via Campesina in Morogoro, whose declaration ended with “Free Palestine!” Within days of that meeting’s close, Israeli forces bulldozed the seed multiplication unit of the Union of Agricultural Work Committees, the Palestinian farmers’ organization in Hebron, destroying more than seventy local varieties. The same year, MVIWATA and the International Association for Popular Cooperation ran an Africa Bioinputs School in Tanzania, with participants from thirteen countries, including Palestine, learning how farmers make their own fertilizers and pest controls. On the conference’s final day, the Tanzanian farmers resolved to build their own bank of indigenous seed. In Hebron and in Morogoro, the seed is where the struggle over who feeds whom begins. MVIWATA Chairperson Apollo Chamwela told the ambassadors that the organization stood ready to do for Cuba what it had tried to do for Palestine: collect food if mechanisms for transporting it can be arranged. MVIWATA is a membership organization of smallholder farmers, founded in 1993 and organized across mainland Tanzania and Zanzibar. It was a meeting with a government minister as its guest of honor. Its statement on Palestine and Cuba came in the same breath as its demands for land, seed, and dignified prices for its members’ produce at home. For these farmers, sovereignty over food and sovereignty over territory are one question, whether the territory is a village in Kilosa or a hillside in Hebron. Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaaf (PTI), the main opposition party in the country demanded, on Monday, October 29, the whereabouts of several of its leaders, including the Chief Minister of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP), Sohail Afridi, kept in “preventive detention” by the state. The PTI has claimed that Afridi, Speaker of the Provincial Assembly Babar Saleem Swati, and at least six other members of the KP provincial assembly were “missing” since the dismissal of the elected government in the province on October 11 . Afridi and other PTI legislatures were arrested on October 11, hours after Pakistan’s federal government imposed a state of emergency and governor’s rule in the province. Though the Pakistan government has maintained that the concerned leaders are all in preventive detention, it has neither produced them in court, disclosed their location, or allowed anyone to meet them. The police has, meanwhile, filed fresh cases of violence and arson against Afridi and other PTI leaders, accusing them of being involved in May 2023 protests against the attempted arrest of the party’s leader and former Prime Minister Imran Khan. Calling the arrest of its leaders an unprecedented and unconstitutional crisis, the PTI has filed several petitions in the courts for their release. On Monday it also demanded an urgent discussion on the matter in the National Assembly. “The reported detention of elected representatives holding constitutional and parliamentary offices raises serious concerns regarding the functioning of democratic institutions, the representation of the people, and the protection of the rights and privileges of the elected representatives,” the PTI notice for the discussion in the NA says. Act of political vendetta The Afridi government in the state was dismissed by the federal government, led by Prime Minister Shehbaz Sherif, on October 11, over allegations of failing to maintain law and order and prevent terrorism. However, the dismissal is linked to Afridi leading a “long march” of PTI supporters to the capital, Islamabad. The protesters were demanding the release of Khan, who has been incarcerated since August 2023 on various charges, including the misuse of power, corruption, and violence. The PTI claims all the charges against Khan are fabricated and politically motivated and were part of the plan of the ruling parties and the armed forces to keep him out of national politics. Afridi’s dismissal and arrest led to popular protests in different parts of KP in the last week and security forces fortifying the provincial capital Peshawar. The PTI has also alleged that its workers and offices have been attacked since the imposition of Governor’s rule. At least five people were injured when security forces clashed with the protesters in Bara last week. Hundreds of others were arrested from different parts of the province for attempting to protest the dismissal of the only government led by the opposition party. The PTI has also filed a petition in the Peshawar High Court against the federal government’s move to dismiss the state government in KP, calling it a violation of the popular right to choose their government and other constitutional provisions. Its leaders reiterated their allegations about the Sharif government, calling it illegitimate and a stooge of the armed forces. They claimed that the KP government was the “only elected” government in the country and its dismissal was part of a political vendetta. The PTI, along with other groups in the country have repeatedly alleged that the 2024 national elections were rigged to prevent Imran Khan from coming back to power.

Cereal production rises to a historic high: Burkina Faso’s pursuit of food sovereignty

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Pavan Kulkarni ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-20T20:26:32+00:00

Cereal production in Burkina Faso has risen 53% to a record high under the government of Capt. Ibrahim Traoré, who has made food self-sufficiency an urgent priority since coming to power. ​ The country has produced 124% of the national cereal needs, the agricultural minister, Commander Ismaël Sombié, said earlier this month at the 3rd edition of the National Days of Patriotic Engagement and Citizen Participation (JEPPC). Paddy rice production for the first time exceeded a million tons in 2025, while Maize production reached well over 2,68 million tons, he added in his address to a panel titled “From field to market: producing and consuming Burkinabe to strengthen our sovereignty.” Including all other cereals, the total production of over seven million tons is a historic high. It was only 4.66 million tons in 2021-22 . It was a tumultuous period. Amid mass protests calling for the expulsion of the troops of Burkina Faso’s former colonizer, France, the regime of President Roch Marc Kaboré, widely perceived in the country as a French puppet, was ousted in a coup. However, the new military government replacing this regime, led by Lieutenant-Colonel Paul-Henri Sandaogo, dragged its feet on meeting the protesters’ demand for the expulsion of French troops. In this backdrop, another coup followed that September, bringing Captain Ibrahim Traoré to power. In less than half a year after taking power, he expelled them, asserting Burkina Faso’s sovereignty. However, his government, backed by the anti-France protest movement, trade unions, and progressive sections of the civil society, has repeatedly insisted that sovereignty cannot be consolidated without reducing the country’s dependence on imports for basic food needs. With domestic agro-pastoral potential undeveloped, Burkina Faso was spending about 148.5 billion CFA francs on rice and wheat imports in 2022, while almost a fifth of the country’s population remained undernourished . 2023-2025 Agro-Pastoral and Fisheries Offensive Soon after swearing in as transitional president that October, Traoré launched the Presidential Initiative for Agricultural Production (2023-2024), which later became part of the larger, more ambitious 2023-2025 Agro-Pastoral and Fisheries Offensive (OAPH), launched in September 2023. Spending 273.99 billion CFA francs on this initiative, the state developed 11,774 hectares of irrigated areas and 41,275 hectares of lowland areas, making them available to farmers.​ It invested in water management, installing 485 high-yield boreholes. It increased mechanization, including by deploying 1,261 tractors. And through this initiative, the state supplied inputs, including 160,560 tons of mineral fertilizer, to farmers and created 418 new storage facilities. A concrete impact on consumer plate​s The 1,008,737 tons of rice produced in 2025 is 101% of the target set under the OAPH. For maize, the 2,686,531 tons produced amounted to 134% of the target achieved. Presenting these figures to the Council of Ministers on July 31, Amos Kiénou, OAPH’s executive secretary, said that these gains have had a “concrete impact” on the consumer plate.​ A bag of maize, for example, “used to cost between 35,000 and 40,000 CFA francs” during lean seasons before this campaign by Traoré’s government, he said. “Today… a bag does not exceed 20,000 CFA francs,” even as the government has set minimum prices of several crops to ensure that farmers are not forced to sell at a loss.​ Beyond these carbohydrate sources, the government has also prioritized greater protein availability for the population. Against OAPH’s poultry target of 18,000,000 chickens, 18,262,480 were counted. Fish production rose from 30,574 tons to 58,476 tons during this offensive, although it remained below the target. OAPH also produced 51,818 tonnes of potatoes and renewed 3,170 hectares of mango orchards.​ This campaign aimed at achieving food self-sufficiency has generated 91,027 jobs, including 26,202 permanent positions. Women have secured over a third of them. 4,724 Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs) have also found work. During this OAPH, agricultural GDP rose by an average of over 12% annually, while household incomes grew by 4.3%. Agropastoral, Hydraulic and Fisheries Offensive 2026-2028 With this success to show, the government is set to expand the campaign, launching an even more ambitious Agropastoral, Hydraulic and Fisheries Offensive 2026-2028 in late July this year. The state funding of 839.99 billion CFA francs is expected to finance this new offensive, which is to develop another 50,000 hectares of irrigated areas and 100,000 hectares of lowlands.​ Plantain, sunflower, pineapple, rabbit, oil palm, and cocoa are included as the six emerging sectors in the 2026-2028 offensive. The gains made in maize, potato, mango, livestock-meat, fish and poultry are to be consolidated in this new phase, while rice, milk, soybeans, sesame, peanuts, wheat, cassava, and shea fall under priority sectors. The plan also targets the building of 418 more storage facilities , including 11 cold rooms and 63 processing units. ​The government’s aim is to develop domestic capacity across the entire agricultural value chain – from cultivation to processing to final consumption – to consolidate Burkina Faso’s sovereignty, said agriculture minister Sombié in his remarks on October 7.
