Toward Simp Liberation?

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Evelina Johansson Wilén ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-20T19:11:34+00:00

In late July, New York magazine’s Cut published Ezra Marcus’s “ Revenge of the Simps ,” the story of Nikita Tyukalo, a twenty-one-year-old Andrew Tate acolyte who built a webcam sex-trafficking operation out of luxury rentals in the Seattle suburbs. Astonishingly, Tyukalo was taken down by a community of self-described “simps,” obsessive online fans of the young women he exploited who noticed something was amiss while watching hour after hour of their streams. Tyukalo modeled his enterprise directly on Tate’s “Pimping Hoes Degree” course, which teaches men how to groom women into online sex work and confiscate their earnings. The men who worshipped his models from the other side of the screen, meanwhile, formed an amateur investigative unit called the White Knights, compiled evidence of abuse, and fed it to police until a SWAT team came through Tyukalo’s door in June. Jacobin editor Meagan Day spoke with Evelina Johansson Wilén, a regular Jacobin contributor and socialist feminist scholar from Sweden, about the article and the real-life events it depicts. In addition to writing on dating , romance , fertility , family , childcare , and women’s labor , Wilén has conducted research on incel communities and the sugar-dating industry. Day, meanwhile, has contributed articles about the Donald Trump–era gender wars , the manosphere , and offshoots like looksmaxxing . Day and Wilén discuss the many questions raised by the Cut story: the entrepreneurial core of the Tate phenomenon, the brutal hierarchy that red-pill ideology imposes on men as well as women, and the problems intrasexual status competition poses to the resurgent project of male domination. Meagan Day This story caught our attention at Jacobin partly because many of us didn’t realize that Andrew Tate isn’t just an ideological figure — he literally provides his followers with a blueprint for running a pimping operation. That’s alarming enough on its own, particularly given how massive his global audience is among young men in particular. But then to learn that twenty-one-year-olds are successfully implementing this blueprint to exploit girls their own age and younger is beyond shocking. So it bears some discussion. Can you give a quick explanation of the Tate phenomenon? Why is this guy so popular? Evelina Johansson Wilén The focus with Tate has mostly been on his sexist language, not on Tate as an entrepreneur and mentor to other aspiring entrepreneurs. I think that’s a mistake, because people aren’t drawn to him only because they agree with his ideas about women. They’re drawn to him because he embodies a possibility of success, of rising and becoming something else. If you look at Tate’s movement not only as an ideology of misogyny but also of male self-improvement, it makes much more sense. Social mobility has stalled; wages are stagnant. The sentiments of meaninglessness and blocked mobility that Tate speaks to are real. The ideological move is to redirect those sentiments away from an analysis of social relations, and toward hating women and hating weakness in general. Tate even has, for example, a truly intense hatred of people who are gluten intolerant. And this is where his worldview simply reinforces capitalist ideology. You are self-made; you can never blame anyone or anything for your lack of success. Likewise, your gluten intolerance is just a question of attitude. You’re passive, you don’t really want it enough, and this is why you suffer. But what’s interesting about this article is that it suggests fractures in the picture. We tend to talk about the manosphere as one totality — the incels, the looksmaxxers, Andrew Tate, all part of the same awful phenomenon. But I think Tate’s intense hatred of weakness actually creates a real difficulty for men like the simps and incels, who are in many respects self-defined by their weakness or suffering or unmet desire. Meagan Day Tateism taps into a fear among young men that the world is strictly bifurcated into male winners and male losers. You see this among incels too — they believe it just as much as Tate does, the only difference being that they identify with the losers. Tate is not for incels; incels have essentially given up on joining the ranks of the Übermenschen . Tate is for the strivers, the men who believe that if they keep grinding, they can ascend. And the only way to ascend in this vision, or to validate yourself as a “high-value male,” is to establish an undeniable hierarchy over other people, to dominate and exploit them. Tate is living out the theory of male dominance, and the way that looks in practice is utterly sociopathic. Tate himself is probably a born sociopath. His most adept imitators, like Tyukalo, probably are too. But most of the young men interested in Tate’s ideas are not. They’re actively attempting to cultivate sociopathy in themselves, because they’re afraid that if they don’t, they’ll end up the losers of the intrasexual competition game. What’s fueling all of it is a mass panic that there is such a thing as an irredeemable loser guy, and each man’s private terror that it might be himself. Money is a major validator of male status, and so is control over women. Tate offers a blueprint for acquiring both. But ultimately those are means to an end. The linchpin of the whole thing is a cutthroat, zero-sum view of male worth. Evelina Johansson Wilén I agree, and it’s clearly a men’s game. The women in it have no real role except as tools. But here’s what’s interesting: within this game, Tate and his followers effectively tell the simps, “You are the real losers.” And when he pushes them down that deeply, it becomes impossible for them to relate to him. There’s a power struggle going on that breaks down any idea of a male collective. The competition is supposed to remain a competition among men, with women serving as simple currency. But push some men low enough and the whole structure stops working on them. In the case of the White Knights, they started identifying with the women instead. Meagan Day This article is the story of men who have essentially fallen into the loser male category — they literally spend all day chatting on a forum called SimpCity about their favorite porn streamers — and found a kind of redemption by disaffiliating from male exploitation. Sure, they’re mobilizing another male archetype familiar to patriarchy, the benevolent protector. But the article makes it clear they started to see these women as subjects with interior lives, began to genuinely worry about them, and were moved to intervene in their abuse. That’s what’s conjured in the article’s title, “Revenge of the Simps.” Tateism is built like a pyramid, with an apex-predator male at the top. That may work for the strivers who hope to climb the pyramid. But for the other men — the simps, the incels, the broke guys — it’s humiliating, and maybe the thrill of misogyny is not enough to buy them off. Evelina Johansson Wilén This is why I found the piece so interesting. When I was lurking on the incel forums about two years ago, the way they talked about Chads [superior, “alpha” males] was: Chads are, in a sense, stupid. They can’t see the world as it really is, but they’re winning anyway. The incels saw themselves as smarter, wiser, closer to the truth. That was their way of coping with failure. And that actually puts them in conflict with guys like Tate, who sees himself as a Chad. But in the forums I studied, the hatred was still directed almost entirely at women, and the solidarity was directed at other men in the same situation. What’s happening in this story is different, which makes me think something may be evolving. When I followed the incel community, there were constant internal debates. For instance, once they developed the idea of male victimhood, they started making distinctions: if you have mental health issues, if you’re black, if you’re poor, then your victimhood is greater. And others would push back: “We’re supposed to be a collective, and you’re wrecking it by ranking who’s most vulnerable.” My point is that these forums aren’t just people repeating an ideology at each other. There are real discussions going on, real evolutions taking place, and we need to acknowledge that even while maintaining that the main ideas they work from are awful. This is the first time I’ve seen a group connected to this incel-adjacent environment really fighting for the women against the Chads. That’s a notable development. Meagan Day The simps were already interested in cracking the case and protecting these women from the beginning, but their zeal was really inflamed by Tyukalo coming into their chat and denigrating them, calling them losers. He kept calling them the N-word, oddly, which I think was meant to communicate that they occupy the lowest rung of society and thus had no standing to criticize him. He mocked them, and it increased their appetite for revenge. And the form their revenge took was liberating these girls from his trafficking operation. Instead of mass paranoia about being a loser male — and hatred of women in particular for failing to rescue men from that status by sleeping with them and marrying them — maybe some of these men could get fed up and start to turn on the structures of male domination themselves, structures that are demeaning and humiliating to them personally as well as to women. I’m sure I sound too optimistic, but it’s at least a useful thought exercise to map the weak points in the ascendant misogynist ideology. Evelina Johansson Wilén There’s one moment in the article where one of the simps reflects on exactly this. He says, essentially: this is all part of a bigger structural phenomenon, and how have we been part of creating it? It was a beautiful moment. Meagan Day The simp speaks! He basically says that masculinity and femininity are in profound crisis, and that being a simp is the outcome of those crises for him, and simphood puts him face to face with the struggle not to become what he despises. It’s genuinely moving. Evelina Johansson Wilén As you said, maybe we’re being too positive. But in one sense it would be surprising if this didn’t happen. In Tate’s world, not everyone can ascend. Contrast that with Jordan Peterson: whatever else you say about his ideology, there’s nothing in it that structurally requires most men to fail. With Tate, it’s total. Failure is not only assumed but required for most men. Meagan Day For there to be dominant men, there must be loser men. Tateism is not a politics of male solidarity. It relies on the continuation of mortal intrasexual combat. And the question becomes: How long can the men at the bottom be bought off with the promise that women, at least, are inferior to them? That’s the only deal on offer, and it may not be tolerable forever. Plus, men like Tate and Tyukalo may be the women’s pimps, but they’re also the simps’ drug dealers. These men are clearly addicted to porn, and their lives are no doubt suffering for it. It’s not just that the women are being exploited and the men are being humiliated; men’s porn addiction is being cultivated and mined for profit too. That should give them another incentive to break ranks. Many people will read this story as a sign of the apocalypse. We don’t want to paint too rosy a picture — there’s much to find disturbing. But what you and I are saying is that there’s a surprising little glimmer of hope in it. Evelina Johansson Wilén Maybe what we should be thinking about is not the revenge of the simps but the emancipation of the simps. Revenge keeps you inside the same frame of combative masculinity. Emancipation implies the beginning of something new. We don’t know if it will happen, but we can see how it might.

How China Picks Its Winners and Losers

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Daniel Cheng ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-20T17:09:49+00:00

For those who went through the American education system, China’s National College Entrance Exam or gaokao may seem like a simple Chinese equivalent of the SAT. But the gaokao is vastly more important than any standardized test in the United States. College admissions in the United States are holistically determined, involving a consideration of a wide range of variables of which standardized tests are only one. For the vast majority of students in China, gaokao performance is the main factor determining whether they will be admitted to university. This grueling test helps decide the fate of millions every year. In The Highest Exam , Ruixue Jia and Hongbin Li show how the gaokao’s influence goes far beyond the education system. Jia and Li, who themselves took the gaokao, define the exam as the final showdown in a “centralized hierarchical tournament.” Every year, the gaokao is taken by over ten million high school students and repeat candidates. The student’s score doesn’t really matter; what does matter is their position relative to other students within their own province. The logic of the system is inherently zero-sum. One’s success is predicated on the failure of others. The stakes of this zero-sum tournament are extraordinarily high. In the United States, the importance of one’s undergraduate degree begins to fade into the background as one accumulates work experience or acquires a postgraduate degree. In China, a gaokao-determined undergraduate education will follow you for life. Doors leading to the best careers are permanently slammed shut if your gaokao score is too low. As Jia and Li point out, “attending an elite college affects your economic bracket for the rest of your life.” An Education Affordability Crisis The high stakes and extreme competition of the gaokao means there’s enormous pressure on parents to invest in the educational arms race. While Americans often lament the exorbitant cost of higher education, The Highest Exam shows that Chinese households also face massive education costs, just at different points in the system. China’s elementary and middle schools are free, but generally high school is not. Students and their families have to pay for tuition, uniforms, and sometimes room and board, which makes up the bulk of education expenses. In addition, expensive tutoring is a de facto necessity for urban students. Entry into the best elementary schools is determined by school district residency, although it is not unknown for wealthy parents to pay a large one-off fee or leverage special connections get their kids into the best schools, even though China’s ministry of education has repeatedly ordered localities to eliminate fees associated with admission. Buying an apartment to meet residency requirements is ludicrously expensive, with home prices in Beijing’s best school districts higher than even Palo Alto. In one section of the Highest Exam , Ruixue and Li report instances of parents paying entrance fees reaching as high as $75,000, over fourteen times the annual median disposable income in China. Only parents at the top of the socioeconomic pyramid have the connections to get their kids into these schools. Even with these incredible barriers to elementary school admissions, there are still more applicants than there are spots, meaning students have to take an elementary school entrance exam. To prepare for the exam, parents start their children on the tutoring grind as early as age four. These accumulating costs mean that families spend massive sums on putting their kids through school. Households with students spend an average of 17 percent of their household earnings on education. This financial burden falls much harder on lower-income families desperate to move up the socioeconomic ladder. Despite spending far less in absolute terms on education than rich families, households in the bottom quartile, who “only” average $1,280 a year, spend 57 percent of their household earnings on education. Even if the gaokao itself is the same for all students, the resources that families can bring to bear are not. The intense pressure of China’s education system and its early starting line have created a student mental health crisis impacting students as young as seven. The K-Shaped Labor Market Seen from another perspective, the existence of the gaokao within a nominally communist country is quite ironic. It replicates capitalism’s zero-sum competition between workers for jobs within the education system. Securing a spot in one of China’s best colleges means kicking other students down the ranking ladder. This means that from a very early age, the gaokao instills a gladiatorial mindset into Chinese students. Instead of promoting the warmth of collectivism, China’s education system incentivizes cold individualism. But when compared to the US system, China’s model appears less cruel than it does at first glance. Although rich Chinese families spend more money on their children than their poor counterparts, students from families in the top wealth quintile are only 2.3 times more likely to go to an elite college than those from the bottom quintile. In the United States, the wealthy are eleven times more likely to send their kids to the most prestigious universities. For all of its faults, a univariate standardized testing system is relatively more meritocratic and allows for higher social mobility. But social mobility is not the be-all and end-all. In a society characterised by massive inequalities, meritocracy more often serves as a system for legitimizing these disparities rather than correcting them. If exams are seen to be fair, then it is much harder to take issue with the winners and losers. What The Highest Exam implicitly reveals is that the gaokao is symptomatic of a deeper problem in China: the brutally competitive and highly unequal labor market. China’s youth unemployment rate is almost 18 percent , more than double the United States’. Within the People’s Republic there is intense competition for the best jobs, with 150 to 250 résumés per job opening in the most desirable sectors. Given the K-shaped dynamic at the top and sluggish-at-the-bottom nature of the economy of many advanced capitalist countries, not attaining a high-skilled job can often mean being thrust into the low-wage, long-hour world of gig work . The cushy government jobs allocated to top students are so desirable because they provide housing, education, and health care benefits that few others can access. The gaokao’s ruthless competition “succeeds” in preparing Chinese students for life precisely because this competition matches the post-college winner-takes-all nature of the capitalist economy. Thus, the real solution to the gaokao’s problems isn’t education reform, but dealing with the extreme inequality for which the test is just a sorting mechanism. If China became a more equal society with a progressive tax structure that invested in universal social services, much of the gaokao’s stress would disappear, as the stakes of the tournament would be vastly lower. A poor test score wouldn’t translate into lower wages, bad educational opportunities, or a lack of health insurance. It would be much easier for families across the People’s Republic to slow down the race for educational arms proliferation. The perception of the gaokao’s meritocracy is in practice a barrier to a more egalitarian People’s Republic. China’s social institutions are notoriously corrupt and systematically favor the wealthy and well connected. As Yuen Yuen Ang has shown , bribing officials with “access money” is a key ingredient to business success. Because the gaokao is fairer than these institutions, the distribution of jobs and security that it produces is seen as justified. This has had effects on social attitudes in the country. Surveys show that most Chinese citizens believe that poverty is due to individual failings like laziness or lack of talent. Only 25 percent of survey respondents believed there should be redistribution from the rich to the poor. If the gaokao tournament is fair, then its losers are seen as deserving of their suffering. All of this means that the gaokao’s problems lie in the deeper ills of China’s political economy. The challenges posed by a highly bifurcated K-shaped economy cannot be easily solved by educational reform. Given China’s current economic trajectory, students and parents have little cause for optimism.

The Elisions of American Realism

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Rochester Red Star: News from Rochester DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-20T16:38:13+00:00

by Jean Allen A few weeks ago on Chapo Trap House (#1051), the hosts interviewed Tim from the podcast All Lawyers Are Bastards , and his conclusion was notable in how often I’ve heard permutations of it: “It’s very hard for me to envision right now without a really severe legislative reform, a Congress in the next 8 to 10 years that’s really capable of doing anything of note, right? And that’s one of the ways the Supreme Court’s able to kind of create this space for themselves is they just kick things over to Congress and say, ‘it’s Congress’ responsibility to do this, not us, right?’ And so that’s when they decide not to act. But that does mean that Congress is probably not going to be able to create new federal legislation that carves themselves out a unique role, right? And if they do that, they’re probably not going to be able to effectively craft legislation that actually curtails those rights. So I think working in local municipalities and states can be one area where I think you can see a little more change.” This is the logic that I have seen consistently throughout my life (that it is not realistic for any systemic change to happen), but it is rare to see it all laid out like this. My parents’ generation loved the bumper sticker slogan – “think global, act local.” Yes there are big global problems that we are increasingly connected to, but really, you have the most power locally. Like everything in our postmodern world, this is a strategy that is always new, always obvious, but it can never be said to have been tried and failed. By my sister’s apartment there is a dangerous intersection. After almost a decade of organizing, it finally has a stop sign. That is the “stuff” you get done at the local level if your goals are in any way opposed or even orthogonal to capital. I know people who have spent their whole political lives defending a few acres of wetlands, or trying to get speed bumps installed on one residential street, or trying to get the police of a village to be marginally more accountable. Meanwhile, Elon Musk has made 998 billion dollars, reactionary politics have been on the rise everywhere, hot wars are returning to global life, and the world seems destined to careen towards an unmanageable level of heating. “Act locally” has failed as a project. I do not mean to diminish this kind of work. “Act locally” is a truism for a reason. But I want to focus on the elision here. An elision is a linguistic term for the omission of a syllable in speech, like saying you’re ‘gonna’ instead of ‘going to’. There is an elision between ‘think global’ and ‘act local’ – don’t waste your time on any kind of big demands. We are totally, abjectly powerless at the systemic level. In Rochester a 75-25 vote in support of the Police Accountability Board in our 2019 referendum has been eaten, slowly, by the court system. A half decade earlier, the insurgent mayorship of Chokwe Lumumba in Jackson, MI, tried to raise local minimum wages, only to have that power stripped from him by the state legislature. Any success at any point within our system requires the acceptance of the whole rest of it, requires super-majorities and the base for sustained legal fights; because the government that we have been taught is meant to serve us , is actually tremendously stacked against the empowerment of the working class. That system has not served any of us in the whole of our political adulthoods. And just as ‘Capitalist Realism’ accepts that “it is easier to imagine an end to the world than an end to capitalism”, there is an American Realism which accepts, to a greater or lesser degree, the perfection/permanence of the US constitution, and restricts its political project to fit itself within the disciplinary grounds of that system. I said this in June’s newsletter reviewing a folk album: ‘domination has a blast radius’. Constantly elliding over our total powerlessness at the level of the system is bad for us. Watching 17 years of protest movements be responded to with total indifference from the government which ‘serves us’ is not just a rational problem – it’s something that fucks up our ability to feel empowered in the rest of our lives. That’s to say nothing for the twisted kind of person who really gets into this. There is a kind of attitude developed within our schools and ‘intelligencia’ which says: any call for systemic change, any focus on how unrepresentative our government is, can be seen as the product of a lack of civics education . This person has come to love our system, which gives so many opportunities to explain the suffering it doles out, because obviously anyone with an issue simply does not have enough education to know that their suffering is actually justifiable, is actually fine. There is a popular tendency on the left right now that holds that this is only a problem of will – that if we win hard enough, this all goes away. To that perspective this is all nonsense, since nobody cares about our system of government anyways. I get this perspective, but even while we fight for bread and butter demands, I think it’s important that we say the thing the rest of our political punditry, including many of our comrades, elide: “The government we have been taught is meant to serve us, actually disempowers working-class people, and that is wrong, and the world does not have to be this way.” This is argued in DSA’s new program , which says our enemy is “not just this or that cop, judge, or president, but the entire anti-democratic structure of our society. We are workers fighting to transform society: to win the battle for democracy, draft a new constitution, and create a democratic socialist republic.” I think this is important, because the first part of empowering ourselves is recognizing that we have been disempowered, and training ourselves to see the disempowerment of ourselves and our fellow workers as a moral outrage rather than something to be ideologically explained. Because at the end of the conversation the Chapos had with Tim, Will spelled out what this perspective really means: “[The] best you can hope for [is the] complete collapse of the United States, both as a government, and a country. So maybe some sort of natural disaster, or [something].” It is easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of America’s undemocratic institutions. But accepting these premises only leads to doomerism, rather than action. In order to fight for stop signs, and speed bumps, and wetlands, we need to clear the anti-democratic road blocks. Defending Indians’ Most Basic Rights

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Achin Vanaik ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-20T16:09:29+00:00

With the global rise of right-wing and far-right political forces even in countries deemed democracies, civil liberties groups are under pressure as never before. This is certainly true in India, though the success of the latest spontaneous youth upsurge, under the banner of the ironically named Cockroach Janata Party, got international headlines and provided welcome breathing space. The protests emerged against severe educational manipulation, which damaged students’ future employment prospects. Some leaders from the ruling Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) had to, politically speaking, tread carefully since a very substantial section of these protesting youths came from pro-BJP families. That didn’t stop BJP leaders from claiming that this movement was being manipulated by anti-national elements and groups, meaning, the more left-wing student participants and members of civil liberties groups in Delhi and elsewhere in India expressing solidarity and support. Had this movement not succeeded in achieving its key demand for official accountability, with the resignation of the education minister in late July, there is little doubt that the supposed anti-nationals would have suffered serious retribution. This is what makes a new book-length study by Ankita Pandey about India’s civil liberties groups (CLGs) so timely. She has given us the most comprehensive and sustained study so far of these bodies that are not controlled by political parties or state authorities at either provincial or central levels. Just as the historical evolution of civil liberties activism in the United States (shaped undoubtedly by various progressive social movements and struggles) has given its CLGs a particular character, this is also the case when it comes to grasping the nature of CLGs in postindependence India. Ally activism, or allyship, is a concept that has been around for perhaps more than two decades in the West but has rarely been used in Indian social science studies. It is not a term familiar to Indian civil liberties activists. The minimal, consensual understanding of its meaning is that it refers to the activism of relatively advantaged groups, who act together with disadvantaged/marginalized sections of society but have no “direct, personal or material stake in the cause.” This is accurate enough as a formal description of civil liberties activism in India. But a distinctive aspect is that the initiators and organizers of India’s CLGs have mainly been either self-conscious Marxists influenced by different Maoist parties and sects outside of parliament, or self-described moderate socialists. These latter have been inspired by the Gandhian tradition or by the anti-party vision of onetime communist M. N. Roy, whose Radical Humanism prioritized individual freedom, reason, and decentralized forms of democratic power and rule. The particular manner in which Pandey has developed and applied the notion of a “politics of amplification” reflects this reality. Unlike charities and nongovernmental organizations (NGOs), Indian CLGs are not simple service providers regularly beholden to particular (often foreign) funders having their own biases and concerned not to ruffle the feathers of the powers that be. As self-financing voluntary organizations, CLGs could be said to have greater political autonomy and generally lean more to the left than their counterparts in other countries like the United States. They are unlike social movements whose leaders represent a particular social category and that must mobilize en masse to get what they want — the greater the numbers and the longer the duration, the better! The politics of amplification is very different from the “politics of representation” and direct struggle. As Pandey rightly puts it, “Allyship mobilizes credibility, not numbers.” However, the ideological alignments of Indian CLGs do affect the way they solidarize with or relate to the oppressed and their concerns. They make them perhaps more forthright in voicing their demands on behalf of, and not just at the behest of, those who are victimized. Indian CLGs forge temporary alliances with social movements and operate over a much wider range of issues because they are event-based responses opposing different forms of state repression. Yet they have also responded to intrasociety iniquities like communal violence and atrocities perpetrated in the course of land grabbing by big landlords. Composition, Origin, Variation The regional and national incarnations of Indian CLGs really emerged in the late 1960s and ’70s. The member-activists of the earlier two regional CLGs in the states of West Bengal and Andhra Pradesh were, in large measure, radicalized youth who strongly identified with the Naxalite struggle on behalf of the rural poor. They sought these activists’ release from incarceration and their rights to be treated by the state as political prisoners, not criminals. While Pandey’s book carries detailed accounts of state-level CLGs, the central focus, which also provides a template for wider overall judgements, is on two national-level bodies: the People’s Union for Civil Liberties (PUCL) and the People’s Union of Democratic Rights (PUDR), both headquartered in Delhi. The author has widely interviewed their many participants and longtime members (these bodies were first formed in 1976) and deeply studied over 250 of their histories and reports. Key here is the period of Emergency Rule (from 1975–77): a watershed moment that exposed the fragility of India’s constitutional commitment to a liberal democratic polity, and which served as great fillip to the subsequent emergence of the PUCL and PUDR. Nested between the introduction and conclusion are five chapters that in turn explore and assess a) the membership and political trajectories of CLGs; b) how they relate to political parties; c) their operating methods and key functions; d) ideological debates within and among CLGs shaping their basic practice; and e) how as one particular embodied form of “concerned citizenship” (there are others like charities, resident welfare associations, and NGOs) they contested the state on behalf of civil society. Who are the CLGs’ members? They mostly come from middle-class, “respectable” professions like lawyers, academics, journalists, social workers, occasional celebrities from the arts and entertainment domains, and so on. This itself lends a certain respectability to the wider public concerning their interventions. Long-standing CLGs, like some regional ones, and certainly the PUCL and PUDR, have a steadier and more regular set of activists than those one-off groups that periodically spring up in response to some dramatic turn of events, do their reports, and then fade away. These actions, too, are testimony to the existence of a wider pantheon of the politics of amplification. Prominent scholars have characterized these CLGs as “non-party political formations,” an understanding that Pandey has rightly criticized as ignoring the more complex relationship between them and political parties. A number of party loyalists (a spectrum that includes left and far-left sects and both Indian National Congress and non-Congress oppositionists but excludes the culturally exclusivist BJP) were accepted as active members of CLGs. But for the most part, they were procedurally and numerically constrained from being able to turn them into party fronts. Fact-Finding Virtues and a Key Difference Fact-finding missions have been a central form of activity undertaken by CLGs. To retain their credibility, these reports — for example on unjustified police violence in Kashmir province — have had to imitate juridical processes of showing impartiality in the accumulation of material evidence, including witness recollections. Their aim is to expose deficiencies and possible cover-ups in official judgments and claims. This also allows a place for help from sympathizers both within and outside the ruling parties at local, state, and central levels. Mainstream media have often focused on the outcomes of such fact-finding reports, either to malign or endorse them in some measure. But beyond that, such missions have helped to legitimize the testimonials of ordinary citizens, ignored by an officialdom whose upper-class and caste ties motivate it to repress the truth. CLGs’ work has also been significantly helped by the establishment, after the Emergency period, of Public Interest Litigation (PIL). Through it, ordinary citizens and citizen groups have the standing to approach the High Court and the Supreme Court on behalf of the indigent and deprived to call for investigations and judgments alternative to otherwise official or dominant narratives. Given this “judicialization” of civil liberties activism, it is hardly surprising that some dedicated lawyers do pro bono work for CLGs and are a key component of such allyship activism. This legalism of both procedure and practice is common to all such groups. Still, there remains an important ideological difference between the PUCL and PUDR (as well as between and, to some extent, within other groups), not only at the level of motivation but also in their basic political and advocacy thrusts. In practice, the more moderate liberal-left wing of legalism aims to get state power to behave as the existing liberal democratic constitution at its best demands. The more radical left wing seeks to create a broader counternarrative, or what Pandey calls “discursive contestation,” to persuade the public to think beyond the constitution about the need for a more “revolutionary transformation” of the social order. Not surprisingly, the former is less prone to rationalize and in part justify the resort to violence by the oppressed or by their agents (far-left groups) to counter overt or more “institutionalized” forms of violence by social oppressors or the state. This may be the case even if this rationale is not explicitly declared by the CLG in question. To put it another way, the general literature on rights discourse has four major areas of debate: 1) the material and moral foundation for the construction of rights: universalism versus cultural relativism; 2) individual versus group rights; 3) the question of civic and political rights versus economic and social rights: Isn’t the sufficient and equal fulfillment of basic needs as important, if not more so, than equality of basic liberties?; and 4) how far to expand the domain of rights. It is really with respect to the last two areas that differences exist, deep down, between the two currents of activism, even when they are not perceived as such. The Concerned Citizen Under Fire In her penultimate chapter, Pandey refers to the “concerned citizenship” embodied by CLGs. Her book deserves much praise for the new ground covered and will instill in the reader greater respect and understanding for the role that particular forms of allyship play in the defense of democratic rights and in their extension to those in India most deprived of these rights. After her book was published, one more recent development has surfaced that obviously could not be featured in her text. It threatens to greatly accelerate the already ongoing erosion of democratic freedoms. For Narendra Modi’s government has declared that there is no single document or set of documents that axiomatically and irrefutably identify one as an Indian citizen. Even the passport will not do. In today’s world, to be a rights-bearing person you have to be a citizen. Only then can one avail oneself of the range of rights whose depth and scale varies according to the country to which one belongs. But what becomes of the capacities to exercise positive forms of “concerned citizenship” such as civil liberties activism, when the government has the arbitrary power to question and decide one’s very status as a citizen? So far, there has been no judicial rejection of this official presumption. It is uncertain that this will indeed happen. Now more than ever, parties, movements and CLGs need to fight together against this Hindutva-ized and thus truly anti-national government.

Immanuel Wallerstein Predicted the Fall of the US Empire

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Gregory P. Williams ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-20T15:09:30+00:00

In July 2002, Immanuel Wallerstein appeared on C-SPAN’s morning call-in program . He had just written an essay for Foreign Policy magazine with a provocative title: “The Eagle Has Crash Landed.” Wallerstein argued that the US position of dominance on the world stage was in decline. As he was speaking, less than a year had passed since the terrorist attacks of 9/11 and the subsequent US invasion of Afghanistan. The Bush administration was preparing its military and the American public for the invasion of Iraq that would begin in March 2003. George W. Bush, it should be remembered, was a popular figure at the time. The president’s approval rating hit a record 90 percent the previous September, and remained over 70 percent when Wallerstein went public with his message of decline. However, it must have seemed to Wallerstein precisely the right moment to intervene. He had written about US hegemonic decline since the early 1980s. Yet he still believed, in the moment between the invasions of Afghanistan and Iraq, that America had the opportunity to effectively manage its decline. Wallerstein did not think it was possible to extend a great power’s global dominance indefinitely. He saw that great powers become hegemonic — a term that refers to unmatched global dominance — due to economic, political, and military factors that also ultimately cause such hegemony to erode. US hegemonic decline began much earlier than 9/11, with the economic downturn of the 1970s and the American war in Vietnam. For Wallerstein, the United States faced the choice of all waning hegemonic powers: to decline gracefully or to decline precipitously. George W. Bush chose the latter. Today Donald Trump has further accelerated US decline by aggressively deploying one of the few weapons left for a dying hegemon: its extremely strong military. Cycles of Hegemony Why was US hegemony in decline? For Wallerstein, American hegemony of the twentieth century hardly differed from Dutch hegemony of the seventeenth century or British hegemony of the nineteenth. These three powers (and only these three, he thought) achieved hegemony by riding a wave of economic forces they could not quite control and by prevailing militarily over a rival. After triumphing, the hegemonic power went on to create organizations in its image. The British, for example, prevailed over France and then created the Concert of Europe in 1815. America’s rise to hegemony began following the global recession of 1873. As Wallerstein told the story, Great Britain’s declining share of world markets was due to the United States and its chief rival, Germany. The United States became a major producer of steel, then automobiles, whereas Germany became a major producer of industrial chemicals. Each state’s economic rise followed an internal political crisis and (relative) stabilization: for America, the Union victory in the Civil War; for Germany, unification under Prussian leadership after war with France. Wallerstein understood the two world wars to be an extended thirty years’ war between the rivals. He noted that the United Provinces of the Netherlands and Great Britain also became hegemonic following wars lasting about three decades (in the Dutch case, this came after the original Thirty Years’ War between 1618 and 1648). After prevailing over Germany and the Axis powers in 1945, US leaders believed that it needed three things to maintain its prosperity: one, international customers for its industrial production; two, a world order guaranteeing commerce at a low cost; and three, a compact of sorts ensuring that production would be unimpeded. Washington achieved these objectives by reconstructing Western Europe and Japan, by creating major international institutions such as the United Nations, and by striking a kind of bargain with the Soviet Union. The last of these moves, symbolized by the Yalta conference of February 1945, was for Wallerstein much more significant than the founding of the UN two months later. In the conventional view, the Cold War of roughly 1945 to 1991 (to use the longest possible measurement) was a contest between American and Soviet adversaries. For Wallerstein, this period was really structured around an implicit compact between the great powers. The American sphere of influence covered about two-thirds of the world, with the Soviet sphere of influence accounting for the rest. Each power could dominate its zone of influence and use the threat of the other to corral reticent partners. Yalta was enforced by a “balance of terror” between the nations. The peak of American hegemony ran approximately from 1945 to 1970. Like all hegemons, the US became a victim of its success. By 1970, Western Europe and Japan were in Wallerstein’s view the economic peers of the United States. In the process of building up trading partners, crucial to American postwar success, the US also built up rivals. Like all hegemons, the United States began to lose its economic advantage in the same sequence through which it was originally built. First, the hegemon loses its advantage in agriculture and industry. Then it loses an advantage in commerce. Finally, it loses its advantage in finance. Also in the 1970s, Washington’s defeat in Vietnam exposed US military weakness and showed its inability to impose its will on the world. Vietnam, Wallerstein wrote, symbolized a rejection of the US world order by the forgotten peoples of the world, sometimes called the Third World, sometimes called the Global South. The independence movement in Vietnam against colonial powers (France, Japan, and the United States) was an extension of the global protest movement that came to a head in 1968. According to Wallerstein, “the 1968ers did not merely condemn US hegemony. They condemned Soviet collusion with the US, they condemned Yalta,” and they saw only “two camps — the two superpowers and the rest of the world.” The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991, again contrary to conventional wisdom, was for Wallerstein a disaster for the United States, because it lost its great Other. In a 1992 essay , Wallerstein insisted that the Cold War was “not a game to be won but rather a minuet to be danced . . . The end of the Cold War in effect eliminated the last major prop of the US hegemony and the US prosperity, the Soviet shield.” Washington’s response to hegemonic decline has been to assert its greatness, in speech and in deeds, seeking to reclaim its position in the world. But, as Wallerstein explained on C-SPAN, “if you’re number one, you don’t have to shout that you’re number one. People know that you’re number one. If you have to shout, there’s something wrong. There’s something going on.” Shooting the Messenger Many C-SPAN callers were not happy with Wallerstein’s message of decline. One asked if flags flew on the Yale campus, given Wallerstein’s apparent anti-American bias. Another accused Wallerstein of being a Vietnam draft dodger, without realizing Wallerstein had been drafted at an earlier stage during the Korean War. A third shivered at the thought of Wallerstein as president: “If a guy like him were to be running the United States, the United States would end up like an armadillo, rolled up in a ball, waiting to be kicked.” Such expressions of outrage were normal for Wallerstein, who had written about the national-psychological toll that hegemonic decline takes upon those who experience it. It was a potentially dangerous period for domestic politics and the leaders to whom the public might turn. On the show, Wallerstein explained he was just the messenger, and that the United States was isolating itself from the world — not simply from nations it needed to court but from its closest allies too. “If you overestimate your power, and you go around acting like a bully,” he said, “you lose more than if you engage in elementary dialogue.” Some callers saw Wallerstein as being engaged in an attempt to reform US foreign policy. One asked how to respond to accusations of anti-Americanism. Wallerstein explained that he tried using reason. He argued that US foreign policy operated against individual interests: I’m not appealing to your altruism. I’m appealing to your selfishness. You are bringing about a much swifter negative impact on the US and the lives you live. In terms of your own standard of living, in terms of your risk of death, in terms of everything that you stand for, you’ll be worse off pursuing these policies than if you shift policies. Decline did not mean catastrophe. Historically, hegemonic powers do not fall very far. They land among the other great powers, albeit with greater material comfort than most (just look at the Netherlands or the United Kingdom today). Furthermore, as Wallerstein wrote in that 1992 essay, decline could be “the greatest blessing of all.” The United States had an opportunity to embrace morality. It “was Abraham Lincoln who sounded the moral note: ‘As I would not be a slave, so I would not be a master.’” In the early 1990s, Wallerstein saw two possible futures for America. One would involve neofascism, characterized by social conflict, with the lower strata subject to brutality and prejudice. The second, more hopeful scenario would be a form of national solidarity through social democracy and the movement toward egalitarianism. Although Wallerstein was not prone to overly optimistic predictions, he thought the egalitarian path was more likely than the neofascist path. He envisaged catharsis from “a shared social stress,” combined with American economic prosperity and popular notions of liberty, that would produce a movement against the major aspects of “unequal allocation”: gender, race and ethnicity, age, and class. He figured that the momentum of 1968, having disrupted American hegemony and dethroned the ideology of centrist liberalism , would lead to a mass movement for progress. Wallerstein also had history as his guide. In the first two instances of hegemony in the capitalist world-economy, Dutch and British, hegemonic decline was followed, eventually, by a movement toward welfare and equality. Why should the United States be so different? However, Wallerstein also left open the possibility of neofascism. If the end of the Cold War meant the end of US dominance over two-thirds of the world, then it also meant the end of centrist liberalism as a cultural guarantor of the capitalist world-economy. At least since the revolutions of 1848, centrist liberalism had been the gravitational force that kept the radical left and conservative right in check. Centrist liberalism promised the realization of democratic rights and economic well-being, not immediately for all peoples, but in a slow and orderly process to be managed by the state over generations. Centrist liberalism, as Wallerstein put it in his book The Modern World-System IV , was used by “the strong [to] temper the discontent of the weak.” Strong individuals tempered the discontent of weak masses, and strong states tempered the discontent of weak states. Yet the US economic advantage faltered as the Global South began to reject the Yalta power structure. After one of the main players, the Soviet Union, fell apart, the weak masses and states proved to be not so weak after all. In this situation, for Wallerstein, “conservatives would again become conservatives, and radicals, radicals. The centrist liberals did not disappear, but they were cut down in size.” The ideological possibilities were now endless. After the Cold War, Wallerstein saw great potential for American hegemonic decline to be accompanied by domestic economic redistribution. Ten years later, the radical movement had not materialized, while conservative hawks were firmly ensconced in power. They sought to flex military might because, in their thinking, “if Washington doesn’t exert its force, the United States will become increasingly marginalized.” But Wallerstein saw their actions having the opposite effect. Given the inevitability of decline, the muscular foreign policy of the Bush administration “will only contribute to the United States’ decline, transforming a gradual descent into a much more rapid and turbulent fall.” In retrospect, Wallerstein’s 2002 essay and television appearance constituted an attempt to move the United States off of its trajectory. He had evidently abandoned hope for a social democratic movement and now redirected his attention to the declining hegemonic power itself. In the summer of 2002, over six-in-ten Americans favored opening up a second war in Iraq. Wallerstein’s opinion was that of an unpopular but persistent group of critics. When faced with such criticism, President Bush would note simply note that history would judge his actions. Today it is clear that the hawks could not preserve American hegemony, nor remake the world in a manner more conducive to US interests. What has transpired between then and now? Decline and Denial The twenty-first century has not gone well for the United States. American leaders have pushed to reclaim lost hegemony. As US economic power has diminished in comparison to the rest of the world, Washington has used its military to demonstrate strength and to achieve policy ambitions. In fact, the United States has embarked on more military interventions since 1991 than it did between 1798 and 1990. This choice was the classic strategy of a declining hegemon in denial, aware of its descent, but unaware that its best viable option was the proper management of decline. The United States has not made the world more peaceful or more prosperous, nor has it improved the nations it invaded. In European Universalism: The Rhetoric of Power (2006), Wallerstein questioned the logic of interventionism. The invading power rarely makes the invaded place better off than if it had not intervened, a conclusion confirmed by US military adventures across the Middle East. Wallerstein’s 2002 verdict could have been written this year (or this week for that matter). He wrote that the United States finds itself “a lone superpower that lacks true power, a world leader nobody follows and few respect, and a nation drifting dangerously amidst a global chaos it cannot control.” The backlash of 1968ers that he described came from the nonaligned Global South as well as discontented masses at home. In the twenty-first century, the concatenation that began at the level of the world-system quickly moved to American domestic politics, and then outward again. The end of the Cold War unmoored radicalism and conservatism from centrist liberalism. The effects were drastic. Decades of neoliberal consensus were challenged by the likes of Donald Trump and Bernie Sanders. When Trump assumed the presidency — first in 2017, again in 2025 — he did so as a critic of America’s liberal order. However, “America first” has not resulted in the revival of domestic production, improved social welfare, or the protection of workers. Instead, Trumpism is characterized by massive, brazen corruption (at home and abroad), militarism abroad, and domestic policies of mass deportation. The United States has continued to expand its military commitments and military spending, but without a commitment to domestic social welfare. Meanwhile, optimism about the future among Americans has overall declined since 2013. In short, the United States has chosen the neofascist path that Wallerstein initially considered the least likely: social conflict, brutality, and prejudice. Members of the upper strata have sought to hold on to their remarkable privileges and rewards. Americans consequently have more precarious lives, living paycheck to paycheck , with fewer protections against the risks of illness, age, and employment instability. In 2026, America’s war in Iran encapsulates two tendencies of US hegemonic decline. One is the inability of the United States to get its way despite an extremely powerful military. The other is the discontent of the American public at a diminished standard of living and a leadership class it no longer trusts. Such feelings of discontent, as Wallerstein noted, can be powerful catalysts for political action. Washington may find itself surprised once again by chaotic global and domestic events it cannot control.

Utopia Is a Promise Worth Fighting For

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Matt McManus ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-20T14:39:13+00:00

The haunting impossibility of a society that lives up to our stated ideals can feel painful when set against the actual societies that fall so far short of them. This is why the dream of utopia, or “no place,” has disturbed the sleep of many thinkers for so long it can feel instead like a nightmare. Given the hypocrisy of a world where cruelty often feels like the point, what business do we have supposing things could be better? How can allegedly wise and intelligent people dream of utopia when wisdom and intelligence all point to its impossibility? David Albertson and Jason Blakely’s new book Utopia for Our Century: A Manifesto of Hope presents a thoughtful history of those daring souls who have dreamed of better. But more than a history, it attempts to think through the political and ideological value of utopian thinking and push against the line that it is just wishful thinking. Albertson and Blakely find much to admire in utopian authors, seeing them as “virtuoso dreamers, who lived realities at least as bleak and fearsome as our own but did so with indestructible hope.” At their best, utopians emphasized hope without guarantees — a wise position that carves a middle path between blind optimism and the resigned inertia of faux “realism.” Their archetype is Thomas More, whose Utopia , published in the sixteenth century, christened the concept. For Albertson and Blakely, More gives us insight into the personality of utopians. They are not the naive optimists of caricature. Far from an airy speculative thinker, More is one of the few major philosophers to have had a genuinely public political career, and during a singularly challenging period. He served as Lord Chancellor of England under the amoral and cruel Henry VIII. This was a tumultuous time for the country. Henry broke with Rome and declared himself head of the Church of England in order to annul his marriage and marry another woman. This produced major pushback, which Henry met with violent repression. That repression eventually caught up with More, a devout Catholic, who was put on trial for refusing to acknowledge Henry’s religious supremacy. More was unwavering in his convictions, leading to his execution in 1535. Albertson and Blakely understand that More’s tragic immersion in realpolitik is part of what inspired his turn to utopianism. His ironic designation of “utopia” as “no place” isn’t just intended to demonstrate the speculative quality of his thinking. More is drawing critical attention to the fact that no place on earth comes close to embodying justice, even though many ruling elites who have the power to change things claim to care deeply about being just. In a memorable passage, More describes a dialogue between Raphael Hythloday — the traveler to Utopia — and an unnamed lawyer. The lawyer claims to be concerned about law and order, and argues that the only way to preserve it is through brutal punishment. Hythloday notes that Utopia is a much safer place, in no small part because it is a far less unequal society where property is widely shared and there are few poor people. Moreover, their punishments for crimes like theft are far more lenient than in England, further expressing their humanist commitments. With “biting humor,” Hythloday observes that realists like the lawyer trap the poor in an impossible situation, “first making them into thieves and then punishing them for it.” Rather than undertaking reforms to achieve the greater peace and justice the lawyer claims to want and believe in, he doggedly doubles down on the “pipe dream” that “England could incarcerate and execute its way out of the problems of poverty (alas, the ruse still works like a charm).” In this sense, utopianism isn’t a fantasy about worlds that could never be, but a damning mirror that reflects how little we live up to principles we say we believe in. Moreover, by failing to commit themselves to justice in the name of “realism,” ruling elites are responsible for producing the very corruption they claim is the biggest barrier to doing better. More wanted to expose how this ideological attachment to the way things are, with all its flaws, was one of the major barriers to making things better. “More’s Utopia gradually submits his European contemporaries to the scrutiny of what if and its unmasking force,” as Albertson and Blakely put it. “Common attitudes of his day toward incarceration, private property, education, and medical care are exposed one by one as unworkable, unsustainable, and indeed unrealistic in light of Utopian achievements.” And yet, in just a handful of centuries, these all changed: “How many self-evident policies of More’s London have endured into our century, whether capital punishment for theft or the divine right of kings? The answer is clear: they have vanished.” Postmodernity; or, the Cultural Logic of Monstrous Utopias Contra our reputation for idealism, many of the sharpest left-wing thinkers have expressed deep skepticism about utopianism. This, of course, started with Karl Marx, who famously chastised socialists he disagreed with by disparaging them as “utopian.” In his more hard-edged moods, Marx mocked utopianism as writing recipe books for the cookshops of the future. Such schemes were nothing but a speculative and ahistorical indulgence (though one Marx himself often indulged in). Following in his footsteps, many orthodox Marxists insist socialism must be “scientific,” or it isn’t socialism at all. More thoughtful Marxists take a different approach. In his seminal Archaeologies of the Future , the late, great Fredric Jameson notes how utopian science fiction has often been a constructive genre for progressives to imagine what better futures might look like, the better to realize them. The genre, of course, began with More himself, whom Jameson describes as a broadly progressive satirist of power offering a damning “point by point commentary on English affairs and the English situation.” Utopian literature carried on in various forms as authors from Edward Bellamy to Ursula K. Le Guin used science fiction as a means of both criticizing the status quo and hypothesizing about what a better future might look like. But ultimately Jameson expresses wariness of the utopian genre’s transformative power and suggests it may well have been exhausted by the postmodern twenty-first century. He notes how today the utopian genre has all but evaporated, giving way to bleak depictions of the future where it is easier to imagine the end of the world than an end to capitalism. In a telling sign, even works that start by presenting utopias usually end by revealing them to be not all they’re cracked up to be. The seemingly idyllically pink world of Barbie (2023) turns out to be riven by suppressed existential dread and gender animosities. The more recent Star Trek series like Picard and Discovery take the bright cosmopolitan future promised by Gene Roddenberry’s United Federation of Planets and show that it has secretly depended on covert manipulation, exploitation of the marginalized, and much else. Contra Jameson, Albertson and Blakely see the utopian genre as very much alive but not well. This is a dire problem since the need for “constructive” utopias is urgent. Albertson and Blakely are very critical of right-wing populism and the havoc it is wreaking, but they argue that much of this flows from the Right’s own (frequently disavowed) utopian aspirations. These include the nationalist dream of a unified ethnos, which Albertson and Blakely describe as a “peculiar kind of failed utopia.” Nationalists conjure an “imagined spirit of the nation that creates an illusion of cultural unity” — one that purports to draw on real history even as they constantly downplay or militantly resist historical facts that don’t gel with the patriotic myths they want to spin. Albertson and Blakely wryly note that nationalists constantly have to “prop up the obvious falsehood that the make-believe geist [national spirit] remains pure from overlapping, constituent, or neighboring cultures, and that it never changes in time.” They also see many of the same fantasies at work in the Right’s “most feverish utopian hope”: that we might one day achieve the “most pristine, geometrical, frictionless vision of the ‘free market.’ Entire generations of Americans have lived as though the market were the only tolerable utopia.” Albertson and Blakely are scathingly critical of this vision, and rightly so, calling this a “monstrous utopia” for asking little of people and giving less in return, all while the money system is treated like a “minor deity.” I would go still further than Albertson and Blakely and suggest that the meritocratic conception of capitalism, that each will be rewarded according to what they deserve, is not just a bad utopian vision — it is the most pie-in-the-sky utopian vision ever conceived. Ancient philosophers had the wisdom to accept that only the very visible and omniscient hand of God could reward people based on what they deserved, and then only in a perfect realm beyond this one. Only American conservatives have ever imagined a world so fantastic that the invisible and mindless hand of the market would reward people based on what they deserved, and in this life. Compared to meritocratic capitalism, even the most rosy-eyed communist is a clear-eyed realist, since they only imagined it was possible to give people what they needed while keeping it an open question what they deserved. Is Utopia Possible Today? By contrast, Albertson and Blakely are more sympathetic to utopias on the Left but think they remain constrained by certain fundamental limitations. They express some sympathy for liberalism, while highlighting how “postwar liberalism stalled” due to a regression into “right libertarianism, sometimes called neoliberalism.” This regression into neoliberalism “betrayed the [liberal] tradition’s original ideals” of achieving liberty, equality, and solidarity for all. Albertson and Blakely are also critical of the Marxist tradition for failing to recognize how true revolutions never start in the economic sphere. Instead we need a transition in values by aiming for higher ethical standards. They don’t spell out what this would entail in detail, but their approving references to humanist and left-wing Christians like More himself, Martin Luther King Jr, and the late Alasdair MacIntyre provide many clues. What makes Albertson and Blakely’s position interesting is the extent to which they call for a utopian vision that will be at once liberal, anti-capitalist, and deeply spiritual. Needless to say, this is a challenging combination to achieve — though one might ask what good is an unambitious utopian vision? Unfortunately, the authors don’t make much of an attempt to spell this out in any kind of detail, even if they provide a suite of moving examples in the lives of More and King. My own Marxist instincts kick in here, leading me to suspect that individual acts of grace simply will not be enough. The lack of a clear theory of power — something to complement the fiery condemnations of injustice and the critique of ideological straitjackets — is a real limitation to their vision. But for all that, Albertson and Blakely are to be commended for the strength and boldness of their vision, even their audacity. They are no doubt right that a kind of utopianism may well be more realistic than a thoughtless adherence to a status quo that isn’t working. Doing the same thing over and over again and expecting different results isn’t wisdom, after all. It’s insanity.

According to the famous “garbage can” model of political decision-making, there isn’t much rationality in politics. Minted in the 1970s, this model resists the idea that collective choices are the outcome of political professionals intentionally looking for optimal solutions for well-defined problems. Rather, it casts these choices as the result of contests between different elite factions who try to convince citizens — and each other — that their preferred “solution” can at least beat their competitors in answering whatever issue presents itself. Half a century later, this model still explains many striking and long-enduring political phenomena. First, that policies never disappear but reappear again and again, zombie-like — or, to stick to the metaphor, reappear as if spewed out by an overfull garbage can. Second, that policies can constantly be adapted to changing political winds by being relabeled and rehashed. Third, at the end of the day, nothing ever seems to be solved at all. Ultimately, the success of an elite faction in peddling its preferred “solution” depends not on it actually solving anything but on its being able to endlessly identify problems that it can at least claim to do something about. The “garbage can” metaphor appears particularly apt in times of fundamental policy shifts like we are living through today. In Europe, issues of sustainability and global warming have suddenly been replaced by a gospel of war, defense, security, and geopolitics, even in the mouths of the same elites who just a few years ago presented themselves as the ultimate climate warriors. Take the latest report on defense and industrial policy, produced by the policy advice branch of Germany’s Green Party. This document on Defense as driver of innovation is meant to help the party give its own subtle twist to the “ Zeitenwende ” or “historical turning point” announced by then Chancellor Olaf Scholz in February 2022. Turning On the Tap In response to the Russian invasion of Ukraine, on February 27, 2022, a majority of the German Bundestag decided to shed the country postwar constitutionalized pacifism and transform it into Europe’s largest and most lethal fighting machine. A goal that, in view of twentieth-century European history, should make a chill run down the spine of everyone with a modicum of historical sense. Reaching this goal first meant abandoning Germany’s ordoliberal insistence on balanced budgets (the “black zero” rule). This was followed three years later, after some hair-raising parliamentary chicanery, by the abandonment of the “debt brake” that constitutionally required the federal government to keep aggregate German public debt at 2009 levels — a fixture comparable to the US debt ceiling. Open went the spigot: defense spending rose from $56 billion in 2022 to $67 billion in 2023 and $88 billion in 2024. The federal government aims to increase this to $152 billion in 2029, by which time Germany should be able to withstand any Russian attack without US aid. As long as, that is, Russia sticks to the script of conventional warfare only. The German Greens have long forgotten their pacifist heritage (the party’s founding manifesto back in 1980 had preached nonviolence and the dissolution of both NATO and the Warsaw Pact). Yet while this latest report takes for granted the need to counter the Russian threat, in fact its attention lies elsewhere. The real focus here is industrial decline and economic depression. As the document’s first paragraphs summarize, the German economy is in dire straits, with its industrial core hemorrhaging 15,000 jobs per month, its famed “Mittelstand” (the manufacturing backbone of Germany of small family-owned firms) closing by the thousands every year, and its GDP flatlining for four years now. All this is spawning the material conditions for potentially huge victories for the nationalist Alternative für Deutschland (AfD) in September’s three state elections. In Saxony-Anhalt, a state in the former East, the AfD is expected to take over 40 percent of the vote, while even in Berlin, long a progressive stronghold, it appears to be on the verge of becoming one of the largest parties, polling at 17 percent. How to ensure that the massive uptick in defense spending does not fill the coffers of American arms producers alone but instead serves the average German voter? How to turn it into a “smart” industrial policy 2.0 for Germany itself? That is the key question the report tries to address. Here is where the “garbage can” origin of the report becomes clear. It joins geopolitical security concerns and industrial policy at the hip in an attempt to bring on board also those who remain unconvinced that the surge in defense spending is worth the inevitable costs in terms of social security, environmental policy, and public infrastructure. The subtext of the report: even if you are not convinced that the Russians are coming and the Americans are exiting, you may well support us, the Greens, because we are going to make sure that our increased defense spending will ultimately benefit us all. It is neoliberal “trickle-down economics” all over again. Or in the report’s own words: History shows that increased defense spending can trigger waves of innovation: GPS, the early internet, or modern material sciences all came out of military research funding. Whether similar spillover effects happen today is still unclear. More money alone isn’t enough. It depends on what it’s used for. Innovation Speak It is not hard to see, in this, a repetition of Mariana Mazzucato’s wildly popular argument about the entrepreneurial state. In her 2011 book , she uses examples of civilian spin-offs from US defense projects to debunk the neoliberal claim that only markets are innovative while the state is always a drag on growth. Although true in the abstract, we might question whether her examples were such a happy choice. These were, after all, part of defense packages that have brought death and destruction to civilians across the Global South, for which the mixed blessings of the internet and the smartphone have been a meager offset, if any at all. Moreover, reasoning that imitates Mazzucato’s now increasingly serves as a fig leaf for former progressives across the European Union to back an arms race that is not going to make the world more secure and will make citizens much poorer. The nub of the question is the claim that, while more money is surely not enough , the problem is all about how it is spent. Here the report gets into its stride and starts to read like your average consultancy think piece. It abounds in terms like “spillover,” “ecosystems,” “innovation agents,” “start-ups,” “system integrators,” “feedback loops,” “dual use technologies,” “strategic multipliers,” and more, plus the necessary paeans to artificial intelligence. The same is true for its list of recommendations. They range from inserting “security” as a key requirement for national research-funding programs to better incentives for academics to turn to defense and security issues and directing research money to “dual purpose” defense projects. It also proposes a lower defense vetting threshold for SMEs (small and medium businesses) by setting up “onboarding programs,” drawing on dedicated “High Tech demand and supply maps.” It further offers full-fledged state guarantees for private business and cofinancing schemes along the lines of infamous public-private-partnership (PPP) models . It always boils down to the same thing: the privatization of gains and the socialization of risks and costs. The language reflects a deep belief in what Thomas Frank in his forthcoming book, The Creativity Con , has identified as a cosmetic innovation speak that itself has a dual use. Indicating that one knows how to talk the language of capital is the first one. It is a goal that is particularly important to the once rebellious German Greens, former outsiders who were invited in and who, now in opposition, want to get back in as soon as possible. Becoming insiders in European politics, as is well known, depends on three commitments: embracing the EU, accepting NATO, and speaking the language of capital to indicate a belief in the sanctity of liberal property rights, the cornerstone of capitalism. Insider Status The Greens had become insiders at least as early as 1999, when party grandee Joschka Fischer, as German foreign minister, backed the illegal NATO bombardment of Kosovo and Serbia, which, according to Human Rights Watch , killed at least five hundred people. The Greens have even ruled with the center right in several states. The Greens’ insider status is clearly reflected in the roster of participants in the working group that signed off on this report. As the German daily Berliner Zeitung revealed, many of the largest German and European arms producers are among its members, including luminaries such as Airbus Defence & Space , Heckler & Koch , Hensoldt AG , and Safran Electronics & Defense . This would suggest that this is not so much a neutral public-policy document but a lobbying report, much like consultancies the world over produce. As for average voters, they find themselves in a Germany slowly but gradually moving toward further military escalation, funded by severe cutbacks to the supposed foundation of modern Europe, namely the much-cherished welfare state. For that is the most striking part of the report: what it does not mention. In the sanitized world of the professional-managerial class that populates the German Greens, war is at worst a game and at best a managerial problem that can be solved by some brainstorming, the right incentives, a spreadsheet, and a bit of cross tabulation. That these weapons are designed and built to maim, kill, and destroy is cosmetically obscured by the euphemistic consultancy speak. The report likewise ignores the massive literature that throws doubt on any claim that defense spending has an economic upside. The political economists Robert Pollin and Heidi Garrit-Peltier had already in 2009 calculated that the employment gains of defense spending are half those of any dollar spent on health care, education, public transport, or construction. And of course, this is referring to the United States, which is home to the largest defense sector in the world. It is in line with the econometric quantification of the “multiplier” (i.e., how much does one unit increase of public spending add to GDP) by mainstream macroeconomists. A Dutch meta study indicates that the multiplier is near zero. That means that an extra dollar or euro spent on defense adds next to nothing to GDP. This is why economists continuously refer to defense spending as “dead consumption,” not investment, meaning that it does not generate a quantifiable return like when states “invest” in education, health care, or infrastructure. And why, whenever you hear politicians relabeling it as “investment,” you know you are being manipulated in a way that comes straight out of the playbook of NATO’s cognitive warfare program. Ramping up arms spending is a shameful waste of scarce public resources and an environmentally destructive one. Disgracefully, the Greens have totally forgotten this. Given that the party is nominally left-wing, we can only hope that some of its disappointed members will pick through the garbage and find its founding manifesto. That document was all about how peace, détente, diplomacy, and the abolition of NATO will help save the planet. The Greens would do well to rediscover this, before disaster strikes.

Les Girondins de Bordeaux is one of France’s most historic and decorated football clubs , with a record of winning six League 1 titles and nurturing star players from Zinedine Zidane to Jules Koundé and Aurélien Tchouaméni. Yet at the start of the 2025–26 season, the club found itself on the verge of collapse, struggling to survive in the fourth tier of French football, after years of financial mismanagement, debt, and administrative sanctions. The fate of Bordeaux exemplifies a broader trend across professional football where financially fragile teams have been pushed to insolvency, accelerated by the pandemic. However, Bordeaux still possesses one of the most respected youth academies in France, an underused stadium that seats 42,000, and a loyal local fan base (including my own family). Earlier this month, Sparta Capital, a British-based investment firm, bought the club for one symbolic euro (after raising €10 million to buy its debt). This “ phoenix strategy ,” a financial-speak term for the practice of private equity firms buying up struggling organizations to turn them around, has become an increasingly important phenomenon in the economy of the world’s most popular sport. It has attracted much less attention than Gianni Infantino’s abortive scheme to sell off rights to the World Cup that is still convulsing the world of football. A New Class of Owners Over the past three decades, football increasingly has moved from local ownership to a new set of proprietors: billionaires, Gulf sovereign wealth funds, and Chinese state-backed capital. Some, like Bordeaux’s Gérard López , mismanaged clubs and ran them into the ground. Others — notably the owner of Paris Saint-Germain (PSG), Qatar Sports Investment — poured in so much money that it inflated costs across the sport, notably for the transfers or salaries of players. It became difficult or impossible for competitors to keep up without risking financial ruin. Having increasingly dominated the French league in the fifteen years since its ownership change, PSG became only the second team to retain the European Cup since 1990 after defeating Arsenal in this year’s final. Over the last decade, there has been a new trend, with private equity firms, primarily American, buying football institutions. These actors have invested over €10 billion in European football since 2016, and financial firms now own 36 percent of all clubs across Europe’s top five leagues (England, Spain, Germany, Italy, and France). While Europe is the sport’s most “mature market,” this dynamic has expanded across the world, from US Major League Soccer to the leagues of Mexico , Brazil , Saudi Arabia , and other countries. According to economist Luc Arrondel , this trend can assume several different forms. Investment firms can acquire a majority stake — RedBird for Toulouse and AC Milan, Apollo Global Management for Atlético Madrid — or buy a minority portion, as Silver Lake did with Manchester City. Investors can even purchase commercial rights for entire leagues, as CVC Capital did for Spain’s La Lisa in 2021, or the right to operational functions: Sixth Street now has the right to participate in running Real Madrid’s Santiago Bernabéu stadium over the next twenty years. Monetizing Football This market is made up of traditional private equity firms, firms that specialize in sports investment, and other financial institutions such as hedge funds or venture capital companies. Private equity does not acquire clubs as personal trophies or a long-term asset. Instead, they invest other people’s money with the objective of generating a return, which often means reselling at a profit. Elias Gouabella, a private equity professional, describes the contrasting logics at work: Yes, individual owners may care about profits, but aside from soft power or fiscal interests, they usually buy clubs for “passion capital.” This means the biggest difference [in the ownership models] is time. Private equity firms are short-termists, their maximum horizon is ten years, and they are entirely focused on rentability. Individual owners can afford to be patient and have the deep pockets to reinvest, while private equity firms are beholden to their internal rate of return. As a result, they usually look for ways to expand commercial revenues, monetize “assets” (such as stadiums, youth academies, fan bases, or legacies), and restructure the club’s management and finances to resemble those of a private company. As Gouabella puts it: “Since they won’t be there long, they are hyperfocused on things like quickly trading players or accumulating titles. This is a totally different approach to the sport.” In addition to higher ticket prices and streaming games on pay-television platforms instead of free-to-air television, another concern for fans is the multiclub-ownership model (MCO). Half of England’s Premier League is now in some form of MCO structure. The model allows an owner to treat several clubs as parts of the same portfolio, moving players, coaches, and money between them. For example, BlueCo, the investment group owned by Todd Boehly and Clearlake Capital, bought Chelsea and then Strasbourg. The firm uses the French club to develop young players and then “sells” them to Chelsea — a transaction between two parts of the same organization. This year, Strasbourg’s manager, Liam Rosenoir, also left the club midseason to take over at Chelsea. Gouabella points to the harmful implications of the MCO model: “In multiclub ownership, the satellite teams essentially become subsidiaries of the flagship clubs. This goes against the culture of football, where every team has its own identity, and serves the interests of its fans.” Balancing across assets is a familiar practice in the financial sector, but it raises fundamental questions about what happens to the sport when a club is turned into an investment product. Infantino’s Plan FIFA President Gianni Infantino was widely condemned for making the 2026 World Cup a consumer capitalist spectacle. This involved a host of tactics, from hiking ticket prices to astronomical levels and installing a dynamic pricing system to the addition of mandatory hydration breaks that doubled the ad revenue time for corporate sponsors. In spite of international outrage, from his perspective, Infantino’s plan largely worked . Viewership broke historic records, and demand for tickets exceeded supply. This testifies both to the unmatched passion for football in comparison with any other sport and to the willingness of the wealthy to hijack this passion, without any qualms about what this could do to the game they claim to love. In the weeks following the end of the World Cup, Infantino once again came under fire for a controversial proposal to spin off FIFA’s commercial interests into an investable vehicle called the FIFA Forward Enterprise (FFE). He believed that FIFA was still “undermonetized” when compared to the Union of European Football Associations (UEFA) or the Premier League and saw US sports leagues , notably the National Football League, as examples to follow. Infantino drew up this plan in spite of the fact that FIFA is a nonprofit organization with no concerns about financial precarity (in fact, it has billions of dollars in reserves to hand). FIFA has since abandoned the proposal after international outrage and outright opposition from the European, North and Central American, and Asian member confederations, with threats of a World Cup boycott. This abortive scheme did not take shape in isolation. It was the most visible manifestation to date of trends that have been unfolding at almost every other level of professional football. Too Important to Sell The increase in private equity investment comes at a time of excess liquidity in the sector. While football institutions are relatively high-risk assets, ones that take a long time to yield returns when compared to traditional private equity investments, the entertainment industry offers a uniquely “reliable” home for surplus capital amid growing financial and social uncertainty about artificial intelligence. There will likely be much change and upheaval in the economy in the coming years, but the willingness of fans to support their local football team and go to games will persist. Private equity sees this as something to exploit. Last month, when all fifty-five UEFA members announced a boycott of FIFA events as long as the proposal to privatize part of the organization remained alive, they explicitly rejected the capture of the sport by private investors or its treatment as an investment product: Some things are simply too important to sell. The FIFA World Cup belongs to football. It always will. And so long as Europe has a voice, it will never be for sale. This opposition was critical and commendable. But we should not only be concerned with these high-profile flash points. If we want to protect the right for everyone to participate in the beautiful game , we also need to pay attention to the less prominent but equally dangerous processes of financialization that are reshaping the people’s sport.

Dressed uniformly in starch white blouses and blue-wash jeans, they proceeded across the lawn, each carrying a sheaf of roses and carnations. The elder women knelt at their mother’s grave and placed the blush pink flowers in a vase. Heads bowed, they lingered a short while. For her father Alberto, who was Peruvian president from 1990 to 2000, Keiko had brought an arrangement in the national colors of red and white. Their widely publicized conflict emerged in 1992, when Higuchi accused her husband’s family of misappropriating charitable donations sent from Japan. Keiko first came to public view during their acrimonious divorce: when she was just nineteen, her father, as president, invited her to replace her mother as First Lady. Alberto Fujimori died in 2024, age eighty-six, shortly after his compassionate release from prison. His demise gave Keiko new freedoms in her latest presidential campaign, not least the chance to rewrite her family’s story featuring a reconciliation between her parents on Higuchi’s deathbed. Similar olive branches have, according to Keiko’s staging, been extended among members of the wider family. At Fujimori’s funeral, Keiko embraced her distraught brother, Kenji, whom she had expelled from her Fuerza Popular party back in 2018 after he refused to support her attempt to impeach sitting president Pedro Pablo Kuczynski. Marching alongside students, socialists, and trade unions, they have resurrected an iconic flag-washing ritual that was used to denounce corruption during the president’s final year in office. Bernabe tells the story of the men and women who kept the dream of Puerto Rican independence alive over the past two hundred years, while focusing on the role that the country’s working class played within the movement. An American Nation While often excluded from the conversation about the Latin American independence campaigns of Simón Bolívar and José de San Martín, the Spanish colonies in the insular Caribbean, Cuba and Puerto Rico, provided soldiers to both sides of the conflict. One such soldier whose life Bernabe discusses is Antonio Valero, a Puerto Rican officer in the Spanish army who participated in the Mexican independence negotiations and decided to join the military of the newly independent nation. However, shortly after independence, Valero’s republican ideals put him at odds with Agustín de Iturbide, who had crowned himself Emperor of Mexico, prompting him to flee to Gran Colombia, where he would enlist in the military and fight for Peru’s independence. Valero never stopped dreaming of liberating his homeland and tried to convince Bolívar to free Spain’s remaining Caribbean colonies. This plan almost came to fruition in 1827 but was abandoned due to the possible threats a continuation of the war could pose to Gran Colombia’s newfound independence. In Obstinate Star , Bernabe shows that the dream of Puerto Rican independence did not die with Bolívar’s abandoned invasion of Cuba and Puerto Rico but reconfigured itself as part of a cosmopolitan Antillean identity promoted by a new generation of Cuban and Puerto Rican intellectual patriots. The two leading Puerto Rican figures of this new generation were Ramón Emeterio Betances and Eugenio María de Hostos, both of whom would prove to be key advocates for Puerto Rico’s independence in the late nineteenth century and the intellectual architects of the Puerto Rican nation. Both appear extensively throughout Bernabe’s history: key supporters of the Lares uprising against Spanish colonial rule in 1868, they connected the fight for Puerto Rican independence with the Cuban revolutionary movement and other struggles for liberation in Latin America and Europe. The book also highlights how the independence movement adapted to changing circumstances after the Spanish-American War, when the island became a US colony in 1898, emphasizing the connections and confrontations between sectors of the movement and the growing labor movement on the island. In the 1930s, ’40s, and ’50s, these connections and differences were most pronounced in the eclectic politics of Pedro Albizu Campos and the Puerto Rican Nationalist Party. Albizu Campos espoused a militant populist nationalism that sought to connect Puerto Rico with contemporary social movements in Latin America, bringing together independence supporters of all ideologies, from conservative nationalists to communists. Bernabe is keen to highlight the ideological contradictions within the Nationalist Party and the ways it struggled to win over the working class from the Socialist Party, which supported statehood. Puerto Rico and the US Left Obstinate Star also emphasizes the strong relationship between the Nationalist Party and the US left in this period. The author focuses in particular on the ties linking Albizu Campos to the Communist Party USA and the left-wing New York Congressman Vito Marcantonio. He goes on to analyze the years following the foundation of the Commonwealth in 1952 and shows how the experience of the 1959 Cuban Revolution revived the independence movement. The Pro-Independence Movement (MPI), much like the 26th of July Movement in Cuba, moved toward an open embrace of Marxism-Leninism and eventually transformed into the new Puerto Rican Socialist Party. The Puerto Rican Independence movement went on to follow a similar path to many left-wing forces around the world between the 1960s and the 1980s, becoming increasingly radicalized. During these years, many small armed organizations developed and engaged in actions against US colonialism like the 1981 bombing of Muñiz Air Base and the 1983 Wells Fargo robbery, at the time the largest bank robbery in US history. Bernabe’s account takes care not to romanticize these events while placing them within the broader historical and political conditions in which they took place, intricately breaking down many of the complex debates that unfolded among the Left at that time. During the same period, the Puerto Rican diaspora in the United States sought to identify itself with the island’s independence movement. For some, this meant joining the diaspora section of the Puerto Rican Socialist Party and seeing themselves as part of a divided nation. Bernabe argues that this approach often led to the prioritization of the independence struggle over support for other movements in the US. A contrasting line came from El Comité, an organization that originally emerged from anti-gentrification struggles and advocated for Puerto Ricans in the United States to organize as an oppressed nationality within the multinational US working class rather than as part of a divided nation. The debate between these two positions led to a polarization that continues to exist today between sectors of the Puerto Rican left. On the one hand, there are those who emphasize the need to focus principally on independence; on the other, there are those who seek to organize within the US left while leaving the question of independence for Puerto Ricans still living on the island. This is a debate that the contemporary US left ought to study when trying to engage with the Puerto Rican diaspora. Beyond Tokenization Even though Puerto Rico remains one of the world’s oldest colonies, its independence movement has a rich history that continues today in the electoral success of the Puerto Rican Independence Party and the growing popularity of the independentista pop king Bad Bunny . Bernabe shows that the independence movement is not a monolith but rather a heterogeneous force made up of activists from a wide range of ideologies and backgrounds. Obstinate Star can serve as a key tool for those on the US left who want to understand more about contemporary Puerto Rican politics beyond the success of Bad Bunny and the occasional tokenization of the island by Democratic Party elites. Bernabe also provides a key resource for those on the island and in other Latin American countries looking to understand how, despite the country’s colonial situation, the Puerto Rican independence movement has reflected wider political trends throughout Latin America, from a historian with direct experience in the movement.

On August 13, 2026, the world marked one hundred years since the birth of Fidel Castro, the man who not only led the 1959 Cuban Revolution and the building of a socialist state, but also went on to become a key contributor in Africa’s twentieth-century liberation history. It is difficult to overstate what that meant for the anti-apartheid movement, a small, blockaded Caribbean nation that had offered enormous solidarity and sacrifice. These relationships were based on a wider political convergence around anti-colonialism, national liberation, socialism, Pan-Africanism and resistance to imperialism. While Western narratives have often presented a contested picture of Fidel Castro and his legacy, there is far less ambiguity in the memory of many African liberation movements and peoples about what Fidel and Cuba represented. For those who experienced colonialism, apartheid and foreign intervention, Cuba’s support was not an abstract Cold War alignment. It was material solidarity. Perhaps with the passage of time, the historical record will allow an even fuller appreciation of what Fidel and the Cuban people did for Africa. If Africa remembers what Cuba did for its liberation struggles, then perhaps the time has also come for Africa to ask what it can do for Cuba. If internationalism means anything, then solidarity must also be reciprocal. Africa’s remembrance of Fidel should therefore also be a call for concrete solidarity with the Cuban people today, for defending Cuba’s sovereignty, opposing the blockade and refusing to allow a country that contributed so much to African liberation to face its present difficulties in isolation. Counterweights: The 50th anniversary of the Conference of the Non-Aligned Movement in Colombo

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Shiran Illanperuma ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-19T17:33:04+00:00

On August 16, 1976, representatives from 86 countries met in Colombo, Sri Lanka, for the Fifth Conference of Heads of State or Government of the Non-Aligned Movement. This was the first summit of the Non-Aligned Movement (NAM) held in Asia and, perhaps, the largest international conference ever held in Sri Lanka. In her inaugural address to the summit, Sri Lanka’s Prime Minister Sirimavo Bandaranaike – the first woman to hold the position of prime minister in the world – raised the issue of economic sovereignty: “If we truly want to disarm imperialism and colonialism, we must certainly forge counter-weapons in the international monetary and financial spheres.” The 1976 Non-Aligned Movement summit marked a decisive turning point for the Third World movement, shifting from the noble ideals of anti-colonialism and national sovereignty to the far more arduous process of building institutions for economic transformation. Two years earlier, in 1974, Third World countries had successfully lobbied the United Nations General Assembly to adopt the Declaration on the Establishment of a New International Economic Order – a set of proposals to reform the world economy and redress centuries of colonial exploitation. The meeting in Colombo aimed to build the institutions for this new order: the Third World’s “counterweights.” Two weeks after the Colombo conference, Sri Lanka’s Permanent Representative in New York, Hamilton Shirley Amerasinghe, transmitted the results to the UN Secretary-General for distribution to the General Assembly. That 154-page document is perhaps the most comprehensive articulation the Non-Aligned Movement (NAM) ever produced of what a different world economy would look like. Half a century later, it reads less as a historical curiosity than as a to-do list. The forging of counterweight weapons The Economic Declaration adopted in Colombo insisted that “only a complete restructuring of existing international economic relations” would resolve the crisis of the developing world. From Sri Lanka to Ghana, political independence often did not translate into true economic sovereignty, as the existing structure of production, trade, and finance was monopolized by a few corporations based in the United States, Europe, and Japan. The declaration highlighted several essential elements of reform that remain remarkable for their accuracy and enduring relevance. In terms of production, the declaration advocated a new international division of labor, with the relocation of industry from the North to the South and the transfer of technology under favorable conditions. Regarding trade, the declaration defended linking commodity prices to the cost of imported manufactured goods; this aimed to counteract the deterioration of the terms of trade, ensuring that a given volume of tea or cocoa would not allow for the purchase of a decreasing amount of machinery each year. In the area of ​​finance, the declaration demanded a radical reform of the international monetary system to “eliminate the dominant role of international currencies in international reserves” and “prevent the dominance of a single country over decision-making.” Regarding debt, the declaration called for the conversion of loans into grants, moratoria, restructurings, and the complete cancellation of debt for the least developed countries, particularly those that had “suffered foreign occupation and aggression.” Perhaps the most significant aspect was the attempt to impose stricter regulations on transnational corporations, whose power had surpassed that of many nation-states. The declaration asserted the right of states to “supervise, authorize, regulate, and nationalize transnational corporations,” and denounced those companies that “deplete resources, distort economies, and infringe upon the sovereignty of developing countries” and “frequently resort to bribery, corruption, and other undesirable practices.” Among the institutions proposed for South-South collective self-sufficiency were: a Council of Commodity Producers’ Associations, a Special Fund to finance commodity stabilization reserves, a Bank for Developing Countries, a Developing Countries Payments Union proposed by Egypt, and studies aimed at “a compensatory currency backed by the economic potential of non-aligned and other developing countries.” The debt crisis and the long decline By the late 1970s, the landscape began to shift rapidly in favor of Northern capital. The 1979 Volcker Crisis sent interest rates soaring in the United States, increasing borrowing costs for the Global South. Mexico’s default in 1982 marked the beginning of the Third World debt crisis and a lost decade for many newly independent states in Africa and Latin America. The reform demands of the Non-Aligned Movement (NAM) were replaced by the IMF’s demands for structural adjustment: the terms of the debate had changed. Negotiations to establish a UN code of conduct for transnational corporations failed spectacularly in the early 1990s. This failure contributed to a long period in which the role of transnational corporations in geopolitics was completely erased. Issues related to good governance, corruption, and human rights were increasingly weaponized against newly independent states in the Global South, conveniently ignoring the destructive role of transnational corporations that controlled production and the arteries of trade and finance. Sri Lanka’s own trajectory condensed all that radical change into eighteen months. Less than a year after the fifth summit of the Non-Aligned Movement, in July 1977, Sri Lanka elected a right-wing government that made it the first South Asian economy to liberalize its economy. In a sense, the host country had abandoned the agenda at the national level even before the ink was dry. The subsequent political trajectory reinforced many of the same economic dynamics that the 1976 conference had sought to reverse. An unfinished project In 2022, Sri Lanka became the first Asian country to default on its external debt in the 21st century. Under its 17th program with the IMF, the country underwent a debt restructuring process largely favorable to creditors – including an externally imposed domestic debt restructuring that targeted workers’ pension funds and novel macroeconomic indicator-linked bonds, which tie debt payments to GDP growth. The Sri Lankan government currently allocates more than 40% of its budget to debt service. When Sri Lanka defaulted, there was no common framework for debt restructuring among middle-income countries. Nor was there significant South-South coordination amid what was essentially a wave of debt crises in the Global South. Sri Lanka’s own situation in 2026 confirms the need to create the counterbalancing mechanisms proposed in 1976. The demands of 1976 echo many of the major political debates taking place in the Global South today. The weaponization of access to the US dollar, US-controlled payment systems, and the US market has once again highlighted the danger of single-country domination. Current demands for national ownership and domestic processing of natural resources (from Indonesia to Zimbabwe) echo the 1976 critique of the international division of labor. The 1976 insistence on concessional technology transfer foreshadows current debates on climate justice and green industrial policy. What has changed is the material basis. In 1976, the Global South’s influence was rhetorical and demographic; South-South trade was scarce, and the institutions proposed in Colombo never materialized. Today, the center of gravity of the world economy has shifted to Asia, trade among developing countries exceeds their trade with the Global North, and Global South-led development finance and local currency settlement projects have expanded. Although the objective conditions for forging these counterweights may have improved, the subjective conditions remain challenging in some parts of the Global South. State formation in much of the South has entrenched elites whose fortunes are tied to the agendas of transnational corporations and foreign creditors. These elites readily succumb to tariffs, credit ratings, and bombings. Therefore, forging these counterweights requires changing the social base of leadership within the Global South itself. Only then can these counterweights be transformed from an idea into a material force. Shiran Illanperuma is a Sri Lankan journalist and political economist.
