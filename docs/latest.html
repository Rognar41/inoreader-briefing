Volunteers Were the Key to NYC-DSA’s Socialist Sweep

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Ashley Bishop ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-31T18:33:04+00:00

Since the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) swept the New York primaries in June, conservative and liberal media alike have tried to make sense of DSA’s meteoric rise by tying them to ascendant New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani and his “kingmaking” abilities. Beyond a cursory nod to the army of volunteers knocking doors and making phone calls, far less has been made of the why behind the volunteer effort that propelled these candidates to victory. Across NYC-DSA’s slate of endorsed candidates, roughly 8,500 volunteers showed up to knock more than 750,000 doors, with over 810,000 attempted door knocks — for an off-year electoral cycle. Many of these volunteers were part of Mamdani’s 100,000-strong volunteer base, who, still electrified from last year’s win, rolled right into volunteer work for the slate’s ten state-level campaigns. “I canvassed for the slate in general because I was super excited to capitalize on the momentum from last year with the Zohran primary and general win. That’s how I got started in electoral work in the first place,” said Caroline Hill, a field lead for Samantha Kattan. But it wasn’t just Mamdani’s previous volunteers who showed up to knock doors and make phone calls. New volunteers inspired by both Mamdani’s successful campaign for mayor and this new slate of candidates grew its ranks. By May, the largest source of new volunteers came from those who were neither DSA members nor previous Mamdani volunteers. Jacobin spoke with some of the most prolific door-knockers and most highly engaged volunteers from NYC-DSA’s slate of endorsed candidates to find out why they continued to show up for their chosen candidates and how they think we can keep the wins coming. People Power The two highest-profile races for NYC-DSA were for congressional seats: Claire Valdez’s run for Nydia Velázquez’s vacated seat in New York’s Seventh Congressional District (NY-7) and Darializa Avila Chevalier’s run against incumbent Adriano Espaillat in the Thirteenth District (NY-13). In NY-7, Valdez brought out a whopping four thousand volunteers for her race — nearly half the total number of volunteers that showed up for the whole slate. “Something we kept telling our canvassers, the establishment wants everyone to believe that Zohran was a moment — just a flash in the pan, viral social media, charismatic personality — and not about the substance and the politics and the policy,” said Christian Long, a field lead for Valdez. “This was our statement to say, no, this is for real. This is a movement, and we’re growing — and this is the next step.” Across the river in NY-13, with one full-time field staffer, Avila Chevalier’s campaign organized 3,500 volunteer shifts with over a thousand canvassers, averaging three shifts each — roughly a quarter of whom were not previously affiliated with DSA. “There was a sense on this campaign that the volunteers were the ones who were driving it, were the ones who are crafting the strategy, who were ultimately responsible for its success,” said Andrew Basta, a volunteer for Avila Chevalier’s congressional race. “I think I took a lot of ownership as well as many, many other people on the campaign to say that this has to be a success.” During the campaign’s final four days of get out the vote (GOTV) alone, volunteers knocked over 155,000 doors and made roughly 250,000 calls — a level of grassroots mobilization more typically associated with statewide or presidential campaigns than a single House primary. For perspective, during the last weekend of October before the 2024 general election, Kamala Harris’s campaign reported knocking one hundred thousand doors across the entire state of Georgia. “We relied heavily on what makes NYC-DSA campaigns so impactful: a mass volunteer-led apparatus,” said Sebastian Leon Martinez, the sole field staffer for Avila Chevalier’s campaign. “It was the field program that benefited from trusting volunteer leaders with new responsibilities.” These volunteers weren’t only knocking doors and making calls. For Avila Chevalier’s campaign, a dozen regular volunteers fulfilled responsibilities typically doled out to staff like training field leads who assigned turf to canvassers, handling peer fundraising, reviewing campaign data, working on video production, serving as points of contacts for other volunteers, and creating campaign merchandise. “That layer of leadership was the strongest I’ve seen in a campaign beside Zohran’s and builds up the confidence to take ownership of projects that lead to potential future staffers,” Leon Martinez said. For downballot races, these campaigns were drawing in hundreds to thousands of volunteers — rivaling, and in some cases exceeding, some of the best-known insurgent campaigns of the past decade. Archival records suggest Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez’s 2018 congressional campaign involved roughly 500 active volunteers, 103 of whom were Queens DSA members. Aber Kawas’s campaign for state senate alone mobilized more than 1,100 volunteers to knock over 104,000 doors. Christian Celeste Tate’s campaign for state assembly drew in roughly 315 volunteers who knocked nearly 45,000 doors in his district. In the “Commie Corridor,” a moniker coined by political writer Michael Lange to designate the zones of Queens and Brooklyn that came out the strongest for Mamdani during the mayoral race, multiple DSA-backed candidates were running in overlapping or adjacent districts. Claire Valdez was running at the top of the ticket, while Kawas, Samantha Kattan, David Orkin, and Tate were running downballot races, alongside Diana Moreno’s campaign for reelection in Mamdani’s old assembly seat. Organizational emphasis on ushering in a socialist bloc up and down the ballot — rather than electing individual candidates — helped turn out volunteers to canvass for multiple candidates at once and pool resources. “Just the idea that we can elect an entire slate that is unified in our goals to make our society work better for the masses — that was an opportunity that I think we all knew we couldn’t let that slip by,” said Sara Abiboutros, an attorney and field lead for the “Sunny Slate” in Sunnyside, Queens. That the candidates were running on nearly identical platforms made it easier to say to perspective voters, “If you like Valdez, you’ll like Kawas and Kattan, too.” The trust by association that came with Valdez running at the top of the ticket — in the same way an endorsement extends trust from current electeds to candidates — proved an advantage at the doors. “There was a lot of joint canvassing happening. And it just felt like coming off of Zohran’s win, it was the perfect opportunity to carry that momentum, and this would be the next step, the next phase of the movement,” Abiboutros said. Part-Time Door-Knockers When it came to knocking doors, some of these volunteers spent more time on their turf than the candidates themselves. Bao, a volunteer for Illapa Sairitupac’s campaign for state assembly and resident of an adjacent district, knocked roughly 1,450 doors — surpassing Sairitupac himself. Despite coming out two to three times a week for six months to canvass, Bao, who asked to be referred to by his first name only, said this came as a surprise: “I always assumed that I was one of the least active volunteers.” Jon Williams, a volunteer for Christian Celeste Tate’s campaign knocked over 1,500 doors, behind only Tate himself. Working from home, Williams saw the additional benefit to canvassing as an excuse to get his daily steps in. “I think I need new shoes, though,” he added. “I’ve totally burned through them.” For field leads, the commitment extended beyond knocking doors and making calls. “It’s not just the time that goes into the canvass itself, but prepping for the canvass,” said Abiboutros. Hill, a twenty-five-year-old stationery printer, commuted an hour and fifteen minutes each way from her home in Sunset Park to Ridgewood, where she worked her field lead shifts for Kattan. She spent her commute reading David Foster Wallace’s Infinite Jest . “It just became easier for me to stay in Queens and pick up a double shift instead of going back home at, I don’t know, four in the afternoon and not getting home until nearly dinner time.” She said “the best day of her life” was when she got to a volunteer shift in less than an hour. Many of these volunteers reported time commitments closer to a part-time job than volunteer work, with some saying they worked twenty hours a week or more. Basta reported spending between twenty and thirty hours each week on Avila Chevalier’s campaign. “It was a big commitment. I think it was probably the hardest I’ve ever worked on a campaign,” he said. Hasan Piker at a canvass for Claire Valdez. (Claire Valdez for Congress / X) Christian Long, a field lead for Valdez, said his initial commitment of one shift every other week quickly became two to four shifts per week. “If you told me two years ago, ‘You’re volunteering twenty hours a week,’ I would say, ‘You’re crazy, that’s so much time.’ But it doesn’t feel like work.” said Long. “You don’t want to miss that rally where 200 people came out because Zohran was there or the rally where everyone got a bandana — that’s a signifier that you were there.” As the campaign went on, Long said that feeling of not wanting to miss out on the defining moments of each campaign only increased. What Brought the Volunteers Out While the number of doors knocked and volunteer hours worked explains how DSA swept the primaries, it does not explain why working people were willing to give so much of their time to seeing their candidates across the finish line. “It just felt like a generational opportunity to have a seat like this open up,” said Long, in reference to Valdez’s race to fill long-time congressional Representative Nydia Velázquez’s seat. NY-7 is one of the most left-leaning districts in the country, and Mamdani carried it with 67 percent of the vote, his strongest performance in any district. “It just felt like such an urgent opportunity to put a socialist in this seat versus just a regular Democrat.” Hill attended Kattan’s campaign launch in February and says she was struck by the level of excitement she was generating so early in an off-year election. “I centered all my focus on Ridgewood, because even though I was pretty certain that she was going to win in that district, one thing that people were talking about with the slate, especially with ‘safe races’ like hers, is that we’re not taking the voters for granted. That’s one of the ‘DSA difference’ things.” Beyond ideological commitment to building a bloc of socialist legislators, the affordability crisis proved highly motivating for volunteers. “If we’re focusing on just Samantha,” said Abiboutros, “she was a tenant’s rights organizer for over ten years. And I feel like that’s something that’s lacking in a perspective in the state legislature. We have such an affordability crisis right now, and she can really speak to that.” Adding, “We just got a rent freeze, but it’s because of people like Samantha who have been organizing for that for so long.” Miriam Bensman, sixty-eight, a veteran of the electoral movement and the most prolific door-knocker on David Orkin’s campaign, also expressed economic precarity as motivation for volunteering her time. Though concern over the cost of living was focused more on her adult children. “My kids feel like they can barely afford to live in New York,” said Bensman, whose daughter lives in East Harlem. “She got a tiny little room in a two-bedroom that was turned into a three-bedroom for close to $1,000 a month, and that takes a lot out of her,” she said. “I want them to — my kids and everybody’s kids — to be able to have decent wages and a nice, dignified job, and home. . . . I feel like we’re leaving them a terrible world, and this was not what we wanted to do.” According to volunteers, economic insecurity emerged as a top concern for voters at the doors too. “What I heard repeatedly was affordable housing. I think people are realizing that yeah, rent is just too high. And unfortunately, when you’re a renter, you’re either beholden to infinite rent hikes in the future or you eventually get priced out. Because the costs always go up, but your income doesn’t,” said Bao. Taxing the rich proved another policy with popular support at the doors. “People are sick and tired of all these tax cuts that they’ve been getting,” said Bao. “They’re sick and tired that the wealthy keep getting richer, while everyone else’s quality of life has gone down.” Sean Hansen, a founding member of the affinity group Labor for Claire, shared similar experiences with voters. “People are struggling to make ends meet, and they want fighters in the halls of power to make their lives easier.” Labor for Claire was established shortly after Valdez announced her campaign as a way to foster and organize labor support. “You could see her at almost every picket line in New York City,” said Sean Hansen, who first met Valdez at an event for federal workers. “Claire showing up constantly — I mean, you heard it with ‘Claire was there, Claire was there, Claire was there’ — it’s not just a slogan, it’s a reality, and it pays off.” Valdez and Kattan aren’t the only candidates who had “organizer” featured prominently on their resumes. All of DSA’s candidates have come from diverse organizing backgrounds. For volunteers, these organizing bona fides were essential experience for a candidate. “They’re all organizers at heart. That’s what they were doing previously, and that’s going to be their perspective when they govern,” said Abiboutros. “They actually made connections with people, and because people trust them, people were able to look at their message, speak to them one-on-one, and say I actually believe that Samantha believes in A, B, C. I believe that Aber cares about this. I believe that Claire is truthful when she says she wants to revive the labor movement.” At the Doors: Palestine, ICE, and Trump 2.0 If organizing experience was critical for volunteers’ support, those issues candidates organized around proved equally determinative. Support for Palestine was more than a bonus check in the green column but instead another absolute prerequisite for support. “I still think Palestine is the litmus test, whether one believes in a collective project of humanity, and I think she passes with flying colors,” said Basta, who cites Palestine as the issue that radicalized him over a decade ago. “Seeing someone who for her whole life has been kind of shaped by that issue, has really been a leader on the campuses — both as a student and as an alumna — just threw open the possibility of what she can do in Congress.” At the doors, volunteers say the shift in public sentiment on Palestine — and antiwar messaging more broadly — was clearer than ever. “When you say money should be going to working families here in NY-7 and other districts where people were canvassing, not destroying working families abroad, that argument resonated far beyond what I thought, and where I thought, it would work,” said Hansen. “It’s a unifying issue that I did not expect to be quite as unifying, but it definitely gave me a lot of hope for the future.” Alex Nappier, a volunteer for Kawas, saw a similar shift in voters. During the last six weeks of the campaign, they say, voters increasingly saw DSA campaigns as opportunities to send a message to Albany that working people want their tax dollars funding programs in their communities — not sent “overseas to genocide.” Fighting to abolish Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) was also viewed by volunteers as a winning issue, particularly in a city where immigrants make up roughly 40 percent of the population. Darializa Avila Chevalier and Conrad Blackburn canvassing with supporters. (Darializa for Congress / X) “We have the army in the street, hunting down people just because they were born in a different country or appear to have been born in a different country. And Claire’s district has to be one of the most diverse in the country — people who were born in every country imaginable, people whose parents were born in every country you can think of,” said Hansen. “People are in a very activist, agitating mood, and Claire really met the moment.” “I think a lot of people — like me, as an immigrant — we kind of see America, at least [we did] in the past, as this bastion of a place where you can come and make something better of yourself or become an American. With the recent administration, it’s very difficult for a lot of immigrants to see that weaponized against them,” said Bao. “I really want us to be back in a place where we’re really proud of this aspect of our culture, and I really want someone in the state assembly to fight for us there.” One of the hallmarks of the Trump 2.0 era is the growing disaffection by voters with both establishment parties, as is a desire to be brought back into the political process. Nappier recalled several conversations with voters who described feeling disenfranchised by parties they felt no longer represented their interests. They described canvassing one “friendly” voter who explained he had become so disillusioned with the Democratic Party he had lost faith in the system altogether. If people knew organizers were having the same conversations with their neighbors, sometimes just one door over, says Nappier, we might all have “a very, very different perspective on what the state of this country and the state of this world is than you would see on social media.” Working a Queens poll site on Election Day, Nappier similarly recalled being at the end of a long line of volunteers from different campaigns passing out flyers. They watched one man, holding his child, turn down the line of flyers, telling volunteers he voted early and was just walking in this direction. “When he walked up to me, with the big picture of Aber and Zohran signs, he goes, ‘Hey, I just lied to all of them. I haven’t done my research. Who am I voting for in this?’” “The voters can tell when you’re talking to them and when you’re BSing them, or when you actually believe in what you’re saying. And I think we just have that, like, magic sauce with our candidates that they truly believe in these things,” said Abiboutros. Authenticity, coupled with canvassers and staffers’ high-level understanding of the candidates’ policies, she says, helps to build trust among voters. Abiboutros believes that if socialists want to do more than merely win but govern effectively, that necessitates gaining the trust of working people — and making them feel like they have a stake in the movement. “And that’s the goal, right? To get more people involved.” “What Else Are You Going to Do on a Sunday Morning?” When so much of our social lives and communities have moved online, volunteers expressed difficulty in making connections and building community in real life. One of the most overlooked rewards of giving their time to these campaigns, said several of the volunteers, were the connections they made with other volunteers. “Everything has been kind of commodified. So, all your interests cost money. Having a personality costs money, right?” said Bao, a software engineer in his early thirties. “One of the beautiful things about being a part of a political movement is that you have a shared sense of values with a lot of the people that you meet.” Bao says that he’s become good friends with many of the people he volunteered on Sairitupac’s campaign with. “Honestly, what else are you going to do on a Sunday morning?” Long had a similar experience volunteering for Valdez. “A lot of these people, a lot of the field leads in the canvasses, were people I was meeting for the first time, and a lot of us are friends now. There are bonds that are formed, and we kind of became a crew through it all.” “As someone who’s kind of introverted, the idea of canvassing was so mortifying, and I’ve come around to being a huge field evangelist,” said Long, who graduated from casual Mamdani canvasser to field lead for Valdez’s race. Long added that Valdez, like many volunteers who move up the ranks, was a reluctant candidate, that “the movement called her.” Pulling people into the community while encouraging them to learn new skills and achieve those things they never thought were within reach, Long says, is one of the main tenants of organizing. “Everyone’s got a role to play; everyone’s got their own strengths when it comes to making change. Not everyone can be the face of a movement or have the charisma to be a politician or a head of state.” said Bao. “I think part of this process is that you learn more about how you can contribute your strengths to help make change.” Maintaining Electoral Momentum While the general elections still loom ahead, one of the biggest questions for the movement as we look beyond the gains of the last two years, is how to maintain the momentum from our electoral successes and grow the movement that made these wins possible. “People keep asking, what are you going to do now that the campaign’s over? You’re free,” said Long. “I’m like, no — there are dozens of projects that DSA is working on that I want to be involved in.” Long, a member of the NYC-DSA antiwar working group, finds the lessons of the Obama years instructive. “[Barack] Obama won, and everyone was so excited, and everyone went back to brunch. . . . And then eight years later, we got [Donald] Trump. With Zohran’s win, and with these wins, the answer is you keep organizing.” Hansen shared a similar sentiment. “Politics doesn’t stop at the ballot box. Politics is everywhere. Each industry, each local, each shop floor — it’s an opportunity to get involved politically.” He hopes to see the energy from these primary wins carry over into fighting for the expansion of labor rights from the shop floor to the halls of power in Congress. “I would encourage people to get involved in their unions, get involved in local community groups — whether that’s DSA, whether that’s something else, and really work on building power where power needs to be built.” Moving forward for Abiboutros means building a mass movement to support our legislative goals. “Whenever these candidates are trying to push a bill through Albany or they’re rallying around a certain issue, we have to support them and back them up, so that it gives them cover as legislators to say, ‘My constituents want this, and I’m going to go to bat for them,’” said Abiboutros. “We really can’t do that without a mass movement. I think that’s what the mayoral victory showed us, and I think that we’re going to continue that.” And Hill issued a reminder. “It is really true that last year’s field leads are this year’s candidates, are next year’s electeds,” she said in reference to recently surfaced photos of Avila Chevalier working as a field lead for Mamdani last year. “There are people we worked with who one day might be running for office, and so everybody, no matter what they’re doing, should always be keeping an eye out for the people that they work with to organize because when we work together, we win, and when we win, we can have more power to actually make New York a place that we can live in and enjoy and love.”

HBO’s Burning Man Documentary Omits the Festival’s Workers

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Keith A. Spencer ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-31T16:33:48+00:00

It’s been a precarious decade for Burning Man. The legendary counterculture gathering was canceled two years in a row due to COVID-19 and has yet to recover its record pre-pandemic attendance numbers. In 2023, the indulgent spectacle was marred by sensational headlines about attendees being trapped by torrential downpours that snared vehicles in mud. Moreover, the past decade has seen a parade of negative headlines about Burning Man — including stories of horrific volunteer injuries and a pattern of worker suicides . It’s a weighty moment to release a new documentary series about Burning Man. Directed by Jehane Noujaim and Vikram Gandhi and shot over five years, the four-part docuseries The Man Will Burn dropped its final episode on HBO on July 30. The four-plus-hour series weaves together boardroom drama and spectacular event footage with narrative biographies of multifarious attendees, ranging from army vets to artists to tea house proprietors. Burning Man is notorious for the production of spectacle, most transparently in the party’s culmination in the burning of the titular effigy. So it might seem surprising that there hasn’t been a similar mass-market, mainstream documentary about the festival that capitalizes on its stunning scale and visuals. That is by design: getting permission to commercially film Burning Man ranges from difficult to impossible, due to the parent organization’s own rules. Filmmakers are required to apply directly to Burning Man Project, the nonprofit that oversees the event, in order to get permission to film for “non-personal use.” Likewise, Burning Man’s media rights page states that professional filmmakers must obtain “written permission” from Burning Man, subject to restrictions and granting a license to Burning Man’s org to use any recorded footage for their own use. Because of these stipulations, The Man Will Burn doesn’t have the patina of evenhanded, independent journalism. And while it’s not entirely an advertisement, the Burning Man Project has been happy to promote it on their own website. The filmmakers’ green light from Burning Man Project is not the only unusual thing about The Man Will Burn . For a documentary that bills itself as providing a “unique behind-the-scenes look” that “chronicles the preparation of staff and volunteers,” the staff featured in the documentary are largely nonprofit leadership. Indeed, there’s no mention of all the crewed labor — underpaid, overworked, and sunburned — that goes into making it possible for a temporary city to blossom in a hostile, lifeless lake bed. To get their take on Burning Man, I interviewed some of that crew.\ The new Burning Man documentary makes no mention of all the labor — underpaid, overworked, and sunburned — that goes into making it possible for a temporary city to blossom in a hostile, lifeless lake bed in a Nevada desert. (HBO) “The Workforce Is Sort of Invisible” Like many crew workers, Jacob started working for Burning Man as a volunteer before getting promoted to being paid an hourly wage. It took a thousand hours of unpaid labor to get there. Working for Burning Man’s Department of Public Works (DPW), Jacob is responsible for helping to build the base infrastructure on the dry lake bed, or playa, of northwestern Nevada’s Black Rock Desert, where the event takes place. DPW’s role encompasses electrical labor, city planning, construction, logistics, and waste management and constitutes just one of several divisions of laborers who arrive to set up long before Burning Man starts and leave long after the party ends. Though Jacob is not sure the exact number, he — along with four other workers I interviewed — all estimate there are about eight hundred workers on crew. “I think most participants think that Burning Man is made by the people, by the participants,” Jacob told me, adding: “They don’t realize [the] logistics it takes to have heavy equipment out here, to place 20,000 flags on all the roads and for all the team camps, to put up the fence. There is an event operations crew that does all this stuff so that there is an appropriate blank canvas of Black Rock City.” The Burning Man organization’s own promotional materials obscure the existence of workers like Jacob. Their website frames the event as participant-led and -constructed. “Every person who inhabits this city helps build it; there are no spectators,” the org’s “ Survival Guide ” reads. This year’s event page explains that Black Rock City “rises for just eight days, then disappears without a trace.” But that disappearing act, through which the desert returns to its untrammeled state, takes a while. Jacob notes that his “playa restoration contract” extends through mid-October. Jacob laments that in real life, as in the documentary, the “workforce is sort of invisible,” as he says. “In theory, the people in the city, the participants build the city — and of course they do the absolute vast majority of the work,” he told Jacobin . “But there is still a lot of paid and volunteer labor that is making that possible. And a lot of participants don’t know that there’s a seasonal workforce. That always feels really sad to me. In so many ways, in so many places in our society, labor is invisible.” Jacob, along with other workers with whom I spoke, said their contracts are typically ten weeks long, consisting of forty hours a week or more of wage labor under the Nevada sun. Indeed, the conditions are positively martian: the Black Rock Desert, the desert lake bed that temporarily transforms into Black Rock City, has average highs above 90 degrees Fahrenheit in July and August. The playa’s dust is alkaline and has a deleterious effect on the skin and mucous membranes. Crew must brace for these conditions for much longer than the ten days that the event lasts. “People just cannot imagine how much it adds up,” Zoe, a six-year veteran crew laborer, says of the environment. “What a toll that takes on your body, on your sleep, on your skin, on your hair. It’s grueling.” (Zoe, along with workers Martha and Jacob, are pseudonyms; citing Burning Man Project’s alleged history of retaliation , all three asked that I not use their real names.) Zoe said that she typically experiences hair follicle destruction and cuticle infections — health problems she characterized as “less serious” compared to what she’s seen in her peers. Beyond exposure, crew jobs have an array of on-the-job hazards, including working with heavy machinery and high-voltage electric lines. Workers say the Burning Man org’s leadership — who are most prominently profiled in The Man Will Burn — are not typically on the playa at the same time as crew. As for board members like Kimbal Musk, brother to Elon, depicted as a main character in The Man Will Burn ? “I never interacted with them,” two-season veteran laborer Maeve O’Donnell, an electrician, says of the board. Zoe says Musk’s commentary in the documentary suggests an ignorance of the role of labor in making Burning Man possible. “I don’t think they know anything,” she told me. “I think they have a prepackaged idea,” and “could talk a good talk about the sizzle reel of what it is that we do” but have no idea of the “reality,” Jacob said. Many of those on the crew are union members in their “default world” day jobs: electricians, carpenters, or mechanics. Yet their nonunion labor to make the festival possible doesn’t pay nearly as well as those “default world” jobs. Likewise, nonmanagerial Burning Man crew are not salaried but mere contractors, at-will employees with few or no benefits. Given the hazards, the lack of housing and medical coverage seems especially surprising. Jacob, who worked five full seasons for Burning Man on crew, notes that he was given “access to power and shade” to erect his own shelter on a ten-by-ten-feet square. Jacob said that some people have been given housing by the org sometimes, but its apportioning is “mysterious and haphazard.” “If you’re making $4,000 in a summer, but you also have to rent a trailer or buy a tent or whatever, then that’s a huge difference,” Jacob noted. “Some people will rent a trailer, which obviously eats into your wage,” Zoe concurred. Burning Man’s ticket prices range from the low $200s to the mid-$1000s, meaning just four of the most expensive tickets cover the $4,000 summer wage for a worker like Jacob. (Burning Man has had over 70,000 attendees each year for the past few years.) Jacob said he was grateful that food, water, and power were provided. “There’s shade that’s built for [workers], but you don’t get housing,” he noted. Jacob noted that food was not provided in years past — a fact that management often held over their heads as evidence that they were “lucky.” “I don’t know if this is the flex that you think it is,” Jacob said. “We’re talking about basic human rights.” Regarding health care, Jacob said there’s an occupational medicine team and emergency medics on hand. Salaried employees, like managers, do have health care, he added. Three of the workers with whom I spoke with said they made between $10 and $20 an hour and characterized this as typical. O’Donnell, who holds an International Alliance of Theatrical Stage Employees (IATSE) union card, said that they started working at Burning Man at $10 per hour in 2019. This was a great contrast to union pay: “If I was doing stagehand work or if I was doing work for the film union, I think the scale was like $40 an hour,” they added. A gathering of bikers at the 2010 Burning Man festival. ( Christopher Michels ) Yet the work was more perilous. O’Donnell said that the current they were dealing with on the playa far outstripped what they worked with in nonunion theater jobs. “To do some of the tie-ins that I was doing at [Burning Man] . . . for liability reasons, they wouldn’t even let me make those connections” when doing nonunion stage work, they said. Though the pay is low, the conditions are dangerous, and the benefits poor, what brings these workers back is the people. “Nobody’s doing this because it gets anybody lucratively rich,” Jacob said. Burning Man workers do have a strong “sense of family and belonging.” “I’d like to give the org the benefit of the doubt and think that they don’t use that against us. But if you look at the world, there’s all kinds of industries that thrive on the idea that if you love your work, then that’s instead of getting paid a living wage,” he added. Notably, a crew lead without hiring authority classified as a salaried manager might constitute a misclassification issue, a frequent subject of labor lawsuits. (We reached out to Burning Man for clarification about this but have not received a response.) Given this situation, it makes sense that Burning Man workers might consider organizing a union. After Romero filed a complaint with the National Labor Relations Board, the board sided with Romero, and the Burning Man organization was forced to make an official apology and offer Romero a settlement. The testing worked initially to stop the spread of COVID in 2022. “My own department tested three times a week and suffered zero illnesses preseason and event week,” the anonymous worker said. “All of the crews remained largely almost completely uninfected — until the event opened and the masses, subjected to zero requirements, arrived. And then our postevent crews were dropping like flies.” O’Donnell, an IATSE member, remembers the “comprehensive list of COVID precautions that had been negotiated between IATSE and the Teamsters and the film unions and then the studios.” O’Donnell says that middle management showed the IATSE agreement to Burning Man higher-ups to model what a safe return to work might look like. Commercial filming would appear to violate this principle, by monetizing the Burn. Moreover, an assumed right to attendee privacy is baked into the DNA of Burning Man, exemplified clearly in the practice of participants using a “playa name” separate from their real name. That’s because attendees and workers alike occasionally do things that a normal employer might frown upon if it were known, from “lewd” behavior to drug use. Martha concurred that the presence of a film crew was incongruent. “This film crew is here just filming everybody at this time where, if this footage gets out into the real world, maybe that person isn’t out to their family or boss. . . . And it’s unconscionable anyway,” Martha said. O’Donnell, who often works in the film industry, was confused at the sudden appearance of cameras filming their postwork, blowing-off-steam moments. “We were like, ‘Listen, unless you’re gonna have everybody sign a fucking release, your footage is dead. You don’t have releases for any of this,” O’Donnell said. Martha had a similar experience: encountering a commercial film crew at the end of the build days and being taken aback at their private, groundbreaking tradition being filmed. “This is a time when there are people who are not wearing clothes,” Martha said. “They are naked or in various states of undress or sometimes various states of intoxication, because their workday is over.” “This isn’t a documentary that’s to expose a corrupt politician or a public figure that owes the public an explanation,” Martha continued. “These are workers at an event, who did not consent to their off time being recorded.” Burning Man’s website features a page about the documentary and how it fits with their principles. The promotional page seems designed for curious folks who might have googled the documentary and transitions into an advertisement. From the inside perspective of the Burning Man crew, the event is made possible by a lot of labor and sweat. And that’s not scratching the surface of all the other labor paid for by attendees to bring all the other pieces — clothes, vehicles, gasoline, art, and so on — together into one film-worthy epic. Though the crew’s toil isn’t depicted, The Man Will Burn tells other stories from the playa, some of them quite compelling. Numerous airborne shots of the playa during day and night serve as eye candy. The story of a PTSD-struggling veteran and the home he finds at Burning Man is heartwarming. And the film does a good job illustrating the rapturous spirit of the party and its incredibly strong, quirky community. But ultimately, The Man Will Burn posits an inverted, top-down view of how Burning Man works — as though the wealthy and powerful manage and dictate much of what happens and the paid labor is nonexistent. This comes to the fore most powerfully in the third episode, in which we hear the execs wax on about the vital importance of having billionaires at Burning Man. “Burning Man needs the wealth,” Musk tells the camera. As the Burn starts anew this weekend, one might remind Musk that labor creates all wealth.

Nancy Fraser’s Theory of Capitalism Is Politically Vital

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Marjan Ivković ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-31T15:33:38+00:00

Nancy Fraser’s work has had a significant influence on anti-capitalist politics over the past couple of decades. Some of her insights about the relationship between “redistribution” and “recognition” or the “dangerous liaison” of liberal feminism and anti-racism with neoliberal economic agendas have spread beyond left-wing academic circles and entered the vocabulary of political activism. This is precisely what the tradition of critical theory, in which Fraser situates herself, hopes to achieve, by providing resources for the self-clarification of progressive political struggles. In a broader sense, Fraser has formulated a unique theorization of capitalism that responds innovatively to some of the most pressing questions of anti-capitalist political strategy. Fraser’s theory of capitalism has evolved gradually over the years, and we could see her problem-driven, pragmatic, and dialogical road to formulating a systematic account of capitalism as a model of how an engaged thinker should arrive at a “grand theory” of society. Analytical Dualism Fraser has laid out her theory of capitalism systematically in her 2022 book, Cannibal Capitalism , but to fully appreciate the depth of her perspective, one should also consult the earlier works upon which her mature theory builds. The cornerstone of Fraser’s social theory is a form of analytical “dualism.” On the one hand, in opposition to the economic reductionism characteristic of orthodox Marxism, she stresses that material and symbolic reproduction in capitalism are mutually irreducible. Rejecting the schema of base and superstructure, she argues that capitalism is both an economic and a cultural order, a system of economic exploitation and one of status hierarchies, a configuration of material and symbolic forms of domination alike. For Fraser, class is one fundamental mode of social differentiation, rooted in the political-economic structure of society, while status group is another such mode, rooted in the cultural (symbolic) order. Every social actor in capitalism has a particular location along both axes, so that her overall standing within the social hierarchy is the composite of the two positions. Social groups are often subjected to both economic and cultural injustices simultaneously. We can already find this argument about the two dimensions of capitalist society, systemic and cultural, in Fraser’s earliest works — for example, in her critique of Jürgen Habermas, “What’s Critical about Critical Theory? Habermas and the Case of Gender.” In this essay, Fraser argues that the capitalist ideal of the “family wage” was historically conceived as a payment to a man supporting his family rather than a payment to a “genderless individual.” According to Fraser, this “gender-sensitive reading” of the institutional relations in capi­talism between family, economy, public sphere, and bureaucratic state shows us that male dominance is “intrinsic rather than accidental to classical capitalism.” A reading of this kind also reveals the multidirec­tional nature of causality in capitalism. Going against the economic determinism of orthodox Marxism, Fraser demonstrates that culturally elaborated gender norms condition the key political-economic trait of capital­ism: the gender division of labor — or rather, the division between paid work and unpaid reproductive labor. Fraser’s analytical dualism helps us iden­tify what she calls the “subtexts” of societal phenomena in capitalism that have traditionally been considered purely (or overwhelmingly) “economic” or “cultural.” Her critique of Habermas conveys a key political message for anti-capitalist movements: “cultural” orders of domination such as patriarchy are not “secondary” or “residual.” They are intrinsic to capitalism, and anti-capitalist politics must endorse struggles against such injustices as indispensable. Disavowal In the works written over the last decade and a half, Fraser has elaborated upon this key insight. Building on classical Marxist feminism, she argues that capitalism is a social order that institutes a historically arbitrary divi­sion between paid labor and unpaid social-reproductive activities (such as childrearing and caregiving), with the latter being inherently gendered and assigned to women. The capitalist economy depends on the free reproduction of labor power. At the symbolic level, however, such dependence cannot be acknowledged because it would mean admitting that reproductive labor is a highly valuable form of work and should be compensated. The capi­talist economy therefore has a systemic need to disavow its depend­ence on the sphere of social reproduction. How is this disavowal achieved? Precisely through the culturally elaborated gender subtexts of key economic phenomena such as the “family wage” that rein­force and naturalize the gender division of labor, obscuring its arbi­trary character. In addition to theorizing the role of unpaid care work as a condition of possibility for capitalism, Fraser has relied on Karl Polanyi’s work to conceptualize other noneconomic aspects of capitalist social reality that add up to form the “extended” struc­ture of capitalism. These aspects include human relations with nature, the realm of liberal-democratic institutional politics, and global imperial domination based on racial hierarchies. This extended concept of capitalism offers a powerful antidote to bourgeois ideology that treats capitalism as a purely economic order compatible with the political ideal of liberal meritocracy. It is a crucial tool for ideological critiques and emancipatory social struggles, exposing the structural role that forms of domination other than clas­sical exploitation, such as patriarchy and racism, perform in capitalism. Dangerous Liasons It is against the backdrop of Fraser’s appropriation of Polanyi that we should consider her well-known argument about the “dangerous liaison” of liberal emancipatory politics with neoliberal economics. She argues that the current multidimensional crisis of capitalism has been triggered by another major Polanyian “move­ment” toward commodification that is analogous to nineteenth-century economic liberalism. However, while Polanyi could clearly identify a unifying logic of a protectionist “countermovement” to free-market liberalism that ran through such disparate political projects as New Deal America, the Soviet Union, fascist Europe, and postwar European social democracy, Fraser observes that no such clear alternative exists today to the neoliberal project. She explains this lack of a countermovement by expanding Polanyi’s conception that defines the basic political dynamic of capitalism as a “double movement” of “marketization” and “protection” to include the third pole of “emancipation.” She argues that each pole of this “triple movement” derives its concrete politi­cal shape not only from its own internal logic but also from its relations with the other two poles, resulting in the internal ambivalence of all three poles. In the case of the emancipation pole, this ambivalence means that postwar emancipatory movements (New Left, feminist, environmentalist) were internally splin­tered. On the one hand, there were factions that fought against culturally based forms of domination (the protection pole) while giving little consideration to processes of marketization or even endorsing them. On the other, there were factions that simultane­ously fought oppressive forms of protection and marketiza­tion alike, which in effect meant that they were fighting to transform what Fraser terms the “ethical substance” of protection. As she notes, the liberal currents became hegemonic over time and formed an alliance with the neoliberal agents of marketization. In the framework of this alliance, Fraser argues, an “emancipatory critique” of protection in oppressive forms “converged with the neoliberal critique of protection per se.” This is where we can find the key to understanding the present peculiarity of the missing countermovement. Fraser develops Antonio Gramsci’s theory of hegemony along Polanyian lines, arguing that a particular historical (hegemonic) bloc is a specific configuration of two given poles within the triple movement. Our current hegemonic bloc, which is now disintegrating, is an amalgam of marketization and emancipation that Fraser terms “progressive neoliberalism.” What anti-capitalist politics needs to forge if it is to build a counterhegemonic bloc is a combination of protection and emancipation. Epistemic Shifts But even more fundamental than Polanyi’s work for Fraser’s theorization of capitalism is what she considers to be Karl Marx’s basic method, that of “epistemic shifts.” What Marx essentially did was dig beneath the bourgeois political economy that focuses on markets — the exchange of com­modities of equivalent value — as the central element of capitalism. Beneath this “front story,” Marx identified the “dirty secret” of how production itself is organized in capitalism: through exploitation, that is, the extraction of surplus value from labor. Here we find the first “hidden abode” of capitalism, as Fraser calls it: the nonequivalent relation of exploitation beneath the equivalence of market exchange. But Marx’s method doesn’t stop there, as Fraser stresses. In the chapter on “primitive accumulation” in Volume 1 of Capital , he shifts the perspective once more, uncovering an “abode behind the abode.” Marx identifies at the historical outset of capitalism the brutal process of dispossession (what Fraser calls “expropriation”) that separated people from their means of subsistence through enclosures, theft, and colonial conquest. The method of epistemic shifts, which allows us to identify ever deeper layers of dom­ination in capitalism, constitutes for Fraser the very essence of critical theory. Relations between capitalist economy and all of its background conditions of possibility — nature, politics, and social reproduction — follow the Marxian logic of primitive accu­mulation, which involves taking whatever one can without any compensation while denying and covering up the whole process. Fraser sums this logic up with the term “freeriding.” To reproduce itself and thrive, capitalist economy must freeride on the unpaid reproductive labor of women, on nature as a free “tap” of resources, and on political (or public, as Fraser prefers) power as a guarantor of the legal frame­work of economic coercion. To enable such freeriding, capitalist society institutionalizes the historically unique boundaries that define its basic structure. The first such boundary is that between the economy as a sphere of paid work and social reproduction as the sphere of unpaid reproductive labor. The second is the boundary separating nature and the human world. The dividing line between polity and economy (or to put it more bluntly, between the overt coercion of armed force and the covert coercion of the labor market) makes it possible to remove all “economic” matters from the sphere of political decision-mak­ing. Finally, there is the distinction between “exploitable” and “expropriable” labor. For Fraser, this boundary largely follows the “color line,” one that has been historically constituted in imperialist terms as a divide between exploited workers (who are considered to be white) and expropriable subjects (who are not). Moral Grammars The last boundary is the most complex one. “When properly understood,” Fraser argues in her 2018 debate with Rahel Jaeggi, “it clarifies the structural place of imperial and racial oppression in capitalist society.” She applies her analytical dualism to this institutionalized division of capitalism, defining it as simultaneously economic and political. Viewed economically, exploitation and expropriation are two mechanisms of capital accumulation, with the former structurally dependent on the latter, not just in the early days of capitalism but throughout its history up to the present. Expropriation helps sustain the official capitalist economy of exploitation by lowering the costs of production and reproduction of labor. Viewed politically, on the other hand, the exploitation/expro­priation boundary is a status boundary that is institutionalized through the actions of states. If we combine the two vantage points, we may reasonably conclude, as Fraser does, that what we call “race” is simply “the mark that distinguishes free subjects of exploitation from dependent subjects of expropriation.” Since capitalism is built around constitutive boundaries, Fraser argues, it inevitably gives rise to bound­ary struggles. These are multisided historical conflicts around the exact location of the boundary line, in which actors mobilize various “moral grammars” of capitalist society against the logic of capital and its own moral grammar of efficiency and meritocracy. In the sphere of social reproduction, the norms of reciprocity, care, and solidarity challenge the capitalist grammar. In noncapitalist human interactions with nature, the norms of stewardship, sustainability, and justice between generations are operative. Finally, in the sphere of democratic politics, norms of democracy, equal citizenship, and the public interest take precedence. Many Fields of Struggle According to Fraser, boundary struggles over the demarcation lines between economy and polity, production and reproduction, society and nature, and exploitation and expropriation are just as fundamental to capitalism as class struggles within the field of capitalist economy. Fraser’s mature works thus reiterate the key political insight of her earliest ones in a more elaborate form: capitalism is a social order built on multidimensional forms of structural domination, and anti-capitalist politics must endorse and incorporate all struggles that challenge the various dimensions of this domination without discriminating between them. This includes environmentalist, feminist, and anti-racist movements as well as struggles to deepen and radicalize liberal democracy.

The People’s Dolly

I cannot easily remember a national treasure that has inspired a larger outpouring of eulogies, memories, and declarations than the passing of Dolly Parton. Personally, after hearing the news, I unplugged and had a day listening to the music, and — of course — watching the 1980 blockbuster 9 to 5 (which was only outsold by The Empire Strikes Back).The madcap comedy starring Dolly, Jane Fonda, and Lily Tomlin gave voice to all the girls in the typing pool, the women of what Tomlin calls “the pink-collar ghetto,” where they are underpaid, overworked, sexually harassed, insulted, demeaned, overlooked, unrecognized, micromanaged, surveilled for any hint of dissent — or even complaint — and — of course — stolen from, by shitty bosses who get ahead by taking credit for somebody else’s work and ideas. And all while they are beset by dumb exes, family responsibilities, and comprador middle-management secretarial tyrant traitors to working women — right in their own office.After elaborate fantasies of revenge, the three ladies — by a comedy of errors set off by sheer exhaustion and frustration — end up kidnapping their boss and imprisoning him in his own home. In his absence, they decide to run the company “on his behalf,” equal pay is instituted, and we see women of color and disabled people in the office (and even men!). The dress code is relaxed; the unfairly fired are rehired; a daycare is formed; flex and part-time hours are introduced. It’s a full-fledged office takeover (and there’s a happy ending too).Fonda herself produced the movie, inspired by meetings with 9to5, National Association of Working Women (which eventually affiliated with the Service Employees International Union as District 925), founded by labor leaders Karen Nussbaum and Ellen Cassedy while they were working as secretaries at Harvard University. (Check out Julia Reichert and Steven Bognar’s 2021 documentary 9to5: The Story of a Movement to learn more.)9to5 organizer Mary Jung said that most of the members “didn’t consider themselves feminists” and considered themselves “mainstream women,” recalling that “we couldn’t have a ‘demonstration,’ because a demonstration was considered too radical for our members, so we would have ‘actions.’“Reichert emphasized how well Jung read the room: “She’s a really good organizer, and a good organizer . . . recognizes ‘what is the community we’re trying to reach? And let’s not have a litmus test.’“That was the strategy of the 9to5 movement as well. Dolly was initially reticent to take part in an all-female cast with open feminists Fonda and Tomlin, but the fact that it was a comedy with a message, rather than a didactic propaganda film, got her to sign on. In a 2019 interview, Dolly reaffirmed her position, saying, “I still believe that women should get paid equal and should be treated with respect, I’m all about that. I don’t get out and have to preach it or march in the streets; I write about it.”She also put her money where her mouth was when it came to labor.Like most women, Dolly didn’t relate to Valerie Solanas, Germaine Greer, and the bra burners (though ever the self-deprecating charmer, Dolly once joked, “I was the first woman to burn my bra — it took the fire department four days to put it out.”). And finding common ground with “most women” isn’t just politically strategic; it represents a deep love and appreciation of humanity. It also helps to have a sense of humor and a killer song (when Dolly agreed to write the theme, she likely didn’t imagine getting a number one hit on the Billboard Country, Adult Contemporary, and Hot 100 charts).And while she wasn’t reading Karl Marx, she was a card-carrying member of multiple artists’ unions for nearly sixty years. Dave Pomeroy, president of American Federation of Musicians (AFM) of the United States and Canada Local 257 in Nashville, saluted her commitment to her fellow musicians, saying, “Dolly and her team consistently filed AFM union contracts for gigs, sessions, and tours, ensuring that the bands, singers, and studio musicians working alongside her received the benefits of union membership.”All writers should consider the room when writing anything, and eulogies even more so. So I know where this is being published, but while celebrating Dolly’s commitment to justice is great, for the love of God, let’s not Gritty-fy her.Sigmund Freud said “the delusions of paranoiacs have an unpalatable external similarity and internal kinship to the systems of our philosophers” . . . or something like that (give me a break, it was all originally in German). What he meant though, was that the endless, pathological need to interpret things, to find hidden messages and deep subtext, is . . . kind of crazy. It’s a liability for anyone — and particularly political people — who end up cracking a code that isn’t really there. A lot of people know that Freud said, “The paranoid is never entirely mistaken,” but they never catch the part right after: “He has a keen eye for something that escapes other people — only he completely misinterprets it and attaches an exaggerated importance to it.”Sometimes leftists have a habit of latching on to people and art and artists by projecting their own motives, values, and worldviews onto them, scrutinizing, analyzing, and decoding secret meanings that aren’t really there, or at least aren’t really all that deep. And Dolly wasn’t a mascot; she was a brilliant artist and a wonderful person.She was gracious, sunny, funny, and glamorous. She took no shit, but with class, laughing off criticism and condescension with grace and quick wit, and stayed above the fray when surrounded — and sometimes pursued — by petty celebrity drama. She didn’t care what people thought of her artistic or personal decisions, only the way she treated people.Her philanthropy ran the gamut. She donated millions of books to children and millions of dollars to disaster relief. She was an animal lover, donating to rescues for both household pets and farm animals. She was a conservationist of her beloved Smoky Mountains, restoring native chestnut trees after a decimating blight and opening a bald eagle sanctuary right in Dollywood.She was a star without being a diva and loved working with other people, just as comfortable on harmony as melody. She was experimental and tried new things throughout her career, often with other people, whether it be the trio with Linda Ronstadt and Emmylou Harris, or with gay pride parade grand marshal leather daddy Rob Halford. She was loyal and helped the people who helped her come up when they were on their way back down.She never forgot her roots, as one of twelve children who grew up in the cabin you can see on the cover of My Tennessee Mountain Home. My Papaw (one of nine) and Mamaw (one of thirteen) both grew up in cabins just like it — no plumbing, electricity, or indoor running water — and they have no one to tell except our family. There aren’t a lot of Appalachians with the platform to talk about that history, or what it’s like now, for that matter. But she never stopped singing about it, and it lived in her lyrics as much as her music. Her autoharp on a live version of “Wildflowers“ or the Appalachian dulcimer on “My Mountains, My Home“ are transcendent, instruments that echo through the hollers of the heart.And not to be all “name one album,” but as a genuine, longtime hardcore fan, I cannot stress enough what a brilliant songwriter, singer, arranger, and instrumentalist she was. She covered unexpected songs in unexpected ways until they were all hers. She was experimental but still had her signature sounds, like those acrylic nails clicking out percussion on a shuffling rhythm guitar (or standing in for a typewriter on “9 to 5"). She had that clear, pure soprano and that delicate, precise vibrato that could launch into a sweet but powerful belt. She used and sometimes improvised ingenious harmonies, with deft melismas and glissandos, and dynamic, playful variations in volume, sometimes in the same phrase. And those lyrics. Those melodies . . . Like any good country girl, she sang about love, God, and murder. But also about work and struggle and beauty, betrayal, pain, poverty, freedom, home, and family and joy. She sang about being a woman. She told us stories.And so, dear Jacobin reader, I say that everyone should have a little lumpen in them, because while the divide between America A and America B may not be a brick wall, we live in a time of unprecedentedly little common culture. It’s important to see why so many people who seem to have nothing in common are feeling a sense of collective loss, why the atomized masses are a little heartbroken. Because you don’t need a political reason to love Dolly Parton. She’s Dolly. Our Dolly. Woman of the People.

Israel Has Robbed Gaza’s People of Their Homes

One year ago, on August 3, 2025, Israel destroyed my family’s house in El-Tuffah, a neighborhood in Gaza City. Israel did not bomb the house or demolish it with artillery shells. A remote-controlled vehicle packed with explosives drove down our street before detonating. That is how Israel destroyed a house that stood for four generations. During the genocide, our house became shelter for four families. But when it was destroyed, barely any rubble was left. I know how our house appears in the destruction data, because I helped trace and visualize it. At GazaIndex, we trace the data: 134,422 destroyed structures across the Gaza Strip; 34,666 in Gaza City; 2,575 in El-Tuffah. One of them is ours. But what this record does not convey, besides the sweat and tears, is how many families it sheltered during the genocide. It does not tell us that it was the only thing standing between them and a tent. The record of Gaza’s destruction, so far, cannot tell us what was actually destroyed. This blind spot is about to shape Gaza’s future. Every reconstruction plan, from that of Oxfam to Donald Trump’s Board of Peace, is built from this record. Meanwhile, Palestinians appointed to plan the rebuilding are still not allowed to enter Gaza. Before the reconstruction begins, it is worth asking what the record counts, and what it has refused to count so far. What Did a House in Gaza Mean? A house in Gaza was never just a house. For many Palestinians, a home represents generations of sweat and tears. Under occupation and blockade, Gazans cannot put their savings into pensions, 401(k)s, or similar funds. Many families, including mine, invested everything in building a house that could carry us through the good days and the many bad ones. Our house in Gaza stood firm for four generations, carrying our memories and us. My grandparents always talked about how it took them years to build one floor that housed them and my (young) father at the time. My father continued the tradition, renovating the house when it came to hold me and my sister. It was our safety net, our safe space. For many in Gaza, a house was what preserved a family’s pride through the suffering the blockade and occupation produced. Since October 7, the relationship of Gazans with their homes has gone somewhere deeper. For many, the house became a memory, because Israel destroyed it. Others, like my family, evacuated Gaza. Leaving did not make the house mean less. Our house survived months of bombardment and became a shelter. My aunt and her extended family moved in over the first year and a half of the genocide. However, Israel kept attacking the area, often issuing repeated evacuation orders. My aunt and the three other families living there would leave, wait, and return once the Israeli army moved on from the area. Returning always meant going back to more death and destruction. The last time they returned, much of our house was damaged. But in Gaza, half a house is still plenty of house. Our half meant my aunt’s family had a roof above them. They could sleep without fear of catching cold, and winter would not overwhelm a tent. Theirs was the story of many families in Gaza during the genocide. But that story did not last. At roughly 8:30 am on August 26, 2026, a wall of water, mud, ice, and massive boulders roared down the Lhende Khola into the Bhotekoshi and Trishuli river systems. The surge struck without local rainfall or warning sirens, catching border communities like Timure, mid-morning. Initial reports mistook the ground tremors for an earthquake, but satellite and seismic data later confirmed the real trigger: a high-altitude collapse of rock and ice near Langtang Lirung, on the Tibetan side, spanning nearly one square kilometer and generating a ground shock equivalent to a magnitude 5.2 quake. Plunging 1,200 vertical meters into the valley, the avalanche briefly dammed the Lhende River before rupturing, unleashing a debris-laden slurry that tore through Rasuwa, Nuwakot, Dhading, Gorkha, Tanahun, and Chitwan. Environmental drivers and hydrological cascade The disaster exposed the extreme fragility of high-altitude Himalayan ecosystems under a shifting climate. Rising temperatures and accelerating freeze-thaw cycles are steadily degrading permafrost and the cryogenic bonds that hold mountain bedrock together, priming slopes for collapse. This was not an isolated event: smaller glacial lake outburst flood (GLOF) incidents in July 2025 had already weakened local topsoil and riverbank integrity. The catastrophe unfolded in two stages. First, the avalanche formed a short-lived debris dam across the narrow Lhende Khola. Hydrostatic pressure built rapidly behind the ice-and-rock wall as glacial meltwater and river discharge backed up, until the barrier gave way. Second, the released surge raced downstream at speeds exceeding 75 km/h, scouring up topsoil, riverbed gravel, and boulders as it went and turning into a hyper-abrasive slurry that gouged riverbeds, stripped valley walls, and undercut mountain foundations across the Bhotekoshi, Trishuli, and Narayani corridors. Secondary barrier lakes are still forming along blocked upper tributaries, prompting fresh flood warnings downstream. Human toll and structural damage According to Nepal’s National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority (NDRRMA) and the Nepal Police, the toll on life and infrastructure has been devastating. Confirmed deaths have surpassed 669, with victims swept as far as Chitwan (248), Nawalparasi East (158), and Paschim Nawalparasi (75); some remains were recovered up to 240 kilometres away in India’s Uttar Pradesh. Between 1,000 and 2,426 people remain missing, including 933 hydropower workers, dozens of security personnel from the Nepal Army, Police, and Armed Police Force, customs officers, residents, and over 500 foreign nationals mostly trekkers and Kailash Mansarovar pilgrims from more than 30 countries. On the recovery front, between 3,700 and 4,400 survivors have been treated or evacuated, including nearly 200 workers pulled from flooded underground hydropower tunnels. Damages are now estimated to exceed NRs 200 billion. The flood destroyed more than 80 bridges (35+ motorable, 45+ suspension) and washed out 40 kilometers of the vital Betrawati–Rasuwagadhi trade corridor, halting overland commerce with China. Thirteen run-of-the-river hydropower plants including the 111 MW Rasuwagadhi and 60 MW Upper Trishuli 3A sustained direct structural damage, knocking hundreds of megawatts off the national grid. Ecological disruption and human-made risk Beyond the human and economic cost, the scouring wiped out aquatic habitats and macroinvertebrate populations across Langtang National Park and its buffer zones. Millions of tons of topsoil were stripped from mountain slopes, while heavy silt deposits degraded farmland further downstream. Environmental assessments warn that unstable debris left in the upper tributaries could form new barrier lakes, raising the risk of further outburst floods when the rains return. Human activity in these narrow corridors amplified the destruction. Encroaching highway construction, constricted floodplains, and loose excavation muck from active building sites added extra material to the flood’s path. Once the surge hit, this debris and the narrowed riverbeds turned built infrastructure into secondary hazards that intensified the wave’s force. Emergency response and cross-border cooperation Nepal deployed over 15,000 security personnel from the Army, Police, and Armed Police Force alongside military and private helicopters for air-bridge evacuations, medical drops, and search operations. NDRRMA set up 12 Sector Coordination Teams to manage missing-person tracing, grid restoration, and aid delivery, backed by relief funds and truckloads of food, shelter, and medical supplies. Because high-altitude hazards cross borders, the response demanded fast regional coordination. China activated emergency protocols around Gyirong Port, sending over 500 rescue workers, while Chinese hydrological teams shared real-time water-level and barrier-lake satellite data with Nepal’s Flood Forecasting Division. In India, recovery operations ran in the border districts of Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, gates at the Valmikinagar Barrage on the Gandak River were opened to manage surge volumes, and helplines were set up to trace missing pilgrims. The US, the IFRC, and global relief networks contributed emergency funding, shelter materials, traveler-tracking support, and diplomatic assistance for affected foreign nationals. Preventing a repeat of this scale of disaster will require stronger transboundary data-sharing, strictly enforced riparian buffer zones, comprehensive cumulative Environmental Impact Assessments for river-basin projects, and satellite-integrated early-warning networks built specifically for dynamic, high-altitude Himalayan conditions.
