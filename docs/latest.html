Gloria Steinem Was the Godmother of Neoliberal Feminism

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Jenny Brown ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-04T21:33:27+00:00

Gloria Steinem, the establishment’s favorite radical feminist, died on September 2. The mainstream media has gone all out in praising her, with the Wall Street Journal and The New York Times united in their adulation. The Times called her the “personification of the women’s movement” and credited her with helping to usher in “sweeping cultural transformations across virtually every facet of American life, from the boardroom to the courtroom to the bedroom.” They approvingly quote her self-description as an “entrepreneur of social change.” For many people, she is the only radical feminist they can name, eclipsing the extraordinary women who planned and launched the successful women’s liberation movement starting in 1967. Steinem did wield enormous influence. Ms . magazine and the Ms. Foundation for Women, which she cofounded, came to define what was worthy in the feminist project. But to the women’s liberation pioneers she displaced, this meant silencing the radicals and promoting the anodyne. When Steinem described Ms . as “a kind of connective tissue for women all across this country who felt isolated until we came along and let them know they were not alone,” feminist pioneers saw Steinem as taking credit for work already done by the movement. The women’s liberation movement didn’t need Ms . to publicize it. It had already been on the front page of nearly every mass circulation magazine and had produced ten mass-market anthologies by 1971, selling millions of copies. The movement published one hundred small newspapers and journals, including Off Our Backs , It Ain’t Me Babe , and Woman’s World . And it had already won substantial victories by the time Steinem emerged on the scene. Thousands of working-class women were suing employers for sex discrimination. Abortion was legalized in New York State in 1970. The Equal Rights Amendment passed the House of Representatives in 1971 and the Senate in March 1972, followed immediately by twenty-two states. The Rise of Ms. Magazine So it was with an eye to the market already created by the radicals that Steinem, then thirty-seven and a professional magazine writer, collaborated with New York magazine editor Clay Felker to start Ms . magazine. The inaugural issue appeared as a section of New York in December 1971; it launched separately in July 1972. Washington Post publisher Katharine Graham invested, and Warner Communications put in $1 million but asked only for a 25 percent stake, an unusually generous arrangement. Some women’s liberation pioneers were at first glad to hear of the new publication. Kathie Sarachild of Redstockings , who coined the phrase “Sisterhood Is Powerful” and authored the germinal 1968 “Program for Feminist Consciousness-Raising,” said she initially though Ms . would act as a feminist muckraker magazine, doing investigative journalism on behalf of women’s cause. She and other women’s liberation movement founders were soon disillusioned. It’s not that Steinem didn’t know who had led the movement up to the point she entered. In 1969, writing for New York magazine, she had attended the first-ever Redstockings abortion speak-out. She recalled the scene in her 1983 book, Outrageous Acts and Everyday Rebellions : A local feminist group had asked women to testify about their real life experiences with illegal abortion. I sat in a church basement listening to women stand before an audience and talk about desperately trying to find someone who would help them, enduring pre-abortion rapes from doctors, being asked to accept sterilization as the price of abortion, and endangering their lives in an illegal, unsafe medical underground. . . . Suddenly I was no longer learning intellectually what was wrong. I knew. I had had an abortion when I was newly out of college, and had told no one. Yet the women who had taught her so much largely failed to find a place in the pages of Ms . three years later. Ellen Willis, pop music critic for The New Yorker and a Redstockings founder, did manage to make the cut starting in 1973. She resigned after two years, calling Ms . editorial policy “conservative, anti-left feminism.” In her resignation letter, Willis wrote: “No attempt has ever been made to recruit and hire experienced feminist writers, theorists, organizers. . . . Ms .’s politics [include] a mushy, sentimental idea of sisterhood designed to obscure political conflicts between women.” “Basically, it’s a change yourself line, the women’s magazine line,” Sarachild reflected later. “But the tricky thing about Ms . is that they pretend to be different. I bet there are certain women who wouldn’t bother reading women’s magazines who read Ms .” The editing reflected the politics, and radicals described Ms . editing as torture. Patricia Mainardi, author of the groundbreaking 1968 manifesto the “The Politics of Housework,” recalled: They would use a bullshit word instead of a strong word. I had the word “loved.” They changed it to “had a fondness for.” . . . Cindy Nemser wrote in an article about Alice Neel, “Alice Neel is the foremost portrait painter in our time.” In Ms . this became, “She may indeed be the foremost portrait painter of the last 40 years.” It’s sneaky editing, downgrading women’s contribution to art. Ms .’s line is women are no good in art. They have been oppressed and damaged by their oppression. They’re inferior. It’s a goody-goody line: we have to work harder to overcome our oppression. This made a sad contrast to the radicals, whose daring truth-telling had organized millions on the idea that what appeared to be women’s individual, personal problems were in fact political and rooted in their oppression by men. This was what they meant by the slogan “ The Personal Is Political .” Establishment Feminism Ms . pioneered the use of private foundation funding to steer feminist movement priorities. The Ms. Foundation dispensed money to approved feminist projects and created a database of feminists around the country and the world. It seeded the founding of the National Black Feminist Organization (NBFO), a Ms .-aligned black women’s group. The NBFO is often cited as the very first black feminist organization in the United States. Repeating the pattern for the women’s liberation movement as a whole, the group blotted out the more radical organizing that preceded it, including the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee Black Women’s Liberation Committee, its successor, the Third World Women’s Alliance, and the Patricia Robinson–led group Poor Black Women, all groups with radical and anti-imperialist politics. Before the rise of Ms ., movement originators had received widespread coverage. Kate Millett was on the cover of Time in 1970. That same year hundreds of feminists occupied the offices of Ladies’ Home Journal editor John Mack Carter, demanding an issue they edited (and free daycare for the magazine’s workforce). They won an eight-page supplement titled “The New Feminism.” But soon, the media went to Steinem for their “radical” feminism. Redstockings called it the “historic invisibility treatment”: The women who developed the movement’s strategy were written out of the picture. They became anonymous foremothers — while many were still in their twenties and thirties. Betty Friedan was too well-known to sideline, but the media loved to counterpose Steinem to Friedan, the older, unhip, allegedly less radical feminist who founded the National Organization for Women in 1966. In fact, Friedan’s politics were well to the left of Steinem’s — under her maiden name, Goldstein, Friedan had served as a staffer of the United Electrical, Radio and Machine Workers of America, the union that held out against the demand that US unions expel their leftist leaders. While Friedan presented herself as reformist to evade red-baiting, she argued astutely for undermining the the basis of men’s power over women by advocating for programs and structures that would give women the economic foundation for independence from men. “Without childcare, it’s all just talk,” she argued. Steinem, styled as more radical, volunteered for Angela Davis’s bail committee, posed with her fist raised with Dorothy Pittman Hughes in October 1971, and toured the country with radical black lawyer Florynce Kennedy to lecture on feminism. Steinem loved witty aphorisms that expressed terrible politics. She quoted actress Lee Grant: “I’ve been married to a Marxist and a Fascist, and neither one of them ever took out the garbage.” Aside from garbage being a rare bit of male-coded housework, the quote implies that communists and fascists are equally bad. You wouldn’t expect a fascist to do housework. She liked to quote a woman cab driver in Boston who told her, “If men could get pregnant, abortion would be a sacrament.” While funny, this gets the causality of women’s oppression backward. Founding radical feminist theorist Simone de Beauvoir argued that women are oppressed because we do reproductive labor: if men could get pregnant, they would be the oppressed sex. Steinem’s concept of a “liberated woman” (who, as she quipped, has sex before marriage and a job after) directly contradicted the the very heart of the radical movement’s argument — that women are oppressed as a class and can’t make themselves free individually. Where did her politics come from? She seemed have one foot in the establishment from the start, dating John F. Kennedy speechwriter Ted Sorensen; then Franklin Thomas, who would soon lead the Ford Foundation; and, in the 1980s, real estate developer Mortimer Zuckerman. Vivian Gornick recalled, “Gloria was uptown; we were downtown. She hung out with Establishment figures; we had only ourselves.” Steinem and the CIA In 1967, the left-wing magazine Ramparts exposed the river of secret CIA money flowing into the National Student Association, the United States’ largest student group. After the revelations, Steinem spoke to the press about her role in recruiting anti-communist students to attend and disrupt the World Youth Festivals, which started in the socialist world and planned for the first time to venture outside the iron curtain in Vienna in 1959 and Helsinki in 1962. She worked full-time as director of the Independent Service for Information on the Vienna Youth Festival, later renamed the Independent Research Service (IRS), a CIA conduit that flooded the festivals with anti-communist and pro-US propaganda. At the Helsinki festival, which drew 18,000 people from 137 countries, the IRS produced a daily newspaper published in three languages, coedited by Steinem and Clay Felker. One of the 1959 publications with her name on it, A Review of Negro Segregation in the United States , claimed that the problem of racial discrimination had essentially been solved in the United States and that leftover problems were due to black people’s psychology. Low voter registration was because “in many areas of the South there exists a profound apathy among Negroes to the this paramount responsibility of good citizenship.” The CIA’s goal had been to make the festivals so fraught — by encouraging violent clashes — that the countries sponsoring them would cancel them. As it turned out, the following two were canceled because the planned hosts, Algeria in 1965 and then Ghana in 1966, experienced coups. In Ghana, the CIA was involved, according to agency officer John Stockwell. In the 1967 interviews, Steinem said the CIA funding was no problem. “Far from being shocked by this involvement I was happy to find some liberals in government in those days who were far-sighted and cared enough to get Americans of all political views to the festival,” she told The New York Times . She also said she was not asked to report on people she met there. Since Steinem wasn’t well-known at the time, it was eight years later that the New York–based feminist group Redstockings discovered the connection, after Steinem had rocketed to the front of the movement. Redstockings published an exposé in 1975, suggesting that the substitution of Ms . and Steinem for the “original, authentic activists that started the movement” looked similar to other CIA operations that “create or support ‘parallel’ organizations which provide an alternative to radicalism.” It also pointed out that a report published by Steinem’s organization on the Vienna festival included short dossiers on dozens of student leaders from around the world — contrary to her claim she never reported on attendees. Steinem at first refused to respond to the charges, but when she did, she again defended her role and that of the CIA at the festivals. However, she did put considerable effort into suppressing the argument made by Redstockings. Random House planned to bring out a mass-market edition of the self-published book Redstockings book Feminist Revolution , which included a chapter on Steinem. Steinem threatened to sue Random House, and the publisher caved and censored the chapter. Steinem’s lawyers also threatened The Village Voice and even sent a threatening letter to every member of the board of the Columbia Tenants Union in New York City when its newspaper, The Heights and Valley News , printed a series on Steinem and the censorship. Women’s Equality and the Neoliberal Turn By the late 1980s, Steinem was famous for Ms . and for being the radical feminist but had not yet written a book laying out her vision of feminism (the anthology Outrageous Acts and Everyday Rebellions in 1983 collected her writing in Ms .and elsewhere). When it finally came, Revolution From Within: A Book of Self-Esteem (1992) affirmed everything about her politics that had dismayed the radicals who started the movement. The book, which received a six-figure advance, continued the women’s magazine line that women needed to make internal changes. “Without self-esteem, the only change is an exchange of masters; with it, there is no need for masters,” she wrote. Of course, we don’t have masters because we need them but because they need us . This basic inversion was symptomatic of her politics. By contrast, women’s liberation feminists argued that women, like other oppressed people, are in constant struggle against our bonds — a concept they called the “Pro-Woman Line” — in fact, we take every bit of space available and find ingenious workarounds even when there is none. It was discrimination, exploitation, punishment, and constant everyday pressure that constrained us. This hard-won analysis was developed through the main program of the movement, consciousness-raising . Ms . watered down this important organizing tool, publishing a guide to consciousness-raising that used the name and the form but missed the substance, reducing it to a therapy group where women blew off steam and got sisterly support. The original idea was to use the data that emerged in testimony to assess women’s true situation, dig at the root cause, and analyze who benefits. The program was inspired by the Southern civil rights movement’s “tell it like it is” public testifying and had parallels in the Chinese Revolution’s “speak bitterness” sessions. It was not intended as a safe space. As corporate attacks in the late 1970s and early ’80s destroyed unions and working-class living conditions and wages plunged for all workers, Friedan and feminist radicals alike argued that advancing women’s equality now required confrontation with corporate power to get guarantees of childcare and health care, paid family leave, and shorter work hours for everyone. Friedan called it the “second stage” of feminism in a book by that name. Redstockings called for the abolition of private health insurance companies and a robust social wage, to give women independence from men and employers. The Ms . wing of the movement fragmented into thousands of nonprofits, competing for grants from rich people by each proposing “winnable” small-bore solutions to the wreckage left by the employer offensive. This blinkered feminist vision was reflected in the Democratic Party establishment. Meanwhile, long hours, low pay, and worsening conditions for raising kids made women vulnerable to a right-wing nostalgia for to the “glorious thirty” postwar years of family-supporting jobs and stay-at-home moms. In recent years, Steinem dropped all pretense of radicalism. She supported Hillary Clinton in the 2016 Democratic primary against Bernie Sanders, and in an interview that year on Real Time With will Maher suggested that young women only supported Sanders because “that’s where the boys are; the boys are with Bernie.” She later claimed she misspoke. Media hagiography of Steinem reached a fever pitch in the last decade with a TV dramatization of her life, The Glorias ; a play, Gloria: A Life ; and glowing profiles in the The New York Times , The New Yorker , and elsewhere. This wave of Steinem-mania coincided with a time that young women were questioning the utility of a 1 percent feminism, represented by such figures as Clinton, Madeleine Albright , and Sheryl Sandberg . Had the goal of women’s liberation really been exploitative corporations and a violent US empire run by women? Steinem was again trotted out to steer the discussion into safe channels, but by the time of her appearance with Maher, she was just giving feminists a bad name. Women’s liberation movement pioneers, to a woman, came out of the civil rights movement, the antiwar movement, and the student left. They believed that consistent feminism leads to socialism, and vice versa. Following Beauvoir, they wanted to dig deep and abolish the exploitation of women and the oppression required to maintain it. Starting in the 1960s, they planned and built a successful radical movement that profoundly changed the situation of women in the United States and reverberated worldwide. It’s a crime that their movement was for fifty years personified by someone who never shared its goals.

Organizing from the Bottom: IBEW’s Fight for Sanitary Facilities

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Detroit DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-04T19:38:39+00:00

Workers in high-visibility gear gather at a downtown Detroit worksite, with the city skyline visible in the background. By Kevin Szczesny To get clean Porta-johns in our workplace, construction workers at the Marriott site in downtown Detroit marched on the boss. Workers from at least seven unions of the building trades were represented, with zero union staffers or officers present. Many union halls were in support of the action, but the work and the application of leverage were entirely rank-and-file driven. As an active member of IBEW Local 58 (Electrical Workers), I often hear from sympathetic organizers who “can’t imagine how hard it must be” organizing in a largely conservative and obstinate workforce. Our campaign is a contribution to the death of that narrative, and an answer to the universal follow-up question about how these shop floor conversations go in detail. Day One on the job, as I met my 50 or so fellow electricians, I was immediately overwhelmed with warnings that the bathrooms were going to be unsanitary. I knew before the end of my shift that the issue was widely felt, and every electrician already had the same idea: we wanted the cleaning schedule changed from twice a week to three times a week. I had a long conversation with the steward about what efforts were already in progress to correct the issue. The first instinct of a construction worker is to “drag” (quit), drive over to the union hall, and pick up a new job. As expected, there was a steady trickle of drags and conditions on site had not improved. OSHA had been called and walked the job soon after my arrival, only to say that the number of bathrooms was up to their standard. The electrical contractor had been notified, but because the bathrooms were for all of the trades to use, our bosses didn’t feel it was their responsibility to pay for additional cleaning. The local IBEW contract only gestures vaguely to “sanitary facilities,” and protects us explicitly should we leave site to find a “sanitary” facility. This clause was exercised regularly, but no one could say for sure whether the boss was even aware we were leaving. Over time, smaller issues would pop up and get resolved quickly with small actions. Sweeping compound to control toxic dust, unprecedented changes in break time, trades doing the work of other trades out of convenience: these were things that were easily managed by the steward with one or two voices in support. I got involved with a couple pushes and so was asked to fill in for the steward while he was out for a few days. When I walked the job to make sure everyone had my number, more than half the conversations touched on the bathrooms. I then introduced myself to the stewards from the other trades, who all encountered the same comments from their members. The idea was formed to take the issue above our bosses and straight to the general contractor (who hires out each trade and is responsible for the job site at large). A letter was written titled “Unacceptable Bathroom Conditions,” which opened politely and assumed the boss was ignorant of the problem. The letter referenced the impact on our work and a widespread feeling that we were not being treated with respect by the contractor. We suggested that the person responsible for scheduling use our bathrooms for a week, to better assess the need for more cleanings and a wash station. We gave one week for a response. Every worker who was presented with the letter signed immediately, but reactions were mixed. Most people felt like they would just laugh at our demand, but were interested in the idea of swarming the bosses’ trailer at lunch and forcing them to read it in front of us.We fielded many suggestions, from delivering the letter and going home to tipping over Porta-johns on the way to the boss, and we told our coworkers that if our response date came and went without change, we would be coming back around “to have a different conversation.” This demand had to be made collectively and clearly first so that any future action would be naturally tied to the lack of clean facilities, and we had to start our escalation low enough that further demonstrations would be felt. Many of our one-on-one talks were focused on assuring the workers that we would circle back for an escalation if the letter went ignored. “This is only the beginning”, “they probably will ignore this or lie, but we need to show that we’re all on the same page first,” “dragging only leaves behind the people who can’t quit with shitty conditions,” etc. We warned of possible counter-strategies from the boss and stressed the importance of demonstrating solidarity, especially across the trades, above all else. We also left out the officers at the hall and our reps on purpose, though we did inform them. We had a lot of conversations about the power in the hands of the rank-and-file. “Every member is a steward” has become sort of a slogan for us. In the end we had 105 signatures and roughly 35 folks across four trades who gave up their lunch break to deliver the letter. The contractor read that letter, was brought outside to look at us, and immediately conceded to our terms. Maybe they didn’t know how bad the bathrooms were, maybe they didn’t know how widely and deeply felt the issue was on site, or maybe they figured it was fine to ignore. All we know for sure is, when a seemingly small fraction of us came together with the backing of three times as many signatures, it didn’t matter if we had the hall behind us. We are the union, the working people who don’t walk away from a job which doesn’t satisfy us, and our demands have been met. Kevin is a trade unionist and has been a member of the IBEW for two years and DSA member for five. Ruth First’s Legacy Has Outlived the Regime That Killed Her

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Christopher J. Lee ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-04T15:15:38+00:00

In August 1982, a parcel bomb went off at the Center for African Studies at Eduardo Mondlane University in Maputo, Mozambique. Sent by the South African government’s Special Branch, its target and victim was Ruth First, a lifelong member of the South African Communist Party (SACP), who was fifty-seven years old at the time. Her assassination has since become the stuff of legend, a symbol of the violent brutality of the apartheid government toward its critics in the last decades of its existence. The murder of First was part of a broader effort by the white minority state to eliminate its opponents following the Soweto Uprising of 1976, which resulted in a revival of (and a generational shift within) the anti-apartheid struggle. Inspired by the ideas of Steve Biko, the Black Consciousness Movement took to the streets of townships across South Africa. Facing arrest and often worse, many sought refuge and exile in neighboring countries, the so-called Frontline States, which included Botswana, Tanzania, Zambia, Angola, Zimbabwe, and Mozambique. Counterinsurgency raids by the South African Defense Force and Special Branch units ensued, targeting these activists with little regard for the national sovereignty of the countries where they had taken shelter. Ruth First cut a different profile from many other liberation figures. A generation older than the members of the Black Consciousness Movement, she undermines many stereotypes about who was involved in the struggle, for what reasons, and to what ends. Her assassination underscored the complex and evolutionary nature of South Africa’s liberation history and the extreme lengths that the apartheid government would go to avoid its demise. Beginnings Born in Johannesburg on May 4, 1925, Heloise Ruth First was white, Jewish, middle class, university educated, a wife, and a mother of three daughters. Each of these qualities informed her activism in distinctive ways, positioning her at different moments as a political outsider, a party confidant, a fiercely engaged intellectual, and a mother both protective and absent, depending on the circumstances. Her husband, Joe Slovo, was a key figure in the SACP and one of the founders of uMkhonto weSizwe (MK), the military wing of the African National Congress (ANC) and the SACP that was established in 1961. He later became general secretary of the SACP and minister of housing in South Africa’s post-apartheid government during Nelson Mandela’s presidency. First’s life reflected this personal and political partnership, with her actions and outlook shaped by their marriage, if not always determined by it. Indeed, although the role of the family in liberation struggles has at times been recognized, it has frequently been under-conceptualized as a source of political influence and decision-making, in addition to providing security, comfort, and support during periods of political uncertainty. The patriarchal norms of anti-colonial movements have too often been reproduced uncritically, with the heroes and philosophers of revolution remaining men — think of Frantz Fanon, Patrice Lumumba, and Steve Biko. To guard against this tendency, it is important to grasp how personal networks among families and peers alike contributed to the making of liberation ideals and actions. Alan Wieder has noted in his dual biography of First and Slovo how the anti-apartheid struggle in particular was defined by couples, with Nelson and Winnie Mandela epitomizing such intimate political partnerships. First and Slovo’s marriage was equally emblematic of the intersection between political and private commitments. Ruth First herself grew up in a political household, which shaped her socialist outlook and activist future. Her father, Julius, and mother, Tilly, emigrated to Johannesburg from Latvia and Lithuania, respectively, as part of a broader influx of Jewish immigrants to South Africa during the first decades of the twentieth century. Both became involved with the Communist Party of South Africa (CPSA), as the party was named after its founding in 1921. Ruth First enrolled at the University of the Witwatersrand, where she met Slovo, who had returned to South Africa after having served in World War II. The CPSA had supported the fight against Nazi fascism. She also became friends with Mandela and other student activists like Harold Wolpe, who later became known for pioneering the concept of racial capitalism. First’s student activism soon extended beyond campus through her support of the 1946 miners’ strike, which involved approximately 74,000 Black workers. Following the economic stresses of World War II, it proved to be a pivotal moment in labor organizing, extending a long tradition of industrial action that struck at the heart of the country’s mineral economy and its exploitation of black South Africans. Finding a vocation for journalism, First went on to write for the Guardian in Johannesburg and New Age , a leftist paper aligned with the ANC, where she covered black politics. She and Slovo became romantically involved in 1948, the year the conservative National Party came to power with its extremist platform of racial segregation known as apartheid. They married the following year in 1949. Activism and Repression The 1950s were a heady period of activism and even optimism, when the possibility of toppling the apartheid government seemed within reach. The 1950 Suppression of Communism Act forced the CPSA to dissolve publicly and reform underground as the SACP. This shift converged with a growing alliance between the SACP and the ANC. This partnership had origins going back to the 1920s, when James La Guma of the CPSA and Josiah Tshangana Gumede of the ANC both attended the 1927 League Against Imperialism conference in Brussels. They traveled on to Moscow where together they met with Nikolai Bukharin, then general secretary of the Communist International (Comintern). This meeting resulted in the Native Republic thesis, a two-part political strategy for South Africa that argued for black national liberation in advance of communist revolution. This approach came out of Vladimir Lenin’s “Draft Theses on National and Colonial Questions,” which were issued at the Second Congress of the Communist International in 1920. Though the CPSA had dropped the thesis as an explicit platform by the time of World War II, its general approach informed the ANC-SACP alliance in the decades ahead. For SACP members, the thinking behind the alliance was as follows: the ANC would spearhead a black liberation struggle aimed at achieving a national democratic revolution, which would be succeeded in turn by a SACP-led socialist state. This collaboration generated intellectual debates over the national question and the idea that South Africa constituted a “colonialism of a special type.” Members of both organizations viewed the system of apartheid as fundamentally colonial in nature through its elements of political disenfranchisement and economic extraction, thus aligning their ambitions with anti-colonial movements across Africa and around the world. However, there was less clarity around questions of political timing, tactical coordination, and what a future South Africa might look like. Such questions would preoccupy First in the decades ahead. The defendants at the Treason Trial pose for a photo in December 1956. (Eli Weinberg / Mayibuye Archive) Under the leadership of Chief Albert Luthuli, the ANC spearheaded the multiracial Congress Alliance during the 1950s with mass actions, including the 1952 Defiance Campaign, which involved strikes, boycotts, and civil disobedience against pass laws and other apartheid measures. This public resistance culminated with the Congress of the People in 1955, held in Soweto, which involved the member organizations of the Congress Alliance and resulted in the promulgation of the Freedom Charter, which demanded a nonracial, fully democratic South Africa. The apartheid government responded swiftly by arresting 156 activists for their involvement in the Congress of the People, accusing them of attempting to overthrow the state. First and Slovo were among those detained. The Treason Trial subsequently unfolded from 1956 to 1961, depriving the activists of political momentum but also providing them with a stage to communicate their anti-racist principles to a global audience. The Treason Trial proved to be a moment of public solidarity that unified different activists across organizations, genders, and racial lines. In addition to First and Slovo, among the accused were Nelson Mandela, Oliver Tambo, Walter Sisulu, Moses Kotane, Helen Joseph, Ahmed Kathrada, and Alex La Guma. Mandela would later quip in his autobiography that the trial was “the largest and longest unbanned meeting of the Congress Alliance in years.” Sharpeville Though all the accused were eventually acquitted, the protracted nature of the Treason Trial created an opening for other parties, principally the Pan Africanist Congress (PAC). Founded by Robert Sobukwe, a former ANC member, the PAC emphasized a more strident black nationalism in contrast to the multiracial front of the Congress Alliance. Sobukwe served as a forerunner to Biko and the Black Consciousness movement of the late 1960s and ’70s. On March 21, 1960, the PAC organized a pass law protest involving an estimated five thousand people in Sharpeville. Police opened fire, killing at least ninety-one people and injuring hundreds more. This proved to be a turning point in the history of the liberation struggle. The apartheid government reacted to the Sharpeville Massacre by declaring a state of emergency and banning the PAC and the ANC. Sharpeville also prompted the turn to armed struggle by the ANC and SACP in 1961. Mandela, Slovo, and First were key figures in the development and implementation of this new strategy, which marked a generational shift away from the civil disobedience approach of Luthuli. The next several decades would be defined by the maneuvers of MK, linking the ANC and the SACP with other liberation struggles in Africa as well as similar movements in Southeast Asia, Latin America, and Palestine. The banning of the ANC and the escalation of police surveillance had a major impact on First’s life. She traveled to Swaziland (present-day Eswatini) with her daughters shortly after the state of emergency was declared, at a time when Slovo was detained. She eventually returned to continue her work as a journalist, though her situation began to unravel in 1962 once Mandela and other activists were arrested for their militant activities. The subsequent Rivonia Trial led to Mandela’s sentence of life imprisonment along with seven others in 1964. First herself was detained in August 1963 after police raided her home and found illicit political material. Slovo had already left South Africa in June for what was intended to be a temporary mission for MK. Incarcerated under the ninety-day detention law, First ultimately spent 117 days in prison, an experience retold in her memoir, 117 Days (1965), which conveyed the inhumanity of the apartheid regime for a global audience. “Left in that cell long enough, I feared to become one of those colourless insects that slither under a world of flat, grey stones, away from the sky and the sunlight, the grass and people,” First wrote, depicting the escalating sense of dehumanization she felt. Yet 117 Days also describes her resistance to this process, with the prison symbolizing the broader apparatus of the apartheid state through the cruel spatial intimacies of the prison cell and the brutal Special Branch interrogators she encountered. “In prison, you only see the moves of the enemy,” she later recalled. “Prison is the hardest place to fight a battle.” 117 Days provided a firsthand sense of political incarceration with its gendered and racial dimensions, its psychological challenges through solitary confinement, and its potential for new forms of solidarity. Above all, her account raised a set of fundamental questions. What counted as a “crime” in apartheid South Africa? Who are the victims? Who are the perpetrators? Who is the law for? Where is justice in a situation where the law and morality are diametrically opposed to one another? Exile Released in December 1963, First left South Africa in March 1964, never to return. She was greeted as a celebrity when she arrived in London with her daughters Robyn and Gillian. Yet, as with many others, exile proved to be a complex experience — personally liberating, but at the price of losing touch with one’s homeland. Exile could be long and rife with uncertainty. London was disorienting for First, not just culturally but also because of the new sense of freedom and agency she had. Political work competed with domestic responsibilities. The so-called external mission of the ANC involved diplomatic trips and speeches at numerous venues. Still, though her ongoing activism provided a point of orientation, it was her intellectual work that blossomed. In exile, First’s experience as a journalist translated into longer works of scholarship and political theory after the publication of 117 Days . Above all, First’s writing grappled with questions that animated the policy discussions and debates among ANC and SACP members. Her first book, South West Africa , published in 1963 before she left for exile, presented a critical appraisal of South Africa’s control over what is today Namibia. In effect, the book presented a more expansive engagement with the concept of “colonialism of a special type” by examining South Africa’s colonial rule of the former German territory, which had been granted under the League of Nations mandate system after World War I. First’s later books similarly confronted and tested party ideas through different contexts, as well as through the lens of her personal experience. Her 1980 biography of Olive Schreiner (coauthored with Ann Scott) addressed the life and politics of one of South Africa’s most important socialist intellectuals, a study that undoubtedly held meaning for First. The Mozambican Miner: Proletarian and Peasant , published posthumously in 1983, contributed to discussions of racial capitalism in the wider region of southern Africa. However, it is First’s 1970 book , The Barrel of a Gun: Political Power in Africa and the Coup d’État , which stands out as her major contribution to the literature of revolution and postcolonial political theory. On the surface, it is an account of coups in postindependence Africa, with case studies drawn from Sudan, Nigeria, and Ghana. The book is not an armchair retelling of these episodes. First traveled extensively to gain firsthand knowledge on the ground. As she wrote, she wanted The Barrel of a Gun to be attuned to “the sense of pulsating life in the streets” and to reflect “something of the way people see, and say things about, their condition.” The Barrel of a Gun also reveals a deeper subtext with its focus on African militaries and their political power. First’s keen interest in the topic was shaped by her involvement in the formation of MK and the anti-apartheid movement’s turn to armed struggle. Indeed, her personal papers reveal an early optimistic view of armies as a revolutionary vanguard that gradually shifted as her research progressed. Her conclusions in The Barrel of Gun are very different, underscoring how postcolonial militaries are much more ambiguous in their character. At times, coups could speed up the process of political change to positive ends by ending autocratic regimes. But they could also do the exact opposite by establishing authoritarian rule. First’s most important contribution is to underline the legacy of colonialism in fostering the conditions for postcolonial political instability. In his classic work, How Europe Underdeveloped Africa (1972), Walter Rodney forcefully argued how centuries of European colonialism set back the continent from an economic standpoint. In The Barrel of a Gun , First likewise argues that European colonialism politically underdeveloped Africa, by leaving weak institutions that were vulnerable to military takeover. For this reason, among many, The Barrel of a Gun still stands as a foundational diagnosis of postcolonial Africa in the years immediately following decolonization. Legacies If The Barrel of a Gun marked a certain culmination of First’s thinking, it was certainly not the final answer to her questions about the vicissitudes of political power and the possibilities of revolution in Africa. Her book Libya: The Elusive Revolution , published a few years later in 1974, is a provisional sequel to The Barrel of a Gun , examining the opportunities and limits of the coup that brought Muammar Gaddafi to power in 1969. However, it was the liberation of Mozambique from Portuguese colonial rule in 1975 that opened a new horizon for First. Neighboring South Africa and led by the Marxist-Leninist Front for the Liberation of Mozambique (FRELIMO), independent Mozambique presented her with an opportunity to witness a revolution in southern Africa as it was unfolding. It provided her with a chance to live out her revolutionary ideals. This period of optimism for First, lived in the belief that a Marxist-Leninist revolution would soon cross the border into her native South Africa, proved all too short. The police unit whose members interrogated her in 1963 was the same unit that organized her assassination almost two decades later. Despite her premature passing at the age of fifty-seven, First’s writing and political example live on. Though committed to the South African cause and communism as a political worldview, her work avoided the parochialism of many nationalist thinkers, as well as the constraints of Marxist orthodoxy. Her vision remained expansive by considering case studies beyond South Africa, yet it aligned with questions that preoccupied her comrades and friends in the SACP and the ANC. Like the work of Frantz Fanon, who was a crucial influence for her, First’s perspectives and ideas endure because they were born in the unstable crucible of active struggle, not the sequestered institutional spaces of academia or the conformist atmospheres of party offices. Though the revolution First imagined for South Africa has not occurred, with the two-stage program of the long-ago Native Republic thesis only partly fulfilled, the questions she raised for her country and her continent continue: What is the nature of political power? What is the role of the state in the lives of ordinary people? What is the role of violence in liberation or oppression? How can the lives of workers, both women and men, receive justice? And, finally, what are the conditions for revolution, and what does revolution mean in practice? These questions and the force of politics behind them still deserve answers.

Private Equity’s Pension Heist

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Clark Randall ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-04T14:17:46+00:00

From 2024 to 2025, the two hundred largest pension funds in the United States increased their investment in private credit by 57 percent . It’s now gone up around 650 percent over the past five years. Private credit is the other side of the coin to private equity (PE) — among other things, it provides the loans for PE firms’ leveraged buyouts. The biggest PE firms have massive private credit arms that have, in recent years, overrun their private equity cores. They act as the unregulated winners of the postrecession economy, avoiding scrutiny and taking risks that commercial banks were foreclosed from under the Dodd–Frank Act. Our worker-funded public pensions have historically been the primary capital provider to private credit funds, amounting to around 30 percent of the total market. Many of these same private credit funds have found themselves in a world of trouble over the past twelve months, just as public pensions have loaded them up with increasing capital. The chief investment officer at New York City retirement systems recently told the Financial Times , “We have completely committed to private credit, we are willing to trade illiquidity and complexity for a higher return.” As is the case in all investing, higher ceilings come with lower floors. Credit defaults, according to Fitch ratings, are hitting record highs and nonaccrual status loans, i.e., ones where borrowers are in serious trouble, spiked 40 percent since this March. In response to these issues on the borrower end, private credit funds have extended their lockup period, put hard-line caps on redemptions to prevent runs on capital, and opened up investment to 401(k) holders and individuals to increase capital inflows. It’s unclear whether we have a total crisis on our hands, in part due to shady reporting standards at pension funds, but it is clear that we have a real problem in private credit regardless. The issue is downstream of the problem in private equity where holding periods are up, and by extension, the net asset value (NAV) of funds held over seven years (sometimes referred to as “zombie funds”) has doubled since 2021 . They can’t sell the asset, so they build continuation vehicles to recapitalize and hide the struggle that’s going on. But the real risk of this slowdown weighs on retirees, current and future. When it comes to pension funds, the fees charged by private credit and alternative investment writ large (hedge funds, real estate, and private equity) are unknown to the public. They are also not Freedom of Information Act–eligible, due to bogus trade-secret protections claimed by local governments. That means workers are barred from understanding what their own contributions are going toward. Even if we don’t have smoking guns, instead we have mounting circumstantial evidence that our public pensions are robbed of investment returns by the growth of alternative asset investing and the dark hole of management and performance fee structures. In explaining this problem, the new film Pension Fight Club offers a good, but at times awkwardly laid-out, documentary for those interested in deciphering the relationship between workers’ capital and the excesses of finance capital. Impasse The story of the documentary is twofold: the first being the misrepresentation of investment fees by public pension funds due to either ignorance or quid pro quo exchanges between investment consultants and political officials. We have the example of the Minnesota public pension fund that, following Ted Siedle’s investigation, shifted their investment fee total from $11 million to $188 million across a three-year period. What pension funds often do is report on the fee for assets under management but not the performance fee or the carried interest. That is, after surpassing a set hurdle rate, alternative investment managers take somewhere in the range of 20 percent of profits thereafter. Pension funds don’t count that as investment fees despite the fact that it eats into their rate of return. What’s more: if a pension fund commits $100 million to an alternative investment manager, they pay them for total assets under management, not assets deployed — so, $30 million might sit idly while the pension fund paid a premium for the full amount to be actively managed. The second is the bigger picture: that is, the political economy of public pensions. From political donations to stack the board to investment decisions, public pensions operate in a world of information asymmetry. Those on the inside understand its influence, and everyone else is not let in on the potential costs of pension investment underperformance, the risk of complex exposure, and the behind-the-scenes nature of decision-making. From 2001–2023, fully 99 percent of pension plans failed to meet their assumed rates of return over that period in the aggregate. That means that actuarial calculations of what these funds owe are misunderstood at best and severely underestimated at worst. Despite this overreporting of assets on hand, cities are still spending increasing portions of their budgets paying down pension debt. Sarah Anzia, leading scholar on public pensions, found that over a quarter of US cities and counties more than doubled their nominal pension expenditures between 2005–2016 alone. The context, however, is an era of underfunding, punting, or kicking the can down the road on pension contributions and “ looting the funds ” amid the aftermath of the Great Recession. Still, Anzia’s findings back the concern of the film, demonstrating that public pension troubles are not quite ubiquitous, but they’re intractable once felt. Pension Fight Club traces the impasse facing public pensions. It portrays a dilemma, over whether these funds reveal their exposure to alternative investments and the fees they pay for them or else continue to kick the can down the road as economic conditions tighten. The film depicts the political incentives, bribes, faux policy fixes, and shrouded accounting that make public pensions both the willing and unwitting prey to the new Wall Street — as explored in Brett Christophers’s 2023 book , Our Lives in Their Portfolios . Still, it’s also true that this documentary stumbles in narrativizing a complex story. It jumps from one fund to another, and their struggles with opaque investment policies but leaves the viewer too attached to a singular node, Ted Siedle, as he investigates fund policies — when a larger story could have been told about unions’ (collective) struggles to direct capital investment. It’s a story that is itself complicated enough, showing that finance fixed almost as many issues as it caused unions historically, and can be followed through the work scholars like Michael McCarthy and Brian Judge . In any case, the documentary brings important attention to public pensions and what the years ahead may have in store considering how they invested after the recession. Uphill Struggle Strapped with steep investment losses, surging unfunded liabilities, a shrinking public workforce, and cash-strapped local governments following the 2008 crash, public pension funds wanted out of a hole, fast. Distressed assets flooded the market and private equity, real estate investment trusts, and private credit were there to sweep it up in multifarious, intersecting mechanisms, front the costs, and offload the risk to creditors or the governing bodies they were purchasing from. In this regard, it’s useful to take another look at Christophers’s chapter on the infamous sale of Chicago’s parking meters and underground lots to Morgan Stanley, which resulted in an immediate cash drain on the city. Still, what Christophers’s account misses is that the Illinois Municipal Retirement Fund (IMRF) — the largest in the state — in the prior year’s (2007’s) disclosures , reported over $100 million invested in a Morgan Stanley real estate fund and $17 million in their public stock. The dots don’t always connect in a straight line, but the same story is now decades old: public pensions are funding their own gravediggers . These investments routinely grow the wealth of right-wing, anti-union oligarchs and their corporate monopolies. The fight in Pension Fight Club is as much against the alt-investment managers’ operations as it is the uphill battle to convince the public, especially those without pensions, that banal local pension policies matter. Pension funds are no longer bundled up in fixed-income municipal bonds and Treasuries. Nor are they simply overexposed to equities, which they are and were in the lead-up to 2008. Public pensions acted as the underwriters to an absurd new class of private equity multimillionaires and billionaires — the new power brokers on Wall Street and Silicon Valley. When the Los Angeles Lakers sold to a venture capital consortium recently for a jaw-dropping $12.5 billion, it was a final signal for the sports world that private equity is here to stay. The team’s historic rival, the Boston Celtics, also sold to private equity players for $6 billion a year prior. But where did Joshua Kushner’s (brother of Jared’s) Thrive Capital get its seed funding to make such a purchase? Of the first billion it raised, $300 million came from a CalPERS investment , that is, from California’s state pension fund. To put the matter truthfully but a little too plainly, the workers now own a stake in the Lakers, even though none of them can afford to attend a game at the new Crypto.com Arena. The Pension Fight Club documentary offers valuable evidence that there’s a long way to go in the push-and-pull between reform and deregulation in the field of workers’ capital. My sense is that if the movement to unearth pension funds’ investments remains limited to union activism and siloed lawyers alone, it may not be enough. Any taxpaying resident has good reason to care about how pensions are invested. The first reason is that shoring up these policies will lighten the tax load on local governments which are losing money; the second is the need to quell the idea that pensions are “unaffordable” due to what retirees are being paid. Pensions are a functional structure in need of serious repair, and the crisis can hardly be blamed on meager cost-of-living adjustments or sweeteners offered to working-class retirees. While the media latches onto stories of superintendents and administrators earning six-figure pensions to characterize the problem, the majority of retirees with pensions are bringing in under $40,000. This is a crisis of short-term governance, financial capture, and the unaccountability of this industry.

Francis Fukuyama: The American Right Has Gone Off the Deep End

In 1989, Francis Fukuyama wrote a sweeping philosophical polemic in which he argued that liberal democratic capitalism was the only viable economic and political system. Countries would either converge on its model or drift into dysfunction. What prevented states and individuals from accepting this fate was a fear of being the Last Man, a Nietzschean term for the individual living without ideas worth dying for. A world in which liberal democracy was triumphant was, Fukuyama argued in “The End of History?,” one without the tragedy and pathos of the era of great ideologies — safe but boring.f When he expanded this essay three years later into The End of History and the Last Man , Fukuyama gave voice to a worry that this condition of placid stability may not be able to satisfy the drives of all individuals. Here he echoed G. W. F. Hegel, the philosopher from whom he took the idea of a universal history, who wrote that “within the state, heroes are no longer possible: they occur only in the absence of civilization.” In his upcoming memoir, In the Realm of the Last Man , Fukuyama makes clear that he views figures like Vladimir Putin and Donald Trump as unwilling to pay the cost of progress. They instead hearken back to a time of heroic conflict, refusing to accept the constraints of liberal democratic capitalism. Jacobin spoke to Fukuyama about his own intellectual development from a former Republican policy adviser to the Ronald Reagan administration and member of the RAND Corporation to a Democrat who now describes himself as, with some qualifications, a social democrat. In this interview, Fukuyama also discusses the transformations of the American right, what the Democratic left could learn from Trump, and from Zohran Mamdani’s successes in New York. John-Baptiste Oduor You were about sixteen in 1968 and became a conservative a few years later at Cornell. How did you think about yourself in relationship to the progressive and leftist politics of that period? Did you think you were reacting against them? Francis Fukuyama It was really my teachers at Cornell, particularly Allan Bloom — they were all students of Leo Strauss — who influenced me during that period. Strauss made a complex set of arguments, but basically he argued that the Western philosophical tradition had gotten itself into a dead end with the rise of the relativism that was the core of the teaching of people like Friedrich Nietzsche and Martin Heidegger. That was a problem because people still had moral commitments, which were necessary for society. Strauss argued that it was still possible to use human reason to come to an understanding of basic issues of good and bad that progressive thought had, in a certain way, rejected. I spent a lot of time as an undergraduate going back to Plato and Aristotle because that’s the core of the Western project to use reason to answer big questions about the nature of justice, the nature of human happiness, and so forth. That was really what I was doing when I was an undergraduate. And it wasn’t in reaction to progressivism in particular. I mean, it was a very turbulent time in American politics because the Vietnam War was raging. There was a lot of student activism. And I participated in some of that. Probably my sophomore or junior year, I went to an anti–Vietnam War protest in Washington, DC. John-Baptiste Oduor What in your view was the political content of Straussian conservatism then? Figures like Patrick Deneen and Michael Anton who are close to the current administration take it to imply an ethnonationalist view of Western cultural traditions. Francis Fukuyama I think they have taken a really ridiculous position. I have debated Deneen in the past few years a couple of times. Many people who consider themselves in some way students of Leo Strauss split into different factions. One of the big divisions is between so-called West Coast traditions associated with the Claremont Institute and East Coast students who were more likely to be students of Allan Bloom or possibly Harvey Mansfield. The West Coast traditions, in my view, have just gone off the deep end. A lot of [thinkers associated with them], like Michael Anton, have become die-hard Trump supporters. They’ve forgotten a lot of the basic teachings of people who were students of Strauss like Harry Jaffa, who was really the founder of that whole West Coast school. Patrick Deneen, I think, has discovered a certain celebrity by arguing that we ought to go back before the Enlightenment, to a period when Western countries were united by a single conservative Catholic doctrine. It’s totally absurd that anyone could think this was remotely possible in the year 2026. I don’t associate myself with any of those particular schools. I remain what I regard as a classical liberal, meaning that I believe that liberal democracy is the only practical way to govern a modern society. John-Baptiste Oduor American conservatism has taken an anti-humanist turn over the past ten to fifteen years, particularly under the influence of the tech right. How do you think this happened? Francis Fukuyama I think it’s a complicated story. The person who has written the most intelligently about this is Laura Field. She published a book last year called Furious Minds , where she traces the origins of a lot of this. I believe her account that a lot of this is people who are American nationalists, in some sense, who have dressed up their politics in a theoretical language to defend something that’s just basically American chauvinism. That is part of it. The other part is the opportunism of people who saw Trump rising. They saw that this was a powerful real-world political force that they wanted to attach themselves to. And so, for the last ten years they’ve been tying themselves in knots trying to come up with a highbrow philosophical justification for Trumpism. It just doesn’t work, because I don’t think Trumpism is a coherent doctrine. It’s just the psychopathology of this one individual. All of these theoretical attempts to put him into some a structured framework are bound to fail. People like Peter Thiel and J. D. Vance are good examples. Thiel has started to use this theological language about the Antichrist to explain what Trumpism is about. I think that’s all silly. It’s not serious political theory. John-Baptiste Oduor Speaking of J. D. Vance, figures like him and Tucker Carlson have at points tried to present Trumpism as part of a redistributive pro-worker conservatism. In practice, the politics of the Republican Party has been the same as it has always been: tax cuts and war. Do you have an explanation of why this pro-worker conservatism has so little foothold in reality? Francis Fukuyama In many ways, it was displaced workers in depressed, postindustrial parts of the country that formed part of the original Trump coalition. And I think it’s true that the Democrats and a lot of liberals didn’t do a whole lot for them. Democrats were the ones who were pushing the North American Free Trade Agreement and these different trade liberalization deals that led to the elimination of their jobs. When China was let into the World Trade Organization, it cost a couple million manufacturing jobs. The Democrats did little to combat this. Trump’s voter base was motivated partly by resentment against this. Culturally, also, the Democratic Party moved into a very different space than these voters. Previously, it had been made up of a coalition in which working-class people were a major component. This was true from the 1930s on. But gradually, as you got closer to the twenty-first century, elites within the Democratic Party became college-educated professionals. Culturally, I think they speak a different language from most working-class Americans. I think a lot of those working-class people began to perceive that, culturally, they didn’t have anything to do with the leadership of the Democratic Party. And this came to a head particularly on issues like immigration, where the elites in the party were just taking a very different attitude from a lot of working-class people who weren’t in favor of open borders. John-Baptiste Oduor How did this coalition that mobilized around issues like open borders and police abolition that — bracketing their soundness — were quite unpopular among working-class Americans become so prominent within the Democratic Party? Francis Fukuyama I don’t have a good explanation for this other than a general failure of leadership. John-Baptiste Oduor In a recent essay, you discussed Trump’s attempts to consolidate executive power, motivated by a theory of a unified executive. One of the potential consequences of the success of this project would, you write, be the emergence of a “spoils” system. What would this look like? Francis Fukuyama I’m not sure Trump himself has a coherent understanding of this, but certainly people like Russell Vought, who’s now the head of the Office of Management and Budget, has ideas. It’s all based on an irrational hatred of government. Vought believes the permanent bureaucracy is full of left-wing ideologues who really don’t respond to a democratic choice. This is complete nonsense. If you look at the history of the American bureaucracy in the nineteenth century, you had the spoils system, or the patronage system, where virtually every federal employee was there because of a political favor from a politician. This was changed only beginning in 1883, with the passage of the Pendleton Act, which created a civil service commission. This made it so that you needed to take a civil service exam or have some credentials in order to work for the US government. This didn’t suit the interests of the politicians at the time. It took the assassination of President James Garfield in 1881 by an office seeker to shame Congress into passing the Pendleton Act. But slowly, we developed a professional civil service that operated according to the principle that a civil servant is not a partisan. They exist to implement the wishes of democratically elected leaders, and they do so no matter which party is in power. As the federal government expanded in the 1930s and became much more complex, the expertise of these bureaucrats became much more important. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the Federal Aviation Administration, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration — you have all these new agencies that really can’t be run by political partisans. The staff of the Federal Reserve is full of PhD economists and people with long experience in financial markets. This expansion of expertise is inevitable in a modern economy. You cannot run a government without this expertise, but there’s been this huge populist revolt against the very idea of expertise. There’s very little appreciation on the Right for the fact that you actually do need medical professionals to run a public health service, this sort of thing. The rise of the internet has promoted all of these really wild conspiracy theories. The number of Americans that think that the COVID-19 vaccine was causing more death than COVID itself . . . it’s shocking. But I should say, before I go further, that there was something to the unhappiness with the existing bureaucracy, because it did actually become very difficult to control a lot of bureaucrats. Public sector unions that wanted to protect jobs were really powerful. Compared to the private sector, if you wanted to discipline an employee in a federal agency, it was much harder to do that than in a private company. This created an excessive rigidity, but that does not justify this wholesale attack on expertise and on the idea of a neutral public service, which is really core to the way any modern government has to operate. John-Baptiste Oduor The critique of state bureaucracy is a long-running one. A version of it can be found in the Mancur Olson’s book The Rise and Decline of Nations . He was concerned with rent-seeking coalitions, but a version of this critique has recently resurfaced in the so-called abundance movement. Do you think some of the strength of the MAGA project has come from the Left’s unwillingness to take seriously concerns about the calcified nature of bureaucracies under democracies? Francis Fukuyama I think that’s right. The problem is not that the bureaucrats themselves are calcified. They’re simply responding to the incentives created by the system and to the rules that have been established as a result of pressure or actual state capture by a lot of very powerful interest groups. That’s what’s made it very difficult to do things like build sufficient affordable housing or to build infrastructure in a timely, inexpensive way. The history here is complicated, but there’s a very insightful book by Mark Dunkelman called Why Nothing Works that places a certain amount of the blame for this on the Left — the progressive left, because in the Progressive era and through to the 1950s, many progressives felt that government was the main engine for social progress. The Tennessee Valley Authority, the Hoover Dam, and the Works Progress Administration: these are all federal-government-led efforts to get the economy going again. But beginning in the 1960s, with the rise of the public interest movement led by people like Ralph Nader, a lot of progressives began to argue that actually the government was the problem, that the government had been captured by corporations and was no longer representing the people but was actually defending these entrenched corporate interests. And honestly, that was true in a lot of sectors, but I think that this skepticism toward the state led to a convergence of the Left and the Right. The Right always hated government, and the Left began to be very suspicious of big government as well. Fundamentally, what the abundance movement wants to do is to try to change that cultural mindset, to say, no, actually, government can be a force for change. If you’re going to decarbonize the American economy, it can’t be driven by grassroots movements. It really does require much stronger action to build transmission lines and to incentivize alternative energy. John-Baptiste Oduor But doesn’t capital also need to be disciplined? The discussion around abundance has been couched in terms of removing bureaucratic hurdles and getting rid of rent-seeking mediators. But that on its own is not enough. Francis Fukuyama I think you need a middle position where you can admit that many things, particularly the way that bureaucrats are forced to operate, are overregulated and yet say that the Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE) and the Republican solution is not a solution. You need rules. There are good reasons why you’ve got worker-safety and environmental protections and all sort of limits on what the government can do and how it needs to operate. But you can also make them more flexible and easier to implement. I’ll just give you a concrete example. If a federal agency wants to buy an office computer or a desk, it has to comply with something called the federal acquisitions regulations, which are literally hundreds of pages of detailed rules about how you buy stuff in the federal government. No private corporation has to deal with that volume of rules, which were originally put in place to prevent corruption. But you don’t need hundreds and hundreds of pages of regulations in order to get to that objective. Something similar is true, I think, with environmental law. The National Environmental Policy Act was very important. It set the model for environmental protection by mandating the need for environmental reviews. And that was necessary. But over time, the length of these reviews has grown so that today they are thousands of pages long and take five or even ten years to write. That adds dramatically to the cost of building infrastructure. John-Baptiste Oduor During Trump’s first term, the tariffs he imposed on China were treated as extreme policies at the time, but they have now become part of the center ground of American politics, for better or worse. In his second term, Trump has now expanded the power of the executive. Do you envision this new view of executive power as becoming a new norm for both parties? Could the progressive wing of the Democratic Party use these powers to enact policies that its base wants? Francis Fukuyama This is something that progressives need to think about very carefully, because checks and balances work in both directions. They are a protection against a would-be authoritarian like Trump, but they also limit what a good progressive administration would like to achieve. You actually saw this under both Barack Obama and Joe Biden, where a lot of the things they wanted to do, like Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals, had to be done by executive order. The cancellation of student debt under Biden was done by an executive order because they could not get this stuff through Congress. When residents of a block in Belgrade’s city center woke up to the news last week, they were confronted with a mural celebrating a war criminal, painted overnight. Furious, they plotted a “night action” to destroy this latest tribute to Ratko Mladić with improvised defacement tactics. While the war criminal’s face is ever present across the Serbian capital, this was the first time a mural several meters long and about one meter high had appeared on the building surrounding their park. The news of Mladić’s death, while in detention at The Hague, came in the middle of a campaign for parliamentary elections that have not yet been called but according to President Aleksandar Vučić are expected any day now. What a popular slogan calls the “students who are winning” (after a series of recent mass protests) are campaigning for the vote, as are the current head of state Vučić and his SNS (the Serbian Progressive Party, in name only). The justice minister hastily announced that “the protocols are known” and that Mladić, being a general, would be buried accordingly, with military and state honors. Still, Vučić, the all-powerful president, backed away while pointing to “family wishes” and suggesting there might be no state-sponsored funeral. A few opposition leaders issued statements firmly distancing themselves from Mladić’s legacy and emphasizing the need not to forget or relativize the truth about his victims and the genocide. The European Commission, which is not exactly a moral or political authority here, said there is no room for an EU candidate country to celebrate a convicted war criminal. In Republika Srpska — the Bosnian Serb entity within neighboring Bosnia and Herzegovina, where Mladić commanded atrocities — numerous vigils were held, often featuring well-known football hooligan iconography. In a professionally produced video, students from Republika Srpska in Belgrade held up a huge poster over the Branko Bridge depicting Mladić, bearing the words “From your struggle, our freedom lives.” Survivors and victims’ associations from the genocide site at Srebrenica and across Bosnia were once again shocked at such reactions. While approving of Mladić’s sentence and imprisonment, they pointed out that his death changes nothing. It does not bring the dead back or erase the mass graves. Myth-Building Amid the Breakup of Yugoslavia Ratko Mladić was born in eastern Bosnia in 1942. He joined the Yugoslav People’s Army (JNA) in 1965, when it was still a widely respected institution of socialist Yugoslavia. He climbed steadily through its ranks, eventually becoming a general. Yet his real ascent began with the breakup of Yugoslavia at the turn of the 1990s. He started building his reputation in Knin, where he was sent to fight the forces of newly independent Croatia. When the JNA decided to officially withdraw from Bosnia and the Army of Republika Srpska was formed in 1992, he was appointed commander of its Main Staff, inheriting much of the JNA local infrastructure, including personnel, weaponry, and other resources. Media-savvy and always ready for the camera, Mladić switched his ideological allegiances — from defender of socialist Yugoslavia to an ethnically attuned defender of Serbs and their land. His wartime legacy in Bosnia included the siege of Sarajevo, the genocide in Srebrenica, and war crimes and crimes against humanity across different parts of Bosnia and Herzegovina. After the Dayton Peace Agreement in 1995 marked the end of the war in Bosnia, he was indicted by the United Nations’ International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY). Avoiding trial, he came to Belgrade and lived fairly openly under Slobodan Milošević’s protection until his patron fell in October 2000. He spent the following years in hiding — first aided by a network of former military officers and later by relatives, vanishing from public view entirely by 2008. He was finally captured in 2011 in a village in northern Serbia and extradited to The Hague. He died this August 27 in a prison hospital. No matter what his physical location or his administrative or carceral status, Mladić was present in Serbia all along, as a ready-made poster, mural, slogan, T-shirt icon, overarching symbol, or historical and narrative arc, to be used and reused in endless political and (social) media campaigns. He was ready to serve, dead or alive. He remains a crucial part of government-linked street and hooligan groups’ mass-scale visual campaigns , regularly performed across Belgrade. Tanja Marković, an activist involved in peace and antifascist initiatives since the 1990s, told me: “The state maintains no distance whatsoever. We remember how it guarded the Ratko Mladić mural in Njegoševa, in an organized fashion, and how long it took for that mural to be removed.” Some of the most enduring slogans are those related to genocide: “The only genocide in the Balkans was against the Serbs,” read a huge piece of graffiti, in English, on a building in the city’s main avenue, just across from the Belgrade Cultural Center. Then there are those depicting Mladić, usually featured in close-up, wearing an army hat or, in the case of graffiti, there is simply his name accompanied by an “appropriate” slogan. These murals and slogans often provoke rage among other citizens and peace and youth activists, who throw eggs or try covering them with white paint, only for the murals to resurface again. In nationalist myth, Mladić serves as a defender, preventing a new genocide against the Serbs, within a historical arc that connects the slaughter of Serbs in World War II with the real and threatened ethnic cleansing of Serbs during the Yugoslav Wars. He not only defended Serbs from domestic enemies, such as Croats and Muslims, but from imperialist forces too. Anti-NATO is always a great story hook, especially in Serbia, which was later depleted by sanctions and subjected to the highly controversial NATO bombing campaign ( precedent for which were air strikes against Mladić’s forces in 1995). With a quasi-socialist pivot at first, then transitioning to ethnonationalism with an anti-imperialist flavor, the myth resolves complex histories and some real grievances into a murderous, single-hero story that plays well within a wider narrative framework centered on ancient ethnic wounds and hatred. This framework explains not only the war but also how and why socialist Yugoslavia broke up. Contrary to all the data on mass impoverishment , we did not lose a class war but simply so terribly and murderously hated each other. Genocide Denial to Stay in Power There are only two questions that every contender for political power in Serbia has to answer: Was there a genocide in Srebrenica, and is Kosovo Serbia? Whoever the challenger and whatever they really think, you can always see them being grilled in those TV debate chairs, playing an impossible game rigged to ensure any myth-busters will lose the part that matters: i.e., the elections. For an outsider, both questions could appear not only morally corrupt but intellectually delusional. Kosovo, with or without asterisks (Kosovo*, as it is sometimes styled in the official international documents), does exist. And the genocide in Srebrenica did happen; we could hardly miss the bones of the thousands of victims, brushed clean by forensic experts as they were recovered and identified from mass graves, including the secondary ones, where bodies had been moved and scattered in an attempt to cover up the crime. There were times in Serbia’s recent history when it seemed that society was ready to confront its past and begin rebuilding relations with its neighbors. When rereading the articles and rewatching TV, it is obvious that even Radio Television of Serbia, the public broadcaster, though always under the strong grip of whichever party was in power, did in multiple programs offer its viewers insight into the scale of the atrocities and the burden that this legacy places on those living. In 2010, the Serbian parliament adopted a declaration condemning the crime in Srebrenica, offering an apology and condolences to the families of the victims for the failure to prevent the tragedy. Vučić himself went to Srebrenica for the twentieth-anniversary commemoration in 2015, marking a shift toward reconciliation compared to his earlier political stance. Why then, does the myth persist? Who benefits from it? Who is keeping it alive? Who can break it down and introduce a new myth? In 2024, the UN General Assembly adopted a resolution commemorating the Srebrenica genocide, explicitly condemning genocide denial and the glorification of those convicted for war crimes. On the day of the vote, Vučić was at the UN, wrapped in a Serbian flag, his posture as if in prayer for Serbia not to be charged with genocide. During the evening, a skyscraper that is part of a highly dubious urbanistic mega project, Belgrade Waterfront, which also serves as a visual campaign booth, fluorescently flashed the words “We are not genocidal people” to different parts of Belgrade. The resolution explicitly states that criminal accountability for genocide is a responsibility that weighs on certain individuals; the “collective guilt” framing was instead manufactured, in the lobbying campaign that preceded the vote, by Vučić and Milorad Dodik (leader of Republika Srpska) themselves. After the UN vote backed the resolution, Vučić, as if the chief doppelgänger of a parallel reality, announced a moral victory based on the sixty-eight abstentions, and he continued campaigning in ongoing local elections. The myth, deployed as a technology of staying in power, always intensifies when there are problems, elections, or challengers to power. The historical arc, the narrative, the explanatory frame was set long ago and is state sponsored at all times, in war and peace alike. Regime members are an integral part of it, either as real characters in concrete historical events, as with Vučić, who served as minister of information during the last phase of Milošević’s rule, or as political actors who benefit materially and politically from keeping the myth alive. To stay in power, it was crucial not to let citizens question their newly found capitalist “paradise” but to keep everybody hostage in this imagined community of war crimes and war thugs; complicit through membership in the same ethnic community, complicit through widespread feelings of loss, guilt, and shame that are being worked out through ethnic drama while covering up mass economic and social losses. Students Win, but With Baggage There were also those who challenged the destructive myth. But they, too, sometimes fell into a collective-guilt framing, screaming nationalism where they could have screamed class. In a rather bizarre string of historical permutations, the German model of postwar reckoning with Nazism, Vergangenheitsbewältigung , or “dealing with the past,” was adopted in the local nongovernmental organization (NGO) lexicon and practice. The concept sat well with the tradition of political liberalism in Serbia, while neglecting the economic workings of the breakup of Yugoslavia and dismissing local histories as possible sources of peace and justice. Still, as Marković points out: We would be completely in the dark related to war crimes if it wasn’t for them. It is also true that war crimes concealed the economic crime called privatization and the disenfranchisement of the broad masses of the people. People close to those in power easily got their hands on those former factories for very little money and cemented their wealth. And now, there’s practically nothing we can do about them. Ingenious tricksters on the political right spotted the weaknesses of the model and invented the charge of auto-chauvinism (the self-hating Serb), which played well on the divide between the urban-educated, culturally snobbish on one side and the rest of the country on the other. This is how the narrative and explanatory framework became squared and closed from all sides. The charge of auto-chauvinism, while never in mass usage, has been repeatedly deployed against the current student movement, transformed into clickbait: students have been called “Ustaše” (supporters of the Nazi-collaborationist regime in Croatia during World War II) and accused of being part of a “color revolution” and of working against Serbs and Serbia. The student-led mass social movement in Serbia began at the end of 2024, sparked by the collapse of a railway station canopy in second city Novi Sad. The students and their electoral list, which will not feature students but their supporters from different sectors and walks of life, are the main challengers to Vučić’s power and the biggest to date. The country, or at least its rebellious part, keeps pushing for political reforms and what is perceived as a never-finished democratization. The socioeconomic agenda, once again, does not feature prominently, although there have been certain changes compared with previous protest mobilizations . Students kept silent about Mladić. Supporters are somewhat understanding, with hopes that a change of government will open up the possibility for these issues to be discussed anew. And there are different approaches and alternative histories to the one in which Mladić, the defender of Serbs, is the central character. There are the many people who crossed identitarian and ethnic lines to help those on the other side. Some died while doing so, killed by people of their own ethnic group. Others resisted, to their own detriment, interethnic pressure and involvement in crimes. There are also alternative histories of the breakup that do not fit neatly into the story of primordial ethnic hatred. One day, there will be no murals of Ratko Mladić in Belgrade. Not because of lonely acts of resistance, but because there will be no political force in power interested in keeping that myth alive. But for the Mladić story to fully lose its grip, the larger narrative framework that sustains it also needs to go. We need to explain to ourselves why not enough people understood that a war was a massive project of collective dispossession. And we need to direct the current social movement to take some of that possession back.

The Rise of the Billionaire Lobby

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Ben Case ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-03T16:24:02+00:00

You’ve heard of the oil lobby, the tobacco lobby, the Israel lobby, the charter school lobby. Behind all of these are billionaires who benefit from the underlying industries or sectors, and whose corporations use their resources to lobby the government for favorable policies. Now, in reaction to a California ballot initiative for the world’s first billionaire tax, we have an innovation: a lobby for billionaires themselves. Self-advocacy by wealthy people is nothing new, of course. But faced with the prospect of persuading voters to reject Proposition 40 — a temporary tax on roughly two hundred unfathomably rich individuals to fund health care for millions of people — billionaires must make the case to the public that forcing them to share a small amount of their fortunes would do more harm than good. At a time when half of American families can’t afford basic necessities, and 95 percent of the country understands that we’re in an affordability crisis, that’s going to be a tough sell. A handful of billionaires led by Google cofounder Sergey Brin are out to prove that money can buy anything. They are pouring unprecedented resources into a political campaign to convince — or if that doesn’t work, to trick — California voters into prioritizing billionaires’ wealth over their own livelihoods. When Billionaires Don’t Get Their Way In October 2025, a labor union of California hospital and health care workers announced their intention to qualify a billionaire tax initiative for the state ballot. Written in collaboration with leading progressive economists like Emmanuel Saez and Robert Reich , it would levy a onetime 5 percent tax on the net assets of billionaires residing in California as of January 1, 2026. The tax would raise $100 billion in one shot, $90 billion of which would be used to fund Medi-Cal, the state’s Medicaid program, which is otherwise facing a dire fiscal cliff thanks to Donald Trump’s One Big Beautiful Bill. The remaining $10 billion would go into public education. By December, before the campaign was even circulating petitions, billionaires like Peter Thiel, Sergey Brin, and Larry Page were rushing to move assets out of state. Whether or not they’d end up avoiding the tax (or actually stop spending time and money in California), they’d jump-start the narrative that taxing billionaires would chase them away — a story the billionaire lobby would lean heavily on. A small cohort of billionaires began organizing to undermine the billionaire tax and keep it from reaching voters. They flooded the market for paid signature-gatherers (which all large initiative campaigns use even if volunteers do the bulk of the work) and allegedly even attempted to buy out a key canvassing firm. These efforts multiplied costs for the billionaire tax campaign, while the glut of clipboard-wielding canvassers on the streets made the job of billionaire tax volunteers significantly more difficult. The billionaires also enlisted the help of Governor Gavin Newsom. Shortly after chatting with Brin about the matter at a crypto mogul’s holiday party, Newsom took a stand against the billionaire tax, vowing he’d defeat the campaign one way or another. Following the lead of Newsom and billionaire donors, a handful of liberal nonprofits and labor unions came out against the billionaire tax as well, joining forces with Republicans and business groups to defeat it — all without an alternative plan to fund Medi-Cal once Trump’s cuts kick in next year. So far, all these efforts have come to naught. The billionaire tax qualified by submitting over 1.6 million signatures, more than double the required number, becoming Proposition 40 on November’s ballot. It’s since been endorsed by the California Federation of Labor Unions and a host of other labor groups, the California Nurses Association, and recently the state Democratic Party. Now Brin and his billionaires must convince a majority of voters that closing hospitals, clinics, and emergency rooms is preferable to taxing billionaires who won’t miss the money. Behold a Billionaire Lobby Brin’s anti–Proposition 40 organization is called Building a Better California. It’s the kind of innocuous and unmemorable name given to so many political nonprofits, in this case also seeming to hint at Joe Biden’s bygone Build Back Better infrastructure bill, perhaps to remind us of the billionaire lobby’s bipartisan nature. Building a Better California has already raised more than $150 million, most of it from Brin himself. The group recently dropped its first major advertisement buy, a working-class-centered commercial that also highlights Newsom’s opposition to Prop 40. The ad mentions neither the billionaires themselves nor the fact that Newsom is a longtime personal friend of Brin’s. Brin’s group is expected to spend upward of $90 million on ads, an airtime spending advantage eleven thousand times that of Prop 40’s current budget. The group has already blanketed the terrain with mailers, sending colorful foldouts to every California household warning that the billionaire tax will destroy the state. The purpose, it seems, is to spread confusion and misinformation, and most of all to divert attention from the jaw-dropping profits billionaires have been raking in as day-to-day life gets harder for most Americans. Brin alone, who theoretically would owe something like $9 billion should Prop 40 pass, grew his net wealth by around $100 billion in the past year alone . Billionaires do themselves few favors when they pull back the curtain, giving the public a glimpse of their personalities and priorities. Take, for example, Peter Thiel’s obsession with the Antichrist and Elon Musk’s “transhumanist” desire to implant AI in your brain. Consequently, rather than appealing to the public outright, they know it’s smarter to throw the public off the scent, which is Building a Better California’s raison d’être . The most rational argument against a billionaire tax is that the superrich will relocate to other states, as Thiel, Brin, and Page claim to have done (though some argue the moves were only on paper), depriving California of both the jobs and the taxes these individuals supposedly supply. But this idea has been roundly debunked, from academic studies to recent examples like the 2022 Massachusetts millionaire’s tax, after which the number of millionaires in the Bay State actually increased , with the tax generating billions more in revenue than anticipated. The fact that the billionaire flight argument is still rolled out makes it clear that it is not an evidence-based statement but a threat, albeit a relatively empty one. Texas billionaire Mark Cuban voiced this directly when he said he’d refuse to invest in any California company if Prop 40 passes. The billionaires are essentially saying to the public: “You can’t tax us, otherwise we’ll leave, and then you won’t be able to tax us.” The inherent flimsiness of this argument enhances the need to keep fearmongering , sans credible evidence or details, that the tax will collapse the economy. Evasions abound. Interviewed about Prop 40 on California Insider , Stanford economics professor Joshua Rauh began with the capital flight argument, but before long his commentary took a revealing turn. “There is a principle here of protecting private property,” he said. “It’s a very slippery slope, because who is actually wealthy?” The question was asked with an air of Socratic profundity, even though Prop 40 very clearly defines the taxable group as individuals who own more than $1 billion in assets. “From a philosophical standpoint,” Rauh went on, “would you want the same treatment as what we’re doing to billionaires?” The billionaire lobby needs to convince voters to identify with them — despite the fact that, aside from cellular-level processes like the Krebs cycle, a working person has nothing in common with a tech oligarch worth $258 billion. A skill is a reusable workflow for an AI agent. Its main SKILL.md file explains when the skill should be used and what the agent should do. A skill may also contain scripts, templates, and other reference materials. The agent may select a skill automatically when a task matches its description, or you can invoke it directly. This guide demonstrates how to find, review, install, and use skills with an AI-agent extension in VS Code. If you have not yet set up this workspace, follow Set Up Your AI Workspace (macOS) or Set Up Your AI Workspace (Windows) before continuing. Although interfaces and installation methods may vary, the same basic workflow also applies to other AI agents that support skills. Copy the prompt below into the chat box to install find-skills . Run `npx skills add https://github.com/vercel-labs/skills -g --skill find-skills` and follow the generated skill instructions now. Read its complete output, redirecting it to a temporary file first if necessary. Resolve relative paths from the supporting-files directory it provides. Once installed, you can search for skills through the agent. Ordinary wording still works, but commands such as /find-skills are recommended because they are clearer, faster, and more cost-effective: the model can read the specified skill directly. For example: /find-skills please help me find a skill that can help summarise papers and books. The agent uses the skill and returns a large number of matching results. Artificial Intelligence in the Hands of the People: The Rongjiang Experiment

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: [:en]THE TRICONTINENTAL[:es]TRICONTINENTAL[:] ／ 著者: Author and Editors ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-04T09:22:38+00:00

A small county folded into the mountains of south-west Guizhou, Rongjiang was among the last counties in China to be lifted out of extreme poverty, crossing the line only on 23 November 2020, and as late as 2022 its per-capita output stood at about a third of the national average. ‘Eight parts mountain, one part water, one part field’ is how an old upland saying counted the land in Guizhou — the only province in China without a plain. Its valleys are terraced and narrow, its villages strung along the rivers and ridgelines beneath Moon Mountain. There was never much land here to produce wealth, and no easy road to carry the little produce out. The Red Army crossed these mountains in 1934, on the march that would take it north. Football arrived a decade later, brought by students of Guangxi University, evacuated to Rongjiang to escape Japanese aggression. Villagers have organised their own matches nearly every year in the eight decades since. In May 2023, that tradition found a new expression in Cun Chao , the Village Super League: a competition organised by villagers, played by villagers, and watched by villagers. Within weeks of its launch, the stadium’s stands were filled to the brim, and footage carried by the short-video platforms drew the eyes of the whole country and then of the world. None of it ran itself. Packing a stadium every weekend, keeping order, feeding the broadcast — all of that took an organisational capacity the county had not required before. This organisational capacity became an engine for real economic outcomes. Tourism revenue rose from 8.4 billion yuan in 2023 to 10.8 billion in 2024, and the county’s gross domestic product reached 10.496 billion yuan in 2024, a rise of 9.5 per cent. The league brought more than 2,700 new market entities into being, over thirty per cent of the whole prefecture’s new total, and village collective income climbed from 58.9 million yuan in 2022 to 121 million in 2024. It also opened a door to something else entirely. Since 2023 the county has set itself a less likely task: to put artificial intelligence (AI) into the hands of the villagers. It has built no large language model, no data centre, and no semiconductor plant. What it has built instead is its own people, organised across government offices, villages, and enterprises, so that residents who had never written a line of code, among them elderly farmers and village cadres, now use AI in their everyday work. Building the Institutional and Material Conditions The experiment was made possible by conditions built at multiple levels. Cun Chao was no technology project but a feat of organisation, and the organisational structure was standing before the first ball was kicked. In October 2021, a year and a half before that match, the county set up a leading small group for new media and rural e-commerce, headed jointly by the county Party secretary and the county governor. In a Chinese county the Party committee leads and the government departments administer, and those departments — agriculture, commerce, culture and tourism — each holds responsibility for part of any new undertaking; a leading small group is how a Party committee concentrates the efforts of different departments on a common task. Here the two highest officials in the county took the headship themselves, one from the Party and one from the government, which put new media at the top of the agenda. Beneath the group the county placed a working team at the county level, a new-media service centre in every township, and a new-media service station in every village, and it set up a new-media company besides, a county state enterprise holding shares with private partners. The result was a single organisational chain linking three different but complementary capacities: Party leadership, grassroots mobilisation, and market activity. The Party committee provided political leadership; grassroots cadres reached every household; private firms and traders connected production to the market. When Cun Chao began in May 2023, the county set a dedicated Cun Chao Office on top of that standing structure, to mobilise cadres, coordinate security, and keep the media channels fed across all three levels at once. When the county later turned toward AI, the mechanism had already put thirty-five thousand person-times — a tally of training instances, not unique individuals — through new-media training and built more than two thousand local livestream teams and over twelve thousand new-media accounts. Rongjiang calls the shift the ‘Three New Rurals’: let the phone become the new farm tool, data become the new farm input, and livestreaming become the new farm work. As Party secretary Xu Bo puts it , the aim is to turn Cun Chao into new quality productive forces for local industry. New technology only raises the productive forces once the relations of production change to accommodate it — technology alone doesn’t do it; the social arrangements have to shift first to let it work. Led by the Party committee and carried by the villagers themselves, the county has therefore pursued this transformation by reshaping who is trained to use the tools, who holds the account they feed, and who keeps what a sale earns. The material foundations for all this had been laid at national level. Through the poverty alleviation campaign the state carried roads, power, and broadband into places the market had never reached, in order to connect them to it: by the end of 2020, every eligible township and administrative village in the poor counties had a paved road, a bus service, and a postal route; reliable electricity, fibre broadband, and 4G had been extended across the countryside, with fibre and 4G covering more than 98 per cent of poor villages; and e-commerce services reached every poverty county. Only once these material conditions existed could digital transformation become a realistic strategy for rural development, e-commerce first and AI after it. One of the instruments that carried this into counties like Rongjiang is paired assistance, which binds a coastal province or work unit to an inland county and moves people, money, and technology along the link. It dates from a central decision of 1979, at the start of reform and opening up, following Deng Xiaoping’s strategy of letting some regions grow rich first and then bring the rest along. Rongjiang later applied the same logic to AI training, pairing those with greater digital capacity with those who had less experience. Once these material foundations had been laid, AI became the next stage of national development strategy. This transition built on an earlier digital rural agenda: the central Digital Village Development Outline (16 May 2019) had already named ‘the improvement of peasants’ modern information skills’ as a strategic direction. On 26 August 2025, the State Council issued its Opinions on Deeply Implementing the ‘AI Plus’ Action , proposing six lines of action across science, industry, consumption, people’s wellbeing, governance, and global cooperation, and undertaking to strengthen the unified planning of intelligent computing power. Relying on the variable of the people’s adoption of digital tools, rather than the capital or hardware more often treated as decisive, the county organised its people to take up that opening on its own terms. It translated these national priorities into a local mobilisation plan, set out in a mnemonic it calls the ‘1-2-3-4-5’ target system, which illustrates how a local state translates a national technology strategy into mass participation: One benchmark. By the end of 2027, to have built Rongjiang into a national benchmark county for the universal learning and application of AI. Two pillars of support. A corps of three hundred home-grown technical backbones and thirty thousand ‘application pacesetters’, cultivated across at least five concrete AI application scenarios. The three hundred are not an elite but come from across the county’s cadres, civil servants, teachers, enterprise staff, village cadres, and returning graduates. They are seeded rather than bought: each of the three hundred trains ten, each ten a hundred, until the thirty thousand are reached. Three full coverages. One hundred per cent training coverage of government offices; one hundred per cent basic-AI-literacy coverage of every administrative village; one hundred per cent scenario-application coverage across every sector. Four-tier linkage. A four-tier AI application and promotion network running from county to township to village to enterprise. Five categories of benefit. Measurable gains in the efficiency of government services, in tourist satisfaction, in the coverage of quality educational resources, in the accuracy of medical diagnosis, and in the reduction of enterprise costs, summed up in the slogan: every village has an AI hand; every trade has an AI exemplar; every task has an AI enablement. AI on Four Fronts of Rural Revitalisation The county puts AI at the service of its people, their work, and their days; it turns it to the plain business of rural revival on four fronts. 1. AI Lowers the Cost of Being Seen Before AI and new media reached the countryside, a village’s produce rarely left the mountains, for the only real route to market was to carry the harvest down to the nearest town and sell it there. Farmers had neither the capital nor the skill to present their goods attractively or to advertise them further afield, and a competent poster or promotional video meant hiring a professional crew few could bear. To make AI accessible beyond specialists, the county first trained villagers in practical AI-based content production, inviting outside professionals to teach simple workflows using mainstream AI tools and organising county-wide competitions to encourage experimentation. AI lowers the cost of being seen. With little more than a smartphone, villagers can now generate and refine the promotional material themselves – a poster, a short video, an animation – and distribute it on the same platforms that carried the league. Making a short film to promote Cun Chao , until lately beyond an average villager’s means, is now affordable and easily learned – and that same footage now carries other villagers’ goods to market too. Producing enough good video by hand, across thousands of accounts, turned out to be slow and costly, and the traffic it won came unevenly; so the county integrated AI editing and large-scale distribution into its new-media system, operated by the villagers it trains as the league’s new-media promoters, allowing them to produce and circulate promotional content at a speed and volume the old way could not match. When a match is on, they film it and upload the footage, and the system writes the captions and edits it into a finished clip, ready in under half an hour. Distribution is made as simple as production: a tap on the on-site posting device, or a scan of a QR code, puts the clip on Douyin, China’s TikTok, within seconds. Promotional videos now carry built-in distribution links to local products, so that the same circulation that spreads the county’s stories also generates sales and commissions for those who share them. 2. AI Eases the Grassroots Cadre’s Burden The chronic burdens of the grassroots cadre – the drafting of reports and notices, the compiling of statistical tables, weak policy publicity, slow response to grievances, the sheer weight of administrative toil – are eased by pressing AI into the workflow itself. It drafts the reports and assembles the meeting records, and, more valuable still, it recasts the official register of policy into plain language villagers can follow, which makes policy far easier to communicate. The county reports that a cadre who once spent half a day on a draft now finishes in half an hour, left to localise and refine rather than to assemble from scratch. The figures from Zhongcheng Town are plain: in the first two and a half months of 2025 alone, its village-level monitoring network flagged disputes early enough for staff to resolve sixteen of them before they escalated, AI-assisted forecasting fed into plans for nineteen projects, and cadres saved roughly two working days each. The county audit office , too, runs its own learning routine on cheap, off-the-shelf domestic models. 3. AI Turns Local Talent Into a Reason to Return Where rural policy typically laments the outflow of the young, the programme attempts to reverse the valuation, so that mastery of AI, and the short films and animations that draw hundreds of thousands of views, become an attractive and remunerative occupation worth returning home for and worth staying for, aimed at returning youth, the women who remain in the villages, and individual traders. Liu Qinlan is the county’s own example: a kindergarten teacher earning a little over 2,000 yuan a month, she and her husband gave up their city jobs and came home when Cun Chao went viral in May 2023. She started out trying to sell the county’s own produce — luohan fruit, green and white tea, pickled fish and meat — and learned filming, editing, and livestreaming from scratch through the county’s training. Known online as Cun Chao ‘s ‘Miao sister Lan Lan’, she now runs a batik studio by the Cun Chao ground, drawing on the region’s indigenous textile traditions and giving more than 180 embroiderers and dyers work without leaving their villages. In 2025 her batik and embroidery lines sold more than 2 million yuan. 4. AI Carries Local Culture Out of the Mountains Cun Chao draws on its own peasant, working-class, and ethnic cultures. Made up of vendors, farmers, tilers, butchers, and factory workers, its villages name their teams after what they grow or do – a Monk Fruit team, a Passionfruit team, a Bayberry team, a Homestay team, a Rafting team, a Rice-Noodle team, even one that calls itself simply ‘the commoners’. The side that wins takes home not cash but the county’s own produce: the 2026 champions carried off a Guanling cow, the runners-up a Guizhou sturgeon, third place a crate of local duck. More than eighty per cent of Rongjiang’s people belong to the ethnic minorities of Miao, Dong, Shui, and Yao, home to heritage forms such as the Grand Song of the Dong (choral singing), traditional indigo dyeing, and the drum-tower. But these traditions – peasant, working-class, ethnic minority, even the Cun Chao festival itself – survive only insofar as they are seen and used. As such, AI animation, short video, and digital-human generation are turned to the work of carrying all of it – produce, trade, and heritage alike – out of the mountains and into national circulation, and from there into the commercial logic of cultural-tourism branding. At the provincial level this is already a working layer: a Cun Chao digital-human and smart-companion platform provides AI tour-companion services keyed to the league and to the Grand Song of the Dong, while the county’s enterprise training uses the Cun Chao brand, special crops, and heritage as its worked teaching cases. Tailored Mass Training The Rongjiang experiment rests on its teaching, the means by which an unfamiliar tool reaches farmers, shopkeepers, and cadres with little formal training. Its logic holds throughout: teach each person only what their own work requires, keep every lesson pared down to what can actually be done, and let those who have learned teach those who have not. Nothing is taught one-size-fits-all. The county sorts the people it trains into groups, each with its own syllabus: for cadres those of governance, for merchants those of income, for the young and the heritage-keepers those of cultural creation. The same care goes into matching the task to the person, so that no one, the county insists, is made to learn what they cannot use. The county teaches AI as a practical skill rather than a technical discipline. Algorithms, programming, and the inner workings of the models are left aside in favour of using domestic AI applications and writing simple prompts. A visiting expert may bring a thousand possible applications of AI, but the local trainer’s task is to reduce them to the two or three that match local needs and market demand. The county therefore standardises its training around three practical courses — in AI document drafting, AI short-video production, and AI visual creation. The same philosophy shaped one of the county’s most recent courses , on AI comic drama in June 2026, for sixty returning youth, heritage artisans, and small entrepreneurs: it was designed for complete beginners, emphasised hands-on production, and connected local cultural heritage directly to market-oriented digital content creation. Learning is treated as a rhythm rather than an event. Every Friday evening a trainer takes a livestreamed shift, a class that may draw two or three hundred viewers or, some nights, only one. Even one is considered worthwhile, because one person helped is one more person empowered. Since 2025, the weekly stream has not lapsed. The teaching applies the same logic of paired assistance: those who learn first bring the rest along, with young cadres matched to middle-aged and senior staff to close the digital gap. The records bear it out. Zhongcheng Town designated twelve technology-promotion officers and established forty-two mentoring pairs, and a single round of centralised training covered ninety-eight cadres. At Bakai Town more than sixty cadres trained on four working scenarios, smart document handling, population-data modelling, new-media outreach, and emergency-response dispatch, with those trained to teach the rest, so that village cadres could run the systems on their own. If pairing carries the skill from one person to the next, a chain of four tiers carries the plan itself from the county down to each level, with county, township, village, and enterprise each holding a distinct job. The county designs. It sets the plan, marshals the teachers, builds the demonstration cases, and drives the work through weekly scheduling meetings that hold the lower tiers to account. The township executes. It runs the routine mass training and the cadre drills. The village is the front line. It is where villagers are taught and the locally useful applications are found. The enterprise turns capability into revenue. At the end of the chain, it welds AI to culture-tourism, agriculture, and handicraft. It is this chain, as much as the curriculum, that turns a directive issued in the county seat into a lesson under way in a village. All of this rides on a project discipline recognisably that of a campaigning state, set out as a five-step closed loop for landing the programme in any village or unit. Survey and preparation. An inventory of local industry, governance pain points, population structure, and the digital baseline yields a bespoke plan, using the ‘brainstorming meeting’ — the county’s instrument, inherited from the league campaigns — to identify concerns and potential challenges before they become real obstacles to implementation. Cultivation of the backbone. The core group of technical backbones, the same three hundred named in the ‘1-2-3-4-5’ plan, are trained first. Universal training by the layered method. That core group then cascades the training outward, each backbone teaching the next tier down, until it reaches the wider population. Scenario landing. Training and application proceed together, so that each lesson attaches to a real task rather than staying abstract. Review and quality-raising. What worked and what did not is assessed, and the next round of the loop is adjusted accordingly. The Long March of the AI Era Much of the Global South watches the spread of generative AI with unease: that it will deepen the dependence of poorer countries on a handful of firms in the wealthy core, that it will drive up the carbon cost of computation, that it will hasten the displacement of labour. None of these worries is misplaced. But fear on its own settles nothing, and the harder question is what a place without capital, advanced hardware, or a model of its own can actually do with the technology as it stands. Rongjiang’s answer begins with cost. The computation does not run on the user’s device. The heavy capital sits in the cloud, paid for by firms such as Doubao and DeepSeek that built the models and by the infrastructure of states far from Moon Mountain, and what the villager needs is only an ordinary smartphone and the knowledge of how to speak to the model. That the state should carry the compute is a matter of policy, and the ‘AI Plus’ Action names ‘strengthening the unified planning of intelligent computing power’ among its basic supports. The base model, moreover, has become a commodity: each is substitutable for the next, and the rural user is bound to no single vendor, so that should one tool withdraw or deteriorate, another takes its place. Substitutability leaves the monopoly rent nowhere to lodge, and what remains is an ordinary competitive cost — the production diffusion that China’s own development path has set against the rent-extraction of technological monopoly. Even a smartphone, though, cannot everywhere be taken for granted. Rongjiang has met that difficulty directly. When it first carried new media into the villages, many people’s handsets were too old to run the apps. Its answer was not to wait until everyone was equipped, but to begin with the willing minority who already were, let them earn a living from it, and let their example draw the rest in. Returning youth who used AI and short video to sell heritage crafts that had never before found a buyer, some of them earning a considerable income, pulled in the older women who made the goods. As those women began to earn, they bought phones of their own and began to film. The circle widens with the income. What, then, could travel? Not the machine, since Rongjiang owns none of it, but the method: a way of organising the people around new forces of production. The institutional vehicle already exists. On 10 November 2025, the county and East China Normal University opened the South School in Toutang village, in Guzhou town, as a platform for the international communication of poverty alleviation and rural revitalisation. It opened into a wider moment: the 2025 Global South Academic Forum (Shanghai, 13–14 November 2025), co-hosted by Tricontinental: Institute for Social Research , ran a panel on digital sovereignty and AI in the Global South, and drew extensive involvement from organisations such as Brazil’s Landless Workers’ Movement (MST) . The method has begun to circulate where movements, and not only governments, can take it up. The Global South’s fight for digital sovereignty is usually pictured as building its own stack – its own chips, its own models, its own data centres. For most of the periphery, that whole edifice is out of reach at the moment, and a sovereignty of that kind, if it comes at all, will be won collectively rather than by any poor country alone. Rongjiang works a different layer of the same struggle. Ownership of the machinery is not something one county can secure on its own; what it can secure, at its own scale, is the power of its people to use the tool on their own terms and for their own ends, and to keep the value they create rather than let it drain upward to a handful of firms in the North. That – a people able to use the tool, and to hold on to what it earns – is a sovereignty a poor county can actually build. Ninety years ago, the Chinese Red Army carried the Long March to victory, won not by superior arms but by organised people, their endurance, and the peasants it mobilised as it passed, rousing their energy, their initiative, their creativity. Mao Zedong called that march a seeding-machine, for the revolution it sowed along the way. Rongjiang has set out on a long march of its own, one for the AI era, and it moves in the same spirit: carried by the people, drawing out that same creativity, sowing among them a new capability as that march sowed revolution, and letting the gains of the technology flow back to them, to the many and not the few. It is not a slogan handed down but the patient work of turning a new productive force into a capacity held by the people, in the tradition of mass work. The technology will keep changing, and fast. What does not change is the insight at the centre: for a place that cannot build the stack itself, the question that remains is who, among its people, has made the model their own.
