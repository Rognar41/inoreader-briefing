America Needs a Truth Commission

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Jared O. Bell ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-17T00:16:40+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. A few weeks ago, during a trip to the Philippines, I made my way across congested Manila to Bantayog ng mga Bayani, the memorial in Quezon City to Filipinos who resisted Ferdinand Marcos. The Wall of Remembrance outside, inscribed with the names of those who died resisting, was overrun with grass. I asked one guard where to find the museum, then another, before finding it around a corner. The receptionist asked who I was with. I told her I was a professor and researcher. She indicated to me that there were no tours on Monday and that I’d be free to tour the museum on my own. The museum was worn, its displays far smaller than the story they told of one of the most precarious periods in Philippine history. They traced Marcos’s rise and the movement, and the bloodshed, it took to stop him. The exhibits were heartbreaking and, paradoxically, empowering. Among them was a newspaper front page from 1972. Its banner headline reads “FM DECLARES MARTIAL LAW.” Just beneath it, the paper reassures readers: “But civilian gov’t still functions; no military takeover.” Farther down: “Nation is calm; business, life go on normally.” As I moved through the rooms, I kept thinking about where my own country seemed to be headed. Life did not go on normally. Under martial law, according to Amnesty International , some 70,000 people were imprisoned, 34,000 tortured and 3,240 killed. Related: From Nuremberg to the ICC: America’s War on International Justice What stayed with me was what happened after. Filipinos drove Marcos from power in 1986. But many officials who had tortured prisoners kept their posts , and a law recognizing and compensating victims did not arrive until 2013 , 27 years later. By then, a sustained campaign to recast the era as a golden age was well underway. In 2022, Marcos’s son won the presidency in a landslide . An uprising had removed the father. It did not settle what he had done. I think one of the key lessons from the Philippines is that when a country has no way of dealing with its past, the abuses come back. Three years after that reparations law, Rodrigo Duterte became president. He allowed Marcos to be buried in the national Heroes’ Cemetery and launched a drug war that killed more than 6,000 people by the government’s own count and as many as 30,000 by the estimates of human rights groups. In 2022, Duterte’s daughter, Sara, ran alongside Marcos’s son and became vice president. Their alliance has since fallen apart . Marcos let the International Criminal Court arrest the elder Duterte, whose trial for crimes against humanity opens on Nov. 30. Even that reckoning is happening in The Hague, and only because two political families turned on each other. I have been thinking about that newspaper since voters handed Democrats control of both the House and Senate . Hakeem Jeffries promised to “bring accountability to those who have visited unprecedented corruption on this country.” I have spent more than 15 years in international development, leading governance and accountability programs in Central Asia, Africa, the Balkans, and Latin America, including at the U.S. Agency for International Development. The United States financed truth commissions from South Africa to Colombia. It has never applied those tools to itself. In 2025, to my shock, horror and disbelief, I watched that agency dismantled, and I was pushed out along with nearly all of its staff . I believed in it, regardless of its imperfections. It was a purge of “deep state” operatives, who apparently couldn’t or wouldn’t be loyal to the president or his agenda. We were treated as enemies of the American people and the government we served. Over the past two years, I have watched things happen here that I spent my career helping activists and concerned citizens fight against. In a single night in January 2025, President Trump fired 17 inspectors general . The Justice Department dropped corruption charges against allies, including New York’s then-mayor, Eric Adams , and opened investigations of opponents. A federal judge found probable cause that officials had willfully defied his order halting deportation flights. In Minneapolis, federal immigration agents killed two American citizens, Renée Good and Alex Pretti , an ICU nurse. Related: The War on “Socialism” Is Really a War on the Public Good The new majorities will be tempted to answer all of this with two years of hearings aimed at the 2028 campaign. I understand the appeal. It would also play into the “lawfare” narrative the administration has spent years building. The White House is already making that case. Days after the election, Trump called Democrats’ investigative requests “ extortion at the highest level ” and ordered the Justice Department to look into them. The White House counsel then warned Robert Garcia, the incoming Oversight Committee chairman, that aggressive oversight “ may cross the line into blatant criminality .” Garcia, for his part, had posted “ LAWYER UP ” on election night. Hearings run as a fight between two parties will only help Trump tell his story. There must be justice and accountability, in a sequenced manner. Congress should start by creating an independent truth commission by statute. Of course, it doesn’t have to be called a truth commission, and it may draw some political ire from Democrats and Republicans alike, as it may appear as retribution or an overreaction to the harms done. The House January 6 committee expired with the Congress that created it. A statutory body with subpoena power, bipartisan appointments and terms that outlast the next election could produce a full public accounting of how American institutions broke down. The closest American precedent is the 9/11 Commission . I first came to this work as a doctoral student in Morocco, whose truth commission could not compel officials to testify or publicly name perpetrators. Years later, I taught at a university in The Gambia as the country rolled out its Truth, Reconciliation and Reparations Commission after Yahya Jammeh’s long rule. Both commissions left behind a public record, something congressional hearings rarely do. President Trump will almost certainly veto such a bill. Congress should pass it anyway. A veto becomes part of the record, and the legislation will be ready in 2029. The findings should then drive institutional reform, including real protection for inspectors general and a statutory bar on the new rule stripping civil service protections from career employees. Prosecutions should be left to the courts, without political interference. Deciding who should be barred from public office is the hardest part, and it should come last. That decision has to rest on what a person did and what a court has proven, rather than on which party they belong to. The Philippines let torturers keep their jobs, and it is still living with the consequences. Iraq went the other way, and its de-Baathification turned into a purge of anyone tied to the old ruling party. Related: Mr. Secretary, What America Needs Is an Independent Press, Not an Actual Patriotic Press I understand why many Americans will hear “accountability” as “retribution.” That is why the order matters. A truth process that comes first, run by an independent body, is the strongest protection against a cycle of partisan revenge. And every reform described here would bind a future Democratic president just as tightly as this one. “Nation is calm; business, life go on normally.” In 1972, that headline was meant to reassure Filipinos. When I read it in that museum, it felt like a warning. I spent years writing about and researching how other countries reckon with their pasts. If the U.S. is the society dedicated to the rule of law that we claim to be, we should be making the case for our own reckoning at home. PeaceVoice

Single-Payer Healthcare Deadlock

California would seem to be the most likely state to enact a true single-payer healthcare program [SP]. After all, SP is favored by majorities, including Republicans. However, successive legislative efforts to pass CalCare, the SP plan sponsored by the California Nurses Association [CNA], have been killed by Democratic leaders before they could come to a vote. Actually, that’s not quite true: SP bills were passed by the CA legislature twice, when Republican governor Arnold Schwarzenegger promised to veto them. When Democrat Gavin Newsom, who ran on a SP platform and was supported by CNA, was elected, he made sure that no SP bill ever reached his desk. The reason for the repeated failure of SP legislation is not a mystery: the immense wealth of the health insurance complex gives them an unsurmountable advantage in determining the actions of Democratic politicians in the legislature and the governor’s office. To put it simply, when serious interests are at stake, legislators can be bribed or they can be scared. The SP forces are woefully outmatched financially, and have not yet been able to inspire the sort of public outrage that would effectively scare enough legislators. Thus, it’s not surprising that SP advocates are once again considering the option of a ballot measure that would enshrine healthcare as a right, and SP as the vehicle, in the California constitution. In 1994 CA voters defeated Proposition 186, which would have established a universal, publicly financed, single-payer health care system in California, effectively eliminating private health insurance as the principal means of paying for medical care. There were numerous reasons that effort failed, among them the timing: the Clinton health reform initiative collapsed in Congress in September 1994, just weeks before the California election. But among the major reasons the proposition failed was the enormous financial advantage of the opponents, who outspent proponents $9 million to $2.7 million. Related: Socialist Health Care Organizers Exit DSA The opposition concentrated on three arguments: higher taxes, increased government control of medical decisions, and uncertainty about the quality and availability of care under a completely reorganized system. More than 30 years later, the public is much more sophisticated about the realities of health care and certainly much more aware of the disadvantage of the profit-centered system of private health insurance, which have only become more acute with the invasion of the health sphere by private equity vultures. Any campaign to pass a SP proposition would once again have to counter the false claims that SP would cost citizens more, and would have to show business owners, as well as union members and leaders, how they would benefit by taking health insurance costs off the benefit bargaining table. But these are more achievable goals today, after decades of insurance company over-reach and greed, and with a public increasingly angered and anguished by the rising costs and diminishing benefits of private insurance. Now that the next governor will be Xavier Beccera, who has made it clear that he doesn’t support SP, there’s no point in resurrecting a doomed-to-fail legislative campaign. So what’s to prevent the advocates of SP from embarking on a ballot measure campaign? The standard answer, of course, is the enormous amount of money that would be needed, even assuming a successful grassroots campaign could be organized, with the necessary enthusiastic participation of a broad coalition of labor and community organizations. It’s the money, stupid, is the familiar refrain one hears when the topic is raised. But there might be an answer to that conversation stopper. What if there was a billionaire around who was strongly and sincerely dedicated to achieving SP healthcare in California, and was easily able to seed a ballot measure campaign with the millions needed to launch and sustain the effort ? There just might be. Tom Steyer ran in the recent gubernatorial primary on a platform that was unambiguous about his [newly acquired] support for SP: Tom knows quality health care is a right, not a privilege – and that there’s too much profit, and not enough health care, in our health care system. That’s why he’s been endorsed by the California Nurses Association , and progressive single payer health care champions like Congressman Ro Khanna and Representative Ash Kalra, the author of Cal-Care. Tom believes strongly that the only way we’ll reduce health care costs, expand coverage, and promote public health when it is under attack from the Trump administration is through a single-payer system . And he is committed to getting California there. As the largest and most powerful state, California will set an example for the rest of the United States to follow. Tom Steyer spent a reported $220 million on his unsuccessful campaign for the gubernatorial nomination, which would make spending, say, $22 million on a single-payer ballot measure a real bargain. This is the moment for the advocates of SP in California, including the California Nurses Association, to challenge Tom Steyer to put his money where his campaign platform was, and to provide the necessary funding to launch a broad-based coalition to put a true SP measure on the ballot in 2028.

Inside the Epic Battle Over Control of a CA Grocery Union

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Chris Brooks ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-16T21:22:55+00:00

Eric Marcuz is one of the most naturally gifted rank-and-file organizers I have ever met. Overflowing with passion for his union, love for his coworkers, and C4 Energy drinks, he has been spending what little time he has outside of working full time as a dairy clerk at a Safeway grocery store in Redding, California, driving for hours to visit grocery stores across the state and talk with his fellow union members. Marcuz is running for president of United Food and Commercial Workers (UFCW) Local 8–Golden State as part of the At What Cost slate, a group of seven rank-and-file grocery workers who are committed to giving their coworkers something that should be taken for granted: an opportunity to choose who leads their union. Standing in their way are staff supporters of Jacques Loveall, the local’s longtime president, whose family has maintained tight control over the union for more than four decades. Loveall inherited the presidency from his father, Jack, in 2004, through an appointment by the union’s executive board. Neither father nor son ever faced a contested election for the presidency, and the union’s top officers and executive board have been filled through appointments rather than competitive elections. The Lovealls seem straight out of central casting for a Hollywood depiction of out-of-touch, self-serving business unionists, complete with exorbitant salaries, palatial offices, a private jet, and even a bronze bust of the family patriarch. The At What Cost slate is fighting to introduce real democracy into their union. To do that, they must first collect hundreds of signatures from their fellow union members scattered across California. They have until November 17 to collect and deliver the required number of signatures. If they are successful, ballots will be mailed on November 21 and counted on December 7. The reformers have only the next few weeks during one of the grocery industry’s busiest times of year to campaign — if the union’s staff and leadership don’t stop them. The UFCW is one of the largest private sector unions in North America, representing more than a million workers, primarily in grocery stores, retail, meatpacking, and food processing. These are some of the lowest-paid and most exploited workers in the labor movement, but also some of the most indispensable to daily life, as the COVID-19 pandemic made painfully clear. Nearly everyone comes face-to-face with a grocery worker every week. This gives grocery workers a strategically important position in an increasingly concentrated industry where powerful corporations squeeze workers through understaffing, speedup, and automation while consumers struggle with rising grocery bills. Fights for democracy within megalocals — often the combination of multiple small union locals to create huge bodies that are difficult for rank-and-file workers to have real democratic say in, like UFCW Local 8 — have the potential to connect the fight for better wages and working conditions at companies like Albertsons, Kroger, Vons, Save Mart, and Safeway to the broader working-class struggle against runaway inequality. The stakes of this local election extend far beyond who will run the union. It raises the question of what kind of power tens of thousands of grocery workers could exercise if they were organized and mobilized to fight together. Which is why, after donating to their campaign, I traveled to California to spend a few days observing Marcuz and other slate members as they crisscrossed the state gathering signatures, visiting nearly twenty grocery stores in multiple towns and speaking with dozens of unionized grocery workers. I heard firsthand how grocery workers are being crushed by the rising cost of living and stagnating wages, how excited many are at the possibility of new union leadership, and how terrified some of them are of the union’s current leaders and staff. Dozens of workers described how union staff have been openly sharing their plans to harass and intimidate the At What Cost slate members as they try to gather signatures. Staff are also following behind At What Cost slate members and pressuring the workers they have spoken with into withdrawing their signatures to get them on the ballot, using a special form the staff have made specifically for this purpose. One grocery worker, who asked to remain anonymous out of fear of retaliation, described how union staff were openly discussing efforts to prevent Marcuz from qualifying for the ballot. Staff explained that if Marcuz failed to collect the required 443 signatures, there would be no contested election and Jacques Loveall would remain president. Staff were out doing everything they could to keep him from reaching that threshold. One produce clerk who has been a union member for over thirty years described Local 8’s well-known use of intimidation tactics to crush union democracy as “Jim Crow unionism.” He refused to sign Marcuz’s petition because he didn’t want “to end up in a ditch” for crossing staff. The heavy-handed intimidation tactics I’ve seen here are clearly having the intended effect on some union members. But for others, watching their own union’s staff try to stop a fellow worker from running for office has backfired. Ryan Crahm, a longtime Save Mart employee, said he wasn’t going to get involved until he saw four union staff representatives swarm Marcuz in his store. “It was straight up intimidation, and it made me pissed,” said Crahm. “I want to ask Jacques Loveall why he is so afraid to have someone run against him. And I want to know why it is that we never saw any staff reps before, but now that there might be an election, they’re in our stores all the time.” At What Cost Donning a baseball cap and a backpack filled with petitions, flyers, stickers, and homemade bandanas, the forty-three-year-old Marcuz is excited, urgent, and confident. Visiting store after store from 5 in the morning to 11 at night, he takes his time, listening, asking questions, and sharing his own experiences. His approach to petitioning resembles deep canvassing — a patient, open, and story-driven approach to sharing experiences while engaging with workers during a union drive or voters during an election campaign shown to be effective at winning hearts and changing minds — more than typical rapid-fire signature gathering. Few of his conversations run less than twenty minutes, and they typically cover a range of topics. Workers repeatedly open up to Marcuz about their frustration with the union contract’s pay scale and how the basic building blocks of the good life — home ownership, a reliable vehicle, vacations — feel increasingly out of reach. California fast food workers have a $20 minimum wage, but under Local 8’s contract, it takes members working full time about five years to reach that hourly rate. Since many grocery workers struggle to get full-time hours, it takes many of them seven or eight years, even in the delis and in-store Starbucks franchises, to earn what they could be making on their first day working at a neighboring In-N-Out Burger. Members also described unpredictable schedules, chronic understaffing, and the chaos caused by new artificial-intelligence-assisted inventory systems. Many were frustrated not only by the erosion of their contracts but also by how little say they had in negotiating them. There are no elected bargaining teams, and contracts are negotiated exclusively by the president and other appointed leaders and staff. Eric Marcuz speaks with a produce clerk at a Safeway in Lincoln, California. (Courtesy of Kelly McManus) Members, including several stewards, repeatedly complained about union staff who are rarely in the stores, don’t return phone calls, can’t answer questions about the contract, and are seemingly never around when members need them. The local’s website doesn’t provide information on the twenty-plus appointed vice presidents who allegedly represent members, or which staff representatives service their stores and how to contact them. The local also refuses to provide members with digital copies of their contracts. Remarkably, despite talking to dozens of union members, including more than a few who had worked in these stores for decades, I couldn’t find a single Local 8 member who had actually met Jacques Loveall. If the union was primarily known for anything, it is for its absence. “My mind was blown by how little visibility we had into our contracts, union negotiations, and the leadership’s decision making,” said Marcuz. “I started driving down to the union meetings in Sacramento. They’re two and a half hours from my house and held once a quarter at 8 at night. I went to my first meeting, and it literally lasted five minutes. I was the only member there.” Marcuz says he spoke up at a different meeting and asked whether, given the vast jurisdiction the local covers, quarterly meetings could rotate between different cities across the state to encourage more members to attend. He says the staff laughed him out of the room. So Marcuz started a petition, collecting three hundred signatures from grocery workers in his area demanding that the meetings at least be live streamed over Zoom so members could listen in. This time, he says, staff angrily shouted him out of the room. “Every time we tried to get involved, to make our union more responsive to the membership, we got smacked down. And once we start losing faith in our own union, we are on the road to no longer having one,” said Marcuz. The At What Cost slate is proposing sweeping changes in how the union functions. They want elected bargaining teams, open negotiations that members can attend, local meetings that encourage member participation, a $20 minimum wage in grocery, and aggressive contract campaigns backed by leadership that isn’t afraid to strike over ambitious demands. Marcuz and his small rebel army want to elect new officers, win better contracts, and fundamentally change who is exercising power inside their union, transforming members from mere spectators into active participants in the fight to better their working conditions and lives. Not a Union, but a Family Business The Loveall family has held tight control over UFCW Local 8–Golden State and its predecessor, UFCW Local 588, for more than four decades. Jack Loveall was first appointed president of the union in 1984 and ran it continuously until he retired in 2004, when his son Jacques was appointed by the union’s executive board to succeed him. At the time he retired, Jack Loveall was one of the highest-paid union officials in the country, making over $1 million a year in today’s money. The local owned a twin-engine business jet and had two pilots on staff to shuttle Loveall around the state. He oversaw the construction of a new union headquarters in Roseville, near his personal residence, which featured an 1,100-square-foot presidential office, the size of a modest house, complete with a sauna and marble wet bar. The local also paid $28,000 a year to cover the cost of Loveall’s membership in an exclusive Roseville country club, an expense the union president justified to the press by stressing that it was a place to talk business with employers. During his final year in office, there were seven Loveall family members on staff, comprising a quarter of the union’s payroll. Loveall built his empire through a series of mergers that expanded the union’s membership and assets while consolidating his control over its leadership. According to a 2004 investigation by the Contra Costa Times , critics accused Loveall of securing mergers by rewarding the officers of absorbed locals with lucrative positions in his administration. When Local 498, a Sacramento meatcutters’ union with 1,600 members and $2.3 million in assets, merged into Local 588, its former president saw his annual pay jump from roughly $12,500 to $85,000, while its former treasurer’s pay increased from less than $11,000 to nearly $76,000. The mergers helped Loveall build an increasingly loyal staff and executive board, with well-paid positions that extended to the relatives of some officers. But Loveall wasn’t always successful. Members of Local 1179 put a halt to a proposed merger with Local 588 by organizing an opposition campaign centered on Loveall’s pay and perks, arguing their dues would be better spent if their local remained independent rather than contributing to Loveall’s lavish lifestyle. Responding to these criticisms, Loveall told the press , “Just because we run a union doesn’t mean we’re supposed to be paupers” and said that “my members don’t want me driving around in a stagecoach or working in some shithouse office.” The elder Loveall also bragged about how tightly he controlled the union membership, saying that members were “ready to do what I tell them to do.” Jacques Loveall took over the top spot in 2004 but kept his father on the payroll earning more than $70,000 a year as a consultant, on top of his pension, until the elder Loveall died in 2020. Jacques is also chair of the Loveall Foundation for Children, a nonprofit founded in honor of his father and described on their website as being founded and run by “the staff, family and friends of UFCW 8–Golden State.” The union has financially supported the nonprofit for years, including $25,000 in 2025 alone, and at least one major collective bargaining agreement negotiated by Loveall obligates the employer to contribute three cents for every hour employees work directly to the Loveall Foundation. The salary of the president is set by the union’s top officers and vice presidents, all of whom were appointed over time by other top officers and vice presidents as vacancies arose. At no point over the past four decades have any of the Local’s top officers or the twenty-plus vice presidents actually been elected by union’s membership. Discussing this reality with other union members, Marcuz is blunt: “Our local hasn’t been acting like a union. It’s been run like a family business.” A Long History of Schemes and Intimidation There have been past attempts by union members to challenge the Lovealls’ tight control over the union. But those who have tried have repeatedly encountered a familiar combination of intimidation, interference, and mergers that postponed or eliminated the possibility of a contested election. Union activists in UFCW Local 1179 who were active in the effort to block the proposed merger with Local 588 reportedly experienced aggressive intimidation and surveillance from Loveall’s associates. According to an investigation by the Contra Costa Times , union staff began following reform activist Michael Ross-Smith, staking out his home and trailing him in union vehicles. In June 1994, three Local 588 business agents following Ross-Smith rear-ended his car in Walnut Creek, an incident documented in a police traffic collision report. A union representative acknowledged to the press that staff had been following Ross-Smith but defended the practice as a legitimate effort to investigate Loveall’s political opponents. In 1997, Raley’s grocery clerk Jack Nordby attempted to challenge Jack Loveall for the presidency. Nordby wanted to cut the excessive salaries of union officers and open contract negotiations to the membership. Following his announced run for office, Nordby alleges that union representatives followed him and his family, harassed him while he collected nomination signatures, and pressured workers into withdrawing their signatures from his petition. So many signatures were withdrawn that Nordby failed to qualify for the ballot. Another reformer, Lorna Greenlee, described similar intimidation. Greenlee said two union representatives confronted her while she collected signatures, then berated her for hours. She and her daughter also reported seeing union representatives watching their home. Although Greenlee said she had nearly collected enough signatures to run against Loveall, she eventually withdrew out of fear. The Lovealls have also benefited from mergers that postponed contested elections. In 2006, another reform slate, led by longtime meatcutter Doug Slaydon, was preparing to challenge Jacques Loveall. But before members could vote, Local 588 merged with Local 1288 to create UFCW Local 8–Golden State, postponing the election until 2008. Slaydon, who was fired from Safeway shortly after his attempted run for local union office, believed the merger was designed to prevent his challenge. Nearly two decades later, the same maneuvers again threatened to derail the current election. In October, Local 8 members received an election notice announcing that ballots would be mailed in November. But buried in the notice was a provision stating that the election would be canceled if the local completed a merger with an organization called Staffing Agencies Federated Employees (SAFE). None of the rank-and-file members of the At What Cost slate had ever heard of SAFE. The organization claimed to represent thousands of workers, but there was no evidence of where those workers were organized or what contracts SAFE had negotiated. What they did find were more connections to Loveall: a family member was listed as SAFE’s president on its initial federal filing just eight months before the election. The At What Cost slate, working with the UFCW reform organization Essential Workers for Democracy, on which Marcuz serves on the organizing committee, challenged the proposed merger and called for a Department of Labor investigation. After a federal investigator began asking questions, Local 8 announced that the merger had been postponed indefinitely, five days after it was announced. The election was back on. But that doesn’t mean the rank-and-file reformers would be allowed to campaign without interference. Fear and Intimidation on the Campaign Trail On November 2, Marcuz drove to the union’s headquarters in Roseville to pick up the nomination petitions that he and his slate needed to start collecting the hundreds of signatures required to qualify for the ballot. From there, he headed south toward Fresno to begin collecting signatures from grocery workers. But shortly after leaving the union headquarters, where a bronze bust of Jack Loveall stands in the central hallway, Marcuz noticed a black pickup truck following him. When he stopped for gas, the truck stopped too. He recognized the driver as Jeff Berns, a senior Local 8 staff representative whose brother, Mark Berns, was appointed by Jaques Loveall to chair the election. Multiple members of the Berns family have been on the local’s payroll going back decades. Mark Berns is currently the highest paid nonofficer staff member at the local, earning more than most of the vice presidents. He has repeatedly served as the local’s election chair, including in 2009, when the Department of Labor found that Local 8 had violated federal labor law by burying an election notice inside a union newsletter, mailed just a week after another issue with a nearly identical cover. The notice was so inconspicuous that members could easily have mistaken the second mailing for a duplicate and thrown it away without realizing an election was approaching. Despite finding that the local had violated federal labor law, because investigators couldn’t identify any member who said they would have run for office against Loveall, the department concluded that the violation had not affected the election’s outcome and took no further action. According to Marcuz, Jeff Berns told him that he intended to follow him throughout the day to make sure workers heard the Loveall team’s side of the election. And that’s exactly what he did. Berns followed Marcuz to a Save Mart in Fresno, where Marcuz and fellow slate member Mike Romero say three union representatives, including longtime Vice President John Micheletti, confronted them and interfered with their attempts to collect signatures. According to Marcuz, Berns threatened to invalidate every signature his slate collected. This behavior continued as Marcuz left Fresno and traveled to meet Silas Saldivar, a twenty-six-year UFCW member and head clerk, at the Safeway where he worked in Modesto. Marcuz became so concerned for his safety that he called Saldivar, and they agreed to instead meet at Saldivar’s house. I traveled with Marcuz to the Safeway in Modesto, where Saldivar recounted the story to me. “I walked outside into my driveway to meet Eric and saw Jeff Berns pull up in front of my house in his truck. John Micheletti parked in front of my neighbor’s house and then got out of his car and started walking toward us. I looked at Eric and said, ‘Dude, is this guy serious right now?’ Those two guys sat outside and watched my house for over an hour,” said Saldivar. “I didn’t feel safe. I still don’t, honestly.” While Saldivar was recounting this story to me, two union staff showed up at his Safeway. They kept their distance but made it clear that they were watching us. One pulled out his phone and began recording us. Marcuz estimated that he had been followed for seven hours and roughly three hundred miles on November 2. The following morning, he filed a police report alleging stalking and harassment. The intimidation hasn’t been limited to Marcuz and the other candidates. Less than a week later, Mike Denham, a Local 8 staff representative, showed up at the Perfect Union cannabis dispensary in Shasta Lake, California, where Marcuz had recently visited and received overwhelming support from members. Many workers were frustrated that they had never seen a copy of their union contract and couldn’t get one from the local. One supporter had asked Marcuz to leave a nomination petition so workers on other shifts could sign it, planning to return it to him later. According to multiple workers who asked to remain anonymous out of fear of retaliation from their union, Denham, the union staffer assigned to the dispensary, subsequently arrived and demanded that they either destroy the petition or turn it over to him so he could destroy it himself. According to several members, Denham repeatedly insisted that Marcuz had acted “illegally” by leaving the petition with workers at the store. When workers refused to turn it over, Denham eventually left in frustration. “It’s absolutely insane that our own union staff would come into our workplace to intimidate us to stop us from participating in our own union,” one worker told me. Federal labor law guarantees the right of union members to participate in democratic elections. The conduct of Local 8’s leadership and staff could have legal consequences. “The quantity and variety of the efforts by Loveall’s team are not only shocking, but likely unlawful, and could lead to the Department of Labor having to step in and rerun the election,” said Will Bloom, law professor at City University of New York and board member of the Association for Union Democracy. Discouraging Participation Is Anti-Union At a Vons grocery store in Fresno, multiple workers confessed to us that their union staff representative, Justin Putney, aggressively pressured them to sign the Loveall slate’s nomination petition. Workers told me Putney had forcefully instructed them to call him if Marcuz or anyone else from the At What Cost slate showed up so that he could come down and run them out of the store. It wasn’t long before that happened. Members working in the Starbucks kiosk were signing Marcuz’s petition when Putney came running up. “There he is,” Putney announced. “I wish I could say it was nice to meet you, but it isn’t.” Putney proceeded to lecture Marcuz about how “there is a right way and a wrong way to do things.” Putney, he said, had done things the right way. He had worked his way up through the union, starting as a steward before being appointed to a member advisory committee, then the executive board, and finally landing a position on staff. Putney failed to mention that his father is also a longtime paid executive board member in the union — a point Marcuz raised and Putney didn’t dispute. According to Putney, Marcuz, by contrast, was doing it wrong. “You’re like someone who has been playing rec league soccer saying he wants to jump into the big leagues and play professional soccer.” Marcuz responded that he believed democracy was the right way for members to decide who should lead their union. “And I don’t think you should have the right to run for a job that you have no qualifications for,” Putney shot back. Marcuz challenged Putney over the union’s bargaining record. “Our last contract was horrible,” Marcuz said. “It only provided a dollar raise for topped-out, high-seniority clerks. Nobody else got anything. We are barely able to survive on our wages. I can’t afford a house for my family. I can’t buy a truck. We can’t afford to shop in the stores where we work.” “You can’t show me anywhere in the country where grocery workers have a better contract,” Putney insisted. “Any contract that the membership fights for and wins together is better than one that is delivered from on high without our involvement,” Marcuz replied. “Members should be in the room when negotiating is happening. We should own the outcome.” I introduced myself as a journalist following Marcuz’s campaign and asked Putney why he had rushed down to the store to confront him. “He is a threat to the future of our members,” Putney told me. “It’s my job to protect the membership, and he represents a threat to that.” I asked whether he thought it was appropriate for union staff to confront members who were exercising their democratic rights, and whether he had been instructing workers to alert him whenever Marcuz showed up so he could interfere with his campaigning. “Yes,” Putney acknowledged. Marcuz was incredulous. “How am I supposed to exercise my rights if everywhere I go I’m being accosted by people on the union’s payroll?” he asked. “I have a right to speak with members, and you’re showing up here just to stop that from happening.” Putney maintained that he was simply encouraging workers to be cautious about what they signed. Marcuz wasn’t buying it. “Discouraging people from participating in the democratic process of a union election is anti-union. This process should strengthen us as a union, because it should cause the incumbent union leadership to state and defend their platform. We should debate our visions and what we are fighting for, and the members should get to vote on the best ideas,” said Marcuz. “But that is not what is happening. What’s happening is that I’m being followed, my conversations are being interrupted, and I’m being harassed by staff who have no honest interest in what I have to say and are only trying to stop others from hearing what I have to say. Let’s be honest. Your goal is to make sure that we don’t have a choice.” Putney denied the accusation, but Marcuz continued. “You tried to merge with a fake union that doesn’t really exist and that Jacques Loveall put his cousin in charge of. Staff followed me three hundred miles and sat in front of a member’s house for over an hour to try to intimidate me from participating. You have encouraged people in this store to report to you that I’m here so that you can come here and interfere with my campaigning,” he said. “None of these actions are to prove to the membership that your vision for our union is better. All of those actions are to prevent democracy in our union.” As the exchange continued, a crowd of grocery workers were gathering to listen. Eventually, a distraught-looking manager approached Putney and asked to speak with him privately. Marcuz took that as his cue. We left the store so he could continue gathering signatures, hoping that he wouldn’t be followed. “Solidarity Works” Loveall and the executive board members supporting him are running together on the “Solidarity Works Team” slate. And their materials look deceptively similar to official communications from the union. “Solidarity Works” has been Local 8’s slogan for at least fifteen years, appearing regularly in union communications, especially those concerning contract negotiations. Loveall himself has described it “as the motto of UFCW 8–Golden State.” A recent Solidarity Works Team mailer sent to every Local 8 union member’s personal address is virtually indistinguishable from an ordinary union communication. For one, the return address on the mailers is the union headquarters. For another, it features Loveall’s photograph and title as union president, invokes the local’s record of bargaining victories, and describes the Solidarity Works Team as a “network of member contacts” that lays “the groundwork for the solidarity, communication, and mobilization that will be needed when bargaining begins.” A QR code on the mailer links to the Solidarity Works Team website, where members are encouraged to join the team by providing their contact information. What the mailer and website don’t mention is that the Solidarity Works Team is running for reelection. There is no information on any of the slate’s candidates other than Loveall, and the word “election” doesn’t appear anywhere on the mailer or the slate’s website. The website is carefully crafted to lead members to believe they are signing up as part of the process of preparing for contract bargaining, not agreeing to receive information to vote in support of the current leadership in an election that isn’t mentioned anywhere. While out visiting stores, multiple grocery workers told me that the union staff representatives had asked them to update their contact information to prepare for upcoming negotiations. The representatives handed workers their phones and had them fill out a sign-up page, only for the workers to later discover it was to receive messages from the Solidarity Works Team slate. Those members subsequently received text messages notifying them that “petition signing is under way” and warning, “Reminder: DO NOT sign anything for anyone you don’t know. Solidarity Works!” The Loveall slate’s materials also issue stark warnings about “anti-union campaigns” and “attempts to weaken your bargaining power and possibly your benefits package.” In a video posted on the slate’s website, staff warn: “Be very careful with your signatures. Do not sign petitions. Do not sign cards. Do not trust the word of people who say they are with our union that you are not familiar with. Again, you should not be signing anything for anyone you do not know.” Anyone who has organized a union will recognize these talking points. They’re the same warnings employers routinely deploy during union-busting campaigns: beware of outsiders, don’t trust anyone asking for your signature, don’t believe their promises, and don’t risk the wages and benefits you already have. Only this time, these warnings aren’t coming from an employer but from the union’s own leadership. Jacques Lovall did not respond to an interview request for this story. “We Are at War” At a Safeway in Lincoln, a suburb outside Sacramento, I meet Mary MacDonnell, who has worked in the grocery industry for over thirty years and supports the At What Cost slate. The night before we met, John Micheletti arrived at her store and began badgering her and others to sign the Solidarity Works Team slate’s petition. Micheletti loudly announced to a group of members gathered near her that “we are at war. The union is under attack. That is why I am here.” The author interviewing Mary Macdonnell while being surveilled in the distance by a Loveall supporter. (Courtesy of Kelly McManus) MacDonnell has just finished her shift, and Marcuz and I are sitting on the bench outside the front entrance of the grocery store talking with her. She is emphatic that her union should be an organization of workers, run by workers, to fight for workers. That can’t happen if the union’s leadership is completely insulated from the membership and the staff and executive board are packed with relatives and appointed loyalists. She says she was drawn to the At What Cost slate because they practiced what they preached. The slate is encouraging members to talk and listen to each other, to build relationships, to be courageous and more ambitious, and to see greater member involvement as a source of strength for the union, not a threat to be mocked or ham-fistedly suppressed. As we are talking, one of the Loveall slate supporters from the night before arrives. He posts up with his back against the brick wall on the other side of the store’s entrance, conspicuously watching us. “This has been hella intimidating,” MacDonnell sighs as she eyes the man. But then her resolve seems to harden. “I respect Eric, but at the end of the day, this is bigger than any particular candidate. I want a choice. And I’m just not going to allow them to take that away from us.” This article originally appeared in Jacobin , a democratic socialist magazine publishing long-form essays and analysis on politics, economics, and culture. When this happens, there is often something hinky going on. Matthew 25 recently made it all the way into the New York Times because of the number of politicians with winning midterm campaigns who are proud to call themselves “Matthew 25 Christians.” Had the Times reporter scratched a little deeper, she would have seen that the political deployment of this New Testament chapter, specifically its final section (vv. 31-46), is actually nothing new. Operatives for Barack Obama deployed it in 2008 ; and even before then, progressive evangelical Jim Wallis was calling Matthew 25 his “conversion chapter.” Eloquent Third Reconstruction advocate William Barber often cites this passage. So what exactly is my beef with this? Do I not find Christ’s condemnation of bad actors quite deeply satisfying? Actually, I have several concerns. To begin, church people of a bright pink persuasion (and yes, I am one) will always be wary of using proof texting for any purpose. In Jewish tradition, nobody is supposed to rejoice over the drowning of Pharoah’s army; these kvelling Christians could learn something from their Jewish elders. A second concern: the mandate to stand with and minister to “the least of these” is clearly something Jesus followers are expected to take seriously. I certainly have no quarrel with this mandate or even with the important idea that God judges nations for their behavior. But have Democrats as a whole, even Democrats like Barack Obama who use the Matthew 25 language, been especially consistent about standing with the lost and the least? Seems to me we have a case of cheap grace when politicians claim the righteous mantle for themselves and then fail to deliver the goods, which happens again and again in a system driven by opportunism. My third concern: if you want to bring the Jesus POV into politics, and specifically, if you want to invoke the Great Reversal (“the first shall be last, and the last shall be first”) and lift up a jubilee theology (i.e., cancel the debts, let the prisoners go free), there are plenty of effective ways to do this, using scripture, without going all eschatological on us. Suffice it to say, there is major eschatological entanglement in Matthew 25. The chapter is built into a longer section often called the Olivet Discourse and dealing with last things. In fact, the 24 th chapter, flowing uninterruptedly into ch. 25, belongs with like passages in Mark and Luke that fall under the rubric “Little Apocalypse” and that clearly echo the language of the Book of Revelations. Resistance Runs Deep Lessons of Collective Action and Solidarity from the Underground Railroad At The Water’s Edge In the 1800s, the deep, dark currents of the Detroit River held both danger and deliverance. On the last stretch of a grueling journey, freedom seekers might find themselves at the water’s edge, hearts pounding with fear and anticipation. On the opposite shore lay Canada, where slavery had been abolished and a new life finally hovered within reach. No more stolen labor, stooping over crops sunup to sundown for enslavers’ profit. No more whipping posts and auction blocks and families ripped apart. For the first time, their humanity would be recognized by the law. public domain Info Info “I do not recollect of ever seeing my mother by the light of day. She was with me in the night. She would lie down with me, and get me to sleep, but long before I waked she was gone.” – Frederick Douglass Underground Railroad Leader, Abolitionist, Author, Orator, Formerly Enslaved Freedom Seeker The river became the bridge to the first breath of that new life. At this critical crossing, evading capture could mean clinging to a flimsy raft or rowboat, slipping stealthily aboard a ferry, or crawling through a secret tunnel like the one beneath the original Mariners’ Church. Precise timing and trusted lookouts were key, and that need mobilized communities in Detroit. The odds of even making it this far were low. Only a small fraction of freedom seekers across the country would ever reach their destination. Those brave enough to undertake the journey faced relentless danger and revenge, including branding, whipping and death. The system was designed to shatter their hope. The institutional power behind the slave trade was deeply entrenched. Wealthy interests depended on it. Fugitive Slave Acts protected it. Bounty hunters profited from it. In fact, despite some restrictions, slavery existed in Detroit until it was fully prohibited when Michigan was established as a state in 1837. Yet against those forces stood another kind of power: ordinary people with extraordinary courage, in places like Detroit. “The Negro slave, denied education, de-humanized, imprisoned on cruel plantations, knew that far to the north a land existed where a fugitive slave if he survived the horrors of the journey could find freedom. The legendary underground railroad started in the south and ended in Canada. The freedom road links us together.” – Rev. Anwar Shaikh Gave Us an Emancipatory Economics

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Clara E. Mattei ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-16T18:52:02+00:00

Writing about someone who has changed your life and to whom you owe so much is much more difficult than I would have thought. It is impossible to feel capable of delivering even a fraction of what it would take to fully honor the human and intellectual memory of Anwar Shaikh. I met Anwar at a moment of great intellectual insecurity for myself that he helped me overcome. Now, in front of this blank page, that insecurity is back. At the same time, this state of mind allows me to fully reconnect with the memories of our first encounters and the development of a mentorship and friendship that has deeply shaped me as a scholar and as a person. Grounding Theory in Historical Analysis It was 2015, and I was struggling to pursue the research for my economics PhD in Pisa, Italy. It had been a tough couple of years: after a five-year university degree in philosophy and history, the doctoral program had thrown me into hardcore economics courses. There were intense examinations in mathematics, econometrics, statistics, microeconomics, and game theory, which I had survived by studying nonstop and memorizing formulas that I would obsess over at night, half awake, half in my dreams. After so many examinations and so much peer pressure, it took me a while to find a subject I could feel passionate about. Economics, as my course textbooks presented it, was a dry, abstract discipline, all about technique and not much about intellectual stimulation. I felt demotivated and especially out of place. Was I ever going to be fit for this? I cared deeply about social justice and critique of the system — I read Karl Marx’s Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts and Karl Polanyi’s The Great Transformation — but none of it seemed to fit the current canon of scientificity. My supervisor, Giovanni Dosi, suggested I work on austerity, and I agreed, but I could not find an approach that was not so deeply economistic, focused only on debt and growth. Finally, while desperately roaming the library, I found an article on Fascist economics in 1920s Italy. I realized its deep affinity with contemporary economic policies in Europe, especially Italy. That day, I had a moment of awakening and decided to embark on archival research to trace the genesis of these policies in the thought of the economists who were advising Benito Mussolini. The research was challenging. I adhered to no specific methodological framework. To make sense of things, I realized I had to surpass the narrow limits of what history of economic thought, economic history, or economics meant for the profession. In my scholarly journey, I had found supportive mentors — Nicola Giocoli, Alessandro Nuvolari, and George Peden, for example — but there was clearly not much space for me in Italian academia, especially within economics. I was not doing enough of what economics was supposed to look like, but to maintain my motivation, I could not conform. This is when an email from Anwar changed my life forever. It read: “Dear Clara, I could not stop reading your paper once I started! It is excellent, and I hope you continue to pursue the parallels between the post-WWI rationale for austerity and the subsequent reincarnations in the 1980s and again now. Congratulations on a fine piece of work.” I remember gasping with happiness and a sense of recognition that I had never felt before. Really? A scholar like Anwar was taking my research seriously? And in an economics department? In his role as chair of the Economics Department at the New School for Social Research, Anwar was responding to an exploratory email I had sent just a week earlier, inquiring about a visiting scholar position. I had only recently discovered the existence of a department where Marxian economics was part of the canon, where economics seemed to be about substance as well as technique. In that email, I had attached a chapter of my dissertation, but I had no expectation whatsoever that Anwar was going to open the file. But he did, and from then on he would continue to make clear the importance of understanding the history of economic thought as integral to the analysis of capitalism itself. He wrote again inviting me both as a visiting scholar and to give a talk on my work to the department. That email exchange was a real turning point in my life’s journey. When I arrived in New York City in September 2015, I went to Anwar’s office hours. His wisdom and generosity were traits that struck me immediately. He was not teaching that semester, but I would stop by his office to get his feedback on some ideas. The week before my scheduled department talk, he told me: “You know, we are hiring, and you should apply.” The message was implicit: take this talk as an opportunity to showcase your work. At that point, I was exhilarated. The amount of adrenaline I felt leading up to that 4 p.m. Tuesday seminar was something I would never experience again. Anwar fought hard for the department to hire me. I only learned this after I had begun working as an assistant professor of economics at the New School. He believed in me so strongly. I still have a hard time understanding why and have never felt worthy of such high esteem. My dear colleague Ying Chen, whom the department hired at the same time as me, and I were both so lucky to have his support. We will miss those lunches, sharing different varieties of spicy Chinese food over conversations both fun and serious. A Life’s Mission In the summer of 2016, right before I moved to New York to start teaching, I helped organize a couple of stops for Anwar’s book launch in Italy. The stops were at the International University College of Turin and at my alma mater, the Sant’Anna School of Advanced Studies in Pisa. Anwar had just published his masterpiece , Capitalism: Competition, Conflict, Crises . There is one conversation with him I will remember forever: We were sitting at a café on the Po River, and he started telling me about the commitment and devotion it had taken him to produce his book. It had taken him over thirty years to completely reset the foundations of economics and the study of capitalism. He told me that escaping the traps of dominant economic theory was a sacrosanct mission to change the world, that deep study carried enormous transformational weight, and that it required sacrificing a lot of worldly distractions. He emphasized that he had supported me so strongly because he had seen potential in my research and in my devotion to it. He believed I could make a critical contribution to the study of capitalism and the political dimensions of economics. The connection between so-called pure economics and fascism that I was unveiling was for him key to unsettling some accepted truths about our economic system. It was then that I promised him I would persevere. And in times of demotivation, his words are always there to motivate me. I spent another six years developing my book, The Capital Order . I always wondered whether the final result lived up to his expectations, but luckily, he did seem proud. It was really thanks to Anwar’s influence and mentorship that I was able to give a sturdy theoretical foundation to the archival research, tracing its roots in the deep tendencies of our economic system. I also wanted the archival research to bolster the systemic analysis, especially of the deeply political forces that shape our economy. More generally, the book is the product of the intellectual milieu of the department Anwar introduced me to. The intellectual stimulation and methodological insights I received, including from colleagues like Duncan Foley, completely transformed it. I remember the long conversations on the phone with Anwar during the COVID-19 lockdown, in which he meticulously commented on my interpretation of Ralph Hawtrey’s theory of inflation and explained how best to measure rates of exploitation and profit. His comments, often critical, were not easily digestible, and I would take them bit by bit and dissect them with Aditya Singh, my loyal research assistant, who was also Anwar’s student. Neither the Perfect Nor the Imperfect but the Real What Anwar has gifted us all is his formidable lens through which to interpret capitalism and challenge the mainstream. He offered a lucidity about the systemic tendencies of our economic system and a holistic approach that, as many, including Vivek Chibber , have already stated, bears comparison with that of Karl Marx himself. In my years working at the New School, I attended two of Anwar’s graduate courses. I experienced firsthand how he was able to translate the titanic amount of knowledge in his book into effective pedagogical tools. His story of the chicken pot pie, for example, embodies his capacity to convey the nature of capitalism in an intuitive and lasting way. Once Mary sold her chicken pot pie hobby to a business, it did not matter what was in the pies, which became full of hormone-treated chicken; what mattered was their price. With an image that stuck, Anwar explained the fundamental logic of value as being completely detached from use value, from those qualities that made the chicken pot pie so good. Anwar delivered, like no one else, the antibodies against bourgeois economics, against those “theories” that only help hide rather than explain. In his book, as in his classes, students learned to wear different lenses through which to look at the economic world. They came to understand the slick capacity of the “perfectionist” and “imperfectionist” approaches to idealize markets, competition, and progress. Shifting from one framework to another, examining their explanations of inflation, profits, and unemployment and their policy implications, Anwar was able to deliver a scathing critique of both neoclassical (perfectionist) and Keynesian (imperfectionist) economics. He tore down the edifice not just of neoclassical economics but also of its supposed Keynesian alternative with grace and precision. He unmasked how they shared much more than one would think, especially a common backbone of idealization and an urge to get back to perfection, which Keynesians thought state intervention could achieve. As he put it in his brilliant interview with the Institute for New Economic Thinking: “The notion of imperfection is a dual of perfection; you can’t speak of imperfection unless you are implicitly paying allegiance to perfection, and I do not want to be trapped in that space.” He also recalled something I had felt deeply myself: “When I started as a graduate student, I was surprised to discover that the representation of the world was an idealized fictitious form that makes no sense to me, and that as an economist you had to accept that, then spend your time showing how the world is imperfect.” That is why his blackboard did not have just these two possibilities: next to the perfectionist and the imperfectionist, there was a third column that had vastly more explanatory power. Anwar was not content with merely modifying the existing framework but spent thirty years rebuilding the foundations of economics from scratch. What he built was revelatory. And it had to start from real observation, from confronting the naked reality of capitalism. Real economics, he liked to call it: an approach that vindicated and expanded, both theoretically and empirically, the most important intuitions of Adam Smith’s, David Ricardo’s, and especially Marx’s analysis, without calling it Marxist. In this, Anwar was adamant and would not accept compromises. He thought it important to get out of the niche, to avoid the self-sectarianism typical of heterodox economists. As a matter of fact, he disliked the term “heterodox” for exactly that same reason: identifying yourself as an antithesis to the orthodoxy weakens the capacity of real economics to stand on its own feet and gain hegemony. As he wrote in the introduction to his book: “Its object of investigation is neither the perfect nor the imperfect but the real.” Profit as the Engine Anwar’s whole economic edifice rested on one crucial intuition that, paradoxically, the perfectionist and imperfectionist paradigms had disregarded: profit drives all the system’s dynamics; it is the engine of the whole economic system. The incessant quest for profits drives investment and economic growth. Profit shapes supply (firms produce with the goal of making a profit) and aggregate demand (purchases of inputs, plant, and equipment, and the hiring of workers and their capacity to consume). As he put it concisely in the slides he shared with me: “The profit motive always remains central, and its dominance creates the force field that shapes and channels microeconomic and macroeconomic outcomes. This is the proper meaning of the invisible hand.” He reappropriated the meaning of Smith’s invisible hand not as a metaphysical myth of the eternal efficiency of the market but as the expression of those profit imperatives that operated through real competition. Through the lens of the profit motive, Anwar drew a few fundamental intuitions. First, class struggle is at the heart of the system, and technological dynamism is one of its expressions. Modern economic analysis reduces capital to durable wealth and understands profit as a reward for abstinence and entrepreneurial search. Against this, Anwar reminded us that capital had nothing to do with the qualities of the commodity. Instead, it had everything to do with capitalism’s specific historical process — potentially infinite accumulation — and the class strugglethat grounds it. Indeed, profit mirrors surplus labor, and as he says, the existence of surplus labor is a “social outcome.” He writes: “Surplus product only arises when the length of the working day exceeds the working time to reproduce the standard of living of the employed worker, that is, only when surplus labor is performed.” Technological progress is no proof of the fairy tale that capitalism exists to maximize consumer satisfaction with ever better commodities. To the contrary, firms invest in new technology to squeeze more surplus out of their workers and to cut costs below their rivals’. In real competition, firms that fail to do so don’t survive. Second, the real rests on a conflictual structure, precisely the one that neoclassical economists seek to erase in favor of a harmonious scheme that persuades us to believe we live in the best of all possible worlds. Far from the “ballet” of perfect competition, it is a war of all against all. The business literature showcased extremely well how the profit motive played out through real competition. In Anwar’s words, “Firms set prices; they try to survive; some win; some don’t; they use tools to attack each other. Roughly 70 percent of firms die within the first three years.” As Chibber has pointedly discussed , this theory led Anwar to deconstruct traditional leftist understandings of the dysfunctions of capitalism. Capitalism’s violent outcomes do not stem from monopoly power “corrupting” competition; they arise precisely out of a system working as it should, driven solely by the quest for profit. Third, crisis is the norm under capitalism, an expression of crucial recurrent patterns or “gravitational tendencies” that emerge out of the very dominance of the profit motive itself. As Anwar put it, “The history of market systems reveals recurrent patterns of booms and busts over centuries, emanating precisely from the developed world.” These include the great depressions of the 1840s, the Long Depression of 1873–1896, the Great Depression of the 1930s, the stagflation crises of the 1970s, and the great global crises of the 2010s. In this long-run perspective, excessive financialization was merely the trigger of the 2008 crash; its underlying cause was a crisis of profitability. Anwar’s real economic framework can also explain other devastating features of our economic system, including the structural economic plunder of the Global South and permanent warfare as a stimulus to profitability that does not risk subordinating the profit motive to social goals. Such subordination is correctly seen as a threat to the capitalist order and labeled “socialistic.” My work on austerity’s structural role in managing capitalism — by repressing wages and preserving the social relations underpinning profitability — is charged by this theoretical insight. Indeed, it underscores how capitalist governments must find the “right” stimulus policies — those that do not challenge market dependence or the command of private investment. Turbulent Gravitation These observations are important to avoid easy misunderstandings. Anwar was not a determinist. The patterns he discovered were important to guide systemic knowledge but did not eliminate agency. Rather, Anwar developed a multidimensional structure of influence that is at once hierarchical and deeply historical. This is the significance of turbulent gravitation : a relatively small set of operative principles can explain a great variety of economic phenomena, without reducing the dynamics of capitalism to mechanical laws. There are observable patterns in the system — but humans can organize and change the system through political action. This is the deeply historical and political approach that runs through Anwar’s analysis. He repudiated the bourgeois distinction between, on the one hand, “ahistorical laws” and, on the other, the “contingencies” of history. One must understand that classes, through their historical agency, create the very imperatives upon which they act, even as those imperatives bind them. This is particularly important when it comes to the “gain-seeking behavior” that defines capitalism as a particular social form. The imperatives of profitability are not simply external laws bearing down on historical actors, nor are they merely the outcome of individual choices. Class relations and competition produce them historically, even as they confront those actors as real constraints that shape their actions. The beauty of Anwar’s economic analysis is that he could identify the underlying regularities of capitalism without abstracting them from the historical relations of class and agency through which actors continually reproduce them. This also speaks to Anwar’s particular relationship to empirical data. He was emphatically empirical, but not a positivist. His argument was that classical assumptions are consistent with empirical reality. He understood that data are never innocent. Data are not simply a collection of preexisting facts waiting for an observer. As he writes, “Theory intervenes not merely in the interpretation of events but in their very representation (and occasionally in their suppression, as we know only too well).” His example of unemployment is particularly telling: the official definition counts someone as employed if they did any work at all for pay or profit. Such a definition masks a much bleaker picture of the realities of unemployment and underemployment as structural features of the system. Anwar’s historical proclivity, expressed in his attention to social relations, is particularly clear in his passion for Michał Kalecki’s famous 1943 article, “Political Aspects of Full Employment,” which we discussed at length on many occasions. He thought this was one of the most important pieces of political economy ever written, as it clearly stressed one of the strongest Marxian intuitions: capital not only produces unemployment — it fundamentally depends on it. Keynesian economics, with its utopian delirium of ignoring exploitation, had depoliticized the economy. It failed to realize that its goal — full employment — was technically possible, but that only a fascist dictatorship could achieve it without undermining capitalism. Indeed, only under fascism can you forgo the impersonal discipline of unemployment because political discipline is firmly in place. I draw heavily on these conversations in my most recent book, Escape From Capitalism . Anwar reviewed it in detail and wrote a final blurb for it: “A book for the times. Mattei takes aim at the notion that the forces which regulate market and social outcomes are socially neutral. Mattei argues that we need to reject this framework, to ‘politicize our lives to participate in the transformation of the material and social reality around us’ at the local and global level.” Anwar’s approach deeply inspired the book, and I try to bring it to life for a broader, nonacademic audience. I carry forward his conviction that economics is a social science and therefore a historical and political science. I especially carry forward what I understand to be the fundamental Shaikhian spirit: it is only by explaining how capitalism really works that we can sharpen our tools to overcome it. Indeed, Anwar’s work has inspired generations of young people and won international acclaim. “Don’t Give It Up” Anwar was extremely supportive of my embarking on the journey to the University of Tulsa. He gave a brilliant opening lecture at the inaugural conference of the Center for Heterodox Economics there, which I had organized. He remained deeply supportive throughout the turbulent times at the university and when I began setting up the independent Forum for Real Economic Emancipation (FREE). I helped organize a wonderful retirement celebration for Anwar, which produced a special issue on his work in The New School Economic Review . Afterward Anwar enjoyed his new home at the lake in Amherst, writing, fishing, cooking, and visiting friends like Robert Pollin. We spoke on the phone often. Anwar had helped create the intellectual ethos of the New School for Social Research Economics Department and had opposed academic hierarchies all his life. He was appalled at the administration’s attacks , like firing tenured and untenured faculty members and blocking PhD admissions, which were effectively dismantling the department. His recorded words at the retirement party ring true to all of us: The important thing about democracy is that you have a voice. It does not mean you have to agree with me. You have to have a voice, and you constantly need to protect it, and you have to act on it. Look around you — no department anywhere has this kind of voice. So don’t give it up. No matter what they do. They are going to try to make you give it up. Don’t give it up. Go and strike if you have to. Close the place down. Carrying the Work Forward Iremember the shock of that phone call in which he told me he had cancer. I pretended it wasn’t real: his voice was so combative that it seemed impossible that anything could beat him. To the very end, as he heroically fought his devastating illness, he remained focused on his scholarship and on the possibility of delivering an abbreviated version of his masterpiece, updated with new empirical data. Anwar’s archives at Bard College reveal letters that Joan Robinson and Nicholas Kaldor wrote to him in the 1970s, already recognizing the power of his work. Fortunately, Anwar leaves behind a considerable number of students, now spread across the world. A recent piece by Nikos Chatzarakis perfectly exemplifies their power to speak of his breakthroughs. As his former student Juan Santarcángelo, now a professor in Argentina, has forcefully put it, “Anwar will never truly die; he will live on in each of us and in those who were lucky enough to know him.” It is our task to carry his paradigm forward, advance it, and spread it widely, publishing and making his work available in many different forms. This is something to which I intend to devote considerable energy in the coming years. Anwar was one of the most powerful thinkers of our time. His legacy finds an especially important place in this historical moment, when the violence of capitalism is rampant and the thirst for radical political change so strong. The massive archive that his youngest student, Nicholas Anderson, has digitized, now held at Bard College, is a treasure that we need to cultivate and explore in detail. It is Anwar’s real economics, and its potential for full emancipation, that we need to keep alive as a guide to collective action and the possibility of change. This is what we are trying to do at FREE , an assembly-based project for empowering education and concrete postcapitalist alternatives that is spreading globally. I hope many more people will join us in this collective endeavor for a humane, profit-liberated future. The Executioner and the Loyalist: How Harvard Spent Summer 2026 Gutting Its Largest Union for Financial Gain

A sign held up at the joint HGSU-HAW strike picket line in November 2026, referencing sister union HUCTW’s culling months before (Working Mass) By: Tony A, Adrian Aliano CAMBRIDGE – With the first strike by their workers that year abated, and two other unions’ anger rising, Harvard executives spent the summer of 2026 carrying out far-reaching cuts that gutted the ranks of the largest union on campus: the Harvard Union of Clerical and Technical Workers (HUCTW). In the end, hundreds of people were kicked out of the halls of the university to which they dedicated their lives, just for Harvard leaders to report exorbitant earnings made off their backs only weeks later. The decimation required an executioner and a loyalist, but started with a secretive firm: McKinsey & Company. Leaders in the Faculty of Arts and Sciences (FAS) appointed a task force to address “Workforce Planning” on April 24, 2025. Under their oversight, Harvard University started paying McKinsey & Company in quiet installments starting August 2025. Harvard paid McKinsey a total of $250,000 for FAS consultation. In other words, Harvard hired a firm that not only has contracted with ICE, but where whistleblowers report consultants become “functionally the equivalent of full-time ICE employees” when working for ICE, rather than protect their international students facing deportation from that same agency. Sinister change crept into Harvard within months of the final installment. Hiring the Executioner Warren Petrofsky was at Cornell University when workforce planning at Harvard FAS began. As Associate Dean of Administration from 2023 until his departure in 2026, Petrofsky “helped shape long-term strategic vision and resource allocation for $4000M enterprise,” as he proclaims on LinkedIn. Part of his Cornell tenure included the signing of an agreement with the Trump Administration for money: $60 million, as well as compliance with the federal government’s definitions and orders regarding civil rights definitions. Beyond big deals with bigger devils, according to Cornell Sun reporting, the College of Arts and Sciences at Cornell cut $11 million after “very specific” funding guidelines sent to every department by Petrofsky. He developed a reputation, before his hiring, as the man who would comb through every line and cut any position – ruthlessly. Petrofsky completed his undergraduate education at the University of Pennsylvania and spent a combined over two decades at Penn working in computing and operations. In that time, he completed a Master of Business Administration (MBA) at Wharton before his promotion to Chief Infrastructure Office for the School of Arts and Sciences. That position was charged with saving Penn money, the role he took on at Cornell. Harvard scooped Petrofsky from Ithaca within one fiscal quarter of the University’s last quiet installment to McKinsey & Company. Warren Petrofsky was appointed Dean of Administration and Finance of the Faculty of Arts and Sciences on April 20, 2025: in short, he became Harvard’s money guy, apparently strategically identified following his work saving two other Ivy League universities money. The University’s press release cited his previous experiences in “administrative transformation within complex, decentralized Arts and Sciences environments,” like Cornell. Though Harvard recognized Petrofsky’s role at Penn in the same breath as that at Cornell, Working Mass has not been able to verify if he was responsible for extreme cost-cutting at the expense of others at Penn. How much Harvard University currently pays Warren Petrofsky is unknown. While his role at three consecutive institutions may have been cutting costs, the cost to human beings be damned, Petrofsky could not complete his work without cutting workers. There was one problem: the workers had a union. “You Can’t Eat Prestige” The Harvard Union of Clerical and Technical Workers (HUCTW), the largest labor union on campus, was founded and built through hardwon battles with executives holding the same anti-labor position as the current administration. The union has a memorable slogan: “You can’t eat prestige.” HUCTW represents over 5000 rank-and-file support staffers distributed across the Harvard University system; in every office, laboratory, library, clinic, theatre, museum, and facility their work is evident. The union is the largest labor union on campus and among the oldest in Harvard Yard. Harvard University never wanted to recognize HUCTW. The 1988 NLRB ratification was the culmination of a seventeen-year long struggle. As then-union director Kris Rondeau said to the audience, “their unmistakable goal was to beat the union, and they just failed.” Organizing HUCTW had begun but ultimately failed before reaching a majority of support twice before, both starting in Longwood, but the third attempt in the mid-1980s was successful. A small core organizing committee of Medical Area workers grew the union to win the support of the over-4000 staff members across the Harvard apparatus. The union started independently, without affiliation with the United Auto Workers (UAW) or other internationals. Eventually, HUCTW affiliated with AFSCME Local 3650 in 1987, the year before winning NLRB recognition. Harvard fought with anti-union talking points and booklets strategically distributed to supervisors across the ranks, as well as hostage meetings about why workers should not support unionization, right up till its loss. The democratic vote was not seen as legitimate by Harvard executives. After HUCTW members won the battle for legal representation and the right to collectively bargain in 1988, Harvard still fought against recognition by appealing to the National Labor Relations Board. Harvard tried to get the union election result thrown out by arguing HUCTW coerced workers to join. Harvard saw its case thrown out instead. Harvard did not unconsciously, as a detached force, hire Petrofsky to do the institution’s dirty work. Just like the decision to pay a firm that whistleblowers describe as “morally bankrupt,” people made the decision to hire him. Some are on boards; others are bureaucrats. Hoekstra rose behind Claudine Gay to take over as the Dean of the Faculty of Arts and Sciences when the latter became president of Harvard University in August 2023 – right before Harvard’s hard turn right, the cutting down of Gay, and the ascent of health economist Alan Garber from within the bureaucracy’s top ranks to the presidency in January 2024 by convenient accident . In November 2025, Hoekstra described in a financial update the central need to “reduce our structural deficit through lasting, mission-aligned change.” Her next update followed that winter: the hiring of Warren Petrofsky. On February 27, Hoekstra announced Petrofsky’s appointment by lauding his pivotal work at Cornell University and the University of Pennsylvania, including “helping guide one of the institution’s most consequential operational efforts” at Cornell – an apparent allusion to Petrofsky’s pivotal role in culling that $11 million in Ithaca. “Disciplined financial stewardship,” advised by McKinsey & Company, required finding cuts in the Yard. Sometimes, hired Yes Men say the truths their bosses hide. Answering to Hopi Hoekstra directly, Deming blabbered in one April 2026 open forum that many “back office” jobs doing “things you would never really know or care about, frankly… are the first places I’m looking to try to save money.” The rank-and-file got the message: they were on the chopping block. The institution they built decided they were excess to trim. HUCTW leaders countered in an April 29 email obtained by Working Mass: Would it be invisible to students if their tuition payments were delayed or mishandled because staffing was slashed? Would it be invisible to researchers and students if their labs could not function because materials were not ordered, payments to vendors were not processed, or equipment was not repaired? Would it be invisible to the University if admissions, financial aid, course enrollment, or fundraising—all of which sustain access to education—were disrupted due to large staffing cuts? When contract time arrived, HUCTW leaders negotiated a tentative modest agreement and wrote a strongly worded email to Dean Deming and FAS administrators, as HGSU’s strike continued in the rush towards the end of the semester. Hundreds of union members expressed misgivings with union leaders’ approach. The administration seemed to have no other methods to cost-save except culling worker ranks. Deming spouted vague musings on “new ways to do things” more economically, like renegotiating contracts with outside vendors to alleviate some of the burden of the University’s financial crisis. Executives also showed a knack for effective financial planning when Deming said , with characteristic nuance: We’re looking under the couch cushions for quarters. As hundreds of workers were removed and members of three unions representing other parts of the workforce driven closer to strike action, the Harvard endowment grew by $11 billion between the 2025 (~57 billion) and 2026 (~68 billion) annual reports. The loyalist had turned around her institution’s deficit by casting the workers who built the institution out into the cold. She made a deal with the man who made deals with devils. Her job, whether “mission-aligned” or not, was a success. In a bed afforded by her near ~$700,000 annual salary, Hopi Hoekstra could sleep at night with job safety surrounded by supporters ready to reassure her she did her best. Few others had that luxury. To exfiltrate means “to smuggle [an intelligence agent, soldier, etc.] out of an area under enemy control.” The term belongs to military jargon, rather than to political commentary. Nevertheless, the Brazilian media has been using it to describe the sudden removal of former presidential candidate Flávio Bolsonaro from Brazil’s territory a couple of days after he lost the second round of this year’s presidential elections. José Roberto de Toledo, a veteran journalist, was probably the first to use the word “exfiltration” in relation to Flávio Bolsonaro, in his influential weekly podcast . The circumstances of Bolsonaro’s escape seem to come directly out of a spy film. Smuggled in the middle of the night across the border with the active participation of a former Mossad agent and US government officials, he reappeared in Florida and pledged to take the details of his escape to his grave. An exfiltration is not an act of political sympathy. It is an extremely risky operation to preserve an asset from being captured by the enemy. On the one hand, the exfiltrated asset enjoys safety when he finally reaches his destination; on the other, the exfiltration makes all masks fall, and the fugitive’s true allegiances become transparent. For that reason, if Flávio Bolsonaro was indeed exfiltrated to the United States, this episode should figure side by side with the kidnapping of Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro as a blatant example of US neo-imperialism in Latin America. The political and legal responsibilities for this case cannot be brushed under the carpet of history and will certainly come back to haunt Brazil-US relations for decades to come. A Very Suspicious Escape The details of Flávio Bolsonaro’s escape from Brazil are not entirely known. So far, The New York Times has reported that Jason Miller, a White House operative, arranged the luxury private jet that Bolsonaro and his family used (at the cost of almost one million reais , paid for by an American corporate account). The party crossed the border with Argentina through means that are not yet entirely clear, most likely by land, on the night of Tuesday, October 27, near Foz do Iguaçu. Brazil’s top Sunday night TV news show, Fantástico , revealed that a former Israeli intelligence operative accompanied the fleeing Bolsonaro family. From the Argentinian border location of Puerto Iguazú, the disgraced former presidential candidate took a private jet to the United States, stopping in Ecuador for refueling and avoiding Brazilian airspace. Arriving in the US, Flávio Bolsonaro landed at the infamous Miami–Opa Locka Executive Airport, used six decades earlier to prepare for the US-supported Bay of Pigs invasion of Cuba, one of the worst historical examples of direct US imperial aggression in Latin America. Upon arrival, despite presenting his canceled diplomatic passport, Bolsonaro received “ parole status ” and was escorted by a motorcade to an undisclosed location. Who Takes Responsibility? Donald Trump’s secretary of state, Marco Rubio, was quick to deflect responsibility. “ We didn’t facilitate anything,”claimed Rubio in a press conference on the sidelines of a G20 meeting a few days after the fact. He knew that confessing the exfiltration would mean exposing the State Department to serious internal and international reckoning. Flávio Bolsonaro himself told The New York Times he would take the details of his fleeing “to the grave.” In an open letter released after his arrival in the United States, he compared himself to figures like José Martí and Charles de Gaulle and denounced the election result. He also thanked Argentine President Javier Milei “for his courage, sense of justice, and legality” and expressed his gratitude to Trump “and the wonderful American people” for hosting him. By fleeing Brazilian justice, Flávio Bolsonaro has secured protection, at least for now, from having to answer for his alleged crimes in two different investigations. More important, as long as he is safe in the United States, it becomes more difficult for Brazilian investigators to prove the precise nature of his ties to Washington. He is not the first person to have claimed US protection in suspicious circumstances. His younger brother Eduardo has been agitating from Texas for the Trump administration to impose sanctions against his own country, while the former head of the Brazilian intelligence agency under Jair Bolsonaro is also on US soil, having dodged immigration enforcement under very mysterious circumstances. However, Trump and Rubio cannot shield Flávio Bolsonaro from justice and history indefinitely. It took decades for Washington’s involvement in military coups across Latin America in the 1960s and 1970s to be proven with overwhelming evidence. A comprehensive apology is yet to materialize. Hopefully, we will not need to wait that long this time. A newly elected Democratic-controlled Congress should hold US officials accountable for any interference in the Brazilian democratic process. It should clarify whether US taxpayers’ money was used to facilitate Bolsonaro’s escape from justice and whether he promised his eventual US handlers any specific information or advantages, beyond his public pledges to give the US preferred access to Brazilian rare earths . In due course, Flávio Bolsonaro must be extradited, and cables and intelligence documents pertaining to this case should be declassified, so that the Brazilian and American people can finally learn what really happened on the night of October 27. Claire Wright and Sarah Milner respond to Socialist Majority on the “democratic road to socialism” and DSA’s endorsement process. Signed articles do not necessarily represent the collective views of Reform & Revolution. In August, comrades in Socialist Majority Caucus published an article titled “Why We Endorse: The Case for Nithya Raman,” a supportive piece of DSA-LA’s endorsement of Raman. Raman has become something of a lightning rod for criticism in DSA owing to the relatively large gap between her perceived association with DSA (which is very high) versus her adherence to the principles of DSA and her commitment to DSA’s program (which, even by the standards of elected officials, is quite low, such as her refusal to part with her self-identification as a capitalist). This separation led to Groundwork, a caucus typically aligned with SMC, to oppose Raman’s endorsement in the LA primary. Since the Springs of Resolution resolution in early November to expel and unendorse Nithya Raman ultimately failed, our caucus has drawn severe criticism for voting against it. As such, we have been portrayed as supporters of Raman’s endorsement. In reality, it was our belief that it was naive to try to force LA to overturn their endorsement, through extraordinary measures such as revoking dues share from the chapter and even attacking them through lawsuits in the bourgeois courts. Lost in this, however, has been the vital point — this endorsement was a catastrophic mistake by the chapter. They should have voted Raman down. It is incredibly clear that the debate over Raman isn’t just about Raman, nor just about Los Angeles. It is merely the latest beachhead of a long debate about how DSA should relate to its electeds. Hence, we may use this current debate to reflect on how our strategies and tactics differ within DSA on this question and continue with clarity. SMC proposes three points for understanding endorsements in their article: 1. We endorse candidates to move forward on the democratic road to socialism . 2. DSA’s endorsements are not statements of our values but vehicles for our power. 3. Engaging in mass struggle makes us stronger, abstentionism makes us weaker. At first blush, these positions are a sharp and pointed framework for DSA’s electoral program. These positions also demonstrate the marked difference between SMC’s politics and comrades who desire a higher endorsement standard. In fact, these are framed intentionally to draw a dividing line between themselves and their supporters, framed as reasonable pragmatists, and the DSA left, who they would deride as “ultras”. In the latter two points, SMC have not actually outlined the difference in strategy between caucuses; in fact, with limited nuances, they have described ideas with which almost any caucus could find alignment. They have set up truisms which would be ludicrous to disagree with and then have inaccurately presented these truisms as the dividing lines in DSA. Hence, these final two points are of limited utility for navigating electoral disagreements. There are very few electoral abstentionists left in DSA’s electoral project with any significant influence. The vast majority of members not only support engaging in electoral work and making endorsements, but in fact agree, with varying degrees of subtlety, with the premise that endorsements are as much a tactic as a statement of principles. Consider: There is no candidate among our congressional slate or other prominent elected officials who clearly reflects DSA’s full positions and program. Is any candidate aggressively campaigning on abolition, Jerusalem as the capital of Palestine, and so forth? Has Zohran agitated to abolish the Senate? Assuredly not, yet the leftward cadre of NYC-DSA fought for his election all the same. Beyond that question, consider: most of the DSA “left” voted for a program more radical than “Workers Deserve More”—yet none of us have set that higher bar as the exclusive endorsement threshold. Every caucus endorses candidates they disagree with, and even that the majority of DSA members might disagree with. Almost everyone in DSA knows that candidates with politics worse than the average active DSA leader can still grow the movement. They may not conceptualize these endorsements as tactical but they still, sometimes, support them. “Building the power of the movement” is not actually a certain, objective measure. One can frame this pragmatism with the idea of taking one step back to take two steps forward. Not a worthless perspective by any means. Yet, not every “pragmatic” demand is necessarily the right decision. Take, for example, Zohran’s recent courting of Zionist groups through his Anti-Semitism Task Force. This was billed as a pragmatic decision to reduce opposition to his affordability agenda. A solid logic! Yet, it became clear (and was clear prior) that the Zionist lobby in New York City would hardly be swayed by his overtures. Zohran made one stride back to inch forward, and found himself behind. In electoral debates this can be seen through the “Warren Question”. Would you endorse Elizabeth Warren for senate? Objectively , she is one of the most left wing members of Congress. Objectively , she has played an important role in shifting American politics leftward. Yet, few if any DSA members would openly call for her endorsement. She’s already in office, her winning does little to build the movement, etc. What really separates Warren from Nithya Raman? If LA endorses Nithya to push the needle leftward, why do we not endorse somebody like Warren? Why do we not endorse Abdul El-Sayed or Brad Lander? And so on. Yet just as it becomes clear even the so-called “ultra” wing can compromise, even DSA’s supposedly pragmatic, “against red lines” wing, you will find, has lines. Dig far enough, and you will find that, just as everyone has a point where the gains of a campaign outweigh the problems of a candidate, each and every caucus has a moment where the problems of a candidate outweigh their benefits, where the steps in either direction combine to move too far backwards. Coming to the latter conclusion about any particular race does not an abstentionist make. So what exactly is happening with electoral work? The issue is that we are trying to communicate our beliefs through a tremendous mess of contradictory incentives, interests and messages. Electoral organizing, filtered through the disorienting and chaotic landscape of the capitalist media, with certain points coming through clearly and some very messily, is the terrain we are forced to struggle on. Though many don’t want to admit it, we all agree: There aren’t many candidates who actually share our full DSA politics right now. A better way to navigate this dispute is to drill into the details of what message, exactly, a particular endorsement will convey. In the case of Raman, her failures on Palestine and tepid stance on representing DSA’s program will be the things that cut through the static to the general populace. We did not send the message we wish to through LA’s endorsement. It was an opportunist failure and a mistake. Each caucus would do well to articulate where they draw the line and what factors they consider to be most crucial in determining whether a candidate or campaign’s potential may outpace its political weaknesses or contradictions. A debate on these terms would be much more fruitful. The vast majority of our endorsed candidates are not caucused, cadre members with specific, factionally developed internal positions on how DSA should organize. This leaves all factions, but most often those on the left of DSA, in a reactive, passive role, advancing our politics through critique and response rather than through setting the example. There are exceptions to this to be sure, and the tremendous growth of our organization and blossoming of many 2026 campaigns has begun to turn the tide. But we still have more work ahead of us. Running many caucus members and close allies across DSA’s political spectrum, who can either prove theories in practice, or fail in such a way we are forced to reevaluate, is a critical next step for our political development, and as an extension the socialist project. So fighting abstention as a principle is boxing with shadows. Does that mean the difference between DSA caucuses is merely a matter of degrees, a matter of where a line is drawn? Not exactly. Principles 2 and 3 from SMC create a binary between “Statements & Abstentionism” versus “Action & Mass Struggle,” an absurdity which is intended to muddle the discussion, as no self-respecting socialist outside of the sects which have plagued the American left would find themselves in the latter. That being said — the argument here is not that the differences between DSA’s caucuses on the question of electeds is merely one of tactics. Rather, while we in actuality all exist on the “mass struggle” side of SMC’s binary, that side still has groups with fundamentally different foundations and theories of change. The Democratic Road to Socialism DSA’s new program, “Workers Deserve More,” sets out some conceptions of what we envision as the horizon of our project. It includes lines like “building a party whose goal is a democratic socialism of the working class” and “we know complete victory will require building a new society from the ground up.” These are contentions with which most DSA members would agree. However, when it comes to the specific form that the transformation of society will take, Workers Deserve More leaves the question open. How do we build a new society? What does the transition between this society and that one look like? Within the latter two principles outlined by SMC, there lay more or less two approaches, and their first principle clarifies with which their strategy lies: T he Democratic Road to Socialism. Contrary, perhaps, to its name, the “democratic road to socialism” is not a general Democratic Socialist answer to these questions; rather, it is a specific factional contention which some democratic socialists subscribe to. Rooted in the works of Ralph Miliband and Nicos Poulantzas and developed by many successive generations of socialist organizers, it posits that the contradictions of the capitalist state can be exploited by a surging mass movement of socialists and workers transforming the state from both the inside and the outside, which requires elected officials of the socialist party to take the reins of the bourgeois state. Understanding this framework, one can see where talk of capturing the state and plans for a Project 2029 from publications like Geese, who are aligned with this theory of change, originates. Let us not mince words. We, the authors, are Bolshevists and we are Revolutionists. We do not eschew the electoral realm of struggle, just as those revolutionaries past such as Lenin and Luxemburg did not. Our fundamental demarcation from the “democratic road” as it exists in DSA is twofold. Firstly, we reject what is essentially a modernization of Menshevik two-stage theory. To quote from Groundwork’s Thought Library: “Communists and Socialists must complete the work of reconstruction…and allow a new contradiction (!) between the multiracial bourgeoisie and the multiracial proletariat to become the driving force in the politics of the United States” Herein, the argument is that white supremacy must first be overcome before the socialist transformation of society can be accomplished. To this end, we must shy away from an independent class position to build a broad, “progressive” alliance with liberalism. We reject this entirely. Because racial oppression is the bedrock of capitalism in the U.S., Reconstruction can only be completed through socialist revolution. Our second demarcation, then, is with what is essentially the ultimate blow against the bourgeois order — we know that we cannot use the existing state machinery as a cudgel of emancipation, and as such we do not see the election of socialists to office as a form of “taking state power.” Therefore, what we seek from our electeds is, to quote Lenin, for them to be: …tribune[s] of the people , who is able to react to every manifestation of tyranny and oppression, … who is able to generalize all these manifestations and produce a single picture of police violence and capitalist exploitation; who is able to take advantage of every event, however small, in order to set forth before all [their] socialist convictions and [their] democratic demands, in order to clarify for all and everyone the world-historic significance of the struggle for the emancipation of the proletariat. “But wait!”, the Democratic Roader cries, “could you not make that argument for all of our electeds? Are you not just making a fake bar so you can condemn all of our electeds?” Not so! For a recent and shining example, let us present Darializa Avila Chevalier. She has not so much as entered Congress yet, but following the shooting of a local man by ICE, she was found going door-to-door informing the people of their rights, loudly agitating for our program and to abolish ICE proudly under the flag of the Democratic Socialists, and even taking blows from the police! That is not to say that Darializa is perfect, nor that all of our other electeds are irredeemable opportunists. It is to point out that it is entirely possible for such isolated tribune-like actions to instead become the norm. The standard. However, this sort of action is a rare thing for DSA’s Socialists in Office. Reform & Revolution owes its name chiefly to Luxemburg’s famous pamphlet, but we owe our philosophy of class struggle to Trotsky’s conception of the transitional method. If abstain from day-to-day struggle in the name of revolutionary purity, then we are an unserious and untested clique of propagandists. If we limit our political horizon solely to reform struggles, however, then we reduce ourselves to policy advisers for the ruling class. We fight to build, advance, and link together all the struggles of the workers and oppressed in order to link the felt needs of our class to the need for socialist revolution. The role of a Socialist In Office, like the Militant-led Liverpool City Council in the 1980s, is to fight unambiguously for our movement and for the working class. As we send the biggest slate of socialists to congress in American history this January, it would do us well to ponder on what those demands may be, and how our electeds can champion them. This brings us back to Raman. Considering her record, when those “manifestations of tyranny and oppression” come, will she side decisively with the people? Or will she tidily administer the bourgeois order, as she has said she would? We may debate this point, but let us have no illusions about where the battle lines are drawn. There is only one Global South phrase for the APEC agenda: debt relief

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Vijay Prashad ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-16T21:28:13+00:00

In October, I visited a steel factory in Xinyu, Jiangxi province. There was no dust in the factory. At a desk, a woman wearing an orange hard hat monitored the production of silicon-steel strips. Her attention moved between the machinery and her keyboard. “Keep the belt at the proper speed,” she said. The precision of the process depended on her knowledge as much as on the equipment before her. Here was innovation at work, sustained by the skills of a worker. That scene stayed with me as I considered the forthcoming Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit in Shenzhen. A factory requires more than a machine purchased on the world market, it requires reliable electricity, transportation, technical education, and the accumulated capabilities of an industrial workforce. These foundations take years to build. They depend on sustained investment and on public institutions able to plan beyond the immediate horizon. For countries across the Global South, building this capacity is the substance of development. The leaders gathering in Shenzhen should ask whether the international financial system allows poorer countries to make such investments. How can they train workers and build modern industries when interest payments consume the resources required for schools, energy and infrastructure? Discussions about trade and technology must confront the conditions under which production becomes possible. The promise of shared prosperity will remain unfulfilled unless APEC addresses the destruction produced by war and the suffocation produced by debt. The major countries of the Global South – from Africa, Latin America, and South Asia – are, of course, missing from the APEC participants. But amongst those who will be at the table to represent the Global South are China, Indonesia, Mexico, Papua New Guinea, Peru, and Vietnam. They bring different histories and economic structures to the table, yet they have a shared interest in preserving the capacity of our countries to develop, to strengthen public services, and to cooperate across political differences. Cooperation succeeds when it expands possibilities beyond the region of the Asia-Pacific. The theme of APEC (Building an Asia-Pacific Community to Prosper Together) establishes an important ambition for innovation and cooperation. At Shenzhen, the main agenda ideas will be for paperless trade and building cooperation for digital systems to reduce the cost for business as well as toward a Free Trade Area of the Asia-Pacific. These are worthwhile objectives, particularly when fragmentation of the world economy threatens the productive relationships built across the Pacific. The poorer nations at the table – Indonesia, Papua New Guinea, and the Philippines – will know that talk of digital infrastructure needs public investment, reliable energy, trained workers, and public institutions capable of directing technological change towards social needs. An agenda for connectivity must confront the financial conditions that prevent our societies from connecting on equitable terms. That means insisting on a serious discussion of debt. UN Trade and Development’s latest report , “A World of Debt 2026: Rising Debt Costs and Stalling Development,” describes a financial system increasingly hostile to development. Developing countries’ public debt reached $35 trillion in 2025. Their net interest payments reached $995 billion. In 51 developing countries, home to 3.7 billion people, governments spent more on interest than on either health or education. The injustice becomes clearer when the cost of borrowing is examined. UNCTAD reports average interest rates on public debt of 5.2% for developing countries, compared with 2.2% for developed economies. It estimates that borrowing at comparable rates could save developing countries around $500 billion annually. UNCTAD’s separate 2026 report on external debt sustainability reinforces the warning. Developing countries’ external debt reached $12.3 trillion in 2025, while interest costs on long-term external debt increased by almost 14%. Least developed countries devoted 18.2% of their export earnings to external debt service. Borrowing becomes a treadmill when the income earned through production is continually absorbed by obligations inherited from earlier years. Development plans remain on paper while payments leave the treasury. When interest costs overwhelm public resources because of unequal borrowing terms, the borrowing loses its developmental purposes. Without substantial debt relief for countries whose obligations have become unsustainable, there will be no possibility of the broad advance that the Global South requires. APEC cannot cancel sovereign debt by declaration, but it can raise the issue as a fundamental barrier for its main objectives – such as the importance of a digital landscape. APEC can build political support for action on debt relief and cancellation through the United Nations, the G20, and the development banks (the APEC Finance Ministers’ Process works with the Asian Development Bank, the Inter-American Development Bank, and the World Bank, but it also has project funds to support capacity building and technical cooperation). A credible agenda would begin with timely restructuring and meaningful reductions in unsustainable debt burdens. It would be very useful for APEC to join the debate around debt settlement that helps restore the capacity of poorer countries to develop, with their population’s welfare central to the calculation. In the October 2026, APEC finance ministers’ meeting, they affirmed that financial services should be accessible and affordable, particularly for individuals, households, and small businesses. This commitment to financial inclusion should be extended to the poorer nations, who cannot raise affordable funding for development. The APEC digital infrastructure statement from that meeting talked about the need for multilateral development banks to “ensure that scarce concessional and public resources catalyze, rather than replace, private investment.” But this is not enough. We need to be in discussion about debt settlement and about new finance that is not unfairly expensive. There needs to be an APEC-wide entitlement to concessional development loans or a debt-cancellation mechanism (at least one APEC state, Papua New Guinea is at high risk of debt distress, while two of them, Indonesia and the Philippines, sustain high debt by domestic borrowing with high rates of interest payments as part of their government finances). China can use its convening role to connect these issues. Shenzhen’s productive achievements provide an appropriate setting to discuss the foundations of development: infrastructure, education, industrial capacity, and sustained investment. The most valuable contribution of the summit would be to widen access to those foundations. Cooperation on technology should strengthen productive capabilities across developing economies, supported by financing arrangements that allow them to retain the gains of their own labor. The summit should be judged by whether its commitments enlarge the space for development. A declaration on innovation will mean little to a government forced to cut its education budget. A promise of connectivity will remain distant where interest payments absorb investment funds. The Global South should arrive in Shenzhen with a clear proposition: prosperity requires the resources to build it. That is an agenda worthy of the Pacific and its peoples.
