Socialism Deferred: The Communist Party of Italy and the Historic Compromise of 1976

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 / 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Socialist Forum ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-21T22:08:37+00:00

American politics is at a crossroads. All that was once considered certain or possible is now in question. For the first time since the early 20th century, American Socialists are gaining considerable municipal and state level influence through successful and highly publicized electoral campaigns. After a largely successful midterm showing, the DSA established itself as a small but potent force in American politics. For the first time there is a comparatively large cohort of elected DSA members holding local, state, and federal office. Along with this heightened political influence comes greater political risk. Questions of electoral positioning and maneuver take on greater weight. Examining the past experience of socialist and non-socialist alliances can be instructive. Italy provides a pertinent example of a large socialist party, the Italian Communist Party (PCI), that entered an election in 1976 that seemed to have it poised on the precipice of entering government for the first time since the onset of the cold war. This did not occur. Instead, it offers an example of the critical importance of electoral strategy, social research, and ideological flexibility. Despite a major electoral breakthrough with a historic 34% of the vote in 1976, the Italian Communist Party (PCI) was unable to translate this into a wider challenge to the institutional and economic order in Italy. The Communist Party benefitted from the leadership of Chairman Enrico Berlinguer, widely recognized as a particularly charismatic and talented politician. He and his associates pursued a strategic alliance with the Christian Democrats known as the Historic Compromise to bolster future electoral efforts. In reality the compromise undermined the long-term viability of the PCI project. Its failure stems from a fundamental misunderstanding of the mandate delivered by voters as well as a failure to anticipate the political realities of being a subordinate non-governing party to the Christian Democrats. At no point did the DC share power with the PCI. The party agreed initially to a non-sfiducia regime, meaning it promised not to call for a vote of no confidence. Ultimately, the PCI endorsed the government under long-serving and known conservative Christian Democratic Prime Minister Giuliio Andreotti in 1978 even though the party was denied any ministerial positions. Transitioning from the no-confidence regime to the “National Solidarity Government” the PCI still did not receive a meaningful stake in national power. Forced to defend unpopular measures, it ultimately entered the next election of 1979 in a worse position, presaging a process of decline throughout the 1980s and ultimate dissolution in 1991. the cold war dilemma of the pci The Italian Communist Party in 1976 was a mass based electoral Socialist Party confronted by the harsh political realities of the Cold War. It was viewed by many in the center and on the right as an agent of Moscow, functioning to subvert the democratic order. Simultaneously, to many Italian workers, peasants, and increasingly large shares of the petty bourgeoisie as well as students, the Italian Communist Party was a vehicle to advance democratic struggles that ranged from the armed resistance to Fascism to the right to divorce. Berlinguer and the leadership of the PCI correctly viewed their reliance on the Soviet Union as an obstacle to the democratic transfer of power. They formulated a new political line to demonstrate that the Party was a responsible democratic institution while also remaining faithful to the class struggle. Having its foundation in longserving leader of the Party and prominent Communist International Official, Palmiro Togliatti’s, recognition of Parliament as a legitimate vehicle for class struggle and his belief that there were multiple national roads to socialism, the line adopted by the PCI came to be known as “Eurocommunism” (a view adopted in different ways by the French, Spanish, and Italian Communist parties). Eurocommunism revolved around the idea that it was possible to utilize the parliament of bourgeois republics to peacefully transition from capitalism to socialism. This perspective tied all three parties (though the French would eventually break with Eurocommunism) to electoralism. This raises a basic problem: how does one retain the revolutionary goals and impetus of Socialism while supporting a bourgeois electoral process? The ideological basis of the Historic Compromise was established by Berlinguer in a series of articles released in the main party newspaper and official organ, Rinascita. Berlinguer was starting from a position of perennial opposition to not only the DC, but the entire spectrum of “bourgeois ideology”. To accomplish the ideological shift, he separated, in line with Italian historic experience, anti-Imperialism from absolute anticapitalism: “It is the duty of communists and all who fight for the cause of democratic progress and of the liberation of the people to raise the level of consciousness regarding these truths, in order to direct vigilant attention to all of the threats that imperialism and the ruling bourgeois class poses to the freedom of people and independence of nations.” (1) This opened the line of reasoning that would allow a Party militant or longtime supporter to consider that the cause of democratic progress writ large may constitute sufficient ideological similarity to the Communist commitment to anti-Fascism, thus to allow for the Communists to stand in alliance. This was not sufficient, however. To confront decades of inherited bias against accommodation with the DC, Berlinguer sought to employ one of the most potent symbols of the PCI, the Resistenza. Referencing the Salerno Turn when the Kingdom of Italy joined the Allies in World War Two through an agreement of all of the Anti-Fascist forces in the country, he wrote, “The more profound sense of the turn (at Salerno) was the necessity and the desire of the Communist Party to come to terms with all of Italian history, and with all of the historic forces (Socialists, Catholics, and others with democratic inspirations) who were present on the national scene and who fought alongside us for democracy, for national independence, and unity.” (2) By invoking the broad anti-fascist front of wartime, he was suggesting it might be reconstituted in the present. Berlinguer concluded, “If it is true that a democratic renewal can only be realized if it is supported by vast majority of the population, there is not only a need for a policy of large social alliances but also a determined system of political agreements that fosters a convergence and collaboration between all of democratic and popular forces until there is realized a political alliance among them.” (3) This justified the fundamental political logic of the historic compromise, that it was acceptable for the PCI to explore uniting with non-Communist forces to oppose Imperialism, that there were historic forces in the country (Socialism, Catholics, others) who could be united with today because of their role in the historic anti-fascist struggle, and that a revolutionary democratic program can only be pursued through a system of large social and political alliances. What Berlinguer did not anticipate was that the convergence he spoke of would lead voters toward the DC, not the PCI. the irony of the historic compromise Not only was the PCI suddenly forced to defend, in the name of the Historic Compromise, unpopular government policies such as the partial elimination of the scala mobile, rising costs of living due to inflationary monetary policy, and a partial dismantling of the gains earned by the unions during the previous round of labor militancy; it also faced internal dissatisfaction. There was widespread confusion among the Party: “From the start, much of the PCI membership was surprised and confused by the historic compromise; many were jolted when the leadership proposed an alliance with a political force that had been seen as the enemy for so long.” (4) According to Lucio Magri, author of the Tailor of Ulm and one of the founders of Manifesto, a leftwing journal that split from and ultimately rejoined the PCI, “If anything, younger and more combative sections of the population, less accustomed to discipline and delegation, wanted to participate in decision-making and to see the results sooner rather than later.” (5) This internal confusion was aggravated following the election because, “The PCI constituencies expected the party to use its leverage to push through far-reaching reforms. Instead the leadership devoted all its efforts to working out a formal agreement with the DC, backing down on major issues to keep negotiations going…the DC passed a series of measures that served its own interests…raising prices for the basic consumer goods, public transportation, and utilities; and dismantling the cost of living escalator mechanism (scala mobile), which protected the organized working class against inflation.” (6) Here were the perils of being a junior coalition partner. The PCI was not in government but because of the Historic Compromise was forced to defend policies that undermined its own position among its constituents in the name of a broader goal: namely, to demonstrate its “fitness” to govern. Even those elements within the Party that supported the Historic Compromise, according to Magri, were expecting reform but received only defence of the status quo, “Even those convinced of the rightness of Berlinguer’s project not only hoped for but willed a profound change in economic and social policy, and in Italy’s international position, mode of governance and distribution of power. A compromise maybe – but not subaltern cohabitation.” (7) the road not taken Was this the only path available to Berlinguer? Some both on the left and among disinterested historians saw another potential avenue that could have been pursued but was not explored; a Left Unity Coalition between the PCI and the Italian Socialist Party (PSI) – and smaller Left parties. An effective electoral coalition depended both on ideological unity and a program focused on the desires of the PCI base; namely, defense of the scala mobile and living standards of the popular classes, and an end to the corruption identified as a by-product of DC rule. Both the PCI and the official newspaper of the Italian Socialist Party, Avanti, regularly called out the corruption of the DC. The prospective coalition would also have entailed a defense of NATO, but with a varied foreign policy that at times would have been at odds with the NATO’a wider positions, for example, leaning on traditionally good relations between Italy and the Arab Republics. (8) Historian Paul Ginsborg notes that “…the country was crying out for change and reform. It would not be possible to achieve either by making an alliance with the DC. Berlinguer, it is argued, should have capitalized upon the enduring movement of protest in civil society in order to forge an alternative to Christian Democrat politics entirely.” (9) The PCI could not have taken power immediately, but it could have used the years after 1976 to build a united opposition, and to prepare the terrain for a decisive victory at the next elections.” (10) This suggests a qualitatively different view of the potential for the democratic transition in a bourgeois republic. While it was not realistic for a Left Coalition to assume power in 1976, the process of unity coalition building could have served as a vehicle for the consolidation of the Left into a more viable alternative pole in Italian politics. Nor did the PSI represent the only available alliance partner. There was as well the New Left which existed in various forms, ultimately to be joined by the explosive mixture of new social actors and popular demands that emerged on the scene with the Movement of ‘77. the italian new left To the Left of the PCI was a nebulous group organized around a handful of prominent thinkers such as Mario Tronti, Antonio Negri, and Silvia Federici. While their positions can be considered generally Left, they varied significantly on who or what constituted the proletarian class or revolutionary subject. Loosely, they agreed upon a broad conception of politics that placed the organization of the working class as something to be pursued outside of the boundaries of established organizations such as existing trade unions and the Communist or Socialist Parties. For this reason they adopted “Autonomia” (Autonomy) as the label for a body of wide ranging Marxist thought. Alongside this creative application of Marxism were numerous other ideological projects that embraced a variety of ideologies and identities; together they occupied a nebulous space best defined as the “New Left”. While there were numerous periodicals and journals such as Potere Operaio, Primo Maggio, Quaderni Rossi, etc. that all expressed different currents of thought to the left of the PCI, we can view the New Left coalition party, Proletarian Democracy (DP) as the primary organization in question, at least with regards to electoral matters. In the 1976 election the party received approximately 550,000 votes which earned it 6 Members of Parliament. (11) While not decisive, they represented a viable left-wing ally that when taken together with the Socialists at 9% would have allowed for the emergence of a Left Coalition in Parliament that the DC would have had to accommodate in some fashion, even as broader ideological unity was explored. The PCI’s refusal to engage with the New Left could be understood as a combination of old sectarianism and a fundamental misunderstanding or inability to recognize the new insurgent populations that defined the New Left. It is no coincidence that only several months later, after the elections, the Movement of ‘77 exploded onto the scene of Italian politics, expressing many different tendencies and modes of thought, but overall frustration with the existing political status quo, including the PCI. Magri summarizes the composition of the Movement of ‘77: “It was mainly a protest movement in a period of general downturn, mostly driven by anger and disappointment, and kindling huge hopes for a new world…its social base was new and heterogeneous: various marginal groups (unemployed youth, rank and file union activists in the railway and health sectors, jobless graduates), clustered together, or tried to cluster, around the students, whereas industrial workers were neither much in evidence nor the class centrally at issue in the protests.” (12) Clearly, this heterogeneous movement was far from being composed of the PCI’s traditional base, that is the industrial working class. But it represented exactly the constituency for a broader Left alliance against the Center and Right. It could be seen then that the failure of the PCI to extend a meaningful hand to the New Left for the Election of 1976 was a profound error. The Party’s aversion to ideological compromise, its unwillingness to cede ideological and organizational control of the coalition, was fatal. Of course, it also reflected the Party’s opportunism in seeking to secure a grand alliance with the DC. the politics of the possible Was the Left Unity Coalition realistic? The experience of the prodigal Manifesto group which had split from the PCI in the 1960s to form an independent Marxist journal and returned by the late 1970s and the Proletarian Unity Party for Communism which experienced a similar trajectory, both demonstrate that small ideologically left dissident factions were able to dissolve and return to the PCI proper. This suggests a degree of underlying compatibility. Ironically the PCI by refusing to pursue a meaningful alliance with the wider forces of the left contradicted Togliatti’s own thoughts regarding parliament, “In the second place, it is necessary for the parties that fight for socialism, and the communist parties in the front rank of them, to be strong, numerous, well organised and for them to know how to work and fight in such a way as to win a decisive influence in the decisive strata of the working class and the people. In the third place, it is necessary for these parties to be united in their action, because this not only increases their numerical weight…but can allow them to establish a reciprocal understanding and a collaboration with those political forces that are not hostile to social reforms of a socialist type and to a consequent policy of peace.” (13) Furthermore, had the PCI been basing their perception of the role of parliament in 1976 on the broad call for democratic unity advanced by Togliatti – following the logic of the Historic Compromise itself – the unity of the PCI, PSI, DP, and Italian Social Democratic Party (PSDI) could have been pursued under the wide umbrella of anti-imperialism and in defense of democratic values. There also existed a degree of popular support for this position, reported in Avanti.. A press conference of neighborhood leaders appearing in the Party’s official organ indicated that there also was a degree of PSI support for a broader understanding of the left. (14) The Socialist leadership around De Martino could have been approached by the PCI to draft a new unity program to create an alternative center-left coalition. Martino himself advanced a lukewarm but open position at the PSI party congress of 1976, stating in his address to the Party that it was critical that the PSI consider a left-wing alternative politics and observed that some elements within the Party were insisting that the PSI declare they would not join any government without the PCI. (15) While retaining a desire for autonomy and flexibility going into the election, De Martino recognized that the primary strategic goal of the PSI was, “to build a leftwing alliance and alternative.” The failure of the PCI then is not that it rejected an immediate electoral alternative based on alliance with the PSI, but that it did not, after the election, devote energy to constructing a long-term autonomous left alliance that could in time replace the DC. The difficulties in synthesizing a Left Unity Coalition among the PCI, PSI, New Left, and Others were significant. They represented notable differences in viewpoints regarding many issues. However, given the growing popular dissatisfaction with the DC, historic inter-party relations particularly between the PCI and PSI was not impossible. Ginsborg highlights that, while difficult, the decision to not pursue a Left-Unity Coalition would have fateful consequences as, “In the summer of 1976 the opportunity, albeit fraught with difficulty, existed for building an alternative to the Christian Democrats. The opportunity was not taken, and no such chance was to present itself again. the italian lesson What does the Italian General Election of 1976 teach us about the potential rise of Socialist Governance in the United States in the wake of the 2026 Midterm Elections? The 2026 Midterms are not the Italian General Election in 1976. Unlike the PCI which was a well organized, disciplined, national political force, the DSA is still a pre-party formation employing primarily democratic electoral infrastructure to contest specific races. While the PCI could rely upon professional cadre to coordinate national efforts, the DSA is composed primarily of volunteers. Like the General Elections in 1976 Italy, the DSA has made a tremendous breakthrough, relatively speaking, with six candidates being elected to federal office and numerous downballot or endorsed victories. (16) Despite this success, the DSA remains fundamentally subordinate to the Democratic Party. Because of the need to oppose the Republican Party and the still relatively marginal position of the Socialist Movement in the USA, the DSA like the PCI, is compelled to support policy packages pushed by the Democrats that are fundamentally antithetical to a Socialist vision for the United States. But the DSA may suffer should their bourgeois partners, who constitute the actual power in government, pursue policies that undermine the DSA. To combat the effect of being tied to a larger bourgeois force, the DSA must expand its political imagination which the PCI refused to do. It is imperative that socialists seize the mandate offered by voters and propose meaningful reforms to meet immediate material expectations. Simultaneously, it is possible to begin an ideological process of Left Coalition Building which may mean that while a still marginal force in 2026, the DSA enters 2028 and beyond as an independent political party capable of advancing the socialist project and vision that motivates its membership. To speak of coalition building is also to highlight the stark difference between the possibilities open to the PCI that do not exist in the same measure for DSA. Unfortunately, there are no comparably sized overtly Socialist parties that the DSA can join hands with to create the beginning of a coalition. That said, there are options the DSA can cultivate. Third parties such as the Greens, Working Families Party, and others who are not explicitly Socialist could unite with the DSA in a Popular Democratic Coalition; or at least on specific initiatives like the Green New Deal or Cost of Living, that might prepare the ground for later and broader convergence. Similarly there is the possibility of establishing an explicitly political alliance with progressive and independent elements of organized labor. This would be distinct from the broad imperative of supporting the unions as vehicles for securing tangible gains for working people and would represent an effort to create a specific political project transitioning from solely economic initiatives towards broader related challenges. Progressive Democrats are seemingly a potential coalition partner but represent an acute danger for the DSA. They are exactly the mechanism by which the DSA can be maneuvered into supporting candidates and policies that undermine the position of the DSA which could include continued support for Zionism, continued “legal” deportations, or continued funding for foreign wars. That said, Progressive Democrats could be persuaded to provide indirect material support for the DSA by channeling funds and resources to DSA initiatives. This does not amount to joining a formal Popular Democratic Coalition, but nonetheless could help. The common thread running through all of these options is the necessity for the Popular Democratic Coalition to have a specific end goal in mind. Unity for the sake of unity was not viable nor beneficial for the PCI nor would it be viable for the DSA. David Dinkins Wasn’t Loud Enough for New York City

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: David Duhalde ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-21T18:54:08+00:00

In the past few weeks, New York City mayor Zohran Mamdani has proposed breaking up the Strategic Response Group and closing Rikers by 2032 , the former established by progressive Mayor Bill de Blasio, who also revived a plan to dismantle the latter by 2027. But unfulfilled promises of police and carceral reform efforts go back to the 1990s. How much should the failures of previous mayoral administrations weigh on the decisions of Mamdani and his allies in regard to public safety and police? This question has no easy answer. But a new book by James Barney on the tenure of New York City’s first black mayor, David Dinkins, offers insights on his efforts to rein in police excesses based on new research, especially in the local press of the era — and critically how those attempts failed, despite Mayor Dinkins’s desires and promises. In A Fragile Alliance: David Dinkins, Coalition Politics, and the Struggle to Govern a City in Crisis , Barney, while sympathetic to Dinkins, argues that both Dinkins’s quiet leadership style and inability to create a narrative uniting his own multiracial coalition, much less the majority of the city, proved to be his undoing, dooming his reelection prospects and much of his agenda. Barney, critical of identity politics throughout his history book, sums up his reservations here: . . . in particular, Dinkins’s reliance on the mosaic metaphor betrayed a worldview that privileged difference. While Dinkins repeatedly noted that New York City was a “gorgeous mosaic,” in a “mosaic” each of the pieces is different. They only produce any meaning by being part of a larger piece of art. But this type of thinking was at the heart of Dinkins’s problems as a leader because it emphasized differences rather than commonalities. Barney’s reservations about celebrating difference are at times too determinative. For instance, I doubt Dinkins could have done much to stop the disintegration of the New Deal/Great Society coalition that voted Democratic as white ethnics left the party across the country. But Barney’s concerns about the communication failures of Dinkins’s tenure were real and went beyond narrative to style. While noble at times, the mayor’s quiet leadership style prevented New Yorkers — especially in a time without social media to get news rapidly and easily outside major media like Mamdani enjoys and uses today — from seeing their mayor’s interventions that created more peace than was seen elsewhere in America at the time, for example Los Angeles, which experienced a much deadlier upheaval triggered by the lack of convictions for the police who senselessly beat Rodney King, as well as the killing of teenager Latasha Harlins by a Korean American storeowner. This failure provided an opening to power for demagogues such as Rudy Giuliani to paint a darker picture around crime and disorder than actually existed. While New York City’s political landscape has changed in many ways — the Staten Island secession movement, for example, is almost forgotten today — one thing Barney covers remains very much the same. The New York Police Department (NYPD) and institutions around it enjoy strong independence from civilian control that makes it difficult for any mayor to shape policy and personnel decisions commensurate with other major city agencies. A famous instance of this was when the Sergeants Benevolent Association (SBA) tweeted out personal data of then-Mayor de Blasio’s daughter’s arrest related to the George Floyd uprising. Former SBA head Ed Mullins eventually faced disciplinary charges over this, though by then it was overshadowed by his federal indictment and conviction of stealing from his members. It is difficult to think of any other agency and its unions who would feel so emboldened to reject democratic oversight. Dinkins, unlike most of today’s socialists and progressives, ran on a tough-on-crime agenda. He promised to hire thousands more police officers. The issue of crime polled high across racial and gender categories of voters. But Dinkins targeted his law-and-order proposals to include increasing policing in communities of color with officers of similar backgrounds. A major critic of this policy was Giuliani, who claimed this public safety policy would take police away from white neighborhoods. Giuliani’s racialized law-and-order rhetoric helped sink Dinkins. Progressive mayors following Dinkins, such as de Blasio and Mamdani, have reckoned with the political reality that a public safety reform agenda is territory that isn’t worth attempting battle upon. And losing puts their whole left-wing agenda, not just public safety, at risk. Case in point: after Dinkins’s defeat, New York saw two decades of fiscal conservative and anti-police-reform mayors. Crown Heights as a Turning Point Two chapters of the book that expand on how such demagoguery on policing helped cost Dinkins his mayoralty focus on the Crown Heights riots and Patrolmen’s Benevolent Association (PBA) riots, respectively. These chapters also go beyond those two episodes of public violence to discuss how other instances of excessive police force neither led to uprisings nor successful efforts to advance civilian control of law enforcement. Barney’s research uses these events to help us understand how perceived disorder would be viewed as a key reason Dinkins lost, even though the crime rates were falling, and how his relationship with the police leadership and rank and file did not recover despite his own law-and-order narrative. Dinkins won a surprise primary victory against three-term incumbent and bombastic erstwhile liberal Ed Koch in 1989. Barney credits Dinkins’s win through efforts to build a black-led, multiracial coalition out of the efforts in and around Jesse Jackson’s 1988 presidential campaign and subsequent Rainbow Coalition. Voter registration and mobilization, especially of black voters, to back Jackson were then used to elect Dinkins. Those new voters proved fruitful for Dinkins’s own Rainbow Coalition. Dinkins won over half the vote in the multicandidate Democratic primary against Koch. But his candidacy against the Republican in the general was unusually closer. In New York, the Democratic Party primary more often than not is the de facto election. Not for Dinkins. He faced Giuliani, famous at the time for his federal prosecution of the mafia in New York’s Southern District, in 1989 and 1993. Each time, the victor only bested his opponent by around 2 points. Not only were the margins similar, but Barney argues that themes in each election centered around crime, racial tension, and divisive ethnic politics. What made the difference was how a slim number of voters (turnout was roughly the same, 1.9 million in both mayoral races) eventually shifted toward a harsher response to perceptions of disorder, even as crime rates were declining. Dinkins lost ground partly because of both the Crown Heights riot and PBA riot. Each contributed to the public perception of an ineffective Dinkins, which undermined his promises to create a safer city and less brutal policing methods. The Crown Heights riot story officially begins on August 19, 1991, when a vehicle in a motorcade following the chief Lubavitcher (a sect of Jewish Hasidim) rabbi hit two Guyanese American children. The immediate lack of medical attention for the children by emergency medial technicians, explained by the Jewish volunteer service that initially responded to the accident lacking professional skills needed to treat the young people’s injuries, led to a retaliatory stabbing of a rabbinical student days later. The following unrest, in which 150 people were arrested and one hundred police injured, led the media to publicly question Dinkins’s ability to heal the racial and ethnic divide in the city and portray him as a weak leader. Footage of the 1991 Crown Heights riot in Brooklyn, New York. (Wall Street Journal Opinion / YouTube) But the conflict in Crown Heights went further back than these two incidents. The riot itself reflected the tension between the religious Jewish enclave and their black neighbors, largely of West Indian descent. In a city where fights over resources can feel zero-sum, the well-organized and self-isolating Lubavitcher community appeared to gain at others’ expense. Their leadership, politically well-connected as a result of their well-organized followers voting as a bloc, primarily looked after their own. Barney notes, for example, that the police protection for the Lubavitcher headquarters was seen as taking officers away from the crack epidemic, fostering further estrangement from their black gentile neighbors. Dinkins, viewing this as a battle of “good and evil,” according to Barney, responded to the restive state of Crown Heights by sending in a thousand police officers to quell the violence. Black civil rights leaders and organizers such as the Reverend Al Sharpton and Sonny Carson, at times previously allied with the mayor, turned on him. Sharpton even characterized Dinkins’s action as setting up an “apartheid state.” But a second, seemingly contradictory viewpoint sprung up from Giuliani and his allies that Dinkins actually held the police back from acting. This narrative fit well with voters that Barney says wanted to lump Dinkins, Carson, and Sharpton together. Dinkins couldn’t win. To make things worse, Dinkins publicly stated — repeating doctors’ misdiagnosis — that the stabbed rabbinical student would recover. But his wounds were fatal. The Jewish man died a few hours after Dinkins’s remarks. The death made Dinkins look, even if unfairly, incompetent. Barney credits much of the success the mayor did achieve in facilitating conservations between different Crown Heights faith leaders. These dialogues between Christian and Jewish leaders were rarely reported in the press. To Barney, this was just another example of Dinkins’s “quiet leadership” that, while noble, undermined public perception of governance. Ultimately, a 1993 internal city investigation called the Girgenti Report vindicated Dinkins from the viewpoint that he had not, in fact, held back the police nor abetted the prosecution of the rabbinical student’s killing. But the report’s timing was poor for the incumbent. Barney concludes it did more to remind voters in an election year of the chaos of two years earlier than absolving Dinkins in their eyes: “For a mayor who longed to unify, Crown Heights became the moment when broad segments of the public lost confidence in Dinkins and when his vision of a gorgeous mosaic, while laudable, was found insufficient to meet New York City’s realities.” The narrative that Dinkins held the NYPD back in Crown Heights was one of many that undermined his relationship with the police department and its officers. Another was that the overwhelming white police force felt the mayor was giving preferential treatment to people of color and criminals. The Police Riot as a Point of No Return In 1992, a police officer shot a Dominican immigrant in Washington Heights, which sparked a series of events that led to a police riot by September. The Washington Heights shooting seemed to be heading toward similar unrest — this time, sparked by young people who felt overpoliced. Dinkins was able to de-escalate the situation well, such that even his critics like Cardinal John O’Connor gave the mayor credit for his handling of the shooting’s aftermath. The mayor’s initial success and efforts included allies he recruited. Dinkins had an ally in Lee P. Brown, an African American criminologist and former police chief of Houston, Texas, that took the same role under Dinkins. As commissioner, Brown supported community policing, which brought more officers who looked like the people of the neighborhoods they patrolled. Again, this policy was not favored by some white people who felt it took resources from their residential areas. But this balance between reform and political reality in policing became unstable for both Dinkins’s coalition and the NYPD brass. At the time, the police practiced stop and frisk, an invasive and violating search practice that nearly exclusively targeted young men of color. Dinkins would come to regret this policy. Beyond the ethics, stop and frisk also fostered a schism in his coalition between the young people who felt brutalized and the older residents who wanted more police. Roscoe C. Brown Jr, a Tuskegee airman veteran quoted by Barney, summed up this division in the simple fact that black people had been victims of both crime and racist policing. Solving one requires tackling the other. But doing so for Dinkins became impossible. Brown resigned as commissioner in 1992, because, according to Barney, even this reformer did not want to go as far as Dinkins in empowering the police watchdog city agency, the Civil Complaint Review Board. Dinkins, unlike Brown, determined the NYPD was unable to hold itself accountable. Still, Brown’s departure was from the worst rebuke Dinkins would experience by the NYPD. A banner seen at the 1992 Patrolemen’s Benevolent Association Riot reads, “Dinkins, you sold out the NYPD.” (NY1 / YouTube) On September 16, 1992, organized by the police union (PBA), nearly ten thousand off-duty officers rallied outside city hall. Fueled by alcohol and rhetoric from Giuliani and PBA union leaders saying Dinkins favored criminals over cops, some police wore anti-Dinkins shirts, and others overturned barricades. While the media and some allies like Sharpton returned to Dinkins’s camp after this because of the racist slurs hurled at Dinkins and noxious nature of the police riot, the damage was done with enough of the public. The perception that Dinkins had lost the police overrode the actual declining crime rates with a sufficient number of voters to eventually swing the election toward Giuliani the next year. This would end a progressive holding the mayor’s office until 2014 with the election of Bill de Blasio. NYPD Reform Returns Today not only is the mayor a progressive but, like Dinkins, a member of the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA). Zohran Mamdani also shares the challenges of balancing the wishes of his coalition and campaign promises with those of political reality. Policing is no different. Recently, Mamdani has pledged to disband the Special Response Group (SRG), a controversial police unit with both counterterrorism and protest control duties, and also backed socialist councilmember Chi Ossé’s CURB Act, which would limit tactics employed by SRG. As the SRG’s responsibilities will be reassigned to other police units, a move reportedly supported by NYPD Commissioner Jessica Tisch, limiting existing practices that police reform advocates say they are most against is critical to the success of reform. Unlike Commissioner Lee with Dinkins, Tisch was not the incumbent mayor’s appointment but a holdover from the previous mayor. Mamdani has resisted calls from parts of his coalition to fire her; these allies identify her support of Zionism and a strong carceral state as incompatible with a socialist vision. Except for not hiring nearly six hundred more police officers after pushback from NYC-DSA and others, the recent moves against SRG nearly a year into his term are a first real push to tackle unaccountable and abusive police power. While New York City crime stands at record lows , the experience of Dinkins should serve as a warning that statistical realities such as declining rates are sometimes no match for scare tactics. The police still have soft and unchecked power, with council allies allegedly quietly opposing the CURB Act. Hidden opposition can become public, and allies can return. But sometimes it is too late, as Dinkins experienced after the police riot. As the title of Barney’s book reminds us, holding power can be fragile. It will take a balance of principle and strategic planning to successfully rein in the police. Or else we will set back again. A lesson the current mayor can also learn from Dinkins is in a communication style to avoid. Instead, he should continue to present an accessible vision and visible actions so voters and other members of the public can easily understand and repeat his policy priorities. Had Dinkins been able to enjoy the social media platforms and fluency that Mamdani does to get around mainstream gatekeepers and parochial local press, he might have been able to overcome the idea he was ineffective. But even with such tools, it requires a willingness to be public, even boastful, which people adverse to demagoguery understandably avoid. But good intentions can be fatal. Quiet leadership, while noble, may be silent even to your supporters. And it can be used against you by your enemies. The base needs to know what you’re doing to counter the inevitable lies and distortions thrown at left-wing politicians by their reactionary political opponents. The success of a progressive and socialist public safety agenda depends on winning the majority. That requires a tremendous amount of organizing from the grassroots, but also political leaders who can help echo a narrative that can cut through the noise trying to maintain the status quo or reserve the gains made by reformers so far. A Fragile Alliance is worth reading for anyone trying to comprehend how the past can weigh on today’s New York. It is a valuable resource in seeing how old attack frames, especially around identity, which seem outdated but once worked against Dinkins, could return against Mamdani. But more critically, how previous efforts of mayors around police reform failed and succeeded. Learning from that history can better prepare activists to push those in power, even our allied electeds, in a more strategic direction. Inflation Is About Resources — and Power

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Oskar Brandt ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-21T16:31:32+00:00

After prices began rising and rising in the wake of COVID-19 and Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, some blamed welfare and public spending for the inflation. Yet in many economies, weak demand and falling public investment after 2008 held back investment and productive capacity. Austerity can also weaken productivity and future growth. In the United States, changes to trade policy during Donald Trump’s first presidency added to uncertainty about the economic situation. Trade policy uncertainty between 2017 and 2018 may have lowered investment by 1 to 2 percent. During the pandemic, automakers canceled orders and halted production. Demand returned before factories, suppliers, and transport networks could restart. The Cleveland Fed documented how factory shutdowns, parts shortages, and shipping delays during the pandemic disrupted supply chains. The oil crisis of 1973 is a reminder of how a real supply shock can also become a lasting case for austerity. Joe Biden’s American Rescue Plan expanded the child tax credit. Child poverty fell from 9.7 percent in 2020 to 5.2 percent in 2021, then rose to 12.4 percent in 2022 after Congress let the expansion expire. From 2019 to 2023, low-wage workers’ real pay grew by 13.2 percent. Real manufacturing-construction spending rose by 85 percent from January 2021 to a six-decade high in part as a result of new investment laws. These gains helped poor families, workers, and industry, though they cannot all be attributed to Biden’s policies. US inflation also fell earlier than in the eurozone. Consumer price index (CPI) inflation peaked at 9.1 percent in June 2022 and fell to 3 percent by June 2023, while eurozone inflation was still 5.5 percent . Global supply pressures eased, energy prices fell, and the Federal Reserve raised rates sharply. Still, stronger wages and public investment can coincide with falling inflation. The gains for poor families, however, can disappear when that policy ends. Sweden brings the question into focus in 2026. In its March 2026 forecast , the Swedish Agency for Financial and Public Management projected a general government deficit of SEK180 billion for the year. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) describes the fiscal policy as expansionary but expects headline inflation to remain below target. Inflation can rise when spending meets shortages of labor or natural resources, limited production, or firms with the power to raise prices. But spending and higher purchasing power do not cause inflation by themselves. The result depends on resources, production, prices, and import dependence. The deficit forecast and the IMF’s outlook show why. Production Is Power Imagine a low-paid worker with rent due and a child to feed two weeks until payday. A pay raise may help, but it cannot create affordable homes or medicine. When the costs of essentials rise faster than incomes, people with little room in their budgets absorb the shock first. Inflation policy decides who loses purchasing power, whose costs get protected, and whose prices remain beyond public control. A government that issues its own floating currency creates money as it spends and withdraws money from the private economy through taxes. But it still faces real limits: labor, technology, materials, energy, and ecological capacity. Reliance on imports, foreign-currency debt, and exchange rate pressure can tighten those limits. If spending exceeds what producers can supply, prices may rise. A deficit alone cannot tell us where that point lies. An economy with available workers and room to raise productivity can absorb spending without necessarily generating inflation. Banks create money when they lend . Each loan creates a matching deposit; as the loan is repaid, that deposit money disappears. But money cannot summon nurses, electricity, medicines, homes, or skilled workers. Private credit can sustain demand when wages and public spending lag, but households and firms cannot increase debt indefinitely. When borrowers repay loans more quickly than banks issue new ones, this bank-created money contracts. Austerity can exacerbate this fall in demand. If households and businesses try to pay down debt at the same time, a financial crisis can ensue. Globalization gave rich countries access to abundant goods and helped keep some prices down, but it tied factories to long shipping routes and distant suppliers. Military power can protect routes, resources, and property rights, but it also depends on domestic industrial capacity and state support for its industries. Japan, South Korea, and China built technical capacity through public investment, industrial policy, directed credit, protection, and managed trade, while many Western economies have become dependent on finance, services, and imported goods. This has weakened the West’s ability to sustain the military power that keeps globalization in place. Globalization also exposed the costs of weakening domestic industry. Trade shocks left factory workers with lasting job losses and lower incomes. Research has linked layoffs to far-right support , unequal exposure to economic shocks to racial animus and right-wing voting in the United States, and local trade shocks to authoritarian values . A company that dominates its market sector can raise prices with less fear of losing customers. Across industries in fifteen European economies, the average market share of the four largest firms rose from 26 percent in 2000 to 32 percent in 2019; average markups rose about 7 percent. A US manufacturing study found that mergers were associated with higher markups and little evidence of higher plant productivity. Firms can also use monopsony power to hold down wages and hiring. In a study of French labor markets, a 10 percent increase in labor market concentration was associated with nearly 0.9 percent lower wages for new hires and 12.4 percent fewer hires. Shocks Give Price-Setters Cover COVID caused real supply problems . Lockdowns disrupted production. Households saved more, while spending shifted from services to goods. Auto demand plummeted in 2020, causing carmakers to cut their chip orders. In turn, chipmakers shifted production to other customers, and when auto demand returned, they could not quickly meet carmakers’ renewed need. Shortages can make it easier for powerful firms to raise prices. When rivals face the same shock and customers expect increases, price hikes can spread even without a cartel. In 2022, profits rose faster than production across the eurozone and accounted for the largest share of price increases. Real cost shocks, like those associated with the recent US wars of 2025–26, can nonetheless coexist with wider margins. In food markets, financial speculation and stockpiling can drive price rises beyond what shortages alone would explain, especially when war creates expectations of shortages. When COVID arrived, manufacturers expected a prolonged recession and cut production, capacity, and orders. Demand and employment recovered faster than expected, leaving shortages of semiconductors, lumber, and other inputs. Fiscal expansion did not invent these bottlenecks; it revealed them. Repeated periods of weak demand can close factories, disperse skilled workers, and delay investment, making supply slower to recover. In the 1960s, large industrial firms had built capacity but struggled to sell everything they produced. Some responded by buying unrelated businesses, selling off or outsourcing production, and relying more on short-term finance. This shift away from production gave financial markets greater influence over industrial decisions. In the decades that followed, private debt could temporarily sustain productive capacity despite weak wage growth, but when borrowing slowed, spending and sales fell. Debt-led crises also enabled investors to buy distressed property, companies, and assets from poor households cheaply, at a high social cost. Debt and Austerity Some Mesopotamian rulers in antiquity periodically canceled debts , freed people from debt bondage, and restored property, in part to check mass indebtedness and land concentration. These measures were episodic, not part of a regular, universal system. Debt was contested in Ancient Greece too, as seen in Solon’s reforms . Rome faced recurring credit crises and public-land conflict . Debt crises and land struggles shaped the politics of the late Roman Republic. The two world wars showed that markets could be governed. During World War I, the US War Industries Board coordinated production. After the war, advocates of austerity in Britain and Italy promoted balanced budgets and hard-money policies and accepted high unemployment as ways to restore economic hierarchies at labor’s expense. During World War II, the United States used price ceilings, rationing, taxes, wage policies, credit controls, and resource allocation to restrain inflation, raise revenue, and direct production toward the war effort. One historical estimate put the price level more than 30 percent below where it would have been without general price controls. Controls did not abolish scarcity. Many goods were rationed or simply unavailable, but alongside public management of demand and physical resources, the controls worked. World War II was one of the few times that the US came close to full productive capacity. During the war, labor shortages and stronger unions also improved many workers’ bargaining position . A study of 166 elections in 16 OECD countries from 1980 to 2016 found austerity associated with increased electoral abstention and support for nonmainstream parties. Austerity weakens workers’ bargaining power and public services, reduces demand and investment, and can undermine social stability. The late 1990s surpluses, combined with the trade deficit, pushed the US private sector into record deficits. Household debt grew much faster than income as Washington paid down public debt . Levy researchers later linked private sector deficits to the global crisis of 2008. Post-COVID inflation had different causes, including supply bottlenecks , a shift toward goods, and energy shocks. Lessons From Past Shocks Inflation was already rising in the United States before the 1973 oil shock. The US chose to escalate the Vietnam War . The war diverted workers, fuel, and materials from civilian production and sent dollars abroad. In 1971, Richard Nixon ended the dollar’s convertibility into gold. The dollar continued to serve as a major world currency without a promise of redemption in gold. Fiat money still faces real limits: the availability of oil, labor, materials, and productive capacity. During the Yom Kippur War, Arab oil states imposed an embargo and cut production . Separately, the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) raised prices. Iran’s shah, a US ally, pushed for higher oil revenues to finance the country’s military buildup. Nixon had promised to sell him the arms he wanted. A contemporary account argued that Washington encouraged Middle Eastern producers to raise prices from 1971; Washington had earlier accommodated the shah’s push for higher prices, but during the crisis it opposed the Arab embargo and resisted OPEC’s proposed 100 percent price hike. The United States did not create the whole crisis. But US leaders accepted higher oil revenues for a strategic ally. Meanwhile, oil companies, banks, and arms firms gained from the new flows of money, while workers and households paid higher prices. Leaders later used the crisis to attack welfare and full employment, even though the oil shock did not prove that public spending caused inflation. The shift toward policies that tolerated high unemployment, wage restraint, and public spending cuts was a political choice, not an automatic result of the oil shock. Naomi Klein has traced disaster capitalism to Milton Friedman’s argument that crises can open the way for sweeping economic change. She shows how leaders and businesses have used this approach repeatedly, from Chile and post-Soviet Russia to the Iraq occupation and Hurricane Katrina, to push privatization and expand corporate control. This selfsame policy choice — the choice between using inflation to justify austerity, or addressing its causes and effects — is reflected in Sweden’s response to the latest energy shock. The Swedish Case The war in the Middle East disrupted shipments through the Strait of Hormuz this past year, raising Swedish motor fuel prices by 25 percent. Yet Eurostat’s November release put Swedish inflation at just 0.6 percent, compared with 4 percent in the eurozone. On the Riksbank’s target measure , which holds mortgage interest rates fixed, Swedish inflation rose from 0.7 percent in August to 1.5 percent in September before easing to 1.1 percent in October. Excluding energy, it was just 0.1 percent in October. Some measures that widened Sweden’s deficit also lowered prices. The November budget projected a 2026 deficit of SEK158 billion, or 2.3 percent of GDP. After Sweden halved the value-added tax (VAT) on groceries in April, food prices fell 5.5 percent that month; a government review found that retailers passed on most of the cut. The government also subsidized half-price monthly transit passes from July through December. The Riksbank estimates that without the temporary measures’ direct effects on prices, September inflation would have been 3.0 percent, twice the actual rate. Growth continued alongside low inflation. Real GDP grew 1.6 percent, and the European Commission raised its forecast for 2026 growth to 2.8 percent . Household spending, investment, and net exports also lifted growth. The available figures give no separate estimate of how much the relief measures added to output or productivity. The labor market recovery has been slower. A labor force survey put trend unemployment at 8.7 percent in the third quarter, even as employment rose. Sweden has also cut support for people in employment programs for more than 300 days. The government expects the cuts to speed up the return to work. But when households on tight budgets cut their spending , local businesses lose sales; and if financial pressure pushes people into jobs that make poor use of their skills, productivity could suffer. Sweden’s 2026 figures do not yet tell us how large these effects are. The Riksbank expects inflation to exceed 2 percent in 2027 as the recovery strengthens and higher energy and import costs feed through. The government forecasts 2.8 percent in 2028 when the grocery VAT is due to return to 12 percent. Further disruption in Hormuz, the war in Ukraine, and new trade barriers could also raise costs and weaken demand for Swedish exports. Sweden can issue kronor, but what it can buy depends on the fuel, labor, and equipment available. Higher interest rates can slow spending and hiring, but they won’t bring another tanker through Hormuz. Cheaper fares leave commuters with more money in their pockets today, and better public transport gives them a way to use less imported fuel tomorrow. A state cannot buy resources that do not exist. But concentrated firms may raise prices before resources run out, while austerity can destroy demand and productive capacity. Inflation reflects pressure on real resources, but power determines who controls them and who pays when prices rise. Under Capitalism, Socialism Will Never Disappear

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Alex McCarron ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-21T16:01:47+00:00

If socialists and progressives had a good night on November 3, it wasn’t so much because they brought new ideas to the election, but because it was a chance to relitigate fights the Right thought it had won years ago: universal health care, Palestinian solidarity, and the expansion of the labor movement. We even got a rematch between George W. Bush–era Islamophobia and two of the Muslim Americans who had to live in its shadow, Adam Hamawy and Abdul El-Sayed. Many have lauded Zohran Mamdani’s novel use of the term “affordability” for the recent shift in political common sense. But while his branding is new, his politics are not. Mamdani has never styled himself as a new kind of politician. He seems to be constantly locating himself in history, comparing himself to Milwaukee’s sewer socialists and New Dealers like Fiorello La Guardia. This makes sense when you consider that his policies represent decades of struggle: the project of expanding democracy into the workplace dates to the late nineteenth century, and the fight to guarantee health care as a right dates to the early twentieth. Critics often ask why socialists are bringing a nineteenth-century ideology to bear on twenty-first century problems. The simple answer is that it seems to be what’s working. Since the turn of the twenty-first century, a series of global social movements have sought a new kind of political orientation, one that would allow for a break with the politics of the last century and the historical culs-de-sac they crashed into. Every few years brings a new form of right, center, and left politics: Occupy, Abundance, the Yellow Vests, the Movement of Squares, prison abolitionism. These movements all respond to real problems, but what’s new about them is mostly just branding. Few offer genuinely new critiques or build lasting political constituencies or institutions. Their strength is being novel enough to draw a crowd, drive a news cycle, or launch an academic career. Such innovations move to the same rhythm as capitalism’s own churning drive to simulate invention, revolution, and disruption across the economy. At the same time, one sees the appeal. We can’t exactly blame radicals for wanting a disruption of their current institutions. Everywhere you look, leftists are trying to overcome the NGOization of the Left and searching for a common ground between their alienated, specialized critiques. Ecologists want to talk about the environmental catastrophe of the genocide in Gaza, while anti-racists want to connect their work to the extreme weather events that disproportionally effect the Global South. This is the basic drive of left-wing politics: to establish continuities between spheres of knowledge and to build connections between people. The 20th Century Never Died To find that meeting place in socialism is to recognize that what is happening to you has been happening to you for a very long time. Embracing the old language means realizing that you’re settling unfinished business rather than facing a new foe. Comparing the capitalist class to the aristocracy that came before it enables a person to see how arbitrary and contingent ruling-class power really is. One can understand why Bernie Sanders traded loaded terms like “bourgeoisie” and “proletariat” for the “1 percent” and “99 percent.” But it was just as important that he held onto “democratic socialism.” The term signaled that he was continuing a struggle and adding to an existing tradition of critique rather than declaring a year zero for left politics. We are likely to be haunted for years by how dynamic, inventive, and exciting the twentieth century was. Every decade brought a new way of dressing, a new style of painting, and new stage in the maturation of cinema and popular music. Our hunger for political novelty is not just a reflection of market forces. It’s also a long shadow of that era of continuous revolution in politics and culture. Look closely, though, and you’ll find many of the twentieth-century movements that sought a break with tradition did so while grabbing hold of another tradition buried even deeper in history. Le Corbusier claimed his form of architecture would clear “the charnel house of past ages” that our homes had become, but he did so by marrying the mathematical proportions of the Parthenon to the logic of mass production. As much as Henri Matisse and Pablo Picasso wanted to carry Paul Cézanne’s project into the future, they were also looking for a way to reconnect Western painting to the animism of African sculpture. The French New Wave paid an equally backhanded compliment to the “naive” genre films of Americans like Raoul Walsh and Sam Fuller, whose primitivism could liberate French filmmakers from their own pretensions and refinement. As Long as There’s Capitalism, There Will Be Socialists Even Karl Marx had this moment of paradox. In The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte , he writes that “the social revolution of the nineteenth century cannot take its poetry from the past but only from the future.” A few lines later, he quotes the Gospel of Luke 9:60: “Let the dead bury their dead.” Marx was inveighing against the tendency of revolutionaries to reach for the words and costumes of past eras when faced with the task of changing the world: Rome for the French Revolution, the Old Testament for the English Civil War. This awkward LARPing is what turns history’s repetitions into farce. Yet Marx’s famous line about history occurring “first as tragedy, the second time as farce” is itself framed as an amendment to Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel. Even the poetry of the future has a few allusions in it. The difference between the French revolutionaries’ idealized Rome and today’s democratic socialism is that socialism is no more a thing of the past than capitalism is. Capitalism constantly calls the concept back into existence, raising its own gravediggers out of the grave, over and over again. As long as capitalism exists, it will exploit workers and destroy the planet, and the masses will find a way to fight back. Our present sits on a much longer timeline than the twenty-four-hour news cycle has accustomed us to. One day we’ll have new words and new costumes, but those belong to a new world, and we’re not there yet. Anwar Shaikh Renewed Marx’s Economic Theory for Our Time

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Riccardo Bellofiore ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-21T15:10:25+00:00

Anwar Shaikh, who died on October 6, 2026, was one of the most original economists of the last half-century. Few did as much to rescue the Marxian critique of political economy from its theoretical difficulties and develop it into a rigorous, empirically grounded analysis of contemporary capitalism. Shaikh’s ambition was extraordinary. He reconstructed classical political economy as an alternative to the neoclassical mainstream, understanding capitalism not as a system tending toward harmonious equilibrium but as a turbulent social order whose normal functioning generates competition, conflict, structural change, and crisis. I owe Anwar an immense intellectual debt . For more than fifty years, his writings were a source of inspiration and critical engagement. While our perspectives were never identical, his influence was deeply rooted in my thinking. When Marx Was Supposed to Be Dead I first encountered Anwar’s writings in the mid-1970s. In Italy, where I had studied with Claudio Napoleoni and wrote my dissertation on Rosa Luxemburg, the debate centered on Karl Marx’s theory of value and Piero Sraffa’s criticism of neoclassical economics. The demonstration that prices of production could be determined independently of labor values seemed to many economists to render Marx’s theory redundant. Others retreated into a primarily philosophical defense of Marx, preserving the critique of alienation and fetishism while abandoning much of its economic explanatory content. Anwar refused this alternative. In the 1970s and early ’80s, his writings were decisive in restoring Marx as a scientific analyst of capitalism. His contributions on the transformation problem, technological change, international trade, competition, and crisis challenged both the claim that Marx had been superseded by Sraffa and the tendency to reconstruct Marxian political economy using categories borrowed from orthodoxy. His celebrated 1974 article, “The Humbug Production Function,” exposed how apparently impressive empirical results could arise from algebraic identities rather than the neoclassical theory they supposedly confirmed. Mathematics, Anwar insisted, could not substitute for an explanation of the social relations underlying economic phenomena. His two articles on foreign trade challenged David Ricardo’s understanding of comparative advantage, demonstrating that international competition could reproduce persistent inequalities between nations without requiring monopoly or imperfect competition. His intervention in the transformation controversy showed that Marx’s procedure could be interpreted as the first step in an iterative process leading to prices of production. The essential issue was not simply mathematical consistency. Marx was investigating the historically specific relations of capitalist production, the exploitation of workers, and the processes through which accumulation transforms itself. What mattered was to recover and develop this distinctive object of inquiry. Anwar’s essays on Maurice Dobb and on the history of crisis theories, both published in 1978, were equally important. He showed that capitalist crises were not accidental departures from equilibrium, because they belonged to the system’s normal movement. To understand capitalist reproduction was simultaneously to understand its interruptions. For a generation that had witnessed the collapse of the postwar boom, these arguments were more than academic interventions. They restored confidence that Marxian theory could develop as a living research program rather than survive as a defensive orthodoxy. The Turbulence of Capital Anwar’s reconstruction of real competition was perhaps his most enduring theoretical contribution. From this perspective, competition was neither the harmonious mechanism imagined by neoclassical economics nor something abolished by the concentration of capital. It was a relentless struggle among capitals for profitability and survival. As part of this struggle, firms introduced machinery to reduce costs, undercut competitors, and obtain temporary advantages. Successful innovations spread, transforming productive conditions and undermining the advantages that initially motivated them. Decisions that were rational for individual capitalists could have contradictory consequences for capital as a whole. Mechanization, driven by the pursuit of extra profits, could contribute to a declining general rate of profit. As Vivek Chibber has reminded us, this view of competition also posed a challenge to the influential monopoly capital tradition associated with Paul Baran and Paul Sweezy. Anwar argued that its claim that concentration had superseded competition rested, paradoxically, on accepting the neoclassical identification of competition with perfect competition. This conception was central to my own interpretation of Luxemburg. In my dissertation, completed in 1976, I had explored how technological change emerged from both the antagonism between capital and labor within production and competition among capitals for extra surplus value. These processes could generate problems of realization and insufficient investment, without supporting a theory of capitalism’s inevitable collapse. Anwar gave me indispensable analytical tools for developing these insights. Probably, my subsequent reading of his work was more Schumpeterian than he would have accepted. I saw capitalist innovation as a dynamic of endogenous structural transformation that repeatedly displaced existing productive configurations. Anwar was more skeptical of Joseph Schumpeter, whose account he considered insufficiently emancipated from equilibrium thinking. My 1985 article on Schumpeter’s monetary theory of development and my 1989 essay, “A Monetary Labor Theory of Value,” developed this difference further. My intellectual formation owed much to Napoleoni as well as Augusto Graziani, Meghnad Desai, and Duncan Foley. For me, Marx’s theory of value was a theory of exploitation within the monetary circuit of capital, not primarily a theory of relative prices. I distinguished labor-power from living labor, arriving at the category of living labor-power: the capacity to work inseparable from the human beings who bear it. Its actual expenditure as living labor depends on capitalist command, cooperation, conflict, and resistance. Capital cannot purchase a predetermined quantity of living labor as an ordinary commodity. The extraction of surplus labor is contested; new value originates in this process and acquires its social expression in money. Anwar understood exploitation and capitalist command, but our approaches diverged over the connection between labor, value, prices, and money. The difference was especially over money. Anwar’s account of real competition and prices of production retained the classical notion of moving centers of gravity and treated money primarily in relation to exchange, prices, and profitability. I drew instead on Schumpeter’s bank-created purchasing power, Graziani’s monetary circuit, and Hyman Minsky’s financing of an uncertain future. For me, money is constitutive of capitalist production from the outset: bank finance gives firms command over labor-power before production begins, while sales subsequently validate the value produced. Money as initial finance is not merely money as universal equivalent, nor is financing production identical to financing investment. This did not make Anwar indifferent to credit or finance; it marked a different theoretical starting point. Different Crises of Different Capitalisms Our views of capitalist crises were different, but also complementary. Anwar’s distinctive contribution was his theory of profitability crises. Capitalist accumulation, he argued, follows recurrent long rhythms, in which phases of expansion eventually give way to prolonged downturns. Beneath shorter business cycles lies the movement of profitability, shaped by real competition, capital-intensive technical change, and the struggle over wages and working conditions. Particular historical shocks operate within these longer movements. For Anwar, the crucial incentive to accumulation was the rate of profit-of-enterprise, the difference between the general rate of profit and the interest rate. This distinction was central to his interpretation of the Great Recession, which he called the first great depression of the twenty-first century. The financial collapse of 2007–8 was the trigger, not the underlying cause. But he did not argue that the general rate of profit had simply continued to fall throughout the neoliberal era. On the contrary, he insisted that the attack on labor from the 1980s onward had restrained the decline in profitability, while the dramatic fall in interest rates had raised net profitability and sustained a substantial economic expansion. Against this backdrop, cheap credit simultaneously encouraged growing indebtedness and financial speculation. The recovery after the end of the postwar boom in the 1970s was real, but the conditions sustaining it could not be prolonged indefinitely. In his mature reconstruction, Anwar distinguished the normal maximum rate of profit, persistently eroded by capital-intensive technical change, from the normal rate of profit, whose decline could be counteracted by a rising profit share. His account of the neoliberal boom thus gave an essential place to countertendencies, without relinquishing the underlying logic of profitability-driven accumulation. I read Marx’s falling rate of profit as a metatheory of crisis, not a law generating identical outcomes in every period. Countertendencies can prevail yet generate new contradictions: rising exploitation may sustain profitability while aggravating realization problems, and struggles over living labor can challenge capitalist command directly. My reconstruction suggested a further historical distinction: the nineteenth-century Long Depression and the crisis of the 1970s were both crises of profitability, though arising from different mechanisms; the Great Crash of 1929 and the Great Recession beginning in 2007 revealed instead how the conditions sustaining capitalist expansion could generate crises of realization and financial breakdown. Anwar recognized the distinctive historical forms of these crises but sought their underlying unity primarily in the recurrent movements of profitability and accumulation. The crisis of the 1970s was for me not merely a wage squeeze but a crisis of the capital-labor relation itself: a difficulty in extracting the quantity and quality of living labor required by capital. The subsequent neoliberal restructuring transformed both production and finance, developing what I called privatized Keynesianism, sustained by asset inflation, private indebtedness, and politically managed monetary policy. Finance did not simply conceal the contradictions of a stagnant capitalism. It helped sustain accumulation and reorganize the conditions under which labor was employed and exploited. These were not arguments against Anwar’s theory of competition and profitability. They were attempts to develop a complementary account of how capitalist contradictions take different historical forms. Anwar engaged seriously with effective demand, credit, and debt, especially in his writings on Marx, John Maynard Keynes, and Michał Kalecki. Our disagreement was not about profitability versus demand but about how monetary finance, class struggle, and accumulation should be connected in a critique of changing capitalist forms. An Expanding Critique of Political Economy Over time, Anwar’s project expanded from the reconstruction and defense of Marxian political economy into an ambitious alternative framework grounded in classical political economy. Marx remained central, but the wider constellation included Adam Smith, Ricardo, Keynes, Sraffa, Roy Harrod, and Joan Robinson. In an autobiographical essay published in 2014, Anwar explicitly characterized his approach to growth as following a “classical-Keynesian-Robinsonian path.” This broadening of his intellectual horizons did not mean abandoning Marx, still less weakening his criticism of capitalism. It expressed his conviction that the classical tradition could provide the foundations for a modern science of capitalist society. Empirical investigation had been integral to Anwar’s work from the beginning. With E. Ahmet Tonak , he developed a Marxian reconstruction of national income accounts, distinguishing productive and unproductive activities and challenging the seemingly neutral categories of conventional statistics. His research on exchange rates, international inequalities, value-price relations, and profitability similarly connected theoretical arguments with systematic investigation of economic reality. Anwar participated in the international conference I organized at Bergamo in 1994, marking the centenary of Friedrich Engels’s publication of the third volume of Capital . His contribution, subsequently published in a volume I edited, Marxian Economics: A Reappraisal , investigated the empirical strength of the labor theory of value, drawing also on the work of Luigi L. Pasinetti. It illustrated a crucial aspect of his intellectual trajectory: Marx’s theory was not merely to be rescued through conceptual reconstruction. Its explanatory propositions had to confront empirical evidence. His association with the Levy Economics Institute, beginning in 1990, further enlarged his research. He engaged with macroeconomic modeling and with approaches related to Wynne Godley’s work without abandoning his classical foundations. All this culminated in his monumental Capitalism: Competition, Conflict, Crises , a synthesis of decades of research. Few economists have attempted anything comparable. Anwar brought together competition, prices, wages, profitability, investment, effective demand, finance, international trade, and crisis into an integrated alternative to mainstream economic theory. Capitalism as he understood it was regulated through turbulence, not by the imaginary perfection of equilibrium markets. His intellectual curiosity remained undiminished: his later research ranged from international trade and exchange rates to econophysics, income distributions, and the measurement of inequality. Perhaps We Are Closer Than We Appear We met again at a conference in İzmir in October 2014 devoted to the causes of the global crisis. Our discussion returned to a question that had accompanied us for decades: How could Marx’s critique of political economy be developed to account for the transformations of capitalism, drawing on Keynes, Kalecki, and Minsky without losing its theoretical foundations? On this occasion, Anwar argued that our differences concerned primarily the movement from abstract categories to concrete capitalist reality. Marx had left his theoretical project unfinished, and subsequent economists had chosen different points at which to depart from it. Money, effective demand, and financial instability required further theoretical development but could not simply be added to Marx from outside. Anwar suggested that Keynes, Kalecki, and Minsky could be integrated into an account of accumulation driven by profitability, provided their contributions were critically transformed. He then concluded: “Perhaps we’re much closer than we appear to be.” I replied that I agreed with him word for word. My project was not an eclectic combination of Marx and later economists. At the beginning of my presentation, I had insisted on a distinction fundamental to my understanding of Marx: critique is not mere criticism. Marx did not simply reject Ricardo’s political economy as false. He appropriated its scientific insights, exposed their limitations, and transformed them within a different theoretical framework. The same task confronted us in engaging with subsequent theories and the changing realities of capitalism. This may also explain why, in his later work, Anwar became increasingly comfortable with a classical-Keynesian characterization of his theoretical project. An Unfinished Conversation Anwar’s intellectual independence was inseparable from his political commitments. He belonged to a generation for whom radical political economy emerged from struggles against war, racism, imperialism, and capitalist exploitation. He rejected both the false neutrality of mainstream economics and the reduction of critical theory to political slogans. As E. Ahmet Tonak has movingly recalled, he was also an extraordinary teacher. My MA students in Bergamo organized a reading group on his book, concluding with a video discussion with Anwar. He explained that a theory of capitalism could not emerge merely by adjusting orthodox assumptions: it required different questions and foundations. His generosity extended beyond academic life. I remember spending New Year’s Eve with him in 2017, shortly before a difficult period in my life. Our friendship mattered no less than our theoretical projects. We continued to exchange ideas in later years. In 2021, we discussed Sraffa, Marx, and money at considerable length, defending different interpretations while remaining attentive to each other’s arguments. At the conclusion of one exchange, Anwar wrote: “No doubt there are other issues on which we differ, but the mutual respect and affection remains. I raise a glass to our meeting again under better conditions.” Unfortunately, it will never happen. To me, Anwar’s legacy is not a closed theoretical system. It is an extraordinary example of how the criticism of capitalism can combine scientific rigor, historical understanding, empirical research, intellectual independence, and political commitment. For those of us who encountered his work in the 1970s, he helped make it possible to remain faithful to Marx without treating Marxism as an orthodoxy. For younger generations, he demonstrated that an alternative political economy could be developed rather than merely proclaimed. I will miss his friendship, his intellectual generosity, and his determination to pursue an idea wherever it led. Our intellectual dialogue will continue, at least in my mind. After more than fifty years of reading his work, I can think of no better way of honoring his memory. The affordability issues that drove the midterm elections weren’t mitigated by the mere fact of a blue wave. Democratic leaders in Congress and the individual states still face systemic problems, some of which can appear intractable. That’s especially true in California, where some 14 million residents, more than a third of the population, rely on Medi-Cal, the state’s version of the low-income Medicaid program, for their health care. California’s poverty rate is among the highest in the nation, as it has been for nearly 15 years. The challenge to millions of low-wage workers — find a way to afford life in the Golden State — has never stood taller. But that’s not the same as saying there are no answers. And incoming Democratic governor Xavier Becerra doesn’t need to wait to begin initiating meaningful improvements in the lives of those workers in such critical areas as renter protection, child care and the soaring cost of health care. Interviews with multiple stakeholders and political strategists over the past few weeks have identified a number of actions that Becerra, who will succeed Gov. Gavin Newsom in January, can undertake along with the Democratic supermajority in the state Legislature. Some of those actions directly address affordability issues facing the low-wage workers and their families who form the backbone of many of California’s largest industries. These measures certainly aren’t free. “Meaningfully addressing California’s longstanding affordability crisis will require bold solutions, which in turn will require new state funding,” said Scott Graves, budget director at the California Budget & Policy Center. “To raise revenue, state leaders should focus on ensuring that corporations and the wealthy contribute their fair share of taxes.” That said, here are five ideas that Becerra can begin pursuing almost immediately after taking office: Strengthen renter protections and add forgiveness. California’s Tenant Protection Act puts a cap on how much a landlord can raise the rent each year, but that cap remains onerous: 5% plus a cost of living increase, up to a maximum overall raise of 10%. If a landlord follows that law to the max for even a couple of years in a row, a low-wage worker can quickly be priced out of their own home. Considering California has nearly 16 million renters, that’s an ongoing affordability problem. Lowering the allowable rent increase would be a good first step, along with making the TPA permanent (the law is scheduled to expire in 2030). But there’s something else Becerra and the Legislature can do. Currently, California renters have only three business days after receiving an eviction notice to pay off back rent and remain in their homes. But California could join 21 other “redemption states” in the U.S. that allow a tenant to pay the rent owed and avoid eviction long after such a proceeding is under way — weeks, even. Past efforts to do this have run up against the powerful landlord lobby in Sacramento, so it’s no slam dunk. Related: California's Medi-Cal Crisis Deepens as Billionaire Wealth Tax Falls Short Enforce the labor laws already on the books. “Wage theft takes money straight out of low-wage workers’ pockets,” said Enrique Lopezlira, director of the Low-Wage Work Program at the UC Berkeley Labor Center. “It may be the most direct affordability issue that doesn’t need a new law.” Wage theft, by which employers underpay their workers either intentionally or through payroll mistakes, costs low-wage workers in California at least $2 billion per year , according to the Economic Policy Institute. Other reports suggest the figure could be more than twice that . Once in office, Becerra can redirect resources toward enforcing the state’s wage laws. That includes increasing staffing in the Labor Commissioner’s Office, the better to pursue wage theft claims more aggressively and start collecting past unpaid judgments. Expand access to affordable child care. One of the primary drivers of the affordability crisis for low-wage workers is finding a way to care for their children while they’re on the job — often a crushing, self-defeating expense. Newsom has worked on this issue on and off for years. In 2021, he promised to expand affordable child care by phasing in 200,000 new subsidized spots by 2025-26. He’ll fall far short of that goal , leaving Becerra to pick up the ball and continue running. This is an enormously popular issue; more than three-quarters of Californians surveyed are in favor of increasing state funding so that child care programs are available to more low-wage workers and their families. And Becerra could double down on it by simultaneously pushing for something closer to a living wage for child care workers, whose low reimbursement rates likely contribute to a smaller-than-needed talent pool. Related: California Saves Millions on Home Care: Workers Pay the Price Raise funding to backfill safety-net programs. The Republican-passed H.R. 1 spending bill last year gutted federal Medicaid funding, with California suffering a likely $30 billion per year funding shortfall as a result. Paired with new paperwork requirements for eligible families, it’s going to lead to steep cuts in Medi-Cal enrollment and more California low-wage workers going without health care until they have an emergency. As a candidate, Becerra loudly endorsed proposed “fair share” legislation as one way to offset some of the federal cuts. That law would essentially slap fines on California businesses that pay their employees so little, their workers qualify for Medi-Cal insurance. The fines would be used to help replace the missing federal funds. “Why should we subsidize health care for those folks who are working full time because their employer won’t pay them enough so they can afford health insurance?” Becerra said in August. “If you’re not paying enough for your workers, maybe that means we have to raise taxes on some of these bigger companies.” Strike the new Medi-Cal premiums on immigrants. Under a policy change pushed by Newsom, certain groups of immigrants who use Medi-Cal for their health care will begin paying monthly premiums in 2027. Though the initial figure may be as low as $30 per month, it’s still significant to a low-wage working family that is trying to make ends meet. Becerra and state lawmakers have the authority to either lower or eliminate that premium, likely encouraging more low-wage workers to continue using Medi-Cal. That also potentially reduces costs to hospitals, as patients who can’t afford Medi-Cal are statistically more likely to simply go without coverage, then use the emergency room as a source of care once a condition becomes serious. By law, such care has to be provided by the hospital even if the patient cannot pay. Without question, Becerra also bears a responsibility to work on bigger-picture issues. California’s housing crunch, for example, is by now both generational and the most obvious contributor to the affordability gap facing the state’s low-wage workers. State Speaker of the Assembly Robert Rivas “has boldly led and passed the strongest housing reforms in 20 years,” almost all of it aimed at streamlining the construction process and getting more houses into the pipeline, said Democratic political strategist Steven Maviglio. “Becerra can get on that train and lead with courage, too.” The new governor also will take office at roughly the same time that both houses of Congress come under Democratic control. A number of experts suggested that Becerra and California will need to be at the forefront of working with federal legislators to try to roll back some of H.R. 1’s deepest cuts to safety net programs. But those are long-term projects. California’s low-wage work community needs relief much sooner, not down the line. Becerra and state lawmakers are in a position to start providing just that. Photo: Kayla Sharpe By Kayla Sharpe At Ferndale High School, one of our most pressing problems is the dilapidated building, built in the 1950s. The outdated heating and cooling systems are prone to classroom floods — in the last nine months I’ve experienced three. Once boiling hot water sprayed out of the HVAC unit during class, nearly injuring a student. It was late August — our last Sunday night of freedom before our teacher professional development week — when six of my co-workers and I received an email from our principal. She said that a few classrooms had flooded again the previous week, but the damage was limited and our classrooms were “ready for our return.” “Tomorrow is still the beginning of a fresh school year,” she wrote, “and I’m looking forward to welcoming everyone back and starting the year together on a positive note.” She made the mistake of including all affected teachers as recipients, instead of blind copying us, so I knew exactly who to reach out to. That evening I spent lots of time on the phone with my co-workers and my fellow union building representatives. We all had experienced previous flooding, so we were skeptical that our classrooms would actually be ready for our return the following morning. Ever since I began working at this school in 2024, my mission has been to convince my co-workers we should be compensated in some way when our school administrators fail us. Whenever we’ve asked administrators to address issues like poor attendance or facilities problems, we’ve gotten responses like “That is not my fault” or “I do not have the power to fix that.” This energy has been so demoralizing for our teaching staff, and it seemed to me that my co-workers had gotten used to just accepting the b.s. But having cut my teeth as a political organizer on my college campus and read Secrets of a Successful Organizer , I could tell that this issue had the juice we needed to lead a workplace action — everyone cared enough to do something about it. By Monday morning I had devised a plan with the support of my fellow building reps. INDUSTRIAL FANS Our suspicions were correct; words can hardly describe the scene we walked into. Each of the seven flooded classrooms had eight to 15 industrial fans blowing on the floor, dehumidifying machines siphoning water out of the classroom windows, and no functioning air conditioning. Teachers were supposed to spend this day organizing and preparing our classrooms. Most of us spend hundreds of dollars of our own money to purchase supplies, decor, soft lighting, and small comforting touches to make our classrooms the warm, safe environments we know students need. It takes a fair amount of time to unpack those things from the summer months and get the space ready for the school year. When I saw the state of my classroom, I knew it was going to be impossible to get any sort of work done in that environment. My next step was to walk door to door and check in on my six co-workers who had been looped in on that email. No classroom was even close to being a functional workspace. I could feel the swelling anger, and knew the moment was ripe to make an ask. “We do not deserve this,” I told my co-workers. “What if we all went to the principal and asked for something in return?” I told them about the plan the other building reps and I had come up with: The affected teachers could march together to the principal’s office and ask that either we receive pay for the additional hours it takes us to set up our classrooms outside of contract time, or we be excused from a professional development session during the week to set up our classrooms during work hours. Everyone was too angry to be afraid. All but one agreed to join our march on the boss. The six of us found our principal in a teacher’s classroom. She stepped into the hall to see us standing there shoulder to shoulder, looking upset. None of us will ever forget her response: She took out her phone, started filming us, and said, “Oh I am going to put this on TikTok, it’s the first day of school and the teachers already want something from me.” When she saw how deeply stunned we were, she put her phone back in her pocket and asked what was wrong. I reminded her of her email, and informed her that our classrooms were in fact not “ready for our return.” I told her this wasn’t fair to us, and explained how we felt it could be rectified. She said she understood our frustration and would “look into” allowing us to timecard extra hours. We did not feel incredibly hopeful. There’s too much wiggle room in the phrase “I will look into that.” But to our surprise, we received an email from the principal saying she would be able to approve three hours of paid time for classroom setup. We were doubtful that we would win any kind of concession, so we didn’t even think that far ahead. We were able to get the most necessary work done within the three approved hours, but could have benefited from more time. Still, once the rest of our staff heard about our action, our building was buzzing with excitement. I heard co-workers 15 to 25 years my senior say, “That was so cool!” “I didn’t even know you could do that!” “I wish I was as brave as you when I was your age!” So in my mind, it was a success. I think the most powerful thing we can do as organizers is be hopeful, and share that hope. The message of “You do not deserve this mistreatment” seemed to really resonate. In education, we grow so used to getting beat down from all angles that we can forget we deserve to be treated with dignity. I’m very proud to have helped my co-workers work together at that moment. I hope this will help us find the courage to take on our next workplace issue without fear. Amazon is warning customers that the legislation would put small delivery companies out of business, eliminate jobs, and delay their packages. The company has not explained how it calculated the additional $5 per order. The act’s partisans say Amazon’s threats are absurd. “The Delivery Protection Act would require companies like Amazon to pay a $500 licensing fee every other year and comply with workplace and public safety regulations,” Councilmember Tiffany Cabán, the bill’s lead sponsor, told Jacobin . “It’s simply dishonest to claim that this bill would require them to charge consumers $5 for every delivery. The legislation Amazon is fighting in New York would change who employs its sprawling delivery-driver workforce. Cabán’s bill would require operators of last-mile delivery facilities to employ their drivers and warehouse workers directly. Amazon currently contracts out much of that work to smaller companies known as Delivery Service Partners (DSPs). The bill would also impose licensing, safety, and training requirements on delivery facilities, with protections for workers whose jobs are affected by the change. Thirty-four of the fifty-one City Council members have signed on as sponsors, and Mayor Mamdani supports the bill. Council Speaker Julie Menin, however, has yet to bring it to a vote. Councilmembers are negotiating changes to the legislation, including whether to retain the ban on subcontracting, which Amazon is particularly determined to defeat. When the company began planning its own delivery network in 2013, executives worried that entering the transportation business would expose it to organized labor. “A primary concern was whether getting into the transportation business would expose them to unions,” executives involved in those discussions told journalist Brad Stone. When Amazon launched the DSP program several years later, it could run its own delivery network while leaving the drivers on someone else’s payroll. The Teamsters have spent years fighting to establish that Amazon is a joint employer of those drivers and therefore has to bargain with their union. Amazon has threatened to challenge the DPA in court, and the bill is now fueling a steady stream of criticism in the right-wing New York Post . The current version of the bill would spare workers years of litigation at the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) to establish that Amazon is their employer and therefore must bargain with their union. That wouldn’t guarantee a collective bargaining agreement, but it would eliminate one of the company’s principal defenses against unionization. Amazon has funded its multimillion-dollar lobbying and advertising campaign through the Five Borough Jobs Campaign, which operates publicly as New York Delivers. Its advertisements feature DSP owners and drivers warning that the legislation would eliminate small businesses and raise prices. Amazon says that more than forty DSPs in New York City employ over five thousand workers whose jobs could be threatened if the companies were prohibited from contracting with Amazon. The company has also raised the possibility of moving delivery facilities outside city limits. The Teamsters’ Rosario said the concern is misplaced — and that the bill could help DSP owners too. “They’re being squeezed just as much as their employees are,” he said. DSP owners are responsible for maintaining and repairing vehicles and bear much of the responsibility when drivers are injured or involved in accidents. Under the existing arrangement, Rosario said, DSPs serve as a “scapegoat” for the company. Amazon is listed among the organization’s highest-tier corporate donors, a category representing contributions of at least $1 million. Their attorney argued that the legislation conflicts with federal and state law. Menin, who supported an earlier version of the bill, has held off on backing the current one. She has raised concerns about potential job losses, higher prices for consumers, and whether the legislation would survive a legal challenge. A spokesman for the Mamdani administration’s Law Department told The New York Times that he was confident the final legislation would withstand legal scrutiny. The New York Post reports that councilmembers are considering requiring warehouse operators to own the delivery vehicles currently owned or leased by contractors. Other details are still being negotiated, including how large a warehouse must be to fall under the law. Sources familiar with the negotiations expect the text to be finalized next week. “Direct employment is one of the most important provisions of the bill,” Rosario said. He said there had been some discussion of exceptions for truly independent contractors but was adamant that Amazon shouldn’t be exempt from the direct-employment requirement. “There’s no way they’re getting around that language,” he said. Menin has insisted that the council will regulate Amazon while protecting jobs and consumer prices. The uncertainty around that question is part of the general murkiness of this entire affair (no pun intended), one tied into a knot of three separate political threads: the economy’s outsize reliance on the artificial intelligence industry, ruthless corporate anti-competition, and the simmering new Cold War with China. While Huang had a number of infidelities, it’s his affair with business executive Alice Huang that is central to both the global attention this has drawn and to his ouster from the company he had founded over a Denny’s meal with only $40,000 of capital, thanks to his paramour’s alleged role as a spy for the Ministry of State Security, roughly China’s equivalent to the Central Intelligence Agency. Even as the infractions piled up, it’s hard to imagine Huang — a widely admired , politically connected CEO who commanded intense loyalty from his employees — being pushed out of his own company over infidelity if not for the possibility, leaked to the press, that he fell for a Chinese government honey trap. So it’s notable that no one seems to really know whether or not Alice Huang truly was a spy. CNN reports that US intelligence agencies aren’t actually sure, with some officials viewing media reports alleging that she is as overstating the reality. At the same time, Donald Trump , Vice President J. D. Vance , and Attorney General Todd Blanche have all publicly claimed uncertainty about this central allegation. The Singaporean government went out of its way to explicitly deny The Wall Street Journal’s reporting that its officials had been concerned about her alleged spying for Beijing and investigated it. Most explosively, T he Washington Post reported that the spy claim came from one official in particular: China hawk Marco Rubio, who personally reshaped the CIA’s more ambiguous intelligence into a memo forcefully making the spy allegation and circulated it around the administration all the way back in May, to little effect. Let’s take a moment now to note how odd it is that the details of that memo then somehow made their way to the press. Let’s also take a moment to note how odd it is that neither this administration, which has zealously investigated and thrown the book at leakers, nor Rubio, who has called leakers “criminals” and once tried to throw some in prison for a decade, seem to particularly care about this leak. A Win for Hawks So what exactly is going on here? It’s too early to say for sure. But one thing we can say with certainty is that Huang was long given the side-eye by Washington’s China hawks owing to his unabashed advocacy for doing business with China, his closeness with the president, and the fact that he had used that closeness to get Trump to let Nvidia skirt US export controls and sell its chips to China. For those hawks, who Huang publicly lambasted as carrying a “badge of shame,” this was a grave offense and a threat to national security, with the possibility that China could use the advanced chips to win the “AI race” that this crowd is obsessed with. Another certainty is that these same hawks have wasted no time in seizing on the affair to push for harder-line policies against China, at a time when Trump had been edging toward a more conciliatory posture with the country, drawing growing pushback from Washington. The Trump administration has now tightened export controls on AI chips in the wake of this scandal, and a consensus is now e merging in Congress to go beyond this. China hawks are making a renewed push to tighten them even further, with parts of a fourth bill doing so now set to be added to the National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) for 2027. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, reportedly the main figure within the administration resisting a crackdown on the export of Nvidia chips, is being publicly undermined by one of his own fellow cabinet members, facing the possibility of being investigated , and may see his head roll over the episode, all as lawmakers demand he clamp down on the chip exports. China hawks “are thrilled right now” and are “gonna win the day,” one Trump official told T he Washington Sun , while an economist who backs the stricter controls told the outlet that with Lutnick and Nvidia “sidelined, the center of gravity has shifted to Rubio-world,” since they “were the main opposition to any sort of export controls.” A Win for AI Monopolists China hawks aren’t the only ones who stand to benefit from Huang’s ouster. The battle over China’s access to high-end AI chips is, in part, a proxy for a whole other battle being fought within the industry. The removal of Huang is viewed as a blow to the movement behind open-source AI models — those that the general public can download and tinker with, as opposed to closed-source models like OpenAI’s ChatGPT or Anthropic’s Claude, where the developer has exclusive control but chooses to make the model available for public use. Open-source models also happen to be mostly the kind developed by Chinese companies, whose cheaper models US companies are increasingly turning to . Huang also happened to be a major champion for — and, more importantly, financial backer of — such models, probably because the proliferation of more AI models also means more business for Nvidia. One venture capitalist who invested in some of the same start-ups as Nvidia told the Financial Times that Huang getting the boot means “we may end up in a much more concentrated world of model providers.” That put him at odds with companies like OpenAI and Anthropic, whose commercial interest lies in trying to dominate the AI market and preventing the spread of open-source models that would make people less reliant on their products. Anthropic has long been an outspoken proponent of the export controls on AI chips that Huang chafed against, breaking from other industry players over the issue by backing the three restrictionist bills included in the NDAA for 2027. OpenAI, meanwhile, pulled back on its advocacy for export controls, both as Huang’s dealmaking saw it become more financially intertwined with Nvidia and to align more closely with Trump as he came under Huang’s influence, costing China hawks what they had counted as an important industry ally. But Huang’s ouster has “loosened them up a bit,” a source told Politico , with OpenAI now back to arguing in favor of those same three export control bills in the defense authorization bill for next year. In fact, it goes much further than this. Because filings show that both of these closed-source firms have thrown a small mountain of money into lobbying various arms of the federal government on this issue, including the very two organs — the State Department and the National Security Council (NSC) — that happen to be headed by Marco Rubio, China-hawk-in-chief and the main figure responsible for the spy claims about Huang’s mistress. Starting from the third quarter of this year, dating from July to the end of September — the period that came after Rubio reportedly circulated his spy allegation memo in May — OpenAI spent a whopping $1.5 million lobbying on issues that included “foreign AI capabilities and national-security risks,” lining up with Politico’s reporting that the firm had started voicing its support for export controls to Congress in recent months. This was a notable shift from 2025: that year, OpenAI’s in-house lobbying outfit had spent substantially less than this amount per quarter and hadn’t lobbied the State Department, nor does that phrase appear as a lobbying issue in that year’s filings. In other words, it’s not just that OpenAI started pushing for restricting the export of chips right after Rubio circulated his questionable memo linking Nvidia’s CEO to Chinese spying, and that it was that CEO’s removal that had “loosened them up” to do it. They also lobbied Rubio’s own department, and did it just months before that memo mysteriously leaked to the press, forcing his ouster. There is a similar pattern with the other closed-source AI giant, Anthropic, which, in that same July-to-September period paid several Trumpworld lobbying firms to work on this matter. One was Navigators Global, which that very same quarter started lobbying Congress on behalf of the AI firm on the export control provisions in the NDAA. Navigators Global’s cofounder happened to be Rubio’s very first chief of staff when he was a senator, while one of the lobbyists who worked on this issue is Andy Keiser, who for fourteen years served in various roles for ex-congressman Mike Rogers, the Trump ally who ran for Michigan’s Senate seat this year and in 2024. Sit for a moment with the fact that Anthropic was pushing this issue using a lobbying firm closely connected to the very figure arguably most responsible for driving Huang’s ouster. It’s a similar case with Ballard Partners, founded by a longtime Florida lobbyist who counts Rubio as a friend and where Trump’s recently ousted chief of staff Susie Wiles was managing partner for years before joining up with Trump in 2019 — a firm so connected that one departed Trump official has charged it “controlled” White House policy. In the third quarter, it started lobbying for Anthropic on “advocacy regarding US policy regarding AI” to the tune of $380,000, including at Rubio’s NSC, for which it employed two recently departed Trump national security personnel, one of whom had just worked at Rubio’s State Department. Another specifically Rubio-connected lobbying firm used by Anthropic for this was Continental Strategy, one of whose managing partners is Alberto Martinez — a “trusted advisor” to Rubio who worked in various roles for the former senator over the course of a decade, including chief of staff and senior strategist on his first Senate race — and which promoted Wiles’s daughter a day after Trump hired her. Martinez was among the lobbyists Continental used to lobby agencies that included Rubio’s NSC (from April to the end of June) and then Rubio’s State Department (in this just-passed third quarter) on “AI development and national security,” for which it was paid a total of $300,000 by Anthropic for these periods. Both Ballard and Continental’s roles were first reported by ZeroHedge , but this is based on my own separate analysis of Anthropic’s 2026 lobbying disclosures. It’s remarkable that at the same time the interests of Rubio-led China hawks and firms like Anthropic were crossing over, the company was literally employing Rubio’s own former loyalists to lobby the government bodies he was in charge of. This is on top of the $230,000 that Anthropic paid over the past year’s three quarters to Fierce Government Relations — a firm with a couple of Trump-connected employees — to lobby Congress and the Office of the President on export controls and the NDAA bills; another $180,000 over the same period to Aquia for the same issues; $60,000 to the Democratic firm Avenue Solutions to lobby Congress on the NDAA starting in the third quarter; and the nearly $6 million it spent in-house in that period to lobby on issues that included these topics. In that last case, it used a longtime staffer of Sen. Patty Murray, who had been demanding in recent months that Trump impose these stricter export controls. So in summary, the two closed-source AI firms that view China’s AI industry as a threat to their own bottom line have done the following: lobbied heavily for these China-squeezing export controls this year; appear to have in some cases ramped up their lobbying on this after Rubio circulated the sensational but unproven claim that led to Huang’s booting; hired former staffers from Trumpworld to do that lobbying; and in particular, hired lobbying firms and used lobbyists with long-standing connections to Rubio specifically to do it. The picture that emerges is of the unmistakable — some might say convenient — convergence of interests between Washington’s China hawks and AI giants, and the downright eerie overlap between the offices and personnel of what are in theory meant to be two different entities. Even more convenient, all of this has come to a head just as one of these AI giants, Anthropic, was set to embark on its initial public offering, which it did yesterday, raising $81 billion in the largest stock-market debut on record. Like I said: convenient. A Bet Without Consent Less convenient is another thing we can say for sure, which is that, Anthropic’s blockbuster IPO aside, this entire saga is causing heart rates to spike. That’s because Nvidia — and, owing to his domination of the company and personal role in every deal it struck, Huang himself — is a load-bearing force for an industry straining to stay standing up, passing out the necessary capital for flagship AI firms, start-ups, the building of data centers, and the neo-cloud firms that provide the computing power for firms that can’t build their own. In fact, Nvidia effectively came to the rescue of one of those neo-clouds, CoreWeave, when its bonds recently entered distressed territory after skeptical investors started dumping the company’s debt en masse. Huang’s ouster throws the industry into uncertainty as it waits to see, first, if any one person is able to actually fill Huang’s shoes, and second, if the person who does has the same kind of appetite for risk that he did. So far, indications are that the interim CEO does not , and with the firm about to be dragged through an acre of thorns in the form of congressional hearings and other scrutiny, it would not be surprising if it embarks on a more conservative approach going forward. That doesn’t necessarily mean that the AI industry is headed toward an imminent crash. But this is all spectacularly bad timing given the spate of setbacks and growing investor doubts it’s been weathering, the last one being an incoming US Congress dominated by politicians who ran and won on restricting the data centers that AI firms need to keep being built to stay financially viable. At this point, you might stop to marvel at the absurdity that — because of the way we’ve structured our economy, the way it’s intertwined with US foreign policy, and the corrupting distortion of the Washington revolving door and influence-peddling — one man’s horniness threatens to bring down the US economy and ramp up conflict between two nuclear-armed powers. It’s just one of several layers of absurdity to a situation and industry that, without ever being consulted on it, we have all as a society been roped into staking our collective future on. Huang is not the first or last CEO who will have a personal scandal that can be exploited by shadowy forces with their own ulterior motives. But because of the nature of the AI industry, whichever executive draws embarrassing headlines next, it’s gossip that we’re all going to have to care deeply about. Billionaires Always Want to Maintain Control

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Sophie Bandarkar ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-20T17:57:57+00:00

In October, Meta released a filing saying that Mark Zuckerberg had threatened to leave California in anticipation of Proposition 40 , the billionaire wealth tax on the 2026 state ballot, which was defeated 53 to 47 percent. According to the document, Zuckerberg told the board he was “evaluating relocating his primary residence outside California,” and that any steps toward doing so, including listing his residential real estate on the market, “would be public and inevitably noted and commented upon by company employees.” In response, the board quickly mobilized to negotiate a deal: Meta agreed to issue a new class of nonvoting (“Class C”) stock, which will be distributed to all shareholders in January 2027. This would have allowed Zuckerberg to sell his shares to convert a portion of his wealth into the billions in cash he would need to meet his potential tax liability, without losing his near-supermajority voting share. In exchange, he agreed to stay in the state for at least eight years and keep his $1 annual salary. Conveniently, the new stock will also allow Meta to raise capital for artificial intelligence investments without changing the decision-making composition of the company. In the fallout of the proposition’s defeat, it’s useful to reflect on what the Meta deal can help us understand for the long-term movement to tax the ultrarich’s wealth. What Proposition 40 Was Prop 40 was designed as a onetime 5 percent tax, payable over five years, on the net worth of California residents with over $1 billion who lived in the state as of January 1, 2026. The proposition was written by legal and economics faculty and introduced by the health care workers’ union Service Employees International Union-United Healthcare Workers (SEIU-UHW). The goal of the tax was to raise around $100 billion, which would offset roughly 90 percent of the federal budget cuts to health programs, including Medi-Cal, since the beginning of the second Trump administration — and in particular, since the passing of the 2025 “Big Beautiful Bill,” which takes effect this January. According to research by economists Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman, California’s billionaire wealth reached around $2.3 trillion by mid-2026, which is roughly equal to the total income reported by every Californian in a year. This wealth is both extremely concentrated and recent: it’s increased by 144 percent in just three years. The approximately 250 billionaires who would qualify only pay about 0.2 percent of their wealth in California income tax each year, representing 2.4 percent of total California income tax revenue. The four richest billionaires, Larry Page, Sergey Brin, Mark Zuckerberg, and Jensen Huang, pay only 0.07 percent of their wealth. If Proposition 40 had passed, it would have been the first tax of its kind and the culmination of decades of advocacy by policymakers and researchers across the world working to expose how dramatically economic inequality has grown and the degree of billionaires’ tax avoidance. This is particularly salient in California, the fourth-largest economy in the world and home to fifty-eight of the Fortune 500 companies. Companies in the Bay Area alone represent 35 percent of the market cap of the world’s top hundred companies. This makes the state an ideal place to experiment with taxing the ultrawealthy. Why It Failed The initiative failed for myriad, well-documented reasons. Primarily, it faced a concerted and incredibly well-funded opposition campaign. Its opponents, mostly ultrawealthy donors, spent $378.4 million against the measure (compared to the yes campaign’s budget of $33.5 million). Google cofounder and Donald Trump supporter Sergey Brin alone donated $102 million, and also funded two additional Propositions (41 and 42) designed to block the tax if it passed. California Governor Gavin Newsom opposed the measure and endorsed Proposition 42, stating , “Wealth is movable, and it shops for the state with the lowest taxes. The fight belongs at the federal level.” According to Rigel Robinson, former Berkeley city councilmember and Democratic public policy consultant, It would be a mistake to see the electoral outcome of Proposition 40 as a clear rejection of its goal from voters. There was the enormous and well-funded opposition campaign, and competing and confusing ballot measures designed to nullify the measure. Proposition 42, which was put on the ballot to prevent Proposition 40 (and future versions of it) from taking effect even if it did pass, was sold to voters as a protection for retirement savings. However, in California and across the country, candidates won on a message of seizing power from billionaires and championing affordability. In many ways, this is the sort of message Bernie Sanders tried to bring to the Democratic agenda a decade ago. Voters across the country have demonstrated that redistribution and economic justice are clear priorities, and we cannot let the outcome of this proposition diminish the ground won. There were also concerns about the legality and mechanics of the legislation — in particular, that it was introduced as a onetime proposition, was not national, and was technically a retroactive tax, since it applied to anyone living in the state as of January 2026. And as Fred Glass explained in Jacobin , California’s labor and progressive forces were unable to form a unified front in support of the measure. While disappointing, it’s helpful to historicize the initiative and the broader movement to tax the assets of the ultrawealthy alongside other fights for redistribution. The federal income tax, for example, took several attempts before successfully passing. It took the Sixteenth Amendment passing in 1913 to make it permanent. SEIU-UHW has already said it plans to try again in 2028, and Bernie Sanders and Ro Khanna have introduced a bill at the federal level this spring. Billionaire Wealth Is Money Today From the Meta deal, we can learn two things. First, it is possible for tech leadership to convert their wealth into liquidity. One of the most persistent arguments against taxing the ultrawealthy is that their money isn’t “real,” since it’s mostly in shares of companies whose value rises and falls with the market. Because of this, it shouldn’t be treated as income. Stocks are legal claims on future production whose prices are set by expected returns. It is not uncommon for the companies who issue them to have not yet produced anything profitable or concrete. SpaceX’s shares were the most valuable in history at the time of their initial public offering (IPO), even though its AI division had lost $2 for every dollar of revenue it earned in 2025. In other words, a stock is a title of ownership to a slice of the surplus value a company will produce. In the Marxian sense , this is an example of fictitious capital and inherently speculative. However, “speculative” does not mean unreal. As Dimitris P. Sotiropoulos, John Milios, and Spyros Lapatsioras write in A Political Economy of Contemporary Capitalism and its Crisis: Demystifying Finance , fictitious capital is not detached from real production but a necessary phase within its totality, and claims on future income can become commodities in their own right. These can be traded, priced, and converted into money at any time. A stock is not just a promise of future wealth; it is wealth (and command over the labor of others) today. Modern finance for the AI sector also has a remarkable capacity to innovate and reinvent itself, and the financial engineering to create the Class C share is another demonstration of the sector’s remarkable capacity to adapt to secure endless capital for the AI rush. Another Front of Contestation Second, tech executives are most concerned with losing control of their companies. As economist and tax expert Gabriel Zucman told me, “the existence of super-voting shares and the Zuckerberg and Proposition 40 episode more generally highlight that what matters most to billionaires, even more than their wealth, is their power to exert control over the giant businesses they own. This is indeed their greatest power.” He also commented on X that the opposition to the wealth tax could not be driven by concerns about losing money, since the onetime 5 percent liability would “merely bring billionaire’s fortunes back to where they stood a few weeks ago, at the beginning of September.” Meta is not an isolated case, and a dual-class share system (which allows companies to sell shares to the public, while a small group keeps most of the votes) has been on the rise. When SpaceX went public, Elon Musk floated only 3–4 percent of the company and retained 42 percent of its shares, but 85 percent of its voting share through his “super-voting stock.” Anthropic’s IPO includes five types of stock, which specifically gives the cofounders the ability to keep a say over the controlling vote even if they give away 98 percent of their shares. The most valuable companies in the world are increasingly owned by a handful of individuals insulated from markets, shareholders, and other public mechanisms of accountability. As Fred Glass wrote , Proposition 40 is a lost battle, not a lost war. Amid the manufactured panic over the tax, Zuckerberg seized the political moment to negotiate a deal with his company. The outcome of their agreement deepens our understanding of how to anticipate and oppose the coordinated class response against taxing extreme wealth. First, the shares of future value held by tech billionaires are not just abstractions but real money today, necessary to the current cycle of reproduction. And second, billionaires will fight hardest to protect not their fortune but their control. The Left’s political project has to include a new front of contestation beyond fighting for shares of the returns tech companies produce. Countering the enclosure of the AI economy will require reclaiming democratic control over the infrastructures of this intelligence in the first place. Xi, Putin, and Trump meeting concludes with vague commitments, but strong symbolism

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Abdul Rahman ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-20T23:33:53+00:00

Chinese President Xi Jinping held the first ever trilateral summit of Russia, China, and the US on the sidelines of the ongoing Asia-Pacific Economic Council (APEC) summit in Shenzhen on Thursday, November 19. The meeting, attended by Russian President Vladimir Putin and US President Donald Trump, ended with a joint Declaration towards Peace with all three countries agreeing to find ways to work together on common issues including the need for reforms in the UN, global energy security, and rising nuclear risk. The declaration also talked about the principles of the operation of the International Criminal Court (ICC) and underlined the immunity for top state officials. The declaration emphasized that the reforms in the UN are necessary for it to make effective interventions to issues of global concern and to fulfill its mandate of preserving global peace and security. The point was an indirect reflection on recent failures of the UN to make any meaningful intervention in the global conflicts particularly its failure to prevent the Israel’s genocide in Gaza, the war in Ukraine and the US-Israeli war on Iran. All three countries also agreed that they should work together to reduce the risks of a nuclear war breaking out. They underlined the need to conduct dialogue for reducing nuclear risks and preventing the proliferation of nuclear weapons. The three heads of states also agreed it was necessary to maintain the global flow of energy and to minimize the disruptions to global energy supply chains even during conflict and to increase the production and supply of refined petroleum to ease constraints on general production and prices. Thycydides trap is not destiny During his speech in the summit, hinting towards recent conflicts, Xi pointed out that there must be peace among major countries in the world in order to maintain global stability and to avoid chaos. Asking for a more friendly and trust based relations between the great power Xi denounced the conventional notions of great power rivalry repeating that the so-called “Thucydides Trap is by no means a destiny.” Thycydides, an ancient Greek historian, is often quoted to describe conflicts and rivalries between a rising power and an established one. As major countries and the permanent members of the UN Security Council China, Russia and the US must work together to meet global challenges and jointly uphold world peace and stability. They together can make greater contributions to the progress of humanity, Xi argued. Xi recalled how China, Russia and the US have worked together in the past to save the world from fascism during the Second World War claiming the same coordination is required now to “steadfastly safeguard the postwar international order, uphold the purposes and principles of the UN charter, and defend the hard-won outcomes.” Xi proposed a four point code of conduct for the great powers to work together; mutual respect, peaceful coexistence, win-win cooperation and dialogue and consultation. For working together it is necessary that all the countries respect each other’s social structure and distinctive development paths adopted without trying to impose each other’s preferences over the other, Xi underlined. He also emphasized the UN principles of respecting each others’ sovereignty, territorial integrity and non-interference in domestic affairs as fundamental for good relations. “On major sensitive issues concerning one another, it is important to communicate in advance, try to see things from one another’s perspective, act with caution, and in particular, not to challenge the red lines of one another,” Xinhua reported Xi saying. Pointing towards the rising risks of nuclear conflicts and its possible impact on the existence of humanity, Xi talked about the need to push for complete nuclear disarmament. “Nuclear weapons, the Sword of Domcleas that hangs over humanity, should be completely prohibited and thoroughly destroyed over time to make the world free of nuclear weapons,” the Chinese leader said. Greater communication and coordination Indirectly referring to the impacts of US policies on global trade, Xi said that “decoupling and disruption of industrial and supply chains are not the ways to go” calling for stronger communication and coordination to “safeguard the security and stability of the global” markets. On November 18, 2026, acting President of Venezuela, Delcy Rodriguez, presided over the signing by US Secretary of War Pete Hegseth and Venezuelan Minister of Defense Gustavo González López of a military cooperation agreement in Caracas. Speaking from the US Embassy in Caracas, Hegseth arrogantly stated that Venezuela “has gone from our most aggressive regional threat to a compliant security partner.” Rubbing salt in the wound, Hegseth boasted that his trip was only “made possible” by the January 3rd operation that kidnapped President Nicolás Maduro and Cilia Flores, and left 32 Cubans and over 100 Venezuelans dead. For Washington, it marks the return of a military relationship that had been interrupted for twenty-one years by the Bolivarian Revolution. For Venezuela, it represents a definitive close to the “anti-imperialist phase” articulated by Hugo Chávez in 2004-2005, and a de facto alignment with Trump’s project to renew total US control over Latin America and the Caribbean. All countries of the region now have military agreements with Washington, with the exception of Cuba and Nicaragua. The significance of the signing cannot be understood without the historical record that preceded it. The function of US military cooperation US military cooperation with Venezuela has never been a neutral technical exchange. US military training shaped the officer corps that carried out state repression against Venezuela’s left for decades. During the 1950s, Washington backed the dictatorship of Marcos Pérez Jiménez, whose government was notorious for its brutality and whose security forces were trained and equipped by the United States. Later, the US Army’s School of the Americas trained thousands of Latin American officers, including Venezuelans, in counterinsurgency tactics, including interrogation, torture, and summary execution, and since the 1960s, the successive governments of the corrupt 4th Republic had been deploying those methods against the Communist Party and other left organizations, with the national police agency DIGEPOL responsible for extensive human rights abuses. That same institutional lineage was on display in the February 1989 Caracazo, a mass popular uprising against IMF austerity measures, when the US-trained military was ordered to restore order, and hundreds of civilians were killed; as Chávez, himself a military officer, later said, the massacre “created consciousness among many military personnel, especially the younger ones,” and the officers who witnessed and carried out the repression were products of a system long tutored by the United States. The Caracazo became the breaking point that convinced Chávez and his co-conspirators in the MBR-200 movement that they “could not continue to defend a murderous regime,” setting in motion the February 4, 1992 rebellion. Declassified Pentagon training manuals endorsed torture, extortion, and execution, and several Venezuelan generals who later participated in the 2002 coup against Chávez were graduates of the School of the Americas. This was not an aberration but a consistent feature of a security relationship designed to align Latin American militaries with Washington’s Cold War in a counterinsurgency strategy in which close to 200,000 people are estimated to have been forcibly disappeared for their left-wing political activism. The most personal and politically charged example of this history is the 1976 torture and murder of Jorge Antonio Rodríguez, father of Delcy Rodríguez. Rodríguez, a socialist activist and leader of the Socialist League, was detained by Venezuela’s DISIP intelligence service and died in custody after what his family and numerous accounts describe as torture.
