America’s Wealth Inequality Crisis: Why Tax Cuts Won’t Fix the Economy

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Charles D. Hayes ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-05T23:28:59+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. We have a monumental math problem in America, and what is broken cannot be repaired by tinkering around the edges. The Jurassic Republicans were right about one thing: There is no free lunch. But they were mistaken in believing there is such a thing as a truly free market. Every economic transaction depends on infrastructure, education, public safety, environmental protection, and countless other necessities that markets cannot simply conjure out of thin air. Yet the prices we pay for goods and services seldom reflect their full costs to society. The Hidden Costs of the “Free Market” Environmental degradation, pollution, public health consequences, and the depletion of natural resources are often excluded from the price tag, leaving society and future generations to pay for damages that private transactions fail to account for. Economists call these negative externalities , but in plain English, they amount to shifting costs onto people who had no say in creating them. Someone pays for all of it. The question is who. Beginning roughly half a century ago, the political influence of concentrated wealth helped transform taxation into something to be feared rather than understood as the price of maintaining civilization. The wealthy became indispensable job creators, progressive taxation became an assault on success, and government became the enemy. National Debt and the Cost of Neglect The results deserve examination. We have accumulated a national debt exceeding $40 trillion, with annual interest costs around a trillion dollars. The condition of our airports, roads, bridges, and electrical infrastructure on the world stage is a global embarrassment. Wars, recessions, emergencies, spending decisions, and tax reductions all contributed. But repeatedly reducing revenue without corresponding reductions in obligations has undeniably compounded the problem. For decades, politicians have promised Americans the benefits of civilization without adequately paying for them. Now the bill is coming due. A country cannot indefinitely finance its obligations through borrowing while allowing an ever-growing concentration of wealth to escape proportionate taxation. Nor can it sustain a prosperous middle class while permitting the rewards of economic growth to flow disproportionately toward those already wealthy. RAND’s $79 Trillion Finding: What the Bottom 90 Percent Lost $79 trillion. That’s the additional income a 2025 RAND Corporation working paper estimates the bottom 90 percent of American earners would have received between 1975 and 2023 had they continued to receive their 1975 share of income. That cumulative figure is expressed in inflation-adjusted 2023 dollars. For 2023 alone, the estimated shortfall was $3.9 trillion. After sleeping on this revelation, it occurs to me that if the annual shortfall remained at that level for the next three years, the cumulative total would reach nearly $91 trillion by the end of 2026, still measured in 2023 dollars. We cannot tax-cut our way out of this predicament. We cannot deregulate our way out of it. And we certainly cannot trickle down our way out of it. The arithmetic is merciless. We must raise sufficient revenue to meet our obligations, and that means restoring meaningful progressive taxation, particularly on the enormous incomes and accumulated wealth at the top. Right-wing capitalists can huff and puff like the Big Bad Wolf, threatening to blow down the Three Little Pigs’ house of straw, but no amount of bluster about trickle-down economics is going to solve our problems. What is truly amazing, though, is how many times we have fallen for the same empty promises, despite decades of evidence that the promised prosperity has failed to trickle down to those who need it most. Restoring Progressive Taxation to Rebuild the Middle Class A recent study by the RAND Corporation estimates that the bottom 90 percent of American income earners would have received an additional $79 trillion between 1975 and 2023 had they continued to receive the same share of national income they received in 1975. To my mind, that represents an extraordinary transfer of income into the upper echelons of American society. Calling efforts to reverse that trend “socialism” does nothing to explain or justify what happened. The real irony is that we watched this enormous redistribution of wealth unfold in slow motion, scarcely recognizing what was happening until the damage had already been done. Now we are staring at a national debt of $40 trillion, and I repeat, ad nauseam , that the only way to right the ship of state is to restore progressive tax rates similar to those that helped create and sustain the American middle class in the first place. There is no ideological escape from mathematics. No magic formulas. A civilization that refuses to pay for its own maintenance is ultimately choosing its own deterioration. And no amount of political rhetoric can repeal the fundamental reality that what we refuse to invest in today will become a far greater burden on the generations who follow.

The UAW Bet on Abdul El-Sayed and Won

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Alex N. Press ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-05T21:58:37+00:00

Three Rivers, Michigan, is a factory town in a county where Donald Trump won 66.2 percent of the vote in 2024. Its biggest employer is American Axle, whose workers belong to United Auto Workers (UAW) Local 2093. In 2008, the company demanded major concessions, arguing that it needed lower labor costs to remain competitive and keep the plant open. Workers spent nearly three months on strike before accepting a contract that halved some workers’ pay, from $29 an hour to $14.50. The plant survived, but by this spring, production workers still topped out at only $22 an hour. Workers spent years trying to recover what they had lost. Yet when members voted to authorize a strike in 2021, the international union did not support them walking out, according to Rosemary Dodge, a machine operator and trustee of Local 2093. Members were angry at the company and at the union leaders who wouldn’t back a strike. “People’s attitudes toward our international union were very negative,” she told Jacobin . This year, Dodge took time away from the plant to campaign for Abdul El-Sayed in his run for Senate. Her local had struck with the international’s backing in June, and El-Sayed had spent hours on their picket line. “I had not seen that kind of solidarity in our union up until that point,” she said of the contract fight. The UAW, Michigan’s largest union, counts more than 350,000 members , active and retired, in the state. It backed the Medicare for All advocate in a primary where Governor Gretchen Whitmer and retiring Senator Gary Peters supported Haley Stevens, El-Sayed’s opponent, and other major unions stayed neutral or backed Stevens . El-Sayed’s campaign called the UAW’s backing “critical” to his 1 point primary victory. Dodge, sixty-four, works on the paint line and has been at American Axle for almost eleven years. “The starting wage was not enough for people to both have a place to live and have a vehicle to get to work,” she told Jacobin , describing what coworkers faced before this year’s contract fight. “There were people that were either walking or riding bikes to work, or people that were living in their cars.” But by the time Local 2093 prepared to bargain again this year, things had changed inside the international union. Two former UAW presidents were among the officials convicted in a federal corruption investigation. Members subsequently won direct elections of their top officers and elected Shawn Fain president in 2023. That fall, the UAW struck all three major US automakers. In the lead-up to the 2026 contract fight at American Axle, the local bargaining committee had spent a year preparing, including tracking parts shipments and the company’s efforts to build inventory. Members began “ working to rule ” to resist management’s stockpiling efforts, and hundreds attended the local’s meetings. “We had a really strong bargaining committee that was very well prepared, and when the international saw that, it was on board and helping us,” Dodge said. “Since we’ve had the change at the top of the international with Shawn Fain and one member, one vote, the international leadership and its willingness to back us changed a lot of people’s minds.” Fain and Michigan Lieutenant Governor Garlin Gilchrist attended a rally to kick off contract negotiations at Axle, she recalled. “We were like, ‘All right, the lieutenant governor’s here, and we’ve got the president of the international here.’” Members could see that people had their backs, she said. “That made a big difference in people’s attitudes.” On June 1, nearly one thousand American Axle workers walked off the job. They wanted a $30 hourly rate by 2030, no increases in out-of-pocket health care costs, and stronger protections for their personal time . Four days later, the UAW endorsed El-Sayed’s primary campaign for Michigan’s open US Senate seat. The candidate joined Local 2093 members on the picket line that day. “Abdul was the one who spent hours on our line, talking to the people of our union, walking with us, while there were so many other politicians who came and showed up just for a photo op,” Dodge said. “We are going to make sure that they hear you nationwide,” El-Sayed told the strikers at the time, pointing to the concessions workers had made to keep the plant open and accusing the company of failing to share the gains from their work. In mid-June, Axle’s workers ratified a contract with a $30 top rate by 2030, with additional paid time off and no concessions on existing health care costs. Those hired before May 31, 2012, received an immediate $8 hourly increase. “[El-Sayed] genuinely cared about how we were doing, and he was one of the first ones to call and congratulate us when we got a great contract,” Dodge said. In a September Abdul for Senate television ad , Local 2093 Bargaining Chair Josh Jager contrasted his more than twenty years on the line with Rogers’s political career. “Mike Rogers has been in office for over twenty years — not good years,” he said. “Every day, I show up on the line and build stuff. Every day, Mike shows up for his corporate donors.” Dodge joined the UAW’s political program before the August primary, taking release time from the plant to knock doors, make calls, and rally her fellow members to back the progressive challenger. Despite the barrage of attacks on El-Sayed — including from Trump, whom some members had supported — Dodge said the majority of the union households whose doors she knocked on were polite even when they disagreed (though one did call them communist sympathizers and order them off his property). “I believe that Abdul is someone who does respect working people, and that’s what earned my respect for him,” she said. “We were pleasantly surprised when we started at how many people were really receptive to hearing the information we had to share about Abdul,” Dodge said. El-Sayed won both of the city’s precincts. Unofficial returns show him receiving 1,318 votes to Republican Mike Rogers’s 1,060, or 53.6 percent to 43.1 percent. Two years earlier, Rogers beat UAW-endorsed Democrat Elissa Slotkin in the city, while Trump beat Kamala Harris. Choosing Their Candidate The union’s endorsement of El-Sayed in the primary mentioned his stance on Medicare for All, banning stock buybacks, and May Day 2028. Its Big Three contracts expire April 30 that year, allowing a strike on May Day; the UAW has urged other unions to align their contracts so they can strike together. The endorsement decision came through Michigan’s member-elected Community Action Program (CAP) board, following candidate forums and member surveys . Endorsement required a two-thirds majority of the board, where a plant representative’s vote carried the same weight as that of the international president. Three Democrats — Stevens, El-Sayed, and Mallory McMorrow — participated in a candidate forum at the UAW’s national CAP conference in Washington in February. Fain said the union had invited Rogers too, but the Republican candidate “didn’t even bother to respond.” The union invited the candidates to another forum in Detroit. “Again, Rogers didn’t show up, didn’t bother to respond,” Fain recounted. Members quickly warmed to El-Sayed, who secured the endorsement on the board’s first vote. “When he talks to people, they understand who he is, and they understand that he cares about working-class issues,” Fain said. “Our members really drove this decision, and that’s what I’m really proud of.” Back in Three Rivers, rising costs were eating into the contract’s hard-won gains, leaving members open to what El-Sayed had to say. “People were frustrated by that,” Dodge said. “They said we worked so hard to get this better contract, and it’s not really showing. I think they were willing to hear other options.” At a Wednesday press conference after El-Sayed defeated Republican Mike Rogers, Fain recalled hearing from Three Rivers members at the union’s June convention. “There were so many people coming up to me from Three Rivers, Michigan, saying, ‘This guy speaks my language. He understands me,’” Fain said. “This was an area where they were Trump supporters in ’24.” To turn that support into votes, fifty Michigan UAW members left their workplaces to serve as full-time political organizers, the union’s “power squad.” Fain put the program’s totals at more than half a million calls made and more than one hundred thousand doors knocked. The political program emphasized shop-floor conversations, with coworkers talking about wages, health care, retirement, and time off. Those conversations were also meant to get more members organizing inside their plants ahead of a possible strike in 2028. James “Jay Jay” Jewell has worked at Ford’s Sterling Axle plant for fourteen years and is a district committeeman in Local 228. He began working full-time on the election in early September. As a recruitment lead, he made calls and organized one-on-one to get members to volunteer. “This was very structured,” he said. “This is very intentional work happening.” “Money out of politics; money in your pockets; Medicare for All,” Jewell said of El-Sayed’s appeal, echoing the now-senator-elect’s key campaign slogan. “We have a lot of faith that the political structure and system in this country might be ready to turn around if regular working people have it in their hands. It’ll take time and work and more organizing, but the tools are there now.” “It was far from lukewarm,” he said of volunteer recruitment among his fellow UAW members. “It was amazing how many people were raising their hands to do it, how many people were picking up the phone when I was calling.” Jewell lives and works in Macomb County, where Trump won by nearly 14 points in 2024 and Rogers beat Slotkin 53.3–43.6 percent. This year, unofficial returns there put El-Sayed just 7,526 votes behind Rogers, fewer than 2 percentage points. Jewell had seen how a contract win could change members’ expectations of their union. For him, talking with conservative members about the election meant pointing to those gains. “When we have progress and wins, we just have to name it and make it clear and simple,” he said. The 2023 Ford agreement had eliminated wage tiers at Sterling Axle and Rawsonville, bringing substantial raises for workers on the lower wage scales. Jewell himself had benefited from the union’s more aggressive bargaining. “My plant got rid of two tiers, so more than half my plant got a $12 raise overnight,” he said. “Made whole, including myself.” “It’s no coincidence,” he said of the union’s bargaining and electoral victories. “They’re going to feed off each other: we get policy and legislation out of the people that got elected.” Better contracts, in turn, build confidence in the leadership and in collective solidarity. “It’s going to be self-perpetuating if we do this right.” Building Power for 2028 “What we win at the bargaining table can be taken away in the halls of power the next day,” Fain said Wednesday. “The days of us blindly endorsing people because of party affiliation — those days are way behind us.” The UAW put money behind that choice, with its V-CAP political action committee giving Fighting for Michigan PAC, an independent expenditure group backing El-Sayed, $300,000 in July and another $600,000 by October . The union still gave to Senate Majority PAC, but it broke with Democratic Party leaders in Michigan’s primary and put its money and members behind El-Sayed. “We’re going to target races. We’re going to target people. We’re going to put our resources behind candidates that want to carry that agenda and want to stay with working-class people,” Fain said. “And I don’t care if you’re Democrat, Republican, independent. If you’re not with us, we’re not going to be there for you.” Fain described the political program as part of the changes he had run to make inside the union. “I was very frustrated with where we were politically: how we approached politics, how we approached bargaining, how we approached organizing, pretty much every aspect of our union. That’s why I ran for president,” he said Wednesday. “Right out the gate, it was about Big Three bargaining, going into new organizing, winning at places like Volkswagen, and the next stage of that is controlling our politics.” El-Sayed won amid a broader Democratic sweep in Michigan. Unofficial returns showed El-Sayed led Rogers 52.5 to 45.5 percent as of November 5. Slotkin had beaten Rogers by just 19,006 votes (0.3 percent) two years earlier. Democrats also won back the state house, giving them control of both legislative chambers and the governor’s office. El-Sayed ran about 2 points behind Michigan Secretary of State Jocelyn Benson, who received 54.5 percent in the governor’s race. His campaign faced repeated Islamophobic attacks, with Trump labeling him a jihadist and advertisements falsely suggesting he had ties to terrorist organizations. He will be the first Muslim elected to the US Senate. “Corporate Democrats are as big of a problem as any right-wing Republican,” Fain said when asked whether the election results nationwide vindicated the party’s moderates or progressives. “I do believe that the progressive people in the party are speaking more the language that resonates with working-class people.” He warned Democrats against assuming the results showed confidence in the party. “They’re voting Democrat because they’re pissed off, because they’re frustrated with where they are in life right now, and they can’t afford to live,” he said. “If the Democrats do not heed that warning and come out with a platform and go to work to fix the problems in this country,” he warned, “they’ll be voting the other damn way.” Jewell said the objections he heard while canvassing concerned El-Sayed’s race and religion; there was little disagreement over his platform. “I did not have a single member in two months who really wanted to argue against anything that he wanted to deliver,” he said. In Three Rivers, Dodge did hear concerns about Medicare for All from some members who had just fought to preserve their insurance. But she supported El-Sayed’s health care politics. Even with union coverage, she said, a catastrophic illness could leave a worker in debt. Tielece Perry, a thirty-year worker at Detroit Assembly Complex–Jefferson and a member of Local 7, chairs the union’s Region 1 CAP Council. She said conversations with coworkers often concerned their parents and the cost of their care. “We’re raising our kids, but we’re also taking care of our elderly parents, and we still have to work full time,” she said. “So when someone like that has to make a choice between whether they’re going to have medication or groceries, that’s a real challenge.” Asked what she wanted El-Sayed to do in office, Perry said, “We’re going to hold him accountable. I would like to see him be successful with Medicare for All.” “He understands that when you work hard, you want to have a life outside of work,” Dodge said. At American Axle, she said, departments were still being placed on “critical status,” allowing management to mandate up to seven days of work a week. “We tried to get that language improved in our contract; it’s not as strong as we wanted it to be.” El-Sayed had included “the freedom to spend time with your family” among the promises of his campaign. “These principles aren’t radical,” he said . “They’re the baseline for a more equitable Michigan and America.” When Michigan’s new legislature convenes in January, passing unemployment insurance for striking workers will be among the UAW’s priorities ahead of its 2028 contract fight, Fain told reporters Wednesday. “We plan to carry the power that we built in 2026 into our next major fight, which is the 2028 Big Three contract negotiations,” Fain said. “We don’t do this much organizing and winning to get unemployment for striking workers and that’s it. No, we need a whole revamp of the system,” Jewell said. His priorities included the Protecting the Right to Organize (PRO) Act , which would make employers pay fines for breaking labor law and prevent them from indefinitely stalling negotiations for a first contract, and finding a way to undo Citizens United . Asked by Jacobin whether he saw a path to passing the PRO Act or similar legislation, Fain stressed the need for stronger enforcement of workers’ rights. “We would love to see the PRO Act pass,” he said. “They fire people. They threaten people. They intimidate people,” he said of employers. “And there’s zero repercussions for corporate America when they do this.” Jewell expected the candidates the UAW had helped elect to strengthen workers’ rights and curb corporate influence in politics. “I need them to go be aggressive as f — ,” he said. “They already know what we need. It’s not a secret.” In his election night victory speech , El-Sayed mentioned Local 2093 bargaining chair Josh Jager and the strike he and his fellow members had waged at American Axle. “And they won that fight,” he said. “His hope?” the senator-elect continued, referring to Jager. “An America where every single worker gets the right to a union.” This article originally appeared in Jacobin , a democratic socialist magazine publishing long-form essays and analysis on politics, economics, and culture. The Democratic Coalition Still Has a Class Problem

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Jared Abbott ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-05T19:58:07+00:00

Democrats won a massive victory in the 2026 midterms, performing so well that Republicans’ best-laid gerrymandering plans actually backfired in a number of states, most notably Texas and North Carolina . There, Democrats held most of the seats Republicans had targeted and even flipped some districts drawn to be safely Republican. Democrats flipped Senate seats in places like Texas, Kansas, and South Carolina that were considered virtually unimaginable reaches in the very recent past. That’s of course very good for the party, and, I would hasten to add, for our fight against the ever-present threat of the far right. But Democrats’ stunning upset also brings a strong temptation for moderates and progressives alike: to read the result as proof that the party has finally turned the corner with working-class voters and can ride carefree into the 2028 sunset. But provisional data suggest otherwise. Unless noted, the figures in this piece come from the AP Voter Poll, a large survey of voters conducted for the Associated Press around Election Day, and from its predecessor, AP VoteCast, for earlier elections. We won’t know the true shape of the 2026 electorate for months, until validated voter data becomes available, but the AP poll gives us a helpful initial look. It shows Democrats gaining real ground since 2024 with college graduates and voters without degrees alike. But measured against 2018, the last Democratic midterm wave, the party is still deep in the hole with working-class voters, and its coalition is more lopsided by class than ever. That leaves Democrats exposed to another 2024-style loss in 2028. It also keeps out of reach the broad working-class supermajority progressives would need to deliver real change for working Americans. It’s the (Trump) Economy, Stupid Few doubted heading into November that Democrats would take the House of Representatives. The president’s party has lost the national House vote in each of the last five midterms. This year followed a similar script as past midterms, but it was amplified by several additional factors. First, Donald Trump is deeply unpopular. Only 38 percent of 2026 voters approved of his job performance, and 75 percent said the economy was in poor or not-so-good shape. Some 43 percent said they voted to express opposition to Trump, against 16 percent who voted to support him. The second factor follows from the first. Trump’s unpopularity reached into his own coalition, especially the less partisan, less habitual voters who backed him in 2024 because they were pissed off with the Democrats on affordability and immigration. With the economy still souring and Trump himself not on the ballot (despite his many exhortations that voters imagine he was), however, many saw little reason to turn out for Republicans. As a result, according to a rough estimate from the AP data, depressed turnout among Trump supporters alone was worth around 5 points to Democrats. Third, Democrats now have a stronger midterm coalition than Republicans. For most of the twentieth century, Democrats depended on working-class voters who turned out less reliably, so low-turnout midterms tended to favor Republicans. That pattern has flipped. Democrats now draw more of their support from college graduates and other highly engaged voters who tend to show up even in off years. Republicans, meanwhile, rely increasingly on less engaged, lower-propensity voters, many without degrees, who turn out mainly when Trump is a candidate. The upshot is that the lower the turnout, the better Democrats now tend to do. The danger for Democrats is that these advantages are temporary and unlikely to reappear in 2028 when Trump will no longer be the Republicans’ national standard-bearer, and many of the low-propensity conservative voters who sat out this year will flock back to the polls. The Class Divide Grew in 2026 The divide between college and noncollege voters grew again in 2026, even as Democrats gained among both groups. Democrats’ margin among college graduates jumped 12 points from Kamala Harris’s 2024 showing, while their margin among noncollege voters improved by about 8 points and remained negative. If we compare 2026 with 2018, the last time Democrats rode a midterm backlash against Trump, we see that while the party’s national margin was 2 points better in 2026, Democrats actually won voters without college degrees by 3.5 points in 2018, whereas this year they lost noncollege voters by about 5 points. Meanwhile, Democrats’ margin among college graduates grew from 14 points to 26 during the same period. The result is a yawning class gap of over 30 points, up from about 10 points in 2018. That gap matters, because the road to the White House and sustained control of the Senate still runs through voters without college degrees. Noncollege voters remain a majority of the electorate, and in 2020 they made up a majority of voters in every one of the key swing states. With class-based support remaining so lopsided, Democrats remain vulnerable in the key presidential battlegrounds, let alone expanding their reach further into red territory in a durable way that outlasts the political manna Trump delivered to them in 2026. Relying on larger margins among college-educated suburbanites alone is arithmetically insufficient and politically unsustainable . Democrats’ problems with noncollege voters are hardly limited to white voters. Among voters of color without college degrees, Democrats won by 57 points in 2018 but by only 34 in 2022 and 2024. This year’s rebound to 40 points, especially among Latino voters, is real, but it recovers only about a quarter of the ground the party has lost with these voters since 2018. Voters of color without degrees are now less Democratic than voters of color with them, a reversal of the 2018 pattern. Do Democrats Even Need Noncollege Voters After 2026? AP’s data suggest that college graduates supplied most of the national swing toward the Democrats in 2026. Based on my calculation from AP’s published breakdowns of the vote by education, voters with a four-year college degree likely accounted for more than half of the roughly 11-point move toward Democrats from Harris’s 2024 margin to 2026. This invites a tantalizing conclusion: that the party’s future lies mainly with college graduates, and that the noncollege gains are a bonus rather than a necessity. But that idea is clearly belied by the results of the red-state Senate races. In the five red-state Senate upsets, the national pattern did not hold. There, Democrats’ margins among noncollege voters improved by 15 to 24 points over Harris’s 2024 showing, far more than the roughly 8-point gain they made with this group nationally. Had noncollege voters in these states moved only at the national pace, three of the five Senate Democratic hopefuls in red states would have lost, and the other two would have won by less than 2 points. Had college graduates moved only at their national pace, four of the five would still have won. In other words, red-state Democrats did not win by riding the national wave. They won by outrunning it among noncollege voters. Democrats Are Still Unpopular Democrats won a landslide, but not because Americans suddenly warmed to them. In Pew’s polling of all adults, 40 percent of Americans viewed the Democratic Party favorably a month before the election, and 58 percent unfavorably. That is a recovery from a low of 36 percent in August 2025, but it is still well below the 46–47 percent Democrats drew during Trump’s first term. Gallup tells a similar story: 43 percent favorable in September 2026, up from 37 percent a year earlier but below the party’s 49 percent average since 1992. A good share of Democrats’ 2026 margin also came from voters whose support came grudgingly. About 16 percent of voters viewed neither party favorably, and Democrats won them by 25 points, which works out to roughly 4 points of the party’s national margin. Trust in Democrats on the issues is also thin. Asked which party could better handle the economy, 43 percent said Democrats, 38 percent said Republicans, and 19 percent said neither. That is a 6-point edge in a year when three in four voters rated the economy poorly. Republicans still led on immigration, and the parties were tied on taxes. Recent research I conducted with Joan Williams, based on a February 2026 survey, shows how shallow Democrats’ gains on immigration are. Fifty-six percent of working-class respondents agreed that Trump “has gone too far, with masked men arresting ordinary hardworking people instead of just deporting criminals.” Yet only 36 percent preferred Joe Biden’s immigration policies to Trump’s. Many working-class voters are uneasy with how Trump’s policies are carried out, but they still prefer them to Biden’s by nearly two to one. The Dealignment Carousel American politics over the past decade has settled into a self-reinforcing cycle of backlash that virtually guarantees heightened polarization and increasing voter disaffection. The White House has changed parties in three straight elections, and control of the House has now flipped in three consecutive midterms. After each of these elections, the winning party has claimed a mandate to finally address Americans’ growing sense of economic dislocation and political disenchantment, and each time it fails to deliver. This is what dealignment looks like. Neither party has a stable majority coalition, so outcomes swing with the national mood and with which party’s voters turn out. In the current arrangement, that produces a reliable rhythm. In midterms, the electorate is smaller and more educated, which now favors Democrats, and the president’s party is punished, so Democrats win big when a Republican holds the White House. In presidential years, the electorate grows by tens of millions, and the added voters are disproportionately noncollege, less attached to either party, and now lean slightly Republican. But even as each election ping-pongs back and forth between the parties, the education gap has continued to grow from 10 points in 2018 to 27 points in 2024, and now 31 in 2026. The electoral carousel goes up and down, but class dealignment continues apace. That is why even a midterm landslide like this one does little to change the underlying shape of American politics. A wave lifts the party’s fortunes with nearly every group at once, so on election night the gains among college graduates and the modest recovery among working-class voters blurred together into a single triumphant story. But when the tide goes out, Democrats are left with the same lopsided class coalition that has plagued them for more than a decade, and Americans are left in a seemingly endless limbo where neither party can govern for working people. Rather than overreading this year’s victory, Democrats should look closely at where they won on hostile terrain, and how they did it. The red-state Senate races offer the clearest model: as we saw above, James Talarico, Sherrod Brown, Adam Hamilton, Annie Andrews, and Josh Turek won by outrunning the national swing among noncollege voters, not by running up the score in the suburbs. The question for 2028 is whether Democrats can keep making gains like these with noncollege voters through an affirmative economic message that cuts through even without the assistance of a historically unpopular chief executive. There is plenty raw material to work with. Seven in ten voters in the AP Voter Poll said the US economic system favors the wealthy, and those voters backed Democrats by more than 50 points in 2026. That is a ready-made audience for an economic populist message aimed at working-class voters, many of whom agree that the system is rigged but don’t yet trust Democrats to fix it. Democrats again won lower-income voters by more than higher-income voters this year, and union households backed them by 20 points, compared with 8 among nonunion voters. Those are signs that a class-based appeal can still work. But they will not add up to a durable majority unless Democrats also find a way to maintain and expand their margins among voters without college degrees. Trump has handed Democrats a reprieve, not a realignment. They can spend the next two years taking credit for a blue wave delivered largely by outside forces, or they can use it to figure out how to build lasting support among the working-class voters they will need when the wave recedes. A principios de este año, surgió un debate entre miembros de nuestro sindicato, la Unión Nacional de Trabajadores por Aplicación (UNTA), a raíz de un comunicado en redes sociales que condenaba el secuestro por parte de los Estados Unidos, del presidente venezolano Nicolás Maduro y que exigía: “la defensa de la soberanía, la autodeterminación de los pueblos y la paz en América Latina”. TEMAS DE PRIORIDAD Los cuatro ejes son: talleres sobre el Convenio 193 de la OIT; talleres sobre comprensión e interpretación de los Estatutos sindicales de la organización; talleres sobre elementos técnicos para administrar los espacios sindicales; talleres de afiliación/organización. Respecto a los tres ejes restantes del programa de capacitaciones, estos se encuentran íntimamente ligados entre sí. Una persona afiliada que conoce sus Estatutos puede participar activamente en cualquier área del sindicato. Asimismo, es necesario que los actuales representantes del sindicato profundicen en el conocimiento de los Estatutos. De igual manera, para prevenir malentendidos entre las personas afiliadas y los representantes, así como para profesionalizar los procesos internos de la organización, resulta fundamental que todas las personas afiliadas a la UNTA conozcan diversos elementos técnicos para administrar los espacios sindicales. Finalmente, para continuar construyendo poder colectivo, es crucial que los mismos afiliados se consideren organizadores—y podemos fomentar eso mediante talleres de afiliación y organización sindical. El que las personas afiliadas al sindicato tomen la decisión de conectarse para continuar formándose es una muestra del liderazgo y la convicción de las bases. Cabe destacar que, al interior de las capacitaciones, para guiar las discusiones, se dejan cuatro preguntas guía por tema. Al final, cada representación local debe entregar a la Secretaría de Formación y Cultura un escrito breve en el que se responden de manera puntual las preguntas. Con estos ejercicios se ha buscado incentivar la participación y discusión de las bases, fortalecer la habilidad de exposición de los ponentes, y fomentar en los afiliados un sentido de pertenencia y respeto a su sindicato. Por ejemplo, en cuanto al tema de la comprensión de los Estatutos, con las discusiones, las preguntas guía y los documentos escritos por las bases pueden comenzar a plantearse cambios, mejoras o adecuaciones a los mismos. Esto también se hizo durante los talleres del Convenio 193. ABIERTOS AL DESACUERDO A la UNTA siempre le ha preocupado la formación y capacitación constantes por parte de las bases y de los representantes. Entendemos la necesidad de tener conversaciones complejas que pueden llegar a ser incómodas políticamente, porque estar abierto a abordar los desacuerdos hace que el sindicato sea más fuerte. Sin espacios de discusión y análisis se frena el desarrollo de la organización. Somos partidarios de llevar a cabo conversaciones difíciles, pero bajo ciertas reglas que permitan el diálogo y la escucha activa. Por ejemplo, para pensar temas difíciles, hay que plantear preguntas incómodas. El no estar de acuerdo con un tema no quiere decir que seamos enemigos, sino que ilustra la necesidad de pensar en los sindicatos como espacios políticos de personas que tal vez no estén de acuerdo con todo, pero que luchan juntos por metas compartidas. How We Built Our Union’s Member Education Program

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: 不明 ／ 著者: Luis Fernando Mora Reyes ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-05T17:22:34+00:00

Earlier this year, a debate broke out among members of our union, the National App Workers Union of Mexico (UNTA), after the union’s social media post condemning the U.S. government’s kidnapping of Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro and demanding “the defense of sovereignty, self determination and peace in Latin America.” Union members had conflicting views about the kidnapping, which led to a discussion on the subject in our next coordinating assembly—a space where we inform, discuss, and reach agreements between union representatives. Members disagreed intensely, but expressed their views with respect and camaraderie. It made us realize that we needed more spaces like this for face-to-face discussion and analysis within the union, to help us develop a shared politics and grow as trade unionists. Out of these discussions, members decided to create the National Political Council (CPN), a space for debate and training on political, economic, labor, gender, and social issues. Within this space, our office for training and culture proposed to offer a series of workshops, open to all members, meant to accomplish three things: help us think collectively about national and international issues strengthen our skills as union members generate greater discussion within the day-to-day life of the union. Members were invited not just to participate, but also to teach alongside a handful of specialists in their fields. PRIORITY TOPICS The program is structured around four main pillars: the International Labour Organization’s Convention 193, which is the first international labor standard on the platform economy understanding and interpreting our union bylaws technical skills for running a union organizing tactics These were priorities for our union for a number of reasons. In June of this year, our union took part in discussions on developing the ILO’s Convention 193. This Convention is an important tool to help facilitate improvements to Mexico’s existing labor legislation, so we felt it was important for members to understand it. The other three pillars are closely intertwined. A member who knows their union statutes can actively participate in the union—and those who take leadership roles in the union should know these statutes well. To avoid misunderstandings between union members and representatives, and to professionalize our internal processes, it’s important for all UNTA members to understand how the union is actually run. And to continue building collective power, it’s crucial for members to see themselves as organizers. VIRTUAL CONVERSATIONS Given how large and spread out Mexico is, and the nature of app-based work, we decided to run these trainings virtually. Some members join from their phones while they’re working; others take time away from work to engage with the materials. It shows how committed members are to keep growing and developing their skills. So that workers can fit these workshops into their lives, they are held every three weeks. We’ve recently finished the first section (on Convention 193) and started on the second (understanding our statutes). Each training is centered around four guiding questions. For example, in our workshops on union statutes and Convention 193, members have been encouraged to think about possible changes or improvements they’d like to see. At the end of each section, participating locals send the office of training and culture a brief writeup where they respond to these questions. This approach has encouraged members to participate and discuss amongst themselves, offered valuable feedback for our presenters, and built members’ sense of ownership over the union. Importantly, these workshops are spaces for discussion and political analysis, where a variety of viewpoints are welcomed and not seen as an obstacle. OPEN TO DISCOMFORT UNTA takes seriously the idea that members should always be learning and growing. We feel it’s important to have conversations that can be uncomfortable at times, because navigating disagreements makes for a stronger union. Members are encouraged to listen actively and have an open dialogue. A disagreement doesn’t mean that we’re enemies. Unions are political spaces, where people may not agree on everything, but are fighting together towards shared goals. And these words are important to remember today as we continue to raise the specter of abolition and the demand for reparations for decades of harm, including unrequited toil. The US—its wealth and power, its culture and customs, its laws and government—was built on an elaborate architecture of white supremacy that includes the system of slavery and the practice of conquest, war, and genocide (in North America to start, but eventually stretching its deadly talons to Latin America, Asia, and Africa), the defeat of Reconstruction, the regime of lynching, Jim Crow, and more. Everyday bigotry and racist ideas bloom in the nourishing shade of those structures of inequality based on color. For many well-intentioned liberals, the path to repairing past harms is to be found exclusively in individuals, one by one, “unlearning racism” or embracing “diversity, equity, and inclusion.” The definition of the problem leads to a specific theory of action: if the problem is merely a bad idea inside some people’s heads, the solution is in psychology and persuasion. But if instead the problem is structural, then action must involve dismantling those structures. “Unlearning racism” is a doomed project because a collective catastrophe—vast structural inequities never repaired, centuries of exploitation and oppression and generational trauma caused by our laws and backed by armed agents of the state—demands a collective response. The harm was instituted by the government and embraced by the dominant society; any authentic repair must be social and shared as well, borne by the government, the dominant society, and the entire community—and also at the highest levels. Reparations would include, then, institutional transformations and structural rearrangement in every area of social life. Individual attitudes matter, but they cannot drive that kind of repair. In the early Nineteenth Century the enslaved and free people of color in Haiti organized a successful anti-colonial and anti-slavery insurrection against French colonial rule. As a condition of peace, Haiti was forced to pay a tribute (reparations) of 150 million Francs annually to France—into the Twenty-first Century. This got reparations exactly backwards. Similarly, in the US President Lincoln signed a bill in 1862 (before the Emancipation Proclamation) emancipating enslaved people in Washington, DC, and the US government got it wrong, compensating the slaveowners for the loss of their property. Slaveowners got reparations, while the formerly enslaved African Americans got nothing for their generations of unpaid and forced labor. More recently reparations have been paid for war-related damages and for violations of human rights. When well-designed, reparations programs acknowledge suffering and provide forms of redress for harms endured. Victims have a right to receive reparations for injuries; the responsible party has a responsibility to provide redress. The government of Argentina provided reparations to victims of human rights abuses suffered under the military dictatorship of the 1970s and 80s; Italy apologized to Libya for colonization, and paid $5 billion as part of the Italy–Libya Friendship Treaty of 2008; Canada issued an apology to Indigenous peoples in a “Statement of Reconciliation,” acknowledging that the racist Indian residential schools were disastrous for victims, and providing financial over $2 billion to survivors; and in 1988 US President Ronald Reagan apologized and signed a bill authorizing payments of $20,000 to survivors of the World War II Japanese internment camps. And in 2015, Chicago issued an official apology for the systematic torture of victims in Area 2 police district under the leadership of commander Jon Burge—the city paid $100,000 to each survivor, provided free psychological counseling and medical care as well as free tuition to city colleges for survivors and their immediate family members, a mandatory curriculum about the Jon Burge torture cases to be taught in Chicago Public Schools, and a commitment to build a permanent public memorial to honor the victims. Each of these is an example of justice received—uncomplicated and easy to endorse. The forms that reparations take can vary widely, and the practical policies can be worked out by people of good will. But the first essential step is the admission of harm by the established government or institution that caused the harm. Invest massive resources—housing, education, community health clinics, jobs, arts centers, transportation, parks and recreation—into communities that were red-lined by the federal government, and have suffered ever since. Pay the “education debt”—an alternative way of looking at the so-called “education gap,” a manufactured fable covering up the real story, which is a story of disinvestment, isolation, segregation, inferior physical plants, and miseducative curricula. We want peace and justice, democracy and equality, health care, housing, education, and jobs or income now. We want significant work to do and meaningful lives to create. We want to be free—not the reactionary libertarian myth of individual autonomy, but a festival of joy and creativity, of collective hopes and community possibilities unleashed. We are not free if we are fearful of other people or imagine ourselves surrounded by danger—we aren’t free if we organize our lives behind barricades, always poised for combat. We are not free if we are prevented or hindered from voting; we’re not free if we’re forced to attend underfunded, segregated, miseducating schools; we are not free if we have no roof over our heads; we’re not free if we have no privacy and no bodily integrity, but rather are under constant auditing and mass surveillance by the government with closed circuit TV cameras, mobile trailers, and automated license plate readers; we’re not free if pervasive policies and politics are uniformly based on caging and cruelty, exclusion and dehumanization. Freedom is not and can never be an exclusively individual achievement—freedom must be worked out and accomplished in community. In a free society you’re free to swing your arms, of course, but your freedom to swing your arms ends where my nose begins. The abolition of slavery freed the enslaved workers from bondage, true, and it simultaneously freed slave-owners from their evil business, and from the anxiety of wondering when payback would come; when Black people are truly free, white people can leave behind their shaky pretense of superiority with all its attendant loneliness and stress; when women get free, men will be freed from clinging desperately to their precarious perch. Abolitionist values—liberation, community restoration, and shared fate as opposed to the hardening practices of cruelty and punishment, revenge and retribution—motivated the ten renowned words uttered by Justice Harry Blackmun in 1994 when he announced publicly that he had become a death penalty abolitionist: “I no longer shall tinker with the machinery of death.” Blackmun wasn’t searching for ways to make state-sanctioned murder more efficient or more palatable; he wanted to get out of the death business altogether. An aspect of abolition is eradication or removal, true. But the more challenging and more rewarding task of abolition is the work of creation: the building of institutions and relationships, structures and cultures that would make slavery, for example, unthinkable, then prisons and massive police forces unnecessary, and exploitation obsolete. Abolition is the work of “founding a new society” because abolition eliminates a society that could have slavery, and builds a society where slavery is impossible. This is the practice of freedom. So abolition is not best understood as a deletion or an erasure, but rather as a collection of creative and complex acts of “world-building.” What kind of world would we need to build in order to have no slavery? our forebears asked. And what kind of world could we begin to create today that would render prisons and militarized police and militarism obsolete, predation and exploitation relics of a cruel past? We must reimagine, resist, and rebuild in the name of freedom. Striving for freedom and abolishing all manner of unfreedoms is the repudiation and nullification of oppression everywhere, and the struggle against subjugation anywhere: abuse or cruelty of any trace or tone, exploitation in any context. In order to activate and find answers to our common problems and challenges, we must develop a growing sense of our own agency, our own power, and our own possibilities, and we must come to see the situation before us, not as a fixed condition—a closed system from which we have no exit—but as a limiting situation which we can and must transform. Everything can change. And freedom is the horizon. After the Midterms, the Left Has a Huge Opportunity to Seize

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Eric Blanc ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-05T15:58:06+00:00

This week’s midterm results were a historic victory for working people and a devastating blow to Trumpism. After so much cruelty and darkness, millions of people have gotten to experience the joy of defeating the far right. We’re entering an unusually fluid political moment, with enormous opportunities, formidable opponents, and no ready-made road map. Here are my initial thoughts about how we got here and what we should do now. 1. America Is Not a Deeply Right-Wing Country Remember when centrist pundits insisted Trump’s 2024 victory meant America had become permanently conservative? That narrative hasn’t aged well. Roughly 70 percent of young voters backed Democrats in this election, with the margin among young people swinging by about 32 points since 2024. Millions of Americans are angry at an economic and political system that keeps failing them. There’s an enormous opening for an alternative that can actually deliver. 2. Economic Populism Is Becoming More Central to Democrats Democrats of every political stripe won, so it would be silly for any faction to claim a national mandate for its entire program. But a striking and novel feature of this election nationwide was the growing Democratic emphasis on corporate greed, billionaire power, and a rigged economic system — not just affordability, but the fight against those making our lives unaffordable. The old centrist, Third Way assumption that Democrats can only win by “running to the center” is increasingly out of step with the party’s own campaigns, at least when it comes to economics. As one observer put it, the closing Democratic message across swing states and districts focused on “taking on corporate greed, endless war, and billionaire control of politics” — the kind of platform Bernie Sanders popularized in 2016 and Zohran Mamdani ran on in 2025. This shift remains very uneven, and it’s unclear how far mainstream Democratic politicians will go in actually confronting big business, unless forced by organized pressure from below. But their promises nevertheless raise expectations, and organized working people can fight to hold them to those promises. 3. Learn From Texas James Talarico defeated Republican Ken Paxton in a state Donald Trump won by nearly 14 points in 2024. He ran on an essentially Bernie-lite economic platform , arguing that “Republicans and Democrats alike sold the false gospel of trickle-down economics.” As he declared in his victory speech : “We just sent a message to every billionaire megadonor, every trillionaire megadonor, every corrupt defender of this broken political system: in this state, in this country, power belongs to the people.” Texas’s new Democratic governor, Gina Hinojosa , has been similarly explicit: “The one fight in this country is about economic power, and populism is about economic power.” And she’s right that centering bread-and-butter demands and broad fights against the economic elite is more effective than leaning into the culture war. As Hinojosa explained to The New York Times : “I have seen so many Democrats grandstand on culture-war issues, only to vote with corporate interests, and none of us are better for it.” 4. Socialists and Berniecrats Proved Their Electoral Viability Abdul El-Sayed easily won Michigan’s Senate race despite a viciously racist campaign against him and Haley Stevens’s treachery. Angie Nixon came within three points of winning in Florida, and over fifty Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) candidates won nationwide. William Lawrence, a Sunrise Movement cofounder who ran against corporate power and Big Tech, defeated Republican incumbent Tom Barrett in Michigan. Scores of other leftists and economic populists scored impressive victories. The Left has come extraordinarily far since 2016, and there’s no reason to assume its recent successes have exhausted its potential. 5. You Can’t Separate Performance From Money It’s true that moderate Democrats have a short-term advantage in certain relatively conservative statewide races, especially during backlash elections after decades of bipartisan neoliberalism. But part of their apparent electoral advantage in those areas reflects the huge differences in campaign spending and institutional support. I found that economic populist Senate campaigns in 2026 tended to face a much larger Republican spending advantage than their more moderate Democratic counterparts. Chuck Schumer and the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee poured millions into establishment candidates who lost badly, while Angie Nixon received no comparable backing and nearly won in Florida. While centrist pundits like Matt Yglesias are claiming that leftists and populist candidates systematically underperformed compared to those they claim are “moderates,” any serious comparison of electoral performance has to take these disparities into account, as well as the genuinely anti-billionaire emphasis of candidates like Talarico. And there’s a deeper strategic point here: our side has to run to win, but we also have to run to deliver big. That requires generally pushing for politicians prepared to enact ambitious reforms, like Zohran in New York City, even if taking some electoral risks is sometimes necessary in the process. So, while good campaigns have to read their local terrain and adjust accordingly, the biggest risk overall is staying with the same Democratic politics that got us into this mess in the first place. 6. AIPAC and Israel Suffered a Major Political Defeat El-Sayed won despite extraordinary spending against him, including nearly $30 million from the American Israel Public Affairs Committee (AIPAC) during the Democratic primary. Talarico and similar Democrats also publicly rejected AIPAC money. The old assumption that Democratic candidates must obediently support Israel to remain electorally viable is crumbling, finally. 7. There’s a Growing Constituency for Taking On the Artificial Intelligence Oligarchs Voters across the country backed ballot measures restricting or blocking data center development. Voters sided against data centers in forty-three of forty-four measures, including six decided earlier in the year. The billionaire owners of these companies were among the elites quickest to accommodate Trump; we need to make challenging their power a central part of our agenda. 8. This Election Was a Repudiation of Donald Trump, Not a Vindication of Chuck Schumer and Hakeem Jeffries The same Democratic leaders who failed to mount a serious fight against Trump’s authoritarian offensive are now trying to claim credit for his defeat. Their political timidity, ties to the billionaires, and low ambitions make them profoundly unsuited for the fights ahead. Democrats need new congressional leadership willing to confront Trump and champion transformative change. And the close races in supposedly unwinnable states should convince the party to stop writing off large parts of the country. Competing there consistently will require a bold program and much more organizing between elections. 9. The Democratic Party’s Future Is Unusually Fluid The most significant thing about Haley Stevens’s disgraceful endorsement of Republican Mike Rogers is that much of the Democratic establishment continued backing El-Sayed, however reluctantly. Sections of Barack Obama’s political orbit are clearly trying to accommodate the Left. The balance of power inside the Democratic Party is changing, and its future direction is up for grabs. Socialists should take advantage of every opening for cooperation while continuing to build independent strength. 10. We Need a New New Deal Without having any illusions that Democrats on their own will do the right thing, we should also acknowledge that there are no timeless formulas for what comes next. Whatever clues we can find from history are most likely to be found in the 1930s. Franklin Delano Roosevelt, like some of the smarter mainstream Democratic politicians around today, was fundamentally a political opportunist, but he could read which way the winds were blowing. The Great Depression, militant strikes, pressure from the Left, and the growing power of organized labor helped push his administration and a wing of establishment Democrats toward Social Security, the Wagner Act, and a more confrontational stance toward big business. Crucially, those transformational victories involved both independent working-class mobilization and alliances with reformers inside the Democratic Party. The lessons of the 1930s can hardly be reduced to a rule against ever working with liberal Democrats. We need concrete analyses of concrete situations to assess which compromises are needed, which fights to pick, and which alliances to make or break. A new New Deal is possible and necessary. And winning it is the precondition for any further advance toward economic democracy. 11. Celebrating MAGA’s Defeat Is Not “Libbing Out” Yesterday I saw plenty of joking from radicals online that it was ok for one night to “lib out,” i.e. celebrate Democratic wins against Republicans. The implication here is that there’s something not properly socialist about being excited about and engaged with such wins. But this misses something crucial: socialists should be unapologetically part of the broad fight against MAGA, including in electoral battles. And contrary to what some far-left DSA leaders seem to believe , if Democrats now fail to deliver, the main beneficiary will likely be an emboldened far right. Our most immediate task is to organize enough independent working-class power to make the anti-MAGA (i.e., Democratic) coalition succeed in meeting workers’ urgent needs, rather than lob critiques from the sidelines about how betrayal and disappointment are inevitable. 12) The Biggest Danger Is Repeating the Disastrous Missed Opportunity of 2006–8 Like last night, anger at George W. Bush’s presidency propelled Democrats to congressional majorities in 2006, culminating in Obama’s historic landslide in 2008. Democrats had a huge mandate and large congressional majorities. But Obama’s centrism and demobilization of his base squandered much of that opportunity. Millions who had hoped for fundamental change quickly grew disillusioned. Republicans won sixty-three House seats in 2010, and eight years after Obama’s first victory, Trump entered the White House. The stakes today are even higher, and our crisis much deeper. Everything depends on breaking the destructive doom loop of Democratic victories, dashed hopes, and increasingly reactionary Republican comebacks. The good news is that the Democratic establishment is far more delegitimized than in 2008, and the Left is far stronger. History doesn’t have to repeat itself. 13) Congress Needs to Go on the Offensive Immediately Ro Khanna got it right when he said on Election Day : When we win, we need America’s next New Deal. Medicare for All. $25 living wage. End the Iran War. Zero aid [to] Israel. Tax the billionaires. Make Trump Hoover if he vetoes it. Democrats should polarize the country around popular legislation that would materially improve people’s lives. They should fight to repeal the roughly $900 billion in looming Medicaid cuts, protect Affordable Care Act affordability, and save threatened rural hospitals. They should redirect money from Immigration and Customs Enforcement and endless war toward jobs, health care, and public investment. And they need to start fighting now to end the filibuster — part of a wider fight against minoritarian veto points that will eventually have to tackle the Supreme Court, statehood for Puerto Rico and Washington, DC, and other long-needed democratic reforms. We can’t allow appeals to bipartisanship or institutional norms to become another excuse for suicidal inaction. It’s good to investigate and prosecute Trump, his goons, and his corrupt billionaire buddies, but the centerpiece of this Congress should be an ambitious economic agenda that makes the stakes of 2028 clear. 14. AOC Needs to Run for President in 2028 The victories of El-Sayed and other left populists show there’s a real path to winning the White House in 2028. No less important, without Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez in the race, an ambitious anti-billionaire agenda is far less likely to dominate the Democratic primaries. There are genuine risks to running her for president, but the biggest risk is nominating another centrist without the determination or mandate to transform the country. Even if AOC loses the nomination, her campaign would generate massive grassroots power and help move the entire party toward a program capable of meeting this moment. 15. We Need Big Organizing All of our organizations, from unions to Sunrise to Indivisible to DSA and beyond, have a historic opening for growth if we can pivot away from our routines, our online self-selection, and our excessively small goals. We have an opening for on-the-ground left organizing at an unprecedented scale in contemporary America. By 2028, we need our unions to be on the offensive against corporations that bent the knee to Trump, and we need them to set the ambitious goal of organizing a million new workers every year under a new Democratic administration and forcing through labor law reform. DSA should aim to become a mass organization of at least half a million members, much more deeply rooted in the broader working class. That means moving beyond insular, self-marginalizing politics, turning instead to scaling up mass campaigns capable of involving our nonactivist neighbors, coworkers, and friends. The obstacles ahead remain enormous: billionaires, corporate Democrats, a hostile Supreme Court, and a Republican Party determined to sabotage any transformative agenda. We need organized movements capable of forcing recalcitrant Democrats to fight, winning and defending reforms against elite resistance, and keeping up the pressure long after Election Day. There’s still an enormous amount of work ahead. But after two years of darkness, we’ve won an extraordinary victory. The future is unwritten. Let’s make the most of it. Paul Heideman Historically, one of the most successful left political projects in US history was the People’s Party in the late nineteenth century, which was organized as a third party. There were governors and senators and representatives who were elected on the People’s Party ticket. And they won a substantial portion of the vote in the late 1880s and early 1890s in the United States. One might think that if the People’s Party model was among our most successful projects, then maybe third-partyism is the way to go. But there’s a historical chasm separating our moment from that moment because of the reorganization of electoral institutions in the United States since then. A couple features of that stand out. First is the primary system. There weren’t party primaries back then. So if you wanted to challenge a party or if you wanted to oppose what the leadership of a party was doing, the only way to do so was through another party. You couldn’t just enter into their primaries and try to displace them from within. That’s because the nomination process was entirely controlled by party bosses. So, challengers had to be in third parties; it was forced on the Left to an extent. That’s no longer true. Today the Democratic establishment in New York City did not want Zohran Mamdani to run for mayor, let alone win. But he did, and it’s because the parties set up these institutions called party primaries that they couldn’t stop him. In a sense, primaries have opened a door for the Left that was closed before the institution of primaries was mandated through state law all over the country. The second feature that’s really important is the restriction of ballot access for third parties. Since the 1930s and really in response to the growth of the Communist Party, the two main parties have agreed to increase the requirements for ballot access. Generally, this is the number of signatures required; sometimes it’s the number of votes. Ghis has made it much more difficult to organize a third-party campaign in the United States compared to the time of the populists. So, in a way, this has shut a door for third-party challenges. I think these two things together have really changed which strategy makes the most sense for the Left when it comes to electoral politics. Vivek Chibber There’s another factor worth mentioning, even though I don’t think it is as important as the ones that Paul has just laid out. The third factor separating that era from this one is fusion voting. Where there was fusion in that earlier era, candidates could run on more than one party’s ballot line. That enabled upstart, emerging parties to bring out their voters to support the mainstream parties. So something like the People’s Party could run its candidate and also have the Democratic Party support for that same candidate. And then, at the end of the election, if that candidate wins, the People’s Party goes to the Democrats and says, “Look, X number of the voters for your Democratic candidate were actually voting for the People’s Party.” This gave the People’s Party entrée because the candidate owes them something, and it’s able to use that to build the party’s legitimacy and visibility. France’s Police Unions Are Throwing Their Weight Around

“The proportionate use of force should never be an obstacle to the job of restoring order,” French Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez wrote in a September 30 message to police chiefs. High schoolers across the country had only just launched a wave of campus blockages over poor learning conditions, in demonstrations that would soon morph into a full-fledged social crisis. In an October 6 government questions session, Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu dismissed as “slander” any claims of systemic violence in policing and crowd-control tactics. In the weeks since then, little has punctured the pressure for similar deference to the police. Certainly not the persistence of intense protests — and, in some cases, rioting — which have instead strengthened the reflex among France’s political class to celebrate muscular policing as the lone rampart against disorder. Nor have the numerous examples of people, often minors, being maimed or otherwise severely injured by the arsenal of “nonlethal” weapons at the police’s disposal. Then again, political authorities have ample reason to be wary. “I accuse!” wrote Fabien Vanhemelryk in a statement published after the most intense day of protests and looting on October 17, reprising the famous declaration from Émile Zola’s 1898 public letter on the Dreyfus affair. They are pushing for some €500 million in new funding, allowing for salary increases and bonuses in addition to around a thousand new hires. The Interior Ministry has already been among the winners of the Macron years in financing terms. In 2023, police unions won special carve-outs in Macron’s unpopular increase in the pension age to sixty-four, a reform suspended during last winter’s budget negotiations in parliament. Adopted in 2023 and designed to extend over five years, a special budgeting law earmarked €15 billion extra funding to the Interior Ministry, adding to the baseline annual appropriations, which rose from some €22 billion in 2022 to over €34 billion in 2026. Still, with Macronist Premier Lecornu vying for tens of billions of euros worth of savings for the 2027 budget, Interior Minister Nuñez can only go so far in his attempt to satisfy police unions’ demands. But the police want more than just financial concessions. In a controversial bill currently awaiting final approval from parliament, police unions hope to claim a strategic win in one of their long-running campaigns: erecting legal shields to protect police officers who use their firearms from investigation and eventual criminal scrutiny. The title of the proposed law calls it a “presumption of legitimate defense,” but to the bill’s critics it’s better known as the “permit to kill.” According to the legislation , which sailed through the lower-house National Assembly in July, police who use their firearms “are presumed to have acted” in accordance with the law, unless evidence can be shown to justify an investigation. To critics, that amounts to a tautology opening major holes in the rule of law, hamstringing oversight by putting the onus on victims of police violence to prove possible wrongdoing. For Laurent Bonelli, a sociologist of security policy, the government finds itself in a genuine structural dilemma, caught between stretched resources, an acquired dependence on stiff policing in moments of social unrest, and palpable frustration among average troopers. “There’s only so much money to give, so what can be done?” Bonelli told Jacobin . “That’s where symbolic rewards come in. The current push for greater immunity for the police first gained momentum during a series of events not wholly unlike the current one. Over summer 2023, the fatal police shooting of seventeen-year-old Nahel Merzouk in the Paris suburb of Nanterre kicked off days of protests and rioting that fanned accusations of systemic racism and violence among the police. In several respects, the Nahel crisis provided the template for this fall’s conflict over law and order. In an incendiary public declaration that summer, the Alliance Police Nationale and UNSA police unions referred to rioters as “wild hordes” and “pests,” demanding a swift restoration of order. In less-than-veiled threats to political authorities, they also called for “concrete measures of legal protection” for the officer responsible for Merzouk’s killing. During Macron’s first term, his government tried to ban the filming and photographing of active-duty police officers. Legislation approved this past spring authorizes turning off video cameras during temporary police custody, a change that legal observers fear could weaken oversight over interrogation practices. The law also sought to anonymize the identities of police officers appearing in judicial proceedings, though that was struck down by the Constitutional Council. All signs are pointing toward what Tehio called the “construction of police immunity.” The contrast could not be more striking when compared to the evolution of the law as it applies for everyone else. One piece of legislation to emerge out of this fall’s social crisis is the “rioters-pay” law, which is currently being fast-tracked through parliament thanks to consensus support between the Macronist center and the far right. The bill stipulates legal responsibility — and potential financial liabilities — for individuals accused of having “wittingly participated” in a grouping that including acts of rioting and vandalism. A presumption of guilt, you might say, for participating in demonstrations that turn awry. Magical Solutions One-upmanship between the police forces and political authorities eager to satisfy a critical arm of the state is a dynamic that long predates Macron’s presidency. Part of this power is due to the nature of the force itself. France’s National Police is rigidly centralized, making it practically immune to local control and oversight. Concrete practices have also helped drill in the clientelist influence of police unions with a staunch ideological bent. “Comanagement,” granting unions considerable say over things like internal promotions, hardens their influence over the workforce — and marks the Interior Ministry off from many other state administrations. One immediate bugbear among the lower echelons is that superiors were granted a considerable salary increase this year. Police unions are looking to shore up support among their bases before public sector workers hold professional elections in December, with the police counting among the most unionized workforces in the country. But the greatest leverage that hard-line police unions have is structural, drawing on their position as a critical state service in a society on edge. Macron and his predecessors have long developed a dependence on muscular policing to contain conflicts over political decisions, whether the labor law reforms of the mid-2010s, the 2023 pension drive, or the anger with the education system that erupted these last few weeks. From enforcing immigration law to handling more quotidian social problems like drug use and extreme mental health cases, the “forces of order,” as France’s police are often called, offer a one-size-fits-all solution. “To many in the political class, the police has come to appear as something of a magic solution for resolving the consequences of an increasingly unequal society,” said Bonelli, the sociologist. “It should come as little surprise that the police as an institution would try to capitalize on its weight in the ongoing battles within the state itself.” Tensions over policing are so fraught that it’s barely ever asked whether an institution that separates itself so dramatically from the surrounding society is acting in its own best interests. “Promising impunity is in reality to abandon the police — which is indeed in need of many urgent reforms — to those who are pushing for permanent confrontation with the general public,” the minority Interior Ministry branch of the national Confédération Générale du Travail (CGT) union warned in a January press release , when the government threw its support behind the “legitimate defense” law. The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. Why should you still care about AB 32, two decades after Gov. Arnold Schwarzenegger signed it into law? Not just because the “California Global Warming Solutions Act of 2006” remains in effect, and enjoys support of 75% of state residents, according to a 2026 poll by the Public Policy Institute of California. Nor because AB 32 is the foundation for California’s climate regulation regime has reduced per-capita greenhouse gas emissions by 29%. Nor because it is the closest thing the U.S. has to a national climate law, as the Trump administration boosts fossil fuels and cancels regulations. You should care about AB 32 because no law in modern world history better demonstrates what we can do for the planet—and better exposes our shortcomings. AB 32 was the first law anywhere to set legally enforceable, economy-wide standards for reducing greenhouse gas emissions. For climate, it’s like the Magna Carta and Declaration of Independence, rolled into one. Related: The Next Big Shift in California’s Climate Change Movement Just as Britain’s 1215 Magna Carta claimed that no king or queen was above the rule of law, AB 32 established the principle that no polluter or planetary system on Earth was beyond the reach of law—even local, California-only law. Just as the Declaration of Independence defied the authority the British monarchy, AB 32 defied the George W. Bush administration, which had blocked climate action and boosted fossil fuel subsidies. As the world’s nations walked away from the 1998 Kyoto Protocol , it provided an example of climate policy leadership for provincial and local governments worldwide. AB 32-style laws were adopted by Hawaiʻi, New Jersey, Massachusetts, and Connecticut, and regulations from the law have been adopted in dozens of states. But it may be most necessary today as inspiration. In 2026, it can feel like all is lost. Global warming is accelerating . Glacial ice and ocean currents are collapsing. Yet many governments are unresponsive. But in 2006, everything felt lost in California. Climate change was accelerating then , too. The Golden State was fighting another warmongering federal government hostile to the environment. The state budget was a mess, and a disastrous special election in 2005 fueled bitter political divides. This toxic environment nonetheless produced a breakthrough in AB 32. The story of how that positive change happened is worth remembering. Today, we tell ourselves that politics is blood sport, and there is no room for bipartisanship and friendship. But AB 32 was built on friendship and compromise between political antagonists, the Democratic Assembly Speaker Fabian Núñez and Schwarzenegger, the Republican governor. It was also created by politically diverse teams. A leading organization behind the bill, E2, was an alliance of environmentalists and entrepreneurs. And Schwarzenegger’s administration, a mix of Democrats and Republicans, was the closest thing in America’s modern history to a coalition government. Despite Republican allies’ warnings against “employing enemies,” Schwarzenegger relied heavily on advisors who had worked for Gov. Gray Davis, whom he had replaced in the 2003 recall. Núñez and Schwarzenegger, who both lacked scientific training and long track records on the environment, warmly welcomed outsiders and opponents into their conference rooms. Ideas emerged from unusual places. A former America’s Cup sailboat captain came up a precursor bill regulating car exhaust. A crucial rule was drafted in a Buenos Aires bar. The original author of AB 32, Fran Pavley, was a career middle-school teacher, elected to the Assembly almost by accident. She made a point of meeting with every single member of the legislature as she shaped her bill. Instead of despairing during crisis, AB 32’s creators leaned into it. State government had created California’s 2000-2001 electricity crisis and its manipulated markets, rolling blackouts, and surging bills. But, as former Davis aide Kurt Schuparra describes in his book, How the California Electricity Crisis Generated a Green Wave , two administrations’ creative response to the crisis advanced renewable energy and opened the door for AB 32. And when opposition grew, California’s leaders did not narrow their vision, as commonly happens with controversial legislation. They raised the stakes, with Schwarzenegger recruiting international supporters like British Prime Minister Tony Blair and oil giant BP CEO Lord John Browne to create global pressure to pass the bill. AB 32’s backers weren’t sure it would pass until the very end of the legislative session. The federal government said it was illegal, a preemption of U.S. law. Schwarzenegger’s own party sought to sabotage it, and the governor considered vetoing it. The historic victory happened anyway. AB 32 required California to reduce its statewide greenhouse gas emissions to 1990 levels by 2020. It was a massive legal risk, and a demand that tomorrow’s needs take precedence over present-day politics. In several ways, AB 32 delivered on its promise. The state hit its 2020 target for greenhouse gas reductions in 2016 . AB 32 sparked new businesses like electric cars, produced more than 550,000 clean energy jobs, and generated $36 billion through its cap-and-trade program. according to Next 10 , which tracks California’s climate policy. Globally, California has built partnerships with dozens of nations, and shared technology and regulations with provinces and cities, including several in China . Yet, for all its success, AB 32 is also a human story of mistakes and limitations. Political momentum for countering climate change has slowed, even in California, where fights about the balance between markets and direct regulation in reducing emissions have never ended. The state’s 2030 targets for greenhouse gas reductions are unlikely to be met. And global climate change is worsening as earthlings decarbonize too slowly. But for all the follow-up legislation and regulation the law has inspired, as well as its two renewals (most recently last year, through 2045), California has failed to build the technological, political, and legal infrastructure needed to win the climate fight. What might that infrastructure look like? The most compelling answer I can find comes from UC San Diego climate policy scholar David Victor, and Columbia Law School’s Charles Sabel, who argued in a 2022 book that our current system no longer works for a world of such uncertainty. They urge us to embrace “experimental governance,” in which experts, government officials, and everyday people try out different solutions, and make adjustments. Through relentless trial-and-error, implementation creates policy, execution shapes concept, and results accelerate as more of us participate. Does such collaboration seem impossible to you? It shouldn’t. Because AB 32 proved that, even when politics are toxic and the future looks bleak, collaborative governance can change the world. This article was produced by Zócalo .

The Mbuzini plane crash On October 19, 1986, Machel was returning from Lusaka, Zambia, where he had attended a summit of African leaders. His presidential aircraft, a Soviet-made Tupolev Tu-134, went down in the Lebombo Mountains near Mbuzini, close to the borders of Mozambique and Swaziland, now Eswatini, in South African territory. The crash killed Machel and 34 other people, including members of his delegation and aircraft crew. Nine people survived. The location of the crash immediately raised questions. At the time , Mozambique was facing a devastating civil war, with the apartheid government of South Africa providing support to the RENAMO insurgency seeking to overthrow the FRELIMO government. Machel had also been a vocal supporter of liberation movements fighting white-minority rule across Southern Africa, including the African National Congress (ANC). The political relationship between Mozambique and Apartheid South Africa was therefore deeply hostile. Mozambique’s support for regional liberation movements had made it a target of South African destabilization efforts. The apartheid government regarded Machel’s government as a threat to its regional interests. Against this, suspicions emerged that the crash might not have been an accident. Competing investigations and unanswered questions Following the crash, a Commission of Inquiry headed by South African Judge Cecil Margo was established to investigate the circumstances. Its findings attributed the accident to pilot error, concluding that the flight crew had failed to follow the required procedures during an instrument approach and had continued descending without maintaining the minimum safe altitude. However, a Soviet investigative team disputed the findings. Its minority report argued that a false or decoy navigation beacon had misdirected the aircraft, causing it to turn away from its intended route and into the mountainous terrain near Mbuzini. The Soviet delegation also denounced that its expertise and experience had been undermined during the inquiry. It maintained that the aircraft’s unexpected 37-degree turn was central to understanding the crash and rejected the conclusion that pilot error alone explained the tragedy. The different findings left important questions unresolved. The matter was revisited by South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) , established in 1996 to investigate human rights violations committed during apartheid. Its special investigation into Machel’s death examined the earlier inquiries, interviewed witnesses and considered evidence concerning the political and security circumstances surrounding the crash. The TRC did not establish conclusively whether the aircraft had been deliberately brought down. However, its report raised questions about the conclusions of the Margo Commission and highlighted evidence that warranted further investigation. Among the issues examined was the possibility of a false navigation beacon and the failure of South African authorities to warn the aircraft about the danger posed by its flight path. The report also considered testimony about a suspicious electronic device reportedly found near the crash site. A television journalist who attended the on-site investigations by the joint Soviet, Mozambican, and South African team told the Commission that an investigator had shown the television crew a device that could have been a frequency scrambler. While members of the Margo inquiry team reportedly told a journalist that the device had been found to be harmless, an expert on mobile beacons told the TRC that such a device could potentially interfere with an aircraft’s signal. The Commission’s final conclusion was that the investigations had raised questions that remained unanswered. It stated: “The investigations conducted by the Commission raised a number of questions, including the possibility of a false beacon and the absence of a warning from the South African authorities. The matter requires further investigation by an appropriate structure.” That recommendation has never been carried through. Nelson Mandela and Thabo Mbeki both promised to reopen the inquiry, and a police investigation, announced in 2012 after new documents surfaced, produced no public findings. Samora Machel’s revolutionary legacy Beyond the circumstances of his death, the 40th anniversary also brought renewed attention to Machel’s place in the history of African liberation. Born in 1933, Machel became involved in the struggle against Portuguese colonial rule and joined the Mozambique Liberation Front, FRELIMO, which was established in 1962. He rose through its military and political ranks, eventually becoming the movement’s leader following the death of Eduardo Mondlane in 1969. Under his leadership , FRELIMO intensified the armed struggle against Portuguese colonialism, which culminated in Mozambique’s independence on June 25, 1975. Machel became the country’s first president and led the newly independent state through a period of socialist-oriented transformation. His government pursued nationalization and expanded public provision in areas such as education and healthcare, while seeking to dismantle the economic and social structures inherited from colonial rule. It also supported liberation movements across Southern Africa, providing political and material solidarity to those fighting apartheid and white minority rule. This position placed Mozambique in direct confrontation with the apartheid government. South Africa supported RENAMO as part of its wider regional strategy to undermine governments and liberation movements opposed to apartheid. The resulting conflict devastated Mozambique, causing widespread displacement, destruction of infrastructure, and loss of life. Machel’s death therefore occurred within a wider regional struggle in which the survival of independent African governments and the liberation of Southern Africa remained closely connected. His political legacy continues to resonate across the continent, particularly through the principles of anti-colonialism, African solidarity, and self-determination. His famous revolutionary slogan, A luta continua (The struggle continues), remains associated with the liberation movements and generations of activists who challenged colonialism, apartheid and imperialism. The history of Africa’s liberation struggles is also marked by the assassination of other revolutionary figures, including Amílcar Cabral, Patrice Lumumba, Titina Silá, and Chris Hani, as well as the killing of Steve Biko under apartheid detention. While the circumstances of these deaths differ, they form part of a broader history in which political violence was used against leaders and movements challenging colonial and minority-rule systems. Machel’s death continues to be considered within this historical context, although the allegations surrounding the Mbuzini crash have never been conclusively established.

France’s student strikes

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: Tempest ／ 著者: Danielle Bullock ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-05T13:57:44+00:00

F rench students have been protesting in the largest numbers since the historic May 1968 Paris uprising. Protests began last month in Paris’s northern suburbs, where students have struggled to learn in crumbling, pest-infested schools with too few teachers. Students say the government’s latest budget isn’t enough to fix the school system’s failings, and they are demanding more. Public anger over the budget and abysmal school conditions has spread—and so have the protests; students have been blocking high schools and protesting in the streets in the thousands. The government’s response has been heavy-handed and violent; there have been many recorded incidents of police brutality against young and vulnerable high school students. On Saturday, Oct 17th, 550,000 students, parents, educators, and trade unionists across the country flooded the streets to demand drastic, immediate improvements to the school system. Laurent (pseudonym) is a 35-year-old Information Technology teacher in a public school located in a city in Brittany, France. The students at his school are not currently on strike, but have held a few small rallies in solidarity with striking students across the country. I spoke with him to get his impressions as a public educator witnessing student protests from inside the country. Danielle Bullock: What is currently happening in your school? Are students on strike? Laurent: I work in a school near the city of Rennes, and there’s not a lot of protest here (in my school) for basically two reasons. One being that most of our students come from a sort of wealthier background, and they are not that involved in politics because they don’t have to be. So we just have a smaller number of students who are really involved in politics. And one other factor is most students come in with the bus. So when they arrive, it’s just two minutes before classes begin. So you can’t quite block your high school if you don’t arrive one hour before. So what we have, what the director of (my) school said is, it’s okay to protest in front of the building, but everyone who wants to come in has to be able to come in. So what we had the first few days (of the student strike) was one to two hundred students in front of the high school protesting. But my high school has 1,200 students. So mid-range in terms of size. Starting the second day, most of the students were still back in school because parents had to write a note saying their kid was on strike. They had to justify why the kids were not at school. And so we had one or two days with sort of a bigger protest in front of the high school, and ever since, almost nothing. The fact that students come to our school by bus is really a huge factor. In the nearby city (Rennes), there are a lot more protests at the high schools, and those schools are completely blocked. And there’s also quite a bit of vandalism. Because it’s a city where, historically, whenever there is a protest, there is a lot of stuff that is destroyed and vandalized. So with this high school movement, it sort of follows the same trends. DB: And the university students in Rennes, are they on strike in solidarity? L: We have two big universities in Rennes. Big for France —they’re not the same size as American universities. There’s one (university) for science and one more for literature and politics and everything. And the one that teaches literature and foreign languages is always on strike whenever there’s something. So this time as well, they blocked the university . I didn’t hear much about the scientific one, the scientific university. Also, because the campus is really large, it’s really hard to block anything. There are dozens of buildings, smaller buildings. So I didn’t hear much from them, but I know that at the literature one, the students went on strike as well. DB: What do you think is the main reason why the students are on strike? L: It’s quite difficult, actually, because it started as really a local thing because there are some high schools, especially near Paris, that are in really complete disrepair. And their first ask was: we want to be able to study in good conditions. For example, we want classrooms that aren’t 8 degrees Celsius (40º F) in the winter and 40 degrees Celsius (104º F) in the summer. We want teachers because when teachers are absent, or there are vacancies, there are no substitutes. We want chairs for everyone. We want tables for everyone, which is not quite the case in all of these (schools). Some high schools, especially near Paris, are in really complete disrepair. And [the student strikers’] first ask was: we want to be able to study in good conditions. But it’s quite a small number of high schools that have these sorts of issues. And these demands, I’ve been hearing about them for years now. And so the students started to do a bit of protesting. This time it went directly to blocking the high school because, back in my days as a high school student, first it was a protest in the street, and then, if nothing happened, we blocked the high school. This time they went with blocking first. With social media, there have been some videos of bins burning in front of high schools. Also, one big factor was that the political answer [to the strikers’ actions] was to send the police, and heavily armored police, directly. This really escalated the protests from the start. There have also been some videos of police violence against (what are) basically kids, and this is over social media, so it’s really spread rather quickly, which inspired other students who saw the videos and said, okay, we want to be part of that as well, and began to protest. DB: And what are the consequences of students not going to school if they were on strike? L: Where I am, there are no consequences. I mean, since most students go to class, the main consequence is that they don’t attend, so they fall a bit behind. But as far as I know, in my high school, the director is really more into dialogue. So he will let them speak if they want to speak. And if they want to protest, they can, as long as they don’t destroy anything. So where I am, the strategy is really not escalation at all. It’s just, okay, you want to protest? We will give you a place where to protest. They’re in dialogue with the director to talk about any issues, but the thing is, most of the demands are at a country level. So the director, he can’t do anything about these demands. When there’s no dialogue, of course, tension is building. And at some point, it has to burst. DB: Why did it have to come to this point where people are so angry? Students are so angry. L: The thing is that the students from the first high schools near Paris, they said we have been asking nicely for years, and we’re only heard when we start burning stuff. The current president has been ignoring what people in the streets have to say from the beginning. He’s never tried to discuss what people depend on. And even François Hollande before him, whenever there was a huge protest, he would send the police and just try to intimidate as much as possible. So basically, there is no dialogue between what people ask for and the government, and it’s been the case for ten to fifteen years now. That’s basically why everything’s coming to this point, because when there’s no dialogue, of course, tension is building. And at some point, it has to burst. DB: What is the general opinion of teachers about what’s going on with the strikes? L: I’ve been talking with colleagues. I mean, teachers, as everyone, have different political opinions. And people who are more on the right, to the far-right side, they support the police and (they think) all these kids should go back to school. [They say] they’re ridiculous, their demands are childish, and they should go back home or go back to school. From a more left point of view, I mean, of course, we support what they ask for. Because basically what they want is better conditions to work in. So the demands are not quite localized [some demands are more generalized and appeal to wider layers]. For example, there are also huge school days, 8 a.m. to 6 p.m., with big gaps in the middle, so really exhausting for students. Some of them have a one-hour drive to go to school and the same to go back home, and they are completely exhausted. Another thing is, after high school, there is a system, a platform (Parcoursup) , where you register if you want to go on to higher education. And since the supply for higher education in France is not enough to meet the demand, there’s a lot of frustration about that system and that platform. And we can understand that this platform creates a lot of stress because it looks at old school reports for the entire high school [career]. So every bad grade, even two or three years before the end of high school, can mean that you have a worse report and mean that you cannot really get into the school [college] you want. So it’s a lot of pressure for a long time for the students, and that we can empathize with. So we support the demands. Then there are basically two categories of teachers. Some who say, we’re in France, and in France, if you want to get something, you really never get anything unless you break stuff first. So we’re really, yeah, there’s some vandalism, but that’s France; that’s how it is. And some others who are like, yeah, we support the demands, but it would be really better if there weren’t that much vandalism. So basically, you have the two opinions from what I’ve been seeing around me, my colleagues. DB: Have any of the teachers’ unions made statements in support of student strikes? L: You have unions that are more on the right side and many of them that are more on the left side, but there’s the one big one and a lot of smaller ones, which are still quite big. And we have the choice every couple of years. There are some elections to see which are the bigger unions and how they should be taken into account with the secretary of education. [This determines which union] will have a bigger voice speaking with the secretary of education. So in France, teachers can go on strike if a union calls for it. And almost instantaneously, they all said, okay, starting now, you can go on strike. We cover you. DB: So—teachers can go on strike as well as students? L: Yeah, yeah, yeah. DB: When you say the union will “cover you”, what do you mean? L: They [the union] have to say, “We declare that there is a strike starting now. So that if we don’t go to work, it will be considered as participating in that strike instead of just, oh, I decided I don’t want to go to work. DB: Right, but teachers—there will be no other consequences for teachers? L: Right, so you won’t get paid, but you won’t lose your job, or you won’t have any kind of penalty. That’s how strikes work in France. You need the union that will say, ” Okay, as a union, we decided to go on strike.” And then every worker is included in that; we don’t have to provide proof that we are part of that union. We can just participate in that strike. DB: What are the teachers’ main complaints? If teachers had to say what the worst thing about French schools is, what do you think they would say? L: Oh, there’s a lot. Because the first strike for us this year was a week before the student strike started. Because basically, the prime minister announced his plan for next year’s budget. And in that budget, it was said that the pay would remain exactly the same. So basically, it means that we lose money because of inflation. And it’s been the case for basically years now. It started in the 80s. This difference between what we’re actually paid and inflation doesn’t follow exactly. And it means 20 to 30 percent pay cuts compared to what it was in the 80s. So it’s quite huge. That’s basically one major grievance—that teachers in France are quite underpaid. And so much so that there are a lot of problems recruiting new teachers. DB: In comparison to how much they pay police, do teachers make more or less? L: Yeah. The thing is, there’s one major difference that will make comparison quite hard—the level of qualification is really not the same. So if you were a teacher in France until recently, you needed five years of education, a master’s degree. And to become a police officer, you just need to have finished high school. So the bar is really lower. I would say police officers get slightly higher pay. I don’t know quite all the details, but at least in the beginning, their pay is better. DB: So less qualifications, better pay. L: There’s probably way more teachers than there are police officers in France, because almost everything is public. I think there are 800,000 teachers in France. So it’s quite huge. So if you want to raise their pay, it’s billions [of dollars]. Even if the increase isn’t really high, it’s billions instantly. It’s in this context that the state doesn’t have much money; it’s quite hard [for the state] to increase our pay. And the unions, they say, we will not negotiate a pay increase without also negotiating working conditions. Because what we had a couple of years ago, our secretary said, ” Yeah, so we will give you a pay increase.” But what he did basically was create another way for us to do extra hours, which would then get paid, which is not quite the same as, okay, you’re getting a pay increase. No, it’s an increase in labor too. And then a lot of things go with that. Since the pay is not quite good, we have trouble recruiting. So in many places, there aren’t enough teachers. So basically, in these places, in the places where people don’t want to go, there aren’t many teachers. And there aren’t many substitute teachers who are real [credentialed] teachers. There were some posts on eBay, yeah, we need a teacher. You need 15 minutes in front of someone who will say, ” Okay, you’re good. You can go in front of students,” and that is the whole process. That’s how the substitute teachers are recruited in some places. So the quality is really, it can be good because some of them can be good, but there’s no guarantee that the people who are in front of the students are any good at all. But many substitute teachers aren’t being hired right now. They’re not putting them in a classroom. DB: So what happens to a class when there’s no teacher? L: If the teacher gets sick, the class is canceled. There are two things. If the teacher is away for less than two weeks, they won’t even search for a replacement because it’s too difficult. If the teacher is away long enough and substitute teachers are available, you can ask for one. DB: So if somebody just gets sick, you get the flu, and you are out for five days, there’s no class. So what do students do when there’s no class? L: Yeah, usually in high school, they have some rooms where they can sort of relax and/or work if they want to. But if the high school is in a really bad state, maybe those rooms are quite horrible, or they don’t exist. So the inequalities are there as well. And no work for the students is provided. DB: How many students do you have at the most in a class? L: Classes go up to 35, 36, and that’s one of the reasons why I have a lot of students. The demand is that, okay, when we are having a full class all together, 35, 36 is way too many for one teacher to teach properly, so we want smaller groups. Because the state says teachers cost too much and cut the number of teachers, this led to bigger classes, even though the number of kids was rising at the time. Now we have the opportunity [to correct this] because people are having fewer kids, and the number of students in middle school and high school is slowly decreasing. And so basically there are two options. Either you keep the number of teachers the same, and you have smaller classes, or you decrease the number of teachers to follow the decrease in students. And there’s a big issue about how much we want to spend on education in France. That’s basically the one thing: what place we want to give education in our spending. And the politics that have been in France for many years is—we try to not invest much in public stuff. And yeah, probably private will develop. Oh, we’ll see. And that’s [true] for teaching, that’s [true] for health, that’s for most of the justice as well; it’s been underfunded for years now. All the public sector, except maybe police because there’s always money for police, and the army. But other than that, all the public sector, it’s been quite underfunded for years now. But that’s a choice that we have to make as a society. DB: How do you think the student strikes will be resolved? L: Unfortunately, after the riots last Saturday, I don’t have much hope for a happy ending. The videos of vandalism are everywhere to be seen, and they completely eclipsed the legitimate claims from the students. In the news, it feels like it sparked a bigger movement, and we’ll see if the students can keep their own strikes without huge interference from anyone else. In case of interference, the political right asks for a lot more police repression (against protesters). Socialists and AI: No other choice?

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: Tempest ／ 著者: Chenglin Lee ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-05T10:55:14+00:00

Thus [the bourgeois economist] saves himself from all further puzzling of the brain, and what is more, implicitly declares his opponent to be stupid enough to contend against, not the capitalistic employment of machinery, but machinery itself. —Karl Marx A s Marxists, we cannot disentangle Artificial Intelligence (AI) from capitalist political economy. AI is not, in fact, “intelligence” but rather a system of sophisticated algorithms that do not generate new ideas but can only spit out what it has been fed. How tech companies developed these algorithms was through “data scraping,” taking large amounts of personal and private data to teach its machines . AI is also not “artificial.” AI is powered and built by a large force of workers from the Global South, including Kenya, Venezuela, the Philippines, and India, among others. Moderators for digital platforms work 18 to 20 hours a day sometimes, at a rate of $1.46-$3.74 per day, looking at incredibly violent and disturbing text passages and images with no breaks . Of course, these digital companies offer no support to these workers, with many workers suffering from post-traumatic stress disorder and deep psychological damage. Many attempts to organize workers at these digital sweatshops have been crushed. Daniel Montag , a worker in Kenya, told Time that workers at a digital sweatshop subcontracted by Meta were all fired after attempts at unionization . Reading these reports is eerily reminiscent of the piecemeal sweatshops that Karl Marx described in Capital : worked to the bone under confined conditions for pennies. Digital infrastructure is not immaterial: the virtuality of the digital merely mystifies the grueling labor and extraction that enriches tech companies. This is coupled with the intensive fossil fuel and water use that AI data centers require, with AI data center needs exceeding the already heavy burden of other digital services such as Netflix and Zoom . Digital infrastructure is not immaterial: the virtuality of the digital merely mystifies the grueling labor and extraction that enriches tech companies. However, the moral arguments used to reject generative AI unfortunately often fall flat in the imperial core. Many ask, “Which major industry isn’t fueled by the blood of workers in the Global South?” Or, “Is it possible to be morally pure in a capitalist economy?” Of course not. But, if such arguments are not compelling, I offer here a few different arguments about why organizers on the Left should reject AI as it is being developed under capitalism. Community Writing, artistic expression, learning, and thinking are social acts. When we write or create art, we do so with the goal of reaching an audience intellectually or emotionally. When we read and think, we learn from writers and process what society, past and present, has communicated. As I often teach in my own writing courses, a key aspect to becoming a good writer is awareness of your audience and taking the opinions of others into account. For organizers, the social aspect is particularly important. As historical materialists, our writing and thinking should be continually developing with and in community. Generative AI takes away this community aspect, and the only interlocutor is the faceless user interface of Claude or ChatGPT. What potential organizing opportunities do we miss when we outsource community dialogue to machines? And why would we want to outsource idea-generation to machines that aggregate reactionary views? As studies have shown, AI algorithms commonly make decisions based on racial stereotypes . We should not risk reproducing these same reactionary tendencies in our own organizing by trusting machines that are often designed for surveillance and military applications. Also, nothing in the past five years has suggested that those in charge of these large AI companies, such as Mark Zuckerberg and Elon Musk, are interested in fixing the racist and reactionary tendencies of these systems. When organizers use AI, whether for writing or graphic design or anything else, we are feeding tech companies that actively seek to thwart our aims. The social effects of AI are horrifying: AI psychosis, cognitive decline, heightened surveillance, and militarization. When the U.S. military horrifically bombed a girls school in Iran, strong evidence suggests the target was chosen by an AI algorithm . When organizers use AI, whether for writing or graphic design or anything else, we are feeding tech companies that actively seek to thwart our aims. Feeding these tech companies our data also gives the surveillance state more ammunition to use against us. Our thoughts and ideas should be ours, not shaped by AI or given to tech companies as another data point to further suppress and surveil socialist organizing. Also, AI art is sloppy and ugly, and AI writing tends to be poorly written and formulaic. When restaurants use AI art, does the food not look like it’s infested with worms? Relying on AI hurts our ethos as organizers. Earlier this year, Vijay Prashad and Tings Chak were caught using AI to write an article on China and Uyghurs . The deeply problematic politics of their argument aside, the AI article contained several hallucinated sources that detracted from whatever credibility the article and the publication, Monthly Review , had. If our goal is to reach the wider community, we should not give the public easy grounds to dismiss us. And if we believe in the power of labor and creativity, our materials should reflect that. We can learn how to write and draw together, or at the very least pay competent writers and artists for our materials. This belief in community and labor should be centered in our thinking on AI, which leads me to the threat of AI and automation to labor. Labor Taste is ultimately subjective, and some people do genuinely love AI slop when it generates fingerless hands or worm-infested hamburgers. So, when discussing AI with the broader public, our arguments should center on labor. The dream of tech companies is to depress the value of labor power, so the capitalist class can extract more surplus value. We are beginning to see AI used in multiple industries: automated grading and teaching to replace educators, digitally generated art and content to replace artists, and vibes coding to replace software developers and engineers. The proliferation and development of AI poses a grave threat to workers. AI automation is not only targeting jobs of the professional managerial class either. Many companies are building AI-enabled robots to begin replacing jobs in factories and oil refineries. Even gig work is under attack by automation through companies such as Waymo and Starship Technologies. The proliferation and development of AI poses a grave threat to workers. According to one tracking tool , roughly 265,000 jobs have already been cut due to AI. The specter of AI is used in the way Marx describes automation in Capital, Volume I : The instrument of labour, when it takes the form of a machine, immediately becomes a competitor of the workman himself. The self-expansion of capital by means of machinery is thenceforward directly proportional to the number of the workpeople, whose means of livelihood have been destroyed by that machinery. The whole system of capitalist production is based on the fact that the workman sells his labour-power as a commodity. Division of labour specialises this labour-power, by reducing it to skill in handling a particular tool. So soon as the handling of this tool becomes the work of a machine, then, with the use-value, the exchange-value too, of the workman’s labour-power vanishes; the workman becomes unsaleable, like paper money thrown out of currency by legal enactment. That portion of the working-class, thus by machinery rendered superfluous, i.e., no longer immediately necessary for the self-expansion of capital, either goes to the wall in the unequal contest of the old handicrafts and manufactures with machinery, or else floods all the more easily accessible branches of industry, swamps the labour-market, and sinks the price of labour-power below its value. In other words, machinery and automation takes away the labor power of the worker, and the only commodity she has—labor—is rendered unnecessary. Workers who are not laid off now must work more as their workday intensifies to compete against machines. Machinery and automation takes away the labor power of the worker, and the only commodity she has—labor—is rendered unnecessary. Take, for example, an educator. A university begins to lay off instructors as tasks such as grading and curriculum development become automated by AI, and the remaining instructors take on more classes, more students, and ultimately more work to maintain their employment. The quality of education and instruction will decrease across the board for both students and instructors, but the university only sees short term value gains. In a capitalist political economy, where the goal is to extract as much value as possible, technology will always trend (with notable exceptions) toward automation, deskilling, and the suppression of the value of labor power. Spoiler alert for Park Chan Wook’s No Other Choice In Park Chan Wook’s newest film, No Other Choice , the main character, Yoo Man Su (Lee Byung Hun), is laid off from his manufacturing job at a paper factory. A new position at a paper factory opens up, and Man Su decides to eliminate his competition, literally. He kills every other unemployed worker competing for his position, and when Man Su gets the job at the end of the film, he walks into a factory where he is the only person. All other workers have been eliminated, and the only thing for Man Su to do is watch machines make paper. No community, no solidarity. Barely even any work. Park Chan Wook’s vision of automation grows ever closer as tech companies, large corporations, and governments promote and fund AI in service of value extraction, war, and surveillance. AI is another weapon aimed directly at the global working class, whose precarious conditions of daily living are already close to unbearable. Is the time we save outsourcing our creativity and cognition to automated algorithms worth it? “The violence in northern Ethiopia is pushing an already fragile health system closer to collapse,” World Health Organization (WHO) Director-General Tedros Ghebreyesus warned on November 2, as civilian casualties mount from drone strikes in Tigray and Amhara.​ Earlier on October 30, a drone strike by the Ethiopian army killed at least 12 civilians and wounded many more in the town of Rama in the northern region of Tigray, where its troops are fighting the Tigray People’s Liberation Front (TPLF).​ The victims were gathering sorghum scattered across the road from a truck that was hit by another drone strike the previous night.​ In Tigray’s southern neighbor Amhara, where the federal government led by PM Abiy Ahmed is fighting the Amhara Fano National Movement (AFNM), drones struck the Hayiq Jari 2 Turk IDP camp five times in 15 minutes, killing at least 16 and injuring 22.​ While there was no fighting in and around the camp itself, Reuters reported that the AFNM had been nearby. The drone strike killed three pregnant women, a child, and several students on their way back from school were among the killed, Addis Standard reported . Adding that a newborn was also killed, Tedros called these attacks “deeply disturbing”.​ “While these are the latest incidents, drone attacks and civilian casualties have tragically become frequent across Ethiopia.”​ Earlier in September, several armed groups – many of whom were often pitted against one another by Abiy’s government – united to form the Alliance for Survival to overthrow his regime.​ Read more: Ethiopia plunges into civil war again as PM Abiy’s ‘divide and rule’ backfires ​ Drone strikes in October have killed scores of civilians and injured many more. Treating casualties is a major challenge. “Hospitals face shortages of health workers, medicines, and equipment. Ambulance services are severely limited. Insecurity is obstructing the delivery of life-saving medical supplies to some areas,” Tedors said, adding, “The best medicine is peace.”​
