Lula's Stunning Victory

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Roger D. Harris ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-08T15:41:28+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. Just over a month ago, a MAGA-aligned rightwing juggernaut appeared to be on a roll in Latin America. The far right had captured every presidential election since Venezuela’s Nicolás Maduro was reelected in July 2024. In addition, recent parliamentary elections have landed the Bahamas, Belize, Trinidad and Tobago, Guyana, and Jamaica in MAGA-aligned alliances such as the Shield of the Americas . Among the remaining leftist countries in the region, the US had kidnapped Venezuela’s Maduro and imposed a “ negotiated sovereignty ” on that nation. An ever-intensifying blockade continued to asphyxiate Cuba. And a renewed offensive was mounted against Nicaragua. Brazil and Mexico anchored the left-leaning camp, with Uruguay on the center-left. Compared to the heyday of the Pink Tide , when Latin America was the international epicenter of independence from Washington, the region was increasingly looking like a casualty of the Donroe Doctrine . Yet this apparent rightward consolidation concealed vulnerabilities that would soon become evident. Related: Extreme Lawfare and the MAGA Offensive in Latin America The new right Besides its signature sycophantic worship of the US, Latin America’s new populist right shares an ideological antipathy to “wokism,” a fixation on mano dura solutions to crime, an alignment with the US imperialist/Zionist project, and subservience to international capital. Its role model is the neofascist, self-proclaimed “ coolest dictator ” in the world, President Nayib Bukele of El Salvador. Donald Trump recently told an assemblage of obsequious rightwing Latin American leaders that they “know very well” that they owe him. Specifically, he boasted of nine “great election victories” because of his endorsements. The Carnegie Endowment for International Peace describes Trump as a would-be “kingmaker.” Presumably, Trump’s assists included Argentina’s 2025 legislative elections, the 2025 presidential elections in Bolivia, Ecuador, Chile, and Honduras, and the 2026 presidential elections in Costa Rica, Colombia, and Peru. The tally is only eight, but under Trump’s creative accounting, Argentina counted “ twice .” In fact, Trump explicitly endorsed candidates in only Honduras, Argentina, and Colombia. The other five winners were ideologically aligned with Trump but received no explicit endorsement. But that doesn’t suggest that US covert operations failed to interfere in those elections. If there is a silver lining to the MAGA cloud, it is that Trump reveals the intrigues of Yankee imperialism that previous US leaders preferred to obscure. Brazil – the exception to the rightist rule Progressive prospects looked dim in Brazil, especially when far-right Flávio Bolsonaro beat leftist Lula da Silva – with help from Washington – by 47.03% to 45.16% in the first-round election on October 4. Minor-party candidates who had collectively received 5.38% of the first-round vote endorsed Bolsonaro. Compounding the overwhelmingly unfavorable odds for Lula, no candidate trailing in the first round had ever won the runoff. Lula, a founder of BRICS , has been one of the world's most prominent Global South leaders, championing multipolarity, national sovereignty, and greater representation for developing countries. Much was at stake for Brazil, Latin America, and the world. Brazil has the largest population and economy in Latin America. Globally, it ranks second in rare earth reserves and seventh in population, and is approaching eighth in economic size. Brazil encompasses 60% of the Amazon rainforest . Deforestation had declined by approximately 71% since Lula returned to office in 2023; Bolsonaro advocated deregulation. In three short weeks, Lula’s Workers’ Party coalition launched an aggressive social media campaign (“ Lula’s Army ”) and mobilized voters, especially the youth and the elderly. Some 1.7 million older voters who sat out the first round, participated in the runoff, helping Lula win the election by about 4.5 million votes on October 25. Related: Did Venezuela Capitulate to Trump—or Choose Strategic Survival? Weakness on the right Beyond Lula’s upset victory, other weaknesses of the Latin American right are evident. Its electoral successes – to the extent that they were legitimate – do not necessarily correspond to popular mandates, often reflecting a “punishment vote” against incumbents. In first-round contests, José Antonio Kast received only 23.9% of the vote in Chile’s December 2025 election. Rodrigo Paz received only 32.1% of the vote in Bolivia. Neither commanded broad first-round support; both won their runoffs after other rightwing parties rallied behind them. Incumbent Daniel Noboa was in a dead heat with his progressive opponent Luisa González in Ecuador’s 2025 presidential election. Only 0.17 percentage points separated them in the first round. Even sources unfriendly to the left – the European Union and the Organization of American States – reported that Noboa abused the powers of the presidency to gain electoral advantage. In Peru’s presidential election last June, rightwing Keiko Fujimori defeated leftist Roberto Sánchez by a mere 0.27 percentage points. The razor-thin margin of victory came from highly disputed overseas ballots, making the leftist the undisputed choice of Peruvians living in Peru. In Honduras’s November 2025 election, Nasry Asfura won by a meager 0.74 percentage points after a disputed count. Abelardo de la Espriella won Colombia’s June 2026 election by less than a point. Washington’s interference in Colombia and Honduras arguably contributed to those far-right victories, for which Trump claimed credit. The fragility and corruption of the right are further illustrated by Paraguay, where President Santiago Peña was elected in 2023 with only 42.93% of the vote. On November 6, he was impeached and removed amid a banking scandal and subsequently placed under house arrest. MAGA’s declining popularity Nor have electoral victories necessarily translated into durable popular support for the right. Bolivia’s Rodrigo Paz , Chile’s José Antonio Kast, Peru’s Keiko Fujimori, and even Argentina’s Javier Milei suffer from disapproval ratings comparable to those of their mentor in the White House – and for much the same reason: failure to deliver. With the exception of Bukele, none of the new right leaders have significantly positive popularity ratings, and almost all are facing strong popular opposition. They are now expected to solve the problems that propelled them into office, particularly rising crime rates. Washington’s standing in the region has likewise declined, with public trust tumbling from 62% in 2012 to only 40% today. This fallout reflects popular dissatisfaction with politically motivated tariffs, killing people in small boats in the eastern Pacific and Caribbean, racist targeting of Latino migrants, and interference in the internal political affairs of otherwise sovereign states. Respondents in every Latin American and Caribbean country surveyed except the Dominican Republic identified the US as the world's greatest threat in the 2026 Democracy Perception Index . Of the 17 Latin American countries surveyed, 15 viewed China's influence more positively than that of the US, according to a 2026 Latinobarómetro poll. Related: Who Gets to Be a ‘Good’ Left-Wing Woman Leader? Consolidation interruptus Lula’s victory interrupts the consolidation of US-aligned political power in Latin America, demonstrating that its rightward trajectory can be reversed. As a New York Times op-ed observes : “The region’s rightward shift reflects less of an ideological transformation than a widespread frustration with governments that have overpromised and underperformed for decades.” Yet the weaknesses of the right do not necessarily translate into strength on the left. Despite Flávio Bolsonaro’s flight to the US following his defeat, Lula still faces an antagonistic legislature, powerful agribusiness and financial interests, hostile media, a sizable right-leaning evangelical movement , and an unfriendly military and police establishment. Perhaps Lula’s biggest “ stress test ” will come from the hegemon to the north, which has yet to recognize his win. More broadly, curbing US imperialism remains key to a revival of the Latin American left. In that effort, progressives can draw lessons from Lula’s remarkable mobilization of the Brazilian electorate.

Media Covered Hunter Biden

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Julie Hollar ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-08T15:41:08+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. Particularly since the Citizens United ruling, US politics has been awash in corporate and billionaire money, with both parties taking gobs of cash from corporations that have direct interests in government policies. But President Donald Trump has taken corruption to new and breathtakingly bold levels. “I have never seen such open corruption in any modern government anywhere,” said Harvard professor of government Steven Levitsky ( Guardian , 6/17/25 ). So, it’s probably not surprising to hear CNN ( 10/21/26 ) report that voters are increasingly “concerned about corruption,” ranking it only after the economy and the cost of living as a top concern. In a Gallup poll ( 9/2/26 ), a record-high 89% of respondents said they believe corruption is “widespread throughout the government in this country.” But what might be surprising is that, according to a recent NBC poll ( 10/18/26 ), a majority (56%) say corruption “is equally a problem among elected officials in both parties.” (Among those who find it worse in one party or the other, 25% point to Republicans and 17% point to Democrats.) Why would a majority of the public believe that Republicans and Democrats are equally corrupt? A FAIR study suggests that media play no small role: The US media system has given more coverage to the most prominent allegations of corruption under Biden than to those under Trump. ‘The most corrupt administration in history’ During his first term, Trump refused to divest from his business interests, raking in millions through them from foreign governments and people with interests before his administration. His solicitation of Ukrainian intervention in the 2020 presidential campaign led to his first impeachment trial . After the GOP-controlled Senate (with Mitt Romney as the sole Republican exception ) voted to acquit him—and after the Supreme Court ruling granting the president impunity for virtually anything done in office—Trump unsurprisingly entered his second term with far greater boldness. He essentially operated a pay-to-play administration that engages in regular shakedowns of major corporations and offers pardons to those with political or financial ties to him. There have been repeated accusations of insider trading under his administration. Trump made $1.4 billion from the cryptocurrency industry in 2025 alone, including $635 million from his $TRUMP memecoin, which was gobbled up by not only true believers but also those seeking political favors. (While Trump profited from fees on trade activity, investors have lost a total of $3.8 billion because of the memecoin’s unsurprising collapse.) In perhaps the most brazen incident of corruption, Trump accepted a Boeing luxury jet worth an estimated $400 million from the Qatari government to use—after spending hundreds of millions of taxpayer dollars on security upgrades—as his presidential plane. Craig Holman of Public Citizen told FAIR: The second Trump administration is far more corrupt than Trump’s first administration, and stands out as the most corrupt administration in history. Trump has made no qualms about the fact that he is going to exploit his position as president for self-enrichment. In fact, he has said that he was a fool in his first term for overlooking the opportunities for self-enrichment, given the president’s exemption from the conflict of interest law ( 18 USC 208 ). The corruption is led by Trump, but it’s been enabled by his party since their first impeachment acquittal. In Trump’s second term, the Republican-controlled Congress has refused to investigate any of the corruption scandals, implement stronger ethical standards , or include safeguards in cryptocurrency regulation to prevent Trump (and other political officials) from profiting off it. Unequal allegations During Joe Biden’s election campaigns and presidency, the GOP attempted to paint the former vice president as the pinnacle of corruption. When they took control in 2023, House Republicans launched an oversight committee investigation into the Biden family’s foreign business dealings during his vice presidency. They found that relatives and associates took in a total of $10 million from foreign companies during his tenure, but despite months of investigation, involving interviews and thousands of documents, the committee provided no evidence of Biden’s knowledge or influence. (It did lead to an unsuccessful impeachment inquiry .) As perhaps the most memorable story in the right’s campaign to make the Biden family known as the “Biden Crime Family,” readers will be familiar with Hunter Biden’s famous laptop, which purportedly contained evidence of corruption on the part of his father. First published by the New York Post ( 10/14/20 ) just weeks before the 2020 election, the laptop story was pushed by the right for years and even continues to this day , despite its failure to show any actual evidence of corruption. So on the one hand we have unsubstantiated stories of Biden family corruption, totaling $10 million in alleged graft, and on the other, we have stories of Trump corruption that total in the billions, some of which will be hard to legally prove Trump’s direct involvement (insider trading) and others in which Trump isn’t even trying to hide his self-enrichment (most of the rest, including the Qatari jet). If the US had a media ecosystem in which those stories received attention relative to their importance, it wouldn’t even be close: Trump corruption coverage would overwhelm that of Biden. The laptop and the ‘crime family’ In the distorted media ecosystem we are subjected to, reality is reversed. FAIR searched Media Cloud’s US–National dataset , which indexes 246 online outlets including all major corporate outlets, as well as right-wing sources like Daily Caller and left-wing sources like the Intercept , to compare attention to the two prominent Biden stories and two of the most prominent Trump stories. When the New York Post ( 10/14/20 ) “broke” the Hunter Biden laptop story, it prompted a huge spike of attention that has never fully gone away. Coverage increased in spring of 2022, when outlets like the New York Times ( 3/16/22 ) and Washington Post ( 3/30/22 ) published pieces that helped revive it; coverage became particularly intense the next year, which saw the GOP inquiry and the Justice Department criminal investigation. FAIR’s search found a total of 12,161 stories that mentioned “Hunter Biden” and “laptop” from the Post story’s publication through Biden’s withdrawal from the 2024 race (10/14/20-7/21/24). Right-wing outlets led the way, but MediaCloud showed even centrist outlets like the New York Times , Washington Post , USA Today and Newsweek in the top 20 outlets in terms of volume of coverage. Coverage of the GOP investigation into Biden’s alleged “crime family,” which began in January 2023 and was led by House Oversight Committee chair James Comer (R–Ky.), drew similarly high averages and peaks of coverage, and also largely petered out after Biden’s withdrawal from the presidential race (1/1/23–7/21/24). In that year and a half, MediaCloud recorded 4,887 stories mentioning “Comer” and “Biden” and “family.” Not all of these stories necessarily accused Biden of corruption; some centrist reports did work to debunk the false right-wing claims. But those big centrist outlets published enough prominent stories with murky headlines like “Biden Faces Conflict of Interest Questions That Are Being Promoted by Trump and Allies” ( New York Times , 5/1/19 ), or explaining that the Hunter Biden case throws a “ new wrench“ in ”the 2024 election ” because “Democrats will have a harder time highlighting Trump’s indictments,” to muddy the narrative and legitimize the GOP accusations (see, e.g., CJR , 10/29/19 ; Press Watch , 8/14/23 ). The Qatari jet and insider trading Compare the quantity of these Biden corruption stories to coverage of the Qatari jet, a story that broke on May 11, 2025, and continued as Trump poured taxpayer dollars into its security upgrades. In the nearly year and a half from the story launch through the end of October 2026, the media produced 3,364 stories on the jet. That means that in approximately the same amount of time, media covered the investigation into Biden family corruption almost 1.5 times more than the Qatari jet. (Note that like the Biden stories, FAIR did not filter out jet stories that did not accuse Trump of corruption.) Accusations of insider trading in Trump’s second term began in April 2025 , when large call options were placed on the market shortly before Trump announced a 90-day pause on tariffs , sending the market soaring. They continued as more such suspicious and lucrative trades happened just before major Trump announcements on Venezuela and Iran, including within Trump’s personal holdings . Coverage of Trump and insider trading from the first accusations (4/10/25) until the end of October 2026—a month longer than the Qatari jet coverage—has totaled 2,614 stories. The Biden family inquiry, at 4,887, got 1.9 times that amount. Hunter Biden’s laptop, which dominated news headlines for longer, overwhelmed the Trump stories by far more. It got 4.7 times the coverage of insider trading under Trump and 3.6 times the coverage of Trump’s Qatari jet. Even measuring them in terms of percentage of all news stories during their respective time periods, the Biden stories got far more coverage, with the family corruption story and the laptop story each averaging .10% of all content, while the Qatar jet averaged .06% and insider trading averaged .04%. ‘So vast as to be a new phenomenon’ This isn’t to say there’s no coverage of Trump’s corruption that attempts to put it into proper perspective. The New York Times editorial board ( 1/20/26 ) ran a lengthy editorial in January that tried to put a dollar amount to Trump’s self-enrichment just in the first year of his second term: $1,408,500,000. The New Yorker ( 8/11/25 ), looking a bit more broadly and including his first term, came up with a conservative total of $4 billion. Times columnist Jamelle Bouie ( 4/22/26 ) wrote: To say that President Trump is corrupt is to somehow understate the size, scope and magnitude of his corruption.It is as if you were to describe a modern thermonuclear device as a “bomb.” That is true enough, but it is not quite the truth. It does not capture the nature of the thing in full.So it goes for Trump’s corruption, which is so vast as to be a new phenomenon in American politics. Yet, just as media factchecking falters under the sheer number of Trump’s lies, these isolated efforts at contextualization fall increasingly short as Trump’s corruption balloons to nearly unimaginable proportions. That’s especially true as most right-wing media outlets’ interest in corruption would appear to apply only to Democrats. One of the most important jobs of any media outlet in a democracy is to hold those in power accountable. Democracy doesn’t work if the public doesn’t know or understand what’s happening in their government. As polling shows , people clearly care a great deal about government corruption, but if they have little sense of its mechanics, scale or worst perpetrators, they can’t easily address it. With the midterm elections suggesting a mandate for rooting it out, there’s no more critical time for journalists to step up and shine the line—brightly and consistently—on what our elected and unelected officials are doing to us. Billionaires Don't Donate

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Sonali Kolhatkar ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-08T14:51:34+00:00

The 2026 midterm elections have been the most expensive in history, with more than $11 billion being spent on political ads. While Democratic candidates have outraised Republicans in individual donations by appealing directly to voters, Republicans have received the lion’s share of money from super PACs (political action committees) funded by a small number of the wealthiest elites in the United States. The single biggest election-cycle donor is, unsurprisingly, the richest human on the planet. Elon Musk , the world’s first trillionaire, has poured more money into influencing the 2026 midterm elections than anyone has ever done in any election . His unprecedented contribution totals nearly half a billion dollars, all of it going toward electing Republicans. Musk is one of 50 donors whose combined political spending amounts to nearly $3 billion. Sixty percent of those donations are for electing Republicans. This perversion of democracy can be traced to the 2010 Supreme Court ruling, Citizens United v. FEC , which dismantled the last guardrails against political spending in elections. Since then, according to the Brennan Center for Justice , “the composition of the top 40 donors in the midterms has shifted dramatically: more money, more support for Republicans, less transparency, and new industries like crypto surging while older sectors like labor disappear.” Related: The Death of Journalism and the Rise of Paid Political Propaganda There is no reason for such contributions other than an expectation of quid pro quo favors. Republicans (and to a lesser extent, Democrats ) have long made it a central goal to reward wealthy elites and institutions. President Donald Trump’s 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill was designed to overwhelmingly favor tax cuts for the rich . The four top corporations that championed Trump’s bill—Musk’s Tesla, Amazon, Google, and Meta—enjoyed paying a paltry 4.9 percent tax rate on their massive $315 billion in profits in 2025 thanks to the new law. In other words, Musk and his ilk see political spending as an investment that will yield windfall returns. For example, Michigan Republican Senate candidate Mike Rogers is urging voters to elect him so he can “ protect Elon ” and prevent an audit of the federal government. There was a time when top policymakers, such as former President Ronald Reagan , justified enriching the wealthy by relying on the ludicrous claim that throwing money at the wealthy results in benefits trickling down to the rest of us. With decades of documentation that the rich hoard their wealth, theories of “supply-side economics” have been thoroughly debunked . Even prestigious institutions, such as the London School of Economics, have published analyses concluding that tax cuts for the rich merely mean that “[t]he rich get richer, while unemployment and economic growth are unaffected.” Today, instead of relying on junk economic theories, Republican politicians simply lie about the benefits of their tax cuts. Trump has repeatedly said that thanks to the One Big Beautiful Bill, people will pay no taxes on tips, Social Security benefits, or overtime pay—income that low-wage workers and retirees disproportionately rely on. He has even reframed the bill as the “ Working Families Tax Cuts .” Related: Why Economic Justice Requires Racial Justice What he doesn’t mention are the numerous caveats in the law that result in minuscule tax benefits. According to NBC News, on average, the refund for the 2026 tax season was only $350 more than 2025. But the president’s war on Iran has also led Americans to pay, on average, $471 more in gas prices since the end of February, while his failed tariffs cost households an average of $2,500 . On balance, Trump has fleeced Americans in his second term while repeatedly claiming the opposite. Sometimes, though, he can’t keep his lies straight. In a now-viral recording of a campaign speech in Texas, the president promoted his oft-touted bribe of gifting Americans $5,000 checks if his party wins the midterms. He then admitted that such an amount wouldn’t buy much more than “a hamburger and a small dessert” at the Florida resort he owns. The fact is that wealthy people like Trump—who is now nearly $3 billion richer from gaming the presidency to benefit himself—are so cut off from the circumstances of ordinary Americans that their moral compass no longer works. This is by design. Business schools are designed to train empathy out of humans and replace it with a self-serving ethos. University of Cambridge Judge Business School professor Chris Marquis argued in The New York Times that business schools ought to be abolished as they don’t follow curricula based on reality or include scientific inquiry into how businesses work. Rather, they train students to internalize what he calls “the ‘win-win’ story in which the conflict between private gain and public good simply evaporates.” Business school graduates walk away convinced that there are market solutions to all social problems and that when shareholders win, everyone wins. This twisted logic is reflected in treating GDP growth as a measure of a country’s overall economic health. It explains why experts declare strong economies even when the majority of middle- and low-income people can barely make ends meet. The cognitive dissonance between people’s ability to thrive and the assertions that the US economy is strong shows the stark contrast between our hand-to-mouth existence and CEO compensation packages. If we want to save democracy and ourselves, we need to stop unfettered capitalism in its tracks, and that means curbing the ability of wealthy people to influence our elections. Related: JD Vance Wants to Pay Parents to Stay Home—but With Child Care Money In Montana, voters will decide on November 3 whether their state can redefine corporations as entities that cannot influence politics. Hawaii already implemented this idea, called the “ Corporate Power Reset ” (CPR), through its legislative process in June 2026, and many other states are now considering similar legislation or ballot measures. Such laws render the Citizens United decision moot within any state that redefines corporations as politically neutral. Tom Moore, a senior fellow at the Center for American Progress, who conceived of the idea, said in an email interview that CPR “only affects corporations,” not obscenely rich people. While it would not stop men like Musk from spending money in politics, “it would keep him and other wealthy individuals from laundering their political spending through dark money groups,” Moore said. And that’s because “CPR does knock dark-money groups out of the game.” According to Moore, “Rich folks want to spend the money, fine. But they’ll have to have their names attached to it.” And while that’s not enough to reverse the flood of political spending, it is a good start. Portland’s Socialists Have Built a Political Machine

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Roman Broszkowski ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-08T14:07:45+00:00

Over the last two years, the Portland, Oregon, chapter of the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) has developed a reputation for punching far above its city’s weight. The chapter sits just behind the New York, Los Angeles, and Washington, DC, chapters in terms of size, and roughly one in two hundred Portlanders is a member (compared to about one in 530 New Yorkers). Today one-third of Portland’s city council actively participates in the group’s Socialists in Office Committee (SIOC); this election cycle, the chapter ran candidates for state and county office. Yet for most of the chapter’s history, elections weren’t a focus, and candidates for office were often kept at arm’s length. “For a significant period where I was involved, a number of people just believed that candidates couldn’t do anything for you,” said Dave Coburn, the chapter’s former Electoral Working Group cochair. “People really only wanted to do things via ballot measure.” That would start to change with the fallout of Portland’s long-lasting George Floyd protests. The protests supercharged existing frustrations with the city’s political status quo — and created an opening for outsiders to achieve significant electoral victories. Electoral Reforms Open the Gates Few American cities experienced 2020 like Portland. For almost a year, the city’s downtown saw sustained demonstrations and an intense response from local and federal law enforcement. The protests and ensuing criticism of the aggressive actions taken by local police hobbled the tenure of moderate Democratic Mayor Ted Wheeler and energized popular calls to reform the city’s commission, its governing body at the time. In 2022, voters approved Measure 26-228, changing Portland’s legislative structure from four at-large commissioners to a twelve-member city council drawn from four multimember districts elected with ranked-choice voting. Combined with a small donor public financing scheme enacted in 2016, this system significantly lowered the bar for political participation in the 2024 election. In many ways, the new electoral format catered well to DSA’s strengths. Smaller-district and multimember elections benefited candidates who could mobilize large field operations while also being able to hold a clear lane within a crowded field — unlike in previous elections, candidates would only need 25 percent +1 to win. Similarly, ranked-choice voting allowed candidates to pick up votes from coalition allies, benefiting those with platforms that spoke to a wide range of voters, while the matching funds program helped campaigns critical of the city’s traditional donor class to compete. These reforms came right as Portland DSA was trying to revitalize itself. The pandemic had seen a sharp downturn in the chapter’s membership, and organizers began looking for opportunities to rebuild their capacity. One came as part of a push to establish universal pre-K that the chapter had initiated in mid-2019 with the goal of getting a measure on the November 2020 ballot. A month after the Universal Pre-School Now! (UP Now!) coalition submitted the proposed language for the measure, pandemic restrictions were enacted in Oregon, hampering signature collection. Simultaneously, UP Now! faced competition from a more moderate universal pre-K project at the county level called Pre-School 4 All (P4A). That summer, the chapter threw itself into building support for the campaign and managed to collect more than 32,000 signatures. Its success facilitated a merger with an updated P4A in order to avoid both proposals appearing on the ballot and potentially failing due to voter confusion. The unified measure passed overwhelmingly and marked one of Portland DSA’s first real electoral wins. Both of the chapter’s future 2024 city council candidates, Mitch Green and Tiffany Koyama Lane, were involved in the effort. “It was an interesting test of our organizing ability,” said Green. “We were able to pass that, and that built the foundations for our city council races.” The chapter also saw large success during the 2021 Nabisco strike and the 2023 Portland teachers’ strike, when its members organized fundraisers, held rallies supporting strikers, and walked picket lines. Both periods brought in new members and reactivated existing ones. The work also surprisingly helped convince the chapter to take another look at running candidates, according to Coburn. “Having some elected officials weigh in on the side of the strikers proved to be more impactful to the strikers than many of us thought it would be and also ended up sometimes being more impactful on negotiations than many of us thought it would be,” he said. Entering City Council By the time candidates began announcing their campaigns in 2024 under the new system, Portland DSA was well positioned to run. It had proven it could manage a strong ground game, had built a name for itself among the city’s progressive ecosystem, and, importantly, felt that it could recruit strong candidates. Still, worried about potentially overextending itself, the chapter only endorsed two candidates, Green and Koyama Lane. However, when the dust settled, not only had both won, but so had two more DSA-aligned candidates who hadn’t received the chapter’s endorsement. “I think we were concerned about endorsing other folks who were progressive but were not close with DSA,” said Carolyn Roderique, the current cochair of Portland DSA. “But the election showed that we had so much more of a lane than we expected.” Within a single cycle, DSA had gone from no representation in city politics to holding a third of the council. Two additional progressives were also elected, forming a larger progressive bloc nicknamed “Peacock.” “It’s short for progressive caucus (P-Cauc),” explained Green. “And then, also, peacocks are an ostentatious bird.” Following the election, Portland DSA created a Socialists in Office Committee in order to coordinate with councilors and facilitate an ongoing dialogue. “There have been a ton of benefits [to having the SIOC],” said Koyama Lane, who was up for reelection this year. “Just having that time where we can share what’s upcoming, give a heads-up . . . and then also for them to bring back questions to us too.” The SIOC has also helped the chapter’s candidates cement their status as a bloc, which has helped amplify their power within city council and during elections. In addition to Koyama Lane, Green, and Angelita Morillo — one of the DSA-aligned candidates who did not receive the chapter’s endorsement in 2024 — also faced reelection this year. All three won. “[Being part of the SIOC] made it so like me, Angelita, and Mitch — people [could] just donate to all three of us at the same time through DSA,” said Koyama Lane. Increasing Political Success As members of the city’s legislative branch, DSA’s city councilors have focused on oversight and accountability. “We’ve got councilors grilling the city administrator on why [they] didn’t disclose a bunch of housing money and why [they are] spending that on sweeps instead of rental assistance and that sort of thing,” said Green. “We’ve been fighting very publicly and racking up wins.” Those victories — including banning AI price-fixing and fining the landlord behind the South Portland US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) facility — appear to be feeding Portland DSA’s popularity. According to reporting by The Oregonian , recent polling has found that the DSA has a 61 percent approval rating among Oregon Democrats, with 44 percent of Portland voters saying that the group’s endorsement would make them more likely to support a candidate. For Portland DSA members, these numbers reinforce the validity of the lessons they say they’ve taken from their recent victories. Xi Jinping’s Chinese Dream Has Replaced the Maoist Credo

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Teemu Ruskola ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-08T14:07:45+00:00

Since 1978, China has undergone a series of seismic changes in its economic order that have radically altered its citizens’ relationship to both labor and land. They have unmade the socialist working class and are in the process of expropriating the peasantry, transforming both into a propertyless proletariat. At the same time, China’s political structure has not undergone a similar revolution. Accordingly, the contradiction between its systems of economic and political organization is increasingly acute. A Single Story The disjunctive fates of rural and urban China have been part of a single story ever since the establishment of the People’s Republic. It is the impoverished hinterland that subsidized the rise of the urban industry under state socialism. Unlike in England during its industrial revolution, this development did not require the expropriation of rural land and a concomitant displacement of the peasantry. There, the enclosure movement provided both the capital and the labor for industrial development. In China, in contrast, there was no shortage of labor, and the state preferred to keep the peasants precisely where they were rather than allowing them to flow into cities and coalesce into a potentially explosive proletariat of the kind that had become common in much of the developing world. To obtain industrial capital, the state simply extracted the economic surplus produced by the peasants while keeping the peasants themselves in place. This was the essence of primitive socialist accumulation. After China’s transition to state capitalism, the countryside has continued to subsidize urban development, supporting not only state capital but also private Chinese capital and global capital. Urban industry no longer extracts just the product of rural labor but the peasants themselves, albeit on a rotational and provisional basis. This has taken the form of a “floating population” made up of hundreds of millions of semi-proletarianized migrant workers who continue to rely in part on collectively owned, nontransferable rural land cultivated by villagers left behind. With the enclosure of rural land, China is in the process of converting its migrant labor force into permanent city dwellers, transforming an existing reserve army of workers into a reserve army of consumers as well — making them fully proletarianized. Moreover, it is not only the former migrant workers who are proletarianized as their displacement becomes permanent: the arrival of agrarian capitalism proletarianizes even those who remain in the countryside. Internal Contradictions The exploitation of the countryside for industrial development is emphatically not unique to China. Indeed, when viewed from a longer historical perspective, the birth of the new Chinese working class seems rather unexceptional: we have come to take it for granted that people work for wages. However, the sociologist Charles Tilly observes that as recently as 1700, only about 10 percent of the global workforce made their living by selling their labor power — “by selling themselves,” in Karl Marx’s phrase — whereas today, most of us do so. What is distinctive about the fate of the Chinese working class is the specific way in which state socialism and state capitalism have become conjoined in China, with the former providing the foundation for the latter, as well as the temporal and spatial dimensions of this evolution. Processes of commodification of land and labor that took place over centuries in England have been compressed into just a few decades. What is more, the People’s Republic of China claims an imperial footprint more encompassing than any other twentieth-century revolutionary state. Because of both the staggering speed of its transformation and its equally staggering size, China plays an oversize role in the development of global capitalism. Accordingly, the incorporation of rural and urban land, as well as rural and urban labor, into a single regime of state capitalism has both local and global implications. The massive post-1978 reorganization of land, labor, and capital has left China with a series of internal contradictions. Perhaps the most outstanding one is the disjunction between its economic and political orders. The philosopher Jiwei Ci describes it as a contradiction between a radically new organization of desire and a largely unchanged political and ideological structure. As the state has disavowed its material responsibilities to workers, economic relations have been reconfigured as those of freedom — essentially the freedom of being left alone to fend for oneself. Ci call this “de facto freedom,” a kind of freedom by default. While it is negative in origin, signifying the retreat of the state rather than a result of a social struggle, it has opened the way for seeking the satisfaction of private desires. To the extent that the state wants China to become a nation of consumers, cultivating this desiring subject has become an economic imperative: while needs are limited, desires are boundless, and only desire can fuel consumption without limits. Ci contrasts this de facto economic freedom with freedom as a positive moral or political value. Battler Poetry In the absence of a liberal ideology valorizing it, freedom by default is not capable of giving meaning to material prosperity, even if one is fortunate (or well connected) enough to have prospered in the new regime of economic inequality. It is notable, however, that the dominant language of public critique today is overwhelmingly concerned with “social justice” — associated with labor and land protests as well anti-corruption agitation more generally — rather than “democracy” or political freedom more generally. This reflects at least in part the fact that the discourse of social justice allows people to appeal to the state in terms of its own professed commitment to socialism. In some ways, this makes it potentially an even more damaging critique than a direct challenge to the political order: the gaping, and growing, economic inequality is a threat not only to social stability but to the very legitimacy of the regime. At the same time, the turn to the idiom of social justice rather than Maoism is a sign of the diminished nature of Chinese socialism. As a result of four decades of “reform,” there exists today, for the first time in China’s history, a vast industrial proletariat of both urban and rural origin. But despite its unquestionable existence in a socioeconomic sense, it has not yet emerged as a political subject with a collective identity. In addition to material obstacles to labor organization, workers do not have access to an adequate public language in which to articulate their grievances. Responding to these political and economic contradictions, Xi Jinping’s ascendancy has coincided with the rise of neo-Maoist rhetoric. One of the most notable forms worker protest has taken is dagong shige , a form of subaltern writing by migrant workers, often translated as “battler poetry.” However, its political efficacy remains circumscribed: it is subject to state sanction when too critical, and to state co-optation when less so. After Deng Xiaoping’s singular emphasis on economic development and the need to allow some people “to get rich first,” Xi Jinping is mindful of the need to balance growth with escalating demands for social justice and environmental protection, stressing the need for “common prosperity” ( gongtong fuyu ) and the construction of “an ecological civilization” ( shengtai wenming ). The invocations of ecological civilization began under Hu Jintao but are now associated with Xi, who wrote the term into China’s constitution in 2018, in symbolic recognition of the damage wrought by rural industry in particular: land and water contamination, soil erosion, environmental disease, declining food safety, the depletion of fisheries, and so on. In the same vein, Xi has made the eradication of rural poverty one of his signature initiatives. In 2021, he declared that China had achieved a “total elimination of absolute poverty,” with every village in China above the official poverty line. Yet even this victory — partly semantic, given the arbitrariness of any official definition of poverty — relied on neoliberal means, such as teaching electronic marketing to peasants and making entire villages switch to cash crops in order to raise their incomes by the announced deadline. As Ryan Martinez Mitchell observes, what might have been a radical redistributive project turned instead into a campaign “to embed entrepreneurialism as a character trait into society at large,” including rural China. Selective Deployment However, the state’s reliance on neoliberal solutions is far from unqualified. Chinese neoliberalism is similar to the North Atlantic variant to the extent that it makes individuals responsible for themselves in economic circumstances that are beyond their control. However, it does not represent a full subsumption of the state by the logic of capital. It is a selective, strategic, and ultimately political deployment of market institutions in the service of the party state. China’s future is therefore not determined by the dictates of capital alone, nor is the outcome of the rural enclosure preordained. If it so desires, the Chinese leadership can recalibrate its strategies of industrial development and rethink its current trajectory. In fact, because of its centralized structures of political authority, it is able to act more decisively than a liberal capitalist regime. Nonetheless, and despite the push and pull of policy, the long-term trend has been to continue enclosing rural land and commercializing the Chinese lifeworld. At the same time, it is important to recognize that this trend has improved peasants’ lives in significant ways. Most villages now have running water, electricity, paved roads, and internet, for example. The arrival of capital has helped diversify the rural economy. While there is great regional variation, there are villages that bear a resemblance to middle-class suburbs. On the other hand, even the households that manage to hold on to their land in defiance of “dragonhead enterprises” and other new commercial actors become fully reliant on buying inputs from the market and cultivating crops for the market. The less fortunate landless peasants who are unable to find rural employment are in turn compelled to join the urban proletariat, even if material conditions improve for at least a subset of those who make it in the commercialized countryside. Heeding calls by the agronomist Wen Tiejun and others to find a “Third Way,” some villages have sought to return to collective farming. It is indeed the case that, at least in principle, the commodification of rural land need not result in a complete predominance of private actors. If a village chooses collectively not to sell the rights to the use of its land, it can replace cultivation by individual households with a scaled-up agricultural cooperative — one in which each household exchanges its land allotment for shares in a village-based enterprise. The resulting shareholding cooperative is in some ways an agricultural version of the industrial collectives of rural China that marked the beginning of economic reforms in the 1980s, engaging in commercial farming rather than industrial production. As a form of self-development by the village, establishing such cooperatives is undoubtedly preferable to allowing outside commercial actors take over, yet by no means do they herald a return to the socialist collectives of the Maoist era. Even collectively owned farming enterprises are essentially commercial actors, subsumed by agrarian capitalism and relying on markets both for buying their inputs — including labor — and for selling their output. Safety Net In recognition of increasing economic stratification throughout society, the state has sought to build at least a minimal safety net. In this respect, the evolution of Chinese welfare institutions has been broadly similar to the development of poor laws in England, initially enacted in response to the enclosure of the commons and the rising numbers of paupers without any means of subsistence. At the same time, although the Elizabethan Poor Laws were motivated by an anxiety about the state of the realm as a whole, they continued to maintain a medieval commitment to territorially based charity, funded by local ratepayers and available only to paupers settled within a parish. Under the so-called settlement laws, it became as difficult for a pauper to change his official place of settlement as for a Chinese peasant under Maoism to change his hukou registration. By obstructing mobility, both institutions thwarted the development of a national labor market even as land was increasingly transformed into private property. In the wake of revolutionary upheaval in France, the poor laws were further fortified in 1795 by indexing welfare to the price of bread — a reform that Prime Minister George Canning credited with having “saved England from a revolution” of its own. In 1834, the New Poor Law finally abolished the centuries-old parish-based regime and replaced it with a centralized system that limited welfare strictly to the disabled and the ill. The able-bodied poor were left with no other option than the workhouse, so stigmatized and so horrific that only the truly desperate would enter it. Finally, a nationwide labor market emerged, leading to the proliferation of slums in England’s industrial cities. In general outline, Xi’s urbanization policies and his restricted program of social welfare envision a similar transition — except for the state’s determination to prevent the emergence of slums. Even while calling for a more robust welfare system, Xi has consistently held back on public funding, preferring social security programs financed through employers and individual contributions, to prevent a fall into “the idleness-breeding trap of welfarism.” Welfare as such is for orphans, the disabled, and the very old, whereas the able-bodied indigent must find work, or else rely on family for support. Performance Legitimacy In England, however, the abolition of the old poor laws coincided with major political agitation. It was preceded by the Reform Act of 1832, which expanded the franchise to the middle class and opened the way to the eventual inclusion of the working class. China, in contrast, has offered no democratic reforms, nor have they been at the top of protesters’ demands. On the contrary, even as the state is making its citizens turn to the market for their economic needs, it continues to appeal to the political subject of Maoism, one that is expected to sacrifice for the collective good. At the same time, and somewhat improbably, the state has begun to cultivate neo-Confucian rhetoric as well. In lieu of waging class struggle, the people are called on to build a “harmonious society” ( hexie shehui ) — the less protest, the better. In this vein, Xi has promoted the “Chinese dream” ( Zhongguo meng ) as the most urgent political desideratum of the millennium. This dream is not merely a Chinese rendering of its American counterpart. The American dream belongs to the individual, hearkening to the myth of Horatio Alger, whereas the Chinese dream is that of the nation. Sublating socialism into nationalism and fed by an intensive cultivation of memories of the so-called century of humiliation that followed China’s defeat in the Opium Wars, it seeks to “rejuvenate” the nation and return it to its rightful place at the heart of a Sinocentric world. The realization of China’s long overdue dream — one that was once a reality and now must be restored — may entail personal sacrifice, including temporary economic inequality, but everyone must play his part. This is a deferral of equality now for the sake of a future dream. The intensifying personality cult around Xi may be interpreted as an attempt to forge unity in the absence of a political consensus, much as Mao Zedong relied on charismatic leadership to overcome social and political fragmentation. The public promotion of “Xi Jinping Thought” — it has been enshrined in the Preamble to the Constitution, along with Marxism-Leninism, Mao Zedong Thought, and Deng Xiaoping Theory — has a similar aim, although its ideological vitality remains questionable. The anthropologist John Osburg characterizes China as “a post-belief” society. The People’s Republic of China officially remains, and must remain, a socialist state, but its credibility in claiming that label is eroding by the day. At the same time, the party is losing its ability to draw on its historical legitimacy. Mao Zedong and his comrades rightfully claimed credit for having unified the nation and secured its international autonomy. In contrast, the current generation of princelings in charge fought no revolutionary battles but rely on inherited credentials instead. This is indeed why they appeal to a “rejuvenated” future rather than a revolutionary past. The Bases of US Empire Are Crumbling

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Costas Lapavitsas ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-08T14:07:45+00:00

Imperialism is again at the forefront of world politics. Large-scale war has become a permanent feature of the present era, from Ukraine to Gaza. Donald Trump’s second term has added a tariff war, threats to annex Greenland, the abduction of Venezuela’s president, and, together with Israel, an unprovoked war on Iran. This is not the whim of one man, for the confrontation with China began under Trump’s first term and deepened under Joe Biden. At the same time, politics across the mature capitalist countries is in deep turmoil. In the United States, the Left has become a force in mass politics, with a democratic socialist as mayor of New York and widespread opposition to Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), Israel’s genocide in Palestine, and the war on Iran. In France, governments have fallen one after the other, and social unrest is spreading. But the far right is also rising powerfully, from France’s Rassemblement National and Germany’s Alternative für Deutschland to Reform UK and Austria’s Freiheitliche Partei Österreichs, alongside the Trump White House. Left and Right draw on the same realities of stagnant wages, industrial decline, and degraded public services. If the Left is to offer a credible political program to the working class and the poor, it must grasp what links resurgent imperialism to the decay of capitalist accumulation in the historic core of the world economy. With millions of people now looking for answers, this means offering a politics of class rupture. The US Paradox The starting point is the paradox at the heart of US capitalism, which is also the central paradox of contemporary imperialism. In 1945, the United States produced about half of world manufacturing output; today it produces roughly one-sixth . Productivity growth has been feeble since the great financial crisis of 2007–9, investment has remained weak, and real wages have stagnated for over a generation. Yet the dollar’s share of official reserves has slipped only slightly below 60 percent. US productive preeminence has declined, but the monetary hierarchy has hardened. The United States alone settles its international obligations in its own liabilities. Everyone else must acquire dollars, manage dollar exposures, and adjust to the policy of the Federal Reserve. Foreign investors hold around $9 trillion of US Treasury bonds , about a third of the marketable stock, and central banks across the world are stuffed with US public paper. The material basis of US hegemony has thus shifted from domestic industrial strength to the capacity to set and enforce the rules that organize global production, finance, and money. This is also where its imperial power truly lies. US corporations have flourished within this structure. Among the largest globally active enterprises, US corporations take around 44 percent of profits and hold 34 percent of intellectual property, compared to 11 and 14 percent for their Chinese counterparts. US multinationals led the globalization of production from the 1980s onward, exporting productive capital and building the global chains that shifted industrial capacity abroad, while relying on the dollar to run them. This was a triumph for private US capital, but it hollowed out industry within the United States and created the social wreckage that became Trump’s political raw material. Global monetary, financial, and corporate dominance has gone together with domestic deterioration. This is why neither of the two main US parties has been able to reverse industrial decline. Trump’s 2018 tariffs brought little reshoring, since many corporations simply moved their supply chains to Vietnam and Mexico. Biden kept the tariffs and added the subsidies of the Inflation Reduction Act and the CHIPS Act but left US capital’s global strategy untouched. Trump now wants to restore domestic industry while at the same time defending the privileges of multinationals. One of his first acts on returning to office was to withdraw from the Base Erosion and Profit Shifting framework of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), which more than 130 countries had signed, including the Biden-era United States. It would have allowed states to tax a share of the profits generated by multinationals within their borders and to impose a minimum tax of 15 percent, curbing the transfer of profits to tax havens through intellectual property held offshore. But Trump’s aims cannot be reconciled. To see why, we must look at how production and finance are organized around the world today. Lenin, the Dollar, and the F-35 Vladimir Lenin insisted in 1916 that imperialism must be grasped through the dominant mechanisms of accumulation of its time. Drawing primarily on Rudolf Hilferding , he identified these as the fusion of banking and industrial capital into finance capital, the export of capital, and the territorial division of the world among rival powers. His method remains indispensable, but the concrete forms of imperialism have changed profoundly. Applied to contemporary capitalism, Lenin’s method reveals two structural foundations of imperialism: first, the pairing of globalized productive capital with internationalized financial capital; second, the dollar as world money, which turns that pairing into a hierarchy enforced ultimately by US military power. World money is a crucial element of imperialism today but a consideration that classical Marxist theory never developed. Contemporary production is organized through global chains coordinated by multinationals — mostly US, European, and Japanese — that control design, intellectual property, logistics, and access to markets. Finance has become global alongside production, but the two have not fused as in Hilferding’s time. They form a pairing in which neither side simply commands the other. Productive capital generates value and surplus value; financial capital supplies the credit, liquidity, and means of payment without which global production could not run. US capital has led on both sides of the pairing: its multinationals built the chains, and its banks and asset managers globalized finance. The pairing of capitals operates in a world market that has no world state, no world central bank, and no world court of justice. What holds it together is world money, which must serve across separate jurisdictions as unit of account, means of payment, and reserve of value, especially in a crisis. Only the dollar performs these functions. A manufacturing supplier in Vietnam or Mexico ships components today and waits sixty or ninety days to be paid, but wages and inputs must be paid now. The gap has to be financed, and because the lead firm in the chain invoices in dollars, it is financed in dollars at rates priced off dollar benchmarks. This is not an economic choice but the price of admission to the chain. Between 1999 and 2019, about three-quarters of exports in the Asia-Pacific and virtually all exports in the Americas were invoiced in dollars. Through these chains, the monetary policy of the Federal Reserve reaches directly into workshops on the other side of the world. The state that issues the necessary dollars is not just another power pursuing its interests abroad. It is the authority that ultimately guarantees settlement, liquidity, and enforceability for globally mobile capital, deciding whose liabilities count as money and whose balance sheets must adjust. Production remains the foundation, but the hegemon’s command over world money is the hinge of imperial power, the point at which the pairing of capitals is disciplined across the world. The hierarchy becomes fully visible in a crisis. In 2008 and again in 2020, the Fed opened dollar swap lines to a select circle of fourteen central banks. Those inside stabilized their financial systems in days. Those outside, including India, Indonesia, and South Africa, faced currency depreciation, loss of reserves, and austerity. Sanctions and exclusion from payment systems complete the apparatus of coercion, as the freezing of approximately $300 billion of Russian central bank assets in 2022 showed. The fourteen account for roughly 55 percent of US imports and 60 percent of US exports. They also host more than 90 percent of US military personnel stationed abroad. The map of monetary privilege and the map of US military power largely coincide. The dollar and the F-35 are two moments of a single structure of domination, and US air and naval power guards the choke points through which world trade moves. This is imperialism running through the balance sheets of firms, banks, and states, backed by military power. It does not need to plant a flag over a territory or to establish an expensive colonial administration. Financialization Mark II Crucially, the financial side of the pairing has itself been transformed since the great financial crisis of 2007–9. Before the crash there was Financialization Mark I, centered on globally active commercial banks, which turned workers’ homes into a prime terrain of extraction through mortgages and consumer debt. At its peak, bank profits in the United States approached 40 percent of all corporate profits. The crash ended that regime. It was replaced by Financialization Mark II, resting on shadow banks — above all, asset managers such as BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street. Research that I and colleagues have recently done on 426 major asset managers shows that the equity they manage rose from $13 trillion in 2013 to $46 trillion by mid-2025. That is roughly 40 percent of the equity of all billion-dollar listed companies in the world. In every region outside North America, US asset managers are the largest group of foreign investors. They represent imperial power exercised through balance sheets rather than territory. Unlike commercial banks that draw their profits mostly from the spread between the lending and the borrowing rates of interest, asset managers depend on the gap between the rate of profit and the rate of interest — what Hilferding called founder’s profit. Such profits are realized primarily through rising asset prices. Stock market inflation has thus become a leading channel of financial accumulation. The entire edifice of Mark II rests on the state. US gross public debt rose from just over 60 percent of GDP in 2007 to more than 120 percent by 2025. At the same time, the Federal Reserve absorbed public debt securities on a historic scale, created vast amounts of new dollars on its balance sheet, and became dealer of last resort for the shadow banks and others in the money markets. Whatever the neoliberal rhetoric of free markets might say, contemporary capitalism survives through state hyperinterventionism, but on behalf of finance, not to rebuild production. Investment and productivity outside a narrow set of sectors have consequently stayed weak. The vast AI boom does not alter this picture; it confirms it. Spending on data centers and chips is concentrated in a handful of giant corporations, financed from their enormous cash holdings and increasingly by vast debt, while asset managers pour capital into the same few stocks. The promised productivity gains remain largely a matter of expectation, already priced into shares long before they appear in production. This is still primarily accumulation through asset price inflation, not yet a true transformation of production. The historic core of the world economy thus continues to drift in an interregnum. The old order has lost its dynamism, but no new one has emerged. Subordinate Financialization Still, the reach of the Fed extends far beyond the borders of the United States, since the dollars it creates are world money. The dollar dependence created by global production chains accumulates into national pressures: dollar debts that must be serviced, profits that must be repatriated, technology payments that must flow abroad. The burden falls heaviest on the Global South, and the result is subordinate financialization. This is not an immature version of the financialization of the United States or Britain but a distinct form produced by subordination in both production and finance. A peripheral central bank cannot expand its balance sheet at will or ignore the exchange rate when it forms monetary policy, since it operates in the highly precarious environment of loanable capital flowing in and out of the country. Much of that capital is managed by the same US asset managers that dominate Mark II. In October 2025, the average policy interest rate in Brazil, India, Indonesia, Mexico, and South Africa stood 4 percentage points above the Fed’s. This is a liquidity tribute, levied by the monetary hierarchy itself. High rates choke domestic investment, attract speculative inflows, push up the exchange rate, and undermine domestic manufacturing. Peripheral states are forced to stockpile dollars as self-insurance and park them in US Treasuries, while their own governments and firms borrow at far higher rates. Resources that could build factories, railways, and hospitals are, in effect, lent to Washington. Tribute on this scale needs local beneficiaries. Large firms in peripheral countries borrow cheaply in dollars and invest in domestic financial assets at high yields; banks profit from the public debt issued to absorb the inflows; and sections of the middle class enjoy cheap imports. In a peripheral country, the exchange rate is a class weapon as much as an economic variable. And the United States does not need a viceroy to collect the tribute, for it flows through the ordinary workings of the world market. Empire Without Hegemony China presents the opposite picture to the United States. It accounts for close to 30 percent of world manufacturing value added and around 15 percent of world merchandise exports. State-owned enterprises dominate its strategic sectors, and it has built institutions that partly bypass the US-led order, such as the Belt and Road Initiative and the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank. Yet China is not attempting to construct a separate imperial bloc or to acquire territory. It seeks a determining voice in the rules that govern trade, technology, and investment, and, in the longer term, new arrangements for world money. That is where it is at its weakest. The renminbi accounts for less than 3 percent of global reserves and international payments, and less than 5 percent of world trade is invoiced in it, against roughly half in dollars. Chinese public debt does not serve as global collateral, the People’s Bank of China offers no liquidity backstop to the world market, and China’s own payment system processes little more than 12 percent of the dollar volume handled by the Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication. The barrier is structural, not a matter of will. World money requires deep markets in safe public liabilities, legal protection for foreign creditors, and an open capital account. The capital controls and public command over finance that protected China’s industrialization are precisely what prevent the renminbi from becoming world money. China is a productive superpower obliged to settle its obligations in a currency it does not issue and to hold its reserves in its rival’s debt. Nor is Chinese domestic accumulation free of contradictions. Since 2007–9, growth and profitability have fallen to a “ new normal .” China still invests roughly double the US share of GDP, but the returns on that investment have declined sharply. The rational course would be to lower investment and systematically raise the incomes and consumption of Chinese workers, but the economic, social, and political risks of doing so are enormous. The result is an impasse. The hegemon cannot recover its productive foundations, and the challenger cannot issue world money. Neither can impose a resolution, and neither will accept permanent subordination. There is no orderly path of succession within a monetary hierarchy that is singular, coercive, and increasingly contested, and therein lies the danger for the world. The United States has stopped acting as the hegemon, that is, as the power that presents its own interests as the general interest. It acts instead as the biggest and most aggressive contestant in an increasingly open field, treating even its allies as subordinates. When Washington demanded sanctions on Russian energy after 2022, Germany accepted them at severe cost to its own industrial base. Yet US military predominance is no longer uncontested, and the war on Iran has shown that even weaker opponents cannot simply be made to submit. The situation echoes the rivalries before 1914. World war is not inevitable, but proxy wars, reserve seizures, payment exclusion, and technology embargoes have become an embedded feature of world politics. Should the Left, then, cheer for de-dollarization and the BRICS? Weakening the monopoly of the dollar would loosen a crucial mechanism of domination. But the putative challengers are neither a socialist bloc nor a new anti-colonial movement. They are states competing in the world market and seeking ways around the dollar, not a fairer world money. Multipolarity is not a class category. The Left should oppose sanctions, the liquidity tribute, and the forced accumulation of reserves without enlisting in anyone’s camp. The Politics of Class Rupture One of the most encouraging political developments in this gloomy conjuncture is the US left seriously debating how to win and use governing power. Max Elbaum has rightly argued here that it cannot limit its positive platform to domestic issues while saying only what it opposes in foreign policy. The analysis above shows why. The domestic and the imperial are two sides of the same structure, and a left government would confront that structure from day one. Success is not possible without overturning the prevailing capitalist mechanisms of social, economic, and political power. This is precisely the politics of class rupture that the Left should be offering to the millions seeking change. I saw what this means at close quarters in Greece in 2015. Syriza’s election win had little significance in itself — it was at best only the beginning. A left-wing government that truly sought to save Greece from the disaster of the bailouts had to break the established mechanisms of power. That could only be done by using the machinery of the state in conjunction with the mass movement. To deliver results, a left-wing government would be obliged to transform the state. The challenge crystallized on one issue — as it typically does in times of a radical upheaval. That was, to stay in the European Monetary Union (EMU), or to exit? Breaking with the euro would have required capital controls, public command over the banks and money, and a far-reaching reorganization of the economy that could have shifted power toward workers and the poor. Inevitably, there would have been a profound rupture with the entrenched class and imperial interests stifling Greece, both of which threw their weight behind the policy of remaining with the EMU. The mere thought was enough to send shivers down the spine of the timid Syriza leadership. Abject surrender followed. There is a lesson here for the US left — and beyond it — despite the vast difference between the preeminent imperial power and a small European power. If it seeks to offer a politics of rupture that tilts the balance decisively in favor of workers and the poor, it must use and transform the machinery of the state while drawing on the strength of the mass movement. It should also be clear that there will be fierce and violent opposition on all levels. Success will not come without decisively overturning the domestic and the imperial configuration of class power in the United States. Of that there can be no doubt. It is almost redundant to say that the task is of world-historic importance. Popular Sovereignty Against Empire There is, however, plenty of historical experience on which the US left can draw as it seeks a class politics that could bring success. The first step involves developing a program of reversing domestic US productive decline, with the working class in the lead, as has already been argued for other developed capitalist countries. That means public investment on a scale not seen in generations, public banks directing credit to production, and a central bank brought within a democratic economic plan instead of serving as dealer of last resort for BlackRock. It also means restoring capital controls as a normal instrument of policy and extending public ownership in energy, infrastructure, and housing, where social need and private profit sharply diverge. And it further means democratic, worker-based control of corporations, since a bureaucratic state corporation run on the priorities of capital changes nothing essential. Such a program would reverse the global accumulation strategy of US capital, subordinating multinational and financial capital to democratically determined priorities. In short, it would aim to deglobalize and definancialize the US economy. This is neither isolationism nor autarky. The aim is an open economy that continues to trade, invest, and cooperate across borders but on terms set democratically rather than by multinationals and footloose financial capital. Openness should serve the reconstruction of production at home and genuine cooperation abroad, not the global strategy of US capital. A program of this kind ultimately rests on the issue of sovereignty. The problem is that this term has been appropriated by the Right, which uses it to assert the unrestrained power of the US state abroad while defending the interests of capital at home. But for the Left, sovereignty has a different and older content, that of popular sovereignty. If democracy is to have real meaning, workers and the poor ought to have both voice and power at the workplace, the neighborhood, the town, the city, and the country. Popular sovereignty does not mean only electing a government that spends more on social care, housing, and infrastructure while taxing the rich, necessary as that is. It requires building the organized power of working people — channeling the renewed strike militancy of the United Auto Workers and the drives to organize the nonunion factories of the South — alongside the communities and movements that have resisted ICE, the genocide in Palestine, and the war on Iran. Since, moreover, there is no democratic space beyond the borders of the nation-state, a program of class rupture would also rest on national sovereignty. This is an even thornier issue for the Left to grasp. In a subordinate country, such as Greece in 2015, national sovereignty means breaking out of imperialist domination. But the United States sits at the top of the imperialist hierarchy, and the sovereignty of the US state is a lever through which other nations are subordinated. For the United States, therefore, national sovereignty raises directly the question of what the nation is and which class constitutes it. For the Right, the nation is the imperial state and the capital it serves. For the Left, national sovereignty is anti-imperialist at its core and means fighting against the oppression of others. Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels put it plainly , after stating that workers have no country: the proletariat must “rise to be the leading class of the nation, must constitute itself the nation,” and is in that sense “itself national, though not in the bourgeois sense of the word.” This is the opposite of nationalism, which asks workers to line up behind their own ruling class. The task is then clear. The US working class can constitute itself as the nation only by taking command of the instruments of imperial power — the Fed, the sanctions machinery of the Treasury, the military budget — and ending their use to subordinate other nations. US workers gain nothing durable from the dollar order. The more Washington pursues imperial primacy, the more it hollows out the domestic economy. Antiwar politics and anti-capitalist politics are one struggle. Opposition to war that leaves domestic property relations untouched is incomplete, and transformation at home that ignores the imperial order will be crushed by it. The system that extracts value from the periphery is the same system that hollows out the core. That is what gives working-class internationalism a material content, instead of leaving it a liberal slogan compatible with the free movement of capital. It is the ground on which a genuinely internationalist socialist politics can be built. On October 9, European Union Trade Commissioner Maroš Šefčovič announced an agreement on hybrid vehicles, following two days of talks with Chinese Commerce Minister Wang Wentao in Beijing. According to Šefčovič, over the next four years or so, Chinese exports of full and plug-in hybrids to the EU are expected to fall below half of previous forecasts. The specific quotas, minimum prices, or monitoring mechanisms were not disclosed. The agreement is provisional, with further talks scheduled for 2027. Shortly before Šefčovič’s trip, German Chancellor Friedrich Merz and French President Emmanuel Macron had been putting pressure on the European Commission. In early October, in a letter to Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and a joint paper , they called for the commission to be given more powers to act against market distortions by non-EU countries. A new instrument would allow companies, or even entire countries, to be excluded from the EU’s single market in serious cases. The paper names plug-in hybrid vehicles as one of such a potential case. Neither document mentions China by name, but the initiative is mainly aimed at Beijing. For Germany’s Merz, a lot is at stake politically. According to recent polling , his Christian Democrats (CDU, plus their Bavarian ally, the Christian Social Union) now enjoy just 19 percent support, 11 points behind the far-right Alternative für Deutschland (AfD). The looming loss of tens of thousands of jobs at German automakers, led by Volkswagen (VW), and possible plant closures would further weaken his position. Merz himself has cast the issue in fundamental terms. “Germany must remain an industrial country !” he told a CDU rally in Langenhagen near Hanover in early September, shortly after Volkswagen’s supervisory board approved its restructuring plan. “We are currently losing industrial jobs on a large scale.” No German state, he added, “is shaped as strongly by one large industrial company as Lower Saxony.” Olaf Lies, the Social Democratic premier of Lower Saxony, which holds 20 percent of the voting rights in Volkswagen, promised that “the Volkswagen sign should stay on the plants” in Emden, Hanover, and Zwickau. Rising in the polls, the AfD presents the crisis in the auto industry as proof of the government’s failure. Its coleader, Tino Chrupalla , called the possible end of “traditional German car plants” “the result of a failed economic and energy policy” and blamed it on a “one-sided and premature fixation on electromobility.” His conclusion: “Zwickau must remain a car city beyond 2031!” In France, meanwhile, some four hundred thousand people work in vehicle and parts manufacturing. Beyond its specific subject matter, the joint initiative by Merz and Macron is also meant to demonstrate that the Franco-German partnership within the EU is working again, after having frequently stalled in recent times. What’s less clear is whether this show is going to have its intended effects. Protectionism As early as October 2024, the EU had imposed countervailing duties of up to 35.3 percent on electric cars built in China, in addition to the regular 10 percent import duty. Hybrid vehicles were not subject to these duties. Since January, manufacturers have been able to avoid the duties if they commit not to sell an electric car in the EU at a price below a specified minimum. Yet none of this has slowed the influx: according to the industry group Chinese Passenger Car Association, China exported 490,000 plug-in hybrids to the EU in the first eight months of this year, 179 percent more than in the same period last year, and 630,000 fully electric cars, an increase of 49 percent. Not all of these carry Chinese brands; exports from China also include models built there by Western manufacturers. For comparison, according to the European Automobile Manufacturers’ Association ( ACEA ), around 1.64 million fully electric cars and 758,000 plug-in hybrids were newly registered in the EU between January and August, accounting for 21.7 and 10 percent of the market, respectively. Chinese brands accounted for about a quarter of plug-in hybrid registrations in the EU in the first half of the year, and a reported 14.2 percent of fully electric car sales in Western Europe’s eighteen largest markets up to May. In August, Chinese-owned carmakers, including Geely’s Volvo, sold 10.8 percent of all new cars in the EU, up from 6.6 percent a year earlier. Merz and Macron’s renewed effort was a response to the weak outcome of previous measures. For example, the countervailing duty on BYD, China’s largest electric vehicle manufacturer, stands at 17 percent, or 27 percent including the regular import duty. However, even such high tariffs do not offset the cost advantage of Chinese production. At the same time, Chinese manufacturers are increasingly pushing into Europe as a price war rages in their home market and domestic sales decline . And Chinese hybrids, which are seeing the strongest export growth, were subject only to the regular import duty until the understanding reached by EU leaders last month. On October 12, Emmanuel Macron opened the Paris Auto Show by calling for even stronger protection of the European market. On the same day, Volkswagen CEO Oliver Blume, at a joint appearance with French Industry Minister Sébastien Martin on the sidelines of the show, spoke out in favor of closer cooperation between the two countries: “Now is the moment when France and Germany, as Europe’s largest automotive nations, must work together.” He added, “I’m not talking about protectionism. Rather, I’m talking about an industrial policy driven by European interests.” Regarding Šefčovič’s negotiations, he said, “We welcome the progress made in the talks with China.” The CEO can use all the relief he can get, as Volkswagen is in crisis in Germany: On September 3, the supervisory board approved the “ Future Plan 2030 .” It stipulates that a further fifty thousand jobs worldwide are to be cut, with Blume estimating that about half of those will be in Germany. This adds on to a decision already made in December 2024 to cut more than thirty-five thousand VW jobs in Germany by 2030. The company says it can no longer guarantee that it can competitively use its capacity at plants in Emden, Hanover, and Zwickau, and at Audi’s Neckarsulm facility. However, no decisions on closures have been made yet. Alternatives are being explored, including the production of Chinese VW models in Germany. Among other reasons, the company cites an overcapacity of approximately five hundred thousand vehicles in Europe and competition from China. That competition isn’t just about subsidies. In 2024, the European Commission concluded that China’s electric car industry benefits from unfair state subsidies that threaten to harm European manufacturers; this finding is the legal basis for the countervailing duties. But in reality, China’s strength does not rest on subsidies alone. Chinese manufacturers have also advanced technologically. Contemporary Amperex Technology Co., Ltd. (CATL) and BYD are among the world’s largest battery producers. According to consultancy McKinsey & Company , Chinese electric car makers launch new models in about twenty-four months, compared with up to forty-five months for established mass-market manufacturers. Volkswagen itself has taken a stake in XPeng to jointly develop models for the Chinese market, thereby acquiring expertise in China. Added to this is the model strategy. Chinese manufacturers are bringing affordable, compact cars to Europe; BYD’s Dolphin Surf, for example, starts at €23,490 in Germany. Volkswagen and other German manufacturers, on the other hand, have relied on high-margin SUVs and premium models for years. Combustion-Engine Ban As one of the first Western automakers in the People’s Republic, Volkswagen has a long history of dependence on China. In October 1984, the company signed a joint venture agreement with the state-owned conglomerate Shanghai Automotive Industry Corporation (SAIC). Mass production began in 1985: in its first year, the joint venture sold 1,684 vehicles; by 1993, that number had risen to around 100,000. In 2019, Volkswagen delivered 4.23 million vehicles there, accounting for nearly 40 percent of its global sales. In January of that same year, then CEO Herbert Diess said, “The future of Volkswagen will be decided on the Chinese market.” While Volkswagen saw its future in China, the outlook for the internal combustion engine in Europe was in flux. In fall 2022, the European Parliament and member states agreed that, starting in 2035, only zero-emission new cars would be permitted for registration. In March 2023, German Federal Minister of Transport Volker Wissing called into question Germany’s previously given approval; the law was finally passed at the end of the month only after a commitment to electrofuels was secured. Manufacturers, too, made commitments and in some cases backtracked on them: Volkswagen announced in 2021 that it would phase out internal combustion engines in Europe between 2033 and 2035. Mercedes-Benz had declared in 2021 that it would sell only electric cars wherever possible by the end of the decade but abandoned this goal in 2024. At the end of 2025, the European Commission proposed scaling back the 2035 target to a 90 percent reduction in CO2 emissions. China, on the other hand, has pursued a consistent policy for years: from 2010 through the end of 2022, the government paid manufacturers subsidies for every electric or plug-in hybrid vehicle sold in the country, and the “Made in China 2025” strategy designated electric mobility as a key industry. This support may have distorted market competition, but it gave the country’s manufacturers planning certainty. Europe, by contrast, has put its car industry at a disadvantage with its political flip-flopping. Now Volkswagen is also coming under pressure in China: in the third quarter of 2026, the company delivered 405,800 vehicles there, 38.5 percent less than a year earlier. Globally, the decline was 14.3 percent. Volkswagen is thus facing three simultaneous developments: sales in China are falling; the company is increasingly sourcing technology from Chinese partners; and in Europe, the EU is protecting the market from Chinese imports, including vehicles from Volkswagen’s own Chinese plants, with tariffs, minimum prices, and volume agreements. The pressure on Berlin’s political leaders to stand by its carmakers is considerable, and the government has responded. Since May, buyers of electric cars have been able to claim a state bonus of up to €6,000; plug-in hybrids also qualify. The program is funded with €3 billion until 2029. Yet because eligibility does not depend on where a car is made, Chinese models also benefit. Merz has also campaigned against the EU’s planned ban on new combustion-engine cars from 2035, which matters greatly to German manufacturers. “We will no longer hold on to this stubborn and wrong ban on combustion engines in the European Union,” he told a CDU party conference in late November 2025. Last month, Berlin and Paris agreed on a joint position : a 90 percent cut in new cars’ CO2 emissions by 2035, without requiring the remaining 10 percent to be offset. Two committees of the European Parliament have since backed a 95 percent compromise, with additional allowances for European green steel; the plenary is due to vote in December. The political pressure is also coming from German states. In July, Lower Saxony’s Social Democratic premier, Olaf Lies, wrote to Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, Merz, and Vice Chancellor Lars Klingbeil calling for tariffs on hybrids from China. Yet it’s also clear that support for the car industry is not the same as support for German jobs. By the end of June 2027, Volkswagen plans to present a strategy for its European production, which will also determine the future of its plants in Emden, Zwickau, Hanover, and Neckarsulm. The German government faces the question of whether the interests of a corporation that manufactures in China and exports from there to Europe still align with those of its German sites and their workers. When immigration agents take a father from his workplace, the consequences extend far beyond the moment of arrest. His children may lose their primary caregiver. His family may suddenly face eviction or hunger. Relatives may spend days trying to determine where he has been taken, while struggling to raise money for an attorney, telephone calls, or food inside a detention facility. Those were among the experiences described at the October 2026 Left Coast Forum at Cal State LA, where grassroots organizers gathered for a panel titled The Impact of ICE Session I: How Communities Have Responded to ICE Raids and Where We Go From Here. MaryLinda Moss The discussion brought together three activists working on the front lines of Southern California's immigration enforcement crisis: Erika Schwerdt of Strength Based Community Change (SBCC), Jacqueline Méndez of Defend Migrants Alliance and International Migrants Alliance, and John Parker of the Harriet Tubman Center and Community Self-Defense Coalition. Moderated by MaryLinda Moss of NELA Alliance for Democracy and No Concentration Camps California, the session offered something more substantial than a catalog of abuses. It described an emerging infrastructure of community defense, built through cooperation among organizations that recognize they cannot confront federal enforcement alone. The Devastation That Follows an ICE Arrest Erika Schwerdt, who organizes primarily in the Antelope Valley, emphasized that immigration enforcement is particularly difficult to monitor in rural communities. Erika Schwerdt Unlike densely populated Los Angeles neighborhoods, where witnesses may quickly gather when agents appear, residents of sprawling desert communities often live miles apart. Families may not discover that someone has been detained until that person fails to return from work. Schwerdt described receiving desperate calls from relatives asking what to do when a father or aunt simply disappears. She cited ICE data indicating that more than 14,000 people had been detained or arrested in Los Angeles County during the period she examined, while volunteers in the LA Rapid Response Network had verified approximately 3,000 cases. The disparity, she explained, reflects the enormous challenges facing community-based monitoring efforts. But statistics tell only part of the story. Schwerdt recounted an incident in which agents allegedly dragged a man from his vehicle, beat him, and left his truck running on the roadway. When a roadside-assistance worker attempted to help the family recover the vehicle, she said, an agent threatened him with arrest. The family included elderly grandparents, one experiencing dementia, who struggled to comprehend what had happened. Other families face an equally devastating aftermath: children without parents, spouses suddenly deprived of income, and detainees attempting to survive on inadequate food while paying inflated prices for basic necessities. Schwerdt described receiving calls from detained people asking for commissary assistance and worrying about wives and children left behind. For these families, she stressed, the trauma does not end when the immediate crisis passes. Children may need years of emotional support. Relatives must assume unfamiliar caregiving responsibilities. Families struggling financially before an arrest can be pushed into outright destitution. This is why SBCC emphasizes community education, training rapid responders and legal observers, and building relationships that can provide practical assistance long after agents have departed. Schwerdt also warned that enforcement tactics have shifted toward less visible operations, including detentions associated with immigration check-ins and other routine encounters. The absence of dramatic television footage, she argued, should not be mistaken for an end to enforcement. From Detention to Collective Resistance Jacqueline Méndez described a complementary approach: organizing alongside detained migrants and their families rather than treating those affected as passive recipients of assistance. Jacqueline Méndez Through Defend Migrants Alliance and International Migrants Alliance, organizers have supported campaigns involving people held at the Adelanto detention facility, including detainees engaged in hunger strikes. Another campaign concerned Rafael, a Salvadoran asylum seeker whom Méndez said agents detained while he was preparing his family's pupusa stand in Pacoima. Families need funds, legal assistance, public attention, visits, and opportunities to communicate with the outside world. Her message was straightforward: people need not wait until they become experienced activists to contribute. Panelists criticized the role of private detention corporations and contractors, arguing that incarceration has become a lucrative business whose revenues depend on keeping people confined. Families and supporters must pay for expensive commissary items and telephone services, even while believing that these expenses help sustain the system they oppose. The problem is not abstract. Schwerdt explained how responding to calls from detained people can consume hours. Each conversation may begin with basic questions: Where are you being held? Do you have an attorney? What assistance does your family need? There may be another detainee waiting to use the telephone, and another after that. Eventually, the emotional burden becomes overwhelming even for experienced organizers. This reality underscored a recurring message throughout the panel: sustaining the work requires attention not only to the people directly harmed by detention but also to those supporting them. The panelists offered concrete examples of families organizing, detainees resisting abuse, volunteers learning new skills, and organizations setting aside differences to cooperate. They also insisted that affected communities must remain central to the movement. Schwerdt rejected competition over organizational recognition, declaring that the movement and its data do not belong to any individual organizer. It requires durable networks capable of responding when a neighbor disappears, accompanying families through immigration proceedings, sustaining contact with detainees, challenging public officials, and rebuilding the bonds of solidarity that fear and repression seek to destroy. Italy: The rise of the student and youth movement in Europe

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: Tempest ／ 著者: Communist Workers’ Party, Italy ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-08T01:18:38+00:00

T he massive student movement in France—the largest since May 1968— the demonstrations in the streets of Spain demanding the right to housing, the beginning of its potential spread to Portugal, and the prolonged popular uprising in Albania with the explicit demand for the ouster of the Rama government, are ushering in a new chapter in Europe. We are witnessing the rise of the student and youth movement, which has the potential to spread. We cannot predict its future today, but it is essential to understand its characteristics. Palestine led the way Over the past two years, the pro-Palestinian movement has also seen a broad segment of the younger generation in Europe come to the forefront. Amid the barbarity of the Zionist genocide, millions of young people saw more than just the oppression of a people. They saw in it a sign of global injustice: the hypocrisy and complicity of international diplomacy, of all bourgeois governments, and of all their propaganda about the values of “civilization,” “equality,” “democracy,” and so-called “international law.” The ruling classes were suddenly revealed to millions of young people for what they really are: a temple of lies and oppression. Hence the gradual emergence of a collective youth movement that transcended the borders of Palestine and began to evolve into a broader rebellion—a rebellion, above all, against their own circumstances. The plight of a generation that is better educated than previous ones, more globally interconnected, and, at the same time, condemned to a worse life—worse than that of their parents, which in many cases was already worse than that of their grandparents. Schools in ruins in the suburbs, job insecurity, poverty wages, a lack of affordable housing, an out-of-control climate crisis, and the proliferation of military spending and wars. And all of this amid an unprecedented stock market boom and a profit-driven frenzy that has enriched corporations, banks, hedge funds, energy and food giants, and the world’s largest military-industrial complex—precisely those most responsible for the overall decline. The ripple effect of the student movement This overall picture has left an indelible mark. Now, these marks have begun to burn. The continental connection between the various mass movements does not come from above; it cannot be traced back to any international organization or coordination. Rather, it comes from below, through a ripple effect. The French student mass movement undoubtedly paved the way for its dynamic spread and its hold on the public imagination—partly due to its enormous scale, and partly due to France’s historical prestige in terms of revolutions and uprisings. This does not imply homogeneity among the movements, much less in their triggers, which are always diverse and specific (schools in France, housing in Spain, environmental exploitation and corruption in Albania, as well as in Serbia). It does mean, however, that the new generation in Europe tends to recognize itself as such, to identify with its own motivations beyond national borders. Hence the call to emulate. At the student demonstrations on October 16 in Italy, “ Do as in France ” was not only the title of our pamphlet but also a call for a widespread sense of solidarity. This was true regardless of specific knowledge of the French movement, and in particular of its forms of organization. The November 20 deadline for the first European student strike against militarism preceded the outbreak of the current student movement in France. Rather, it was spurred by the experience of German students’ struggle against their country’s massive increase in military spending and related conscription plans. Undoubtedly, the possible coincidence between November 20 and the ongoing mass movements in several countries may lend that date greater significance. The fact that the general strike called by the Italian General Confederation of Labor (CGIL) will take place on that day in Italy is indirect evidence of this. The issue of the “Casseurs” The presence and sudden appearance of the “Casseurs” (rioters) on the fringes of mass demonstrations—or alongside them—represents an unexpected weapon of reactionary propaganda against the movement for the bourgeoisie. It is the only one at their disposal. But this does not justify the labor movement succumbing to such propaganda. The Phenomenon: Riots, especially in metropolitan areas, often exhibit different characteristics depending on the national context and the presence or absence of a connection to mass dynamics. History is also replete with primitive rebellions against the high cost of living, which were outside the bounds of social norms. Certainly, looting stores in the city center, smashing storefronts, and stealing private property are not part of the arsenal that can be proposed to the movement, nor can they even be justified. This is even more true for revolutionaries, who, by the very nature of their anti-capitalist project, must always invest in promoting mass action, its organization, its political consciousness, and even its right to collective self-defense against any aggression by the state or fascist forces. And yet, as revolutionaries, we cannot in any way endorse the hypocrisy of the bourgeois condemnation of “violence,” not even the disorganized violence represented by the casseurs . The main forms of violence are exploitation, the inability of millions of precarious workers to obtain a mortgage, exorbitant rents, the investment of billions in instruments of death, cuts to public healthcare to boost the private sector, air and water pollution, retirement at age 70 or older, if all goes well—with the sole purpose of paying off 100,000 million euros in public debt to banks and insurance companies, and military and economic support for genocidal regimes such as the Zionist state… The entire bourgeois society is sustained, even more so today, by this organized, everyday violence designed to protect capital. Against this backdrop, the broken windows in the city center on a weekend at the hands of young people from the suburbs—often second- or third-generation residents, harassed in a thousand ways in their own neighborhoods by police patrols employing racist methods and attitudes—are simply a consequence of bourgeois violence. A distorted form of revenge for the violence they have suffered. A way to escape everyday invisibility in order to display and proclaim their own presence. Bourgeois governments seek to exploit these phenomena—ultimately driven by their own policies—to further tighten repressive legislation (even against minors), undermine the rights of mass movements, and organize a reactionary social bloc in defense of the status quo. Revolutionary Marxists work in the opposite direction: by fostering the self-organization of mass movements against the ruling classes and their governments, and by building an anti-capitalist social bloc. This is, moreover, the only possible way to reduce the impact and scope of the marginal actions of the casseurs . For the unified self-organization of the student movement: The real lesson from France For us, “ Doing it the French way ” isn’t just about calling for mass mobilization. It also means adopting the French movement’s organizational model, which is so traditionally different from the Italian one. In Italy, we have a loosely organized network of student groups that compete with one another over the timing of mobilizations, forms of action, and the goals of the struggle, often claiming ownership of and representation for the movement. This is detrimental to the movement itself, its growth, and its strength. In France, the situation is different. In France, the movement is based on joint assemblies of schools and colleges, their democratically elected representatives, and their joint coordination at the municipal, departmental, and, ultimately, national levels. Within this joint movement, the various labor and political organizations that comprise it confront one another with their differing positions and disagreements. However, they do so within a unified framework in which the assemblies and their coordinators make the decisions—not the organizations that support them (when such assemblies are held). The definition of each individual option is not left to the supposedly manipulative “synthesis” of this or that leader but is entrusted to the free vote of the assemblies and their coordinators. This is the tradition of workers’ and popular democracy. It is best suited to the movement’s development, its stability, the election of its leadership, collective control over it, and the promotion of a democratic platform of unifying demands as the basis for the national struggle against the government. Of course, this does not mean that there are no problems or conflicts within the movement itself. The larger the movement, the greater the number of individuals involved, leading to an inevitable plurality of competing positions and, consequently, an open struggle for hegemony. But this struggle can unfold within a unified and organized movement, not on the periphery of the movement. And when, for example, the French high school students’ union—controlled by a reformist leadership—claims to speak for the movement by demanding its own alternative seats and a monopoly on negotiations with the government, the united movement rejects it with all the authority conferred by its democratic form of organization. The strong Trotskyist tradition in France has played a decisive role in the formation of movements for workers’ democracy. This is the battle that the PCL and its student members want to wage within the Italian student movement. So that the working class can take center stage The rise of a mass student movement is shaking bourgeois society to its core. In France, it has forced the government to hold emergency meetings to address the movement. Although Prime Minister Lecornu was forced to grudgingly acknowledge “ the legitimacy of the movement and its demands ,” he did so in an attempt to calm tempers and bring the movement to its senses (in vain). This is an indirect measure of the movement’s political weight, a byproduct of its strength. The French movement has presented its own list of demands regarding the budget bill. These are the people’s demands: a significant increase in spending on public education, the large-scale hiring of new teachers, the immediate regularization of temporary staff, smaller class sizes, and the renovation of school buildings, starting with suburban schools… In short, a program of essential goals that requires at least 10,000 million euros. This figure is incompatible with France’s growing public debt crisis, the increase in annual interest payments to financial capital, and France’s plans to acquire new weapons to counter German military competition. This is already forcing the Lecornu administration to adopt a budget of sacrifices and cuts that is particularly provocative in a country marked by social inequality even greater than that of Italy. The French students’ protest movement, therefore, has the government backed into a corner. This is even more true in a political and institutional context in which the government lacks a parliamentary majority, the old two-party system is disintegrating, and political polarization is intensifying on the eve of presidential elections that could prove decisive for the future of the Fifth Republic and the very balance of power in Europe. This impasse cannot be overcome by the strength of the student movement alone. The labor movement must step in. Some sectors of the workforce have already mobilized for their own sector-specific demands (firefighters) and/or in active solidarity with the students, starting with the teachers’ union, which is traditionally left-wing in France. Some former “Yellow Vests” groups resurfaced on October 17, demonstrating against the high cost of living and in solidarity with the students. But the major union bureaucracies are hesitant. On the one hand, they support the movement against the government and its budget; on the other, they fear being swept up in a dynamic of uncontrolled radicalization and do not want to jeopardize their bargaining leverage with employers and the executive branch. Hence their rejection of a general strike based on a unifying platform of struggle, in favor of fragmented and sporadic actions. This is the same bureaucratic method the apparatuses have used to crush the major movements for workers’ rights (the El Khomri Law) and pensions over the past ten years. The truth is that in France, not only the ruling classes but all bourgeois political forces suffer from the “May ’68” syndrome: the specter of a revolutionary crisis. Even Mélenchon’s “France Insoumise,” the left wing of a popular front now in disarray, is careful not to call for a general strike or criticize the union bureaucracies. The solidarity shown and proclaimed toward the student and youth movement goes no further than electoral maneuvering in the presidential elections. In fact, Mélenchon denounces “ the adventurism of a government that risks dragging society into a disastrous conflict. ” For the same reason, the struggle of revolutionary Marxists in France can and must be directed toward unifying the student movement with the working class, and vice versa. This convergence cannot be achieved through mutual solidarity alone. It requires a unifying platform of struggle for all workers in the public and private sectors, and a radical and united shift in their struggle. This is precisely what the union bureaucracies reject today, with the support of the entire reformist political left. These considerations are even more relevant in the case of Italy A year ago, in late September and early October 2025, we witnessed a sudden and dramatic increase in the number of workers in Italy, coinciding with the rise of the Palestinian movement. The youth mobilization. The beginning of a potentially abrupt turning point, the potential for a social explosion. The general strike for Palestine, called jointly by all the grassroots unions under pressure from the masses—a unique event in Europe—was, in a sense, the seismic event itself. But that potential was quickly squandered by those who should have capitalized on it. First and foremost, the CGIL apparatus, but also the leadership of the Union Sindical de Base (USB) (and, therefore, of Potere al Popolo), both embarrassed by the united front imposed by the movement and eager to break free and return to their own confines and rituals. But a year later, the movement in France seems to be signaling a possible new resurgence of the student movement in Italy. This means that, despite everything, the legacy of the pro-Palestinian movement has remained latent. But now, the movement’s own development raises the crucial question of its forms of organization, its platform, and its relationship with the labor movement (and vice versa). The form of organization we propose must be unitary and democratic, as in France. No student organization can claim to “ do as they do in France ” while at the same time ignoring that experience. Every position and organization (Link, UDU, OSA, Cambiare Rotta, Fronte della Gioventù Comunista, etc.) must be fully respected. But each entity must accept the democratic sovereignty of the unified assemblies and their coordinating bodies. Democratic voting in each forum will determine the degree of representativeness of the various student organizations, whether they are labor or political. At the same time, we need a unified set of demands that the student movement can make its own, launching a genuine struggle against the government during the upcoming budget bill. Just as in France, so too here. Starting, for example, with the demand for significant investment in public schools—not in weapons—and a comprehensive overhaul of school buildings, free education, school cafeterias, transportation, and student housing, funded by a 10% tax on the wealth of the richest 10%; the abolition of public funding for private schools in favor of fully public and secular schools… The platform must be defined and approved democratically by the student assemblies and their coordinating bodies, establishing a clear mandate for the leadership appointed by the movement itself. The mobilization supporting it must be sustained and open-ended, as in France, until the established goals are achieved. It will be up to the assemblies and their coordinating bodies to choose the forms and methods of struggle. In France, the occupation of universities has been and continues to be not only the main form of struggle, but also the cornerstone of the movement’s democratic self-organization. The public authorities have ordered the closure of numerous affected schools and colleges precisely to hinder the organization of assemblies, forcing students to seek out other spaces to meet, debate, and make decisions. Why November 20 marks a unified and radical turning point But even in Italy—and all the more so in Italy—the united front between the student movement and the labor movement is crucial. The CGIL’s decision to join the European student strike on November 20, following its own general strike, represents an important opportunity. All class-based labor organizations must come together on this date, free from any sectarianism or minority self-interest, which in this case would be truly grotesque. At the same time, it is necessary to clarify the nature of the strike, the platform on which it is based, and, above all, its perspective. Every year, Maurizio Landini calls for a “social revolt,” only to betray that call in practice. For example, following the riots in Milan, the CGIL (Italian General Confederation of Labor) called for a demonstration in the city “ for social cohesion. ” Cohesion between whom and whom? In reality, this means presenting the union as the safety net for the Republic’s “democratic” order. This is not exactly a call to revolt. Instead, for once, the slogan of the social revolt must be taken seriously, as an alternative to the traditional logic of the bureaucracy. The November 20 strike must be prepared through assemblies in the workplaces, based on a defined platform that must be subject to review. This platform cannot be reduced to a list of grievances or problems. It must translate into precise, quantifiable, and enforceable demands, launching a general and unifying struggle against employers and the government. A significant pay raise of at least €500 net and the introduction of a progressive pay scale must be the main demands. Otherwise, the fight against the high cost of living will become a farce. a. Second, we must demand that investment in healthcare and education be doubled, financed by reducing military spending and implementing a genuine wealth tax (10% on the wealthiest 10%). This goes beyond a simple cut to so-called windfall profits, in a country where banks made $50 billion in net profits in a single year, and ENI alone made a whopping $5 billion. But this is not the place to analyze each individual demand. What matters is the overall sense of radical change: we need to give millions of workers—often demoralized by the repeated betrayals they have suffered—a strong, recognizable, and unequivocal motivation to finally wage a real battle. A battle not just to participate in, but to win. To achieve results. To change their situation. And, above all, to hold their heads high after years of resignation and retreat. For the same reason, a change is needed in the forms of struggle that accompany the dispute If the November 20 strike is reduced to a one-off, essentially symbolic action—before and after which everything continues as before—much of its potential will have been wasted. We need to turn the page. For example, there are hundreds of ongoing labor disputes against companies that have received public funds for years and decades, that have accumulated enormous profits in recent years, and that wish to relocate or restructure to reduce production costs. Each of these disputes is resolved on its own, without even a hint of a unifying slogan—neither in terms of objectives nor methods of struggle. The result is that, time and again—company by company, plant by plant—negotiations take place on the employer’s slippery slope, guided by the eternal logic of damage control, yet inevitably ending in defeat. This adds to the previous defeat in an endless retreat. We cannot go on like this. We must, therefore, turn November 20 and the widespread preparations leading up to it into a moment of unity for this workers’ front, issuing a general call to occupy companies that lay off workers and to nationalize them without compensation and under workers’ control. We must also create a national resistance fund to support the struggle, just as the GKN workers did in their own factory. Of course, it would be absurd to present these proposals for a platform and struggle as a precondition for the strike. It would also be presumptuous, given that we are a small party. But we will fight once again for this general approach—so that the Italian labor movement can set its sails to the wind blowing from France and Spain, to the wind of the new generation. We will fight within every grassroots organization—from the CGIL to the grassroots unions—to advance the demand for this great, unified, and radical change. So that November 20 is not reduced to a symbolic action or, worse still, a mere bureaucratic facade for the student movement, but rather marks the beginning of a united mass front, capable of enduring and subverting the balance of power between the classes. To overthrow the Meloni government and lay the foundations for a real alternative. This is not the same old formula of yet another “broad coalition” with bourgeois parties. It is a systemic alternative: a government of the working people. A crossroads of perspectives: revolution or reaction? This is not just a labor issue, but a political one. Years of decline in the European labor movement—particularly acute in Italy—have paved the way not only for the passivity of broad sectors of the working class, but also for the rise of reactionary populism and its ability to influence millions of workers. The left’s repeated political complicity in anti-worker and anti-grassroots policies has been a catalyst for this dynamic. The rise of the far right in key European countries is a significant result of this. Now, the emergence of student and youth rebellion in Europe could open up a new scenario. But this will only be possible if the labor movement aligns itself with this new momentum and focuses its strength on the class struggle. Otherwise, the decline or defeat of existing movements could provoke an even sharper and more radical reaction, in the name of “restoring order.” Fratelli d’Italia, Rassemblement National , Vox, and others have already geared their propaganda in this direction. In this regard, the dilemma of choosing between socialism and barbarism takes on greater significance today. Raising awareness among young people and the labor movement about this issue is fundamental to the political work of the International Socialist League (ISL) and all its sections and members.
