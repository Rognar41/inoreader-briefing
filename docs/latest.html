Amazon Is Spending Big Against NYC’s Delivery Protection Act

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Alex N. Press ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-14T19:08:11+00:00

When Latrice Johnson learned that her brother had died, she was delivering Amazon packages in New York City. The next day, an Amazon manager gave her contact information for a grief counselor. Johnson called and spoke with the counselor, only to get another call telling her she wasn’t eligible for the service. She didn’t work for Amazon.Johnson delivered Amazon packages for two years out of DBK4 in Queens. Unable to afford more time off after her brother’s death, Johnson went back to delivering packages. As she testified before New York City Council, she sometimes cried as she made her stops.On Thursday afternoon, Johnson was outside the gates of New York City Hall with the Teamsters, pushing for legislation meant to do away with that distinction. The Delivery Protection Act (DPA), introduced by City Council Member Tiffany Cabán, would require delivery companies to directly employ the drivers and warehouse workers doing their core work, as well as set licensing, safety, and training requirements for last-mile facilities. Three days earlier, Mayor Zohran Mamdani endorsed the bill. More than thirty council members are signed on as cosponsors, though Speaker Julie Menin, who supported an earlier version, has not yet backed the latest version.“Corporations like Amazon build billion-dollar business models by insulating themselves from accountability through a system of exploitative subcontracting,” Mamdani said in announcing his support for the legislation. “As last-mile delivery centers have exploded across New York, so too have traffic accidents and worker injuries. The Delivery Protection Act is commonsense regulation that protects delivery workers, safeguards the communities where these facilities operate and ensures that the corporations benefiting from workers’ labor are responsible for the consequences of their business practices.”Latrice Johnson, a former delivery driver for one of Amazon’s Delivery Service Partners, speaks in front of New York City Hall in support of the Delivery Protection Act. (Courtesy ALIGN)Amazon delivery drivers wear Amazon uniforms, drive Amazon-branded vans, and deliver Amazon packages. But the names on their paychecks belong to smaller contractors, known as Delivery Service Partners (DSPs). That distinction has become central to the Teamsters’ attempts to organize Amazon drivers. The union has gone to the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB), arguing that Amazon exercises enough control over drivers to be considered a “joint employer” — and is thus required to bargain with them. Amazon maintains that the DSPs are independent businesses.Amazon has spent more than $5 million to try to defeat the legislation. Between February and mid-May, Amazon made five payments totaling $5,001,377 to the Five Borough Jobs Campaign, a business lobbying coalition affiliated with the Real Estate Board of New York. From March through June, Five Borough Jobs reported spending $4.7 million under the name New York Delivers.New York Delivers has put the money into television and social media ads as well as lobbying, often featuring DSP owners and workers who say the bill threatens their businesses and jobs. One Facebook ad drew more than a million views over several days in May. The campaign also says the DPA would cost the average New York household $664 a year. That estimate comes from AKRF, a consulting firm Five Borough Jobs paid $52,500.The stakes for Amazon go well beyond New York. The DSP program is central to its delivery operation: Amazon says it has created hundreds of thousands of driving jobs through thousands of contractors around the world. The arrangement puts that workforce at a legal remove from Amazon, allowing the company to disclaim responsibility for wages, injuries, and other basic conditions of employment.It has also proved a formidable obstacle to unionization. When DSP drivers organize, Amazon can argue that it is not their employer and has no obligation to bargain with them — precisely the question workers organizing with the Teamsters are now fighting over at the NLRB.Cabán, who has been working on the legislation for more than four years, pointed to the spending when I asked her outside city hall why Amazon was fighting the bill so aggressively.“It’s a record,” she said. “They’ve never spent so much money lobbying for or against the bill in New York State.”Julie Su, Mamdani’s deputy mayor for economic justice and the former acting US secretary of labor under President Joe Biden, called Amazon’s arrangement “a business model built on getting all the benefits of labor while disclaiming any responsibility for it.”“It is an end run around a century of labor protections that have been fought for and won,” she said, a means “for the company at the top, Amazon, to say, ‘We get to dictate all the terms.’”“We get to say what you deliver, when you deliver, how fast you deliver, and what you wear when you deliver it,” she said, describing the company’s position. “But when something happens to you, you don’t get paid what you’re supposed to get paid; you get injured on the job, which is very commonplace, they take no responsibility for it.”Johnson, the DBK4 driver, and her coworkers later began organizing with the Teamsters. Last year, Amazon terminated its relationship with the DSP that employed them, leaving more than one hundred drivers without jobs. Johnson and the Teamsters say Amazon terminated the contract in retaliation for their organizing; Amazon has denied retaliating against workers for union activity.New York City Council Member Tiffany Cabán speaks outside city hall in support of the Delivery Protection Act. (Courtesy ALIGN)“I was laid off last year, back in August, with the rest of my coworkers,” Johnson told me. “The whole DSP was laid off, over one hundred drivers, and we know that it was retaliation for trying to organize the workplace.”“It definitely proves that Amazon has that power to just get rid of us,” she said.Johnson said direct employment would also make a difference when drivers get hurt. “Amazon can back away and say we don’t have anything to do with it,” she said. “That worker who got hurt has no help and is out of work with no pay.” For a driver, she added, “I just bust my ass working and delivering all these packages for all these months or years, and I get nothing.”“Isn’t it crazy in this day and age, that you’re working for Amazon, but Amazon says you’re not working for them?” Vinnie Perrone, president of Teamsters Local 804, told me. Having the drivers become direct employees, he said, “would be a tremendous help.”Local 804 represents United Parcel Service (UPS) workers, whose jobs offer Perrone a point of comparison. “They don’t want a unionized workforce. It’s very simple,” he said. “If UPS could avoid a unionized workforce, they would do it in a heartbeat.” But for unionized UPS members, he said, “a fair day’s work encompasses your salary and your benefits — and Amazon doesn’t want to pay.”Amazon says the more than forty DSPs operating in New York City are independent small businesses, employing more than five thousand people whose jobs would be threatened by the bill. The company argues that direct employment could raise delivery costs and put the contractors themselves out of business. Christine Chan, whose Harlem-based Highgate Logistics employs about 175 people delivering Amazon packages by cargo bike, told the New York Times, “You’re betting that Amazon is going to employ these people directly. I’ll take the other side of that bet.”Deputy Mayor Su argued that Amazon’s accounting leaves out costs New Yorkers already bear. “Amazon has spent a ton of money on supposed data about the costs,” she said. “New Yorkers are already bearing the costs of Amazon’s business model.” She pointed to injuries to workers and pedestrians, but also to the DSP owners Amazon describes as independent small businesses. They are, Su said, “completely under the thumb of Amazon.” If a DSP wants to give drivers time to rest because of the heat, she said, “Amazon tells them no, and even has the power to cut them off.”Amazon has also threatened to move delivery facilities outside the city. In testimony to the city council, the company said passage would force it to consider “the potential for relocating operations and delivery facilities outside of New York City.”Teamsters and local activists rally outside New York City Hall in support of the Delivery Protection Act. (Courtesy ALIGN)The Mamdani administration disputes the premise that moving a facility across the city line would necessarily allow Amazon to preserve the prior employment arrangement, pointing to language in the bill defining “core delivery services” as transporting and delivering goods “to consumers in the city.” But other provisions refer specifically to last-mile facilities located “in the city,” leaving some room for interpretation as to whether Amazon could avoid the direct-employment requirement by moving a facility to New Jersey or Long Island.Theodore Moore, executive director of ALIGN, a New York coalition of labor and community organizations that works on workers’ rights, environmental justice, and corporate accountability, was skeptical of the threat for a more practical reason.“This is New York City: Amazon is here because the business is here,” Moore said. “These are last-mile delivery facilities. ‘Last mile’ is the key. They need to be as close to the door as possible.” Moving further away, he said, would mean spending more on gas and tolls, and taking longer to make deliveries.Cabán pointed to the workers outside city hall. “It’s a lot of black and brown workers just trying to provide for their families,” she said. “They want to go to work and not come home hurt, and they want to go to work and know that they have their job the next day.” New Yorkers are on pace to receive roughly a billion packages this year, around three million a day.“A person has to deliver them,” she said. “And if Amazon doesn’t want to do it, certainly somebody else will. But the entire model hinges on being as close to the consumer as possible.”“It is an age-old argument by corporations who have profited massively off the backs of exploited workers, to say that working people only have two choices: a really bad job in which you’re struggling to get by, or no job at all,” Su told me of the opposition’s argument against the bill. “We reject that as a false choice.”As acting labor secretary, Su dealt with subcontracting arrangements that allow large companies to distance themselves from the people carrying out the work. Now, as Mamdani’s deputy mayor for economic justice, she is trying to address one such arrangement through city law.“We have a federal administration that is wholly uninterested in worker protections and enforcing laws on the books,” she said. A federal response remains important, she added, given “the sheer size and reach of companies like Amazon,” but “in the absence of that work, New York City is not standing by. We’re not waiting for someone else to act.”“This bill gets to the heart of their business model,” Su said. “Drivers have been threatened when they speak out. They’ve been told that they can no longer work for Amazon, and the company has put a lot of money into trying to buy positive reviews from those that they control. But there’s no reason why you, a trillion-dollar company, shouldn’t have to play by the same set of rules that every other company does.”“Everyone around the country is watching this, and this is going to be model legislation for other jurisdictions,” ALIGN’s Moore said. “They have to be worried, because that’s what we’re trying to do. We’re trying to change their model.”“No company is above the law,” Su said when I asked how the administration would enforce the legislation against a company with Amazon’s resources. Mamdani, she said, was “taking a very strong stand. We’re going to put the full force of this administration behind getting this bill passed.”“I just want Amazon to take accountability,” said Johnson, the former DBK4 driver. “Without us, there would be no Amazon. So respect your workers.”

Socialists are Organizing in Western Wayne County

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Detroit DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-14T16:51:03+00:00

By Alissa B., Anthony D., Elijah N., James M., and Jill R. One year into an experimental electoral project to organize alongside our Socialists in Office, DSA is setting down real roots in western Wayne County. Attendees deliberate during a Socialists Organizing Western Wayne (SOWW) meeting in Westland. Below is an approximate transcript of comments made by members at the June 2026 General Meeting detailing progress on Metro Detroit DSA’s organizing in western Wayne County. Anthony D., engineer, Royal Oak, 2025–2026 SOWW chair: At our 2025 Convention, members passed an amendment to the Socialists in Office (SIO) Committee consensus resolution , authored by Anthony D. and Frances R., that created a unique and experimental ‘Geographic Working Group’. It was designed as a space for rank-and-file DSA members living in State Representative Dylan Wegela’s and Congresswoman Rashida Tlaib’s overlapping districts, to regularly meet with and organize alongside them. Our SIOs are surrounded daily by politicians ranging from corporate Democrats to fascist Republicans who serve the same ruling class, so we imagined this group as the socialist antidote and support system. The idea was to experiment with a new concept of how DSA and our SIOs should use elected office and carve a path towards our political independence. The amendment included the clause below. The Geographic Working Group will issue a report at both the six-month (December 2025) and twelve-month (June 2026) mark to: provide an update on progress towards our goals and explain any roadblocks to accomplishing them offer an analysis and critique to consider whether it should be replicated by future similar bodies or other SIO Committees around the country normalize open reporting on the progress of this and other unique experiments in the chapter — all of which can be expected to have misses and fails, which are acceptable so long as the lessons learned are defined and shared with membership similar to past electoral campaigns We conducted the first report-back at our December 2025 GM six months ago and wrote an article for the Detroit Socialist . We are doing so again today at the one-year mark. As of June 1, I am now the former chair of the group after having led it for a year, and have since handed off duties to our newly elected co-chairs, Jill and Kathy. Jill R., stay-at-home mom, Westland, 2026–2027 SOWW co-chair: As of June 1st, I’m one of the new co-chairs of Socialists Organizing Western Wayne (SOWW). I had first heard of DSA from Dylan when he knocked on my door during a canvass for his election a few years ago. Though it sounded interesting, I told him I didn’t want to drive out to Detroit for events. That didn’t stop him from trying to get me to join every time I saw him after that initial meeting. Then, last summer, I went to an event that Dylan and Mike McDermott had hosted just three minutes from my house. Dylan once again brought up DSA, where I once again told him I didn’t want to drive to Detroit. That’s when he told me that a new group was forming and would be meeting in Westland. I told him to let me know the details as that was definitely something I would be willing to attend. I finally attended my first SOWW meeting at the end of October 2025 and joined DSA the following week. I’m now willing to drive to Detroit for General Meetings and other DSA events, but if it wasn’t for SOWW existing in the first place, I wouldn’t be here. One of the projects I’ve worked on was having SOWW co-host the March No Kings event with UAW Local 900 in Wayne. I know a lot of us have mixed feelings about the No Kings events and the mobilization versus organization aspect to them, but attending the October No Kings gave me the little bit of hope I was missing that allowed my ADHD brain to finally contact Dylan for information about SOWW, which led me to DSA. And I wanted to use the March No Kings event as a chance to find some folks who may have been needing a little bit of hope, too. After seeing the Wayne event pop up on the No Kings map and realizing that hosting one in Westland wasn’t going to be an option, I contacted Tonya, the Wayne host, to see if we could support in any way. Tonya is a UAW retiree who had talked her leadership into letting her have the No Kings event at their local union hall. She was able to convince leadership to let us co-host because so many DSA folks had joined the Local 900 members on the picket line during the 2023 UAW auto workers strike. Tonya and her leadership remembered the strong strike support and solidarity that DSA provided during those weeks. During the event, we had a table where we talked to folks about DSA and SOWW and we had members, including newbies, crowd-canvassing to get signatures for the Mop Up Michigan petition. We provided medics and wrote up a safety plan, and Mike and Dylan both gave speeches to the 100+ people that attended. It was one of the smaller No Kings events due to it being a first-time location, but overall it went well and we’re looking forward to working with Tonya and Local 900 again. Tonya and I are still in contact and let each other know about any potential chances to organize together, and her husband Paul, who is a State Field Director at Voters Not Politicians, has attended SOWW meetings and shown interest in joining DSA. Alissa B., retiree union staffer, Romulus, SOWW member: I joined DSA in late July 2025 as a relatively new resident in Michigan. I got a call from Anthony not long after joining and he told me about this experimental Geographic Working Group that we now call SOWW, and invited me to a meeting. I was excited about the chance to connect with my community and like-minded people in the area. Ever since, I’ve been a regular attendee and have been able to observe the growth of this group. SOWW meetings are intentionally structured to foster membership growth and leadership development. This is a space for new folks who don’t know much about DSA but are socialist-curious. It’s easy for non-members to show up to something local and feel comfortable getting to know DSA before joining. They get a chance to “dip their toes in,” and we build political reading and discussion on current issues and socialist theory into every agenda. These productive discussions help expose us to a variety of perspectives. We can gradually ease people into organizing and socialism in a way that isn’t overwhelming, so people can learn about democratic processes and how to be active participants in organizing out in the world. In organizing alongside our SIO’s, you can see the direct benefits of organizing. Another aspect of the membership growth and leadership development model is task rotation. Each meeting, we rotate the standard tasks: facilitators, notetakers, food coordinators, space reservers, political reading and discussion developers, etc., so everyone has an opportunity to try different roles. Lastly is the growth in numbers. In less than a year, we’ve doubled our membership from 30 members in December 2025 with an average of 12–13 meeting attendees to now having 60 members and 20–25 meeting attendees. Seventy different people have attended at least one SOWW meeting, and nine have joined DSA through their participation in SOWW. James M., engineer, Westland, Dylan Wegela re-election campaign co-chair: I joined DSA nearly a year ago after I was spotted at a No Kings Rally by my coworker and DSA member, Dan W. After attending a couple of General Meetings, I was struggling to find a group to get involved with because of the long distances to attend DSA meetings and I did not find Zoom meetings appealing. That’s when I got a call from Anthony about this Geographic Working Group to organize alongside our local SIOs. Fast-forward to today, and I am now one of the co-chairs for Dylan’s re-election campaign. Dylan’s campaign isn’t your typical campaign, as he represents a fairly blue district and is running unopposed in the primary on the Democratic Party ballot line. Dylan having no opponent is surprising given how he has fought the mainstream Democratic Party and corporate interests by voting ‘No’ on every corporate handout brought to a vote and is continuing the fight against corporate handouts and AI data centers in Michigan. Because Dylan is running unopposed, we have a unique opportunity to get creative with our campaign. Not just using it to get people to vote for Dylan, but to get Dylan’s constituents to work alongside him on tackling many of the issues plaguing our district, such as AI data centers, ICE occupation and detention facilities, tenants’ rights, and Flock. So far, we have held some traditional events like a campaign launch party with a turnout of over 200 people. We’ve also held campaign fundraisers and canvasses which we have used to engage with the community about the myriad of issues in our district. At the moment, we are planning to host some social events to bring the community out so that Dylan and SOWW can engage with them, with the goal to recruit more people into SOWW and DSA. We are also working on holding joint canvasses with Rashida’s team and other DSA-endorsed candidates. Elijah, engineer, Livonia, SOWW Flock campaign chair I joined DSA three months ago through my brother and through SOWW. SOWW has started a subcommittee to address Flock and AI-surveillance more broadly. I have been given the privilege of leading this group and bottom-lining our tasks. Our goal is to replicate the success of our sibling group in Ferndale. They were successfully able to push their city to cancel their Flock contract, proving that it is possible in southeast Michigan. In Westland, we are taking two parallel approaches. The first is to build broad public support. Our group has canvassed homes and created literature to spread. The second is to build connections with the city council and ensure we can make a persuasive case against the technology. To achieve both aims, we informed, motivated, and organized many new members who had been attending SOWW meetings. We set up weekly meetings to support each other. With the assistance of Ferndale and other knowledgeable members, we educated otherwise uninformed members about Flock and how best to go about the above goals. We have been successful in leveraging everyone’s unique skills and experience. Members who have never attended a city council meeting before are now showing up and speaking their minds. Other members are helping with creating literature, media, and petitions. Ensuring that we maintain pressure and consistent communication with Westland City Council has been possible thanks to the dedication of all members. With all the above efforts, we are confident in our chances. We look forward to reporting back in July when the contract is canceled [Westland City Council announced on July 9 that they would not be renewing the contract]. Further, we are eager to expand our efforts so that ALL people in southeast Michigan have their privacy rights protected. I would like to take a moment to talk about SOWW itself. SOWW presents a unique opportunity for this kind of work. First, local residents have the greatest impact on electeds in local communities, and second, SOWW is what gave me the opportunity to be a contributing, active socialist. I am grateful to this chapter for backing this initiative and bringing DSA closer to my home. Further, the opportunity to learn how to be a leader and how to organize from experienced members, such as Anthony, is also a privilege I am grateful for. The Next Six Months We plan to continue our recruitment of western Wayne County residents into SOWW and develop them into socialist organizers, through a cycle in which each new cohort is taught by the last. We will report back to membership again in six months at the December 2026 General Meeting. Through both our verbal and written debriefs we hope to continue normalizing open reporting on the successes and failures of our organizing work so we can all learn from it together. Learning From the Byron Campaign for Congress

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Midwestern Socialist -- Chicago DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-14T16:51:03+00:00

Over the last few months, it has been extremely exciting and gratifying to see the way members of the Chicago Democratic Socialists of America turned out to try to get our comrade Byron Sigcho-Lopez on the ballot to run for Congress. Whether motivated by anger at the comically underhanded attempts by Chuy and Patty Garcia to deny voters a choice in who represents them in Congress, a respect and admiration for Byron’s willingness to fight for the working class of the city, or both, our members ran a field campaign that gathered almost 13,000 signatures (65% of all signatures collected by the campaign) as part of the effort to get Byron on the ballot. I am not writing this to pretend that those efforts did not ultimately fall short, nor do I think it is helpful to try to pretend that we don’t all feel a little sad, frustrated, or demotivated about the way things ended. But we are socialists. Workplace organizing doesn’t stop when you run into a wall. Our brothers, sisters, and siblings of United Auto Workers Local 42 working for Volkswagen in Chattanooga, Tennessee lost two National Labor Relations Board elections before winning their union in 2024, and Lula da Silva ran for president in Brazil three times before finally breaking through in 2002. So we know we are in good company. With that being said, we should take a second and look at what we did do. Accomplishments -We turned out 167 of our members and 203 people in total to our canvasses. -We had 56 members turn out to three or more canvases. -We added 12 new people who are trained to be canvass leads. -We made connections on the southwest side with organizations like the Pilsen Defense Alliance and MORENA Chicago. Comrades who had never worked on an election campaign were able to talk to thousands and thousands of working-class Chicagoans about socialism, abolishing ICE, Medicare for All, and a more just, anti-imperialist foreign policy. Members of our South Side branch who had not been active in the chapter started leading canvasses, coming to meetings, and integrating themselves more into the chapter. Our West Cook branch ran a whole field operation of their own, building capacity to run campaigns in the west suburbs themselves. The Democracy Working Group, the Environmental Justice Working Group, the Membership Engagement Committee, and the Executive Committee all turned out members to canvasses they co-sponsored. Comrades in the Chicago Teachers Union organized members in their workplaces to turnout to canvas for Byron. What I was most excited about was the way we showed up across geographical lines. Every branch sponsored multiple canvasses. We organized car pools to assist comrades in getting down to neighborhoods from West Lawn to Pilsen. The branch that had the most members turn out for Byron was the farthest away: the North Side Red Line branch. Their efforts in making sure that comrades were linked to people that could give them rides helped turn out members, and serves as a model we should try to replicate as much as possible going forward. In this campaign, we were able to answer the question of whether or not our membership is willing to travel to electorally organize with a resounding yes. With that being said, Byron did not get on the ballot. The Democratic Party, the Chuy Garcia machine, and Illinois election laws (designed by the very people that benefit from them) conspired to use a rigged system to keep Byron from being able to run to keep their self-appointed heirs from having to face a real challenge. This is an important thing to remember; the people who benefit from the status quo, the people who serve the interests of capital, are not going to give up their advantages without a fight. They will fight dirty, and they will be ruthless in the protection of their interests and their offices. But we should not despair! Losses are not truly losses if we identify lessons to be learned from them and incorporate those lessons into actions we take in the future. Lessons Learned Structural Barriers for Independent Ballot Line The first of those lessons, in my opinion, is that in Illinois, with the way current laws are set up, barring extreme circumstances (like what happened in the 4th congressional district), we should focus on contesting the Democratic primary. It has been said over and over in Byron’s campaign that Patty Garcia only needed to get 900 signatures to be on the ballot, whereas Byron needed to get roughly 10,800. In addition, there is a maximum number of signatures one can gather. Bryon was only allowed to submit 18,700 signatures, which negates a major defense against signature challenges. A candidate trying to run as an independent or with a not-currently-established third party cannot even begin circulating petitions until after the primary is held, and must wait until the end of May to turn them in. The Democratic party is going to be lying in wait for them there, as we have seen. They will unleash an army of lawyers and political operatives to comb through signatures, objecting to anything they find whether in good faith or not. If a candidate survives that, they will start their campaign on the back foot, with even less time to organize the kind of field campaign necessary to win. We spent so much time simply trying to get the signatures necessary to get on the ballot that we were not able to do the kinds of things we would have been able to do if we had our candidate already on the ballot. We were not able to construct a list of likely Byron voters, or start the process of doing the persuasive canvassing that is needed to build on that list. Having to start that process in June against a candidate who has been able to do those things since January was always going to be a very difficult challenge. This state of affairs is not only frustrating, but also anti-democratic. The system is rigged against candidates trying to run outside of the two-party system. But we are socialists. Many of us are Marxists. We do not view the world through an idealistic lens, but a materialist one. These are the laws as they currently exist, and we should fight to change them. At the same time, we must both acknowledge that they are a barrier, and that comrades across the country running in partisan races have been able to elect avowed socialists to both federal and state offices by snatching the Democratic Party’s ballot line away from them, taking these struggles out of the hands of party lawyers and paid hacks and placing them in the hands of the electorate. Of course, these issues will come up even if we run on a Democratic ballot line. Again, the capitalist class and their officeholders will not hesitate to utilize anything they can to put roadblocks in our way. But if we look at the numbers needed to both get on the ballot andd win a primary, the struggle becomes easier. A candidate would have only needed to get around 700 signatures to get on the ballot to run in the Democratic primary in 2026. This is in contrast to needing over 10,000 to run as a third-party or independent candidate. The smaller number allows for a better chance of being able to survive any potential challenges, if only by being able to double, triple, or quadruple the number of signatures needed, a strategy denied to independent candidacies by a signature cap. In addition to that, the win number becomes much smaller. In the last competitive Democratic primary in the 4th Congressional District, Chuy Garcia defeated local goblin Ray Lopez, winning 30,443 votes to RayLo’s 13,286. What I saw floated around for Byron’s win number (what it would take to win the race), what was in the packet to go off to National for a potential endorsement, was roughly 70,000 votes. In almost any case yet seen, the winner of a Democratic primary goes on to win the general election in Chicago. Illinois has laws to prevent losers of primaries from running in general elections. From a purely strategic perspective, barring blatant corruption like we have seen this year, it makes far more sense to contest partisan races on the Democratic ballot line. From an ideological standpoint, I do not think that Darializa Avila Chevalier or Chris Rabb are going to fight for socialism or working people any less because they entered Congress through a Democratic Party ballot line. American laws do not allow for the type of disciplining of politicians that many comrades would like. If every DSA socialist in office was elected on the Socialist Party line, we could not recall them. We could expel them from the party, but they could contest our ballot line in the next election. Simply put, if we choose to push forward with a complete breach with an elected official, no matter what vehicle they were elected through, we still have to raise up a challenger and defeat them on Election Day. In effect, the Democratic Party gets to choose which independents make it on the ballot; they allow rightists, centrists, and weak leftist challengers, but can effectively block socialist and progressive candidates like Byron and Mayra who have a real shot. Given these handicaps, without election reform, I can’t imagine the race where it would be worth running Chicago DSA-endorsed candidates as independents when the Democratic Party ballot line is contestable. The challenges are vast and the benefits relatively small. With that being said, there are certainly things we could have done to make it easy for ourselves. To begin with, we could have been curing (the process of checking signatures to make sure they are registered voters at the address they listed) petition sheets as they were gathered. While this was somewhat out of our hands as a chapter, if we had been curing as we went from the beginning, I believe we would have been able to identify the unregistered voters or misregistered voters we had on our petitions as we were getting them. We would have had a running count and known exactly where we were. I would suggest, going forward, that in any campaign we run, we should have a dedicated group of people going over signatures in between canvasses. Looking through the amount of signatures, I believe that we could have done better in really highlighting to our canvassers the need to make sure that people signing the petition were registered voters in the district. I think part of this was caused by not having as many Spanish-speaking canvassers as we needed, and part of this was caused by a desire to get as many signatures as we could. We probably canvassed in Pilsen for longer than we needed to, and moving to the west of the district where there were more English speakers would have helped with this issue. There was a feeling that ‘when in doubt, have them sign’ that probably should have been tempered with a caution about making sure our signatures were registered voters in the district. Having more trainings independent of the canvasses would have been very helpful in drilling home that we needed to make sure that the voter was registered at the given address. I don’t think going door-to-door to get signatures would have solved the problem as much as it would have created a different one. Simply put, when we went door-to-door, we were just not getting enough signatures for us to have been able to meet our target. In many instances, it was very common for a team of two to come back from a shift with fewer than ten signatures. Where records exist, the average number of signatures gathered from a door-to-door canvass team was six. It’s true that really training people on talking to everyone they came into contact with in a neighborhood would have certainly added to that number, but I don’t think it would have been enough to really put a dent in that difference. There is a formula one can use to figure out how many voter attempts needed to be made to reach the goal you are seeking. For our purposes, we will say the goal is 11,000 signatures. The formula is 11,000, divided by the percentage of people who answer the door, divided by the percentage of people you talk to who will sign the petition. So we would say 11,000, divided by an 18% contact rate, divided by a 75% success rate would mean to get our goal we would have needed to knock 97,777 doors. To place that number in perspective, during the entirety of Chris Rabb’s campaign for Congress in Philadelphia, they made 140,000 voter attempts. That is not to say we used VAN (the voter management software used to do door-to-door canvassing) effectively. It would have been a great tool to check signatures against voter registration tools, as was seen when signatures were challenged. Campaign Operations In addition, there are things we can improve upon that are independent of the specifics of the campaign. We did not do the best job of collecting data. Often, canvassers went out and there was no data collected beyond who showed up and how many signatures were collected. We did not always remember to have people sign in. While this did not prevent us from accomplishing what we set out to do, it does mean that our analysis of what we did is less than perfect. Our communications during the course of the petitioning was uneven. In my opinion, in future campaigns, it will be essential to have regular meetings with the Communications Committee to discuss what we want to say and how we want to say it, and have a schedule of regular communications about the campaigns we are doing and the issues we want to highlight as we go forward. Our comms should be able to inform the wider public about what we are fighting for, and should make electoral organizing seem fun. Member Engagement and Cohesion This brings me to the last item I think we can improve on going forward. We really struggled to add any sense of social cohesion. Ideally, participating in the struggle binds comrades together. We are more likely to spend three or four hours on a weekend canvassing if we are doing it with people that we feel we have a bond with. Some of that bonding is forged in the work, but many of our sister chapters that have been successful in this type of organizing have regular social debriefs after canvasses and other events where comrades can not only talk about the work, but also get to know each other better. It’s certainly true that some of the issues we had with pulling those off had to do with the nature of the campaign. Comrades who were coming from the Northside probably were less inclined to want to stick around facing a 45 minute to an hour commute to get home from the canvass launch. In city council elections that will no doubt be more dispersed across the city, we will probably find comrades more willing to hang out with others afterwards. With that said, however, we will need to work on making these kinds of things more accessible and ‘built-in’ to future campaigns, as I think they will help quite a bit with getting comrades to come back to more canvasses and events. Looking Ahead Any campaign we do as a chapter is going to have more than one vector of determining whether or not it was a success. We failed to get Byron on the ballot, but we actively campaigned in parts of the city we did not have much of a presence in, we rebuilt a cadre of the membership excited and willing to work in electoral organizing, and we identified a core of people that are willing to step into leadership roles in future campaigns we will work on. We should be proud of what we accomplished this spring. We should be excited about what lies ahead for us as a chapter! Chicago DSA has a cadre of veteran organizers with a wealth of knowledge, we have newer members who are excited to talk to people about socialism and are learning how to become more effective organizers, and we have a plan for 2027, informed by the lessons we have learned over the last eight years. As we have seen across the country, nothing energizes people about DSA like electoral victories. If we continue to work, execute our plans, and learn from the inevitable mistakes we will make along the way, there will come a time where the next spike in DSA membership will come from Chicago. Colombia and Israel’s Cynical Disaster Diplomacy

On the morning of August 10, a powerful 7.4 magnitude earthquake struck western Colombia, leaving nearly three hundred people dead, four thousand injured, and potentially thousands more missing by the latest count. The crisis created an early test for new far-right President Abelardo de la Espriella, who was inaugurated days before. A few hours after the quake, as Colombians still lay under the rubble of destroyed buildings, the government published a long statement recognizing Israeli sovereignty over the Golan Heights, a Syrian territory illegally occupied by the country since 1967. The statement, which triggered a diplomatic crisis with the Arab states, placed Colombia alongside the United States as the only other country in the world to recognize Israel’s claim over the territory. While the Colombian government was rightfully panned at home and abroad for the timing of its announcement, it was not incidental. Although de la Espriella’s desire to deepen ties with Israel predates the earthquake, the new administration’s moves since the disaster demonstrate that it plans on using the crisis to justify its larger rapprochement with the Middle Eastern country. This dynamic just played out in Venezuela, where Israel’s provision of humanitarian aid in the wake of deadly earthquakes paved the way for the reestablishment of consular relations between the former foes. The closer alignment between Colombia and Israel is especially difficult to swallow given the former country’s recent role as a leader of the global Palestinian solidarity movement under President Gustavo Petro, de la Espriella’s predecessor. Nevertheless, it’s part of a wider regional trend in which far-right governments brandish their allegiance to Israel or immediately make overtures to its leaders upon taking power. Disaster Diplomacy The August 10 earthquake caused intense damage in the departments of Chocó (the epicenter), Valle del Cauca, Risaralda, Quindío, and Caldas. The power went out, buildings collapsed, and the human toll continues to climb . The government, scrambling to deal with the chaos, mobilized state resources and relied on local communities themselves for much of the immediate disaster relief. The first seventy-two hours are regarded as essential in earthquake response to preserving the life of trapped survivors, yet it took a whole three days for the state to formally accept international rescue teams and, when it finally did, it limited itself to collaborating only with right-wing allies — the United States, Ecuador, El Salvador, and Israel. While the last could come as a surprise, this was a familiar role for the Israelis to play in the region. For decades , the country has provided humanitarian aid and rescue teams in the aftermath of natural disasters. In recent years, the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) and the Israeli Forum for International Aid (IsraAID) have deployed to Peru , Mexico , and Haiti following earthquakes as well as Brazil after its 2019 dam collapse . This aid functions as a form of disaster diplomacy that seeks to launder Israel’s image on the international stage even as it simultaneously wields humanitarian aid in Gaza as a weapon of war against Palestinians. Through these means, the Israeli state seeks to improve diplomatic relations with countries whose governments may at one time have been more critical. In neighboring Venezuela, the provision of Israeli aid in the wake of devastating twin earthquakes set the stage for their reestablishment of consular ties. As Simón Rodríguez recently reported in NACLA , Israel’s rescue mission was explicitly framed by both sides as a way to reinitiate diplomatic ties that had been severed under former Venezuelan leader Hugo Chávez. Early on, Interim President Delcy Rodríguez expressed her hope that the aid would “open new paths and doors” with “countries with which we do not have diplomatic relations”; Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, meanwhile, thanked the Israeli rescue team for “rebuilding ruins and relations.” In Venezuela, the same humanitarian actors taking part in the mission played an active role in justifying and later perpetuating Israel’s ongoing genocide in Gaza. As Rodríguez notes , ZAKA, an NGO founded in the 1990s by a former member of an ultraorthodox terrorist organization , was on the ground shortly after the quakes. The organization, which specializes in emergency response and rescue operations in Israel and abroad, was a prime source for many of the myths that the US media used to justify genocide in response to the October 7 attacks, including the narrative of beheaded babies . Yosef Garmon, its Latin America director, once published a video of himself speaking Spanish from the ruins of Gaza, appealing to Christian Zionists for their support; in Caracas, he met with university students, whom he filmed awkwardly joining him in a chant of “Am Yisrael Chai” (“The people of Israel live”). ZAKA praised the announcement that Israel and Venezuela were formally restoring consular relations. It also took its share of the credit , pointing to its mission as an essential part of the process. The Venezuela Playbook in Colombia Already in the country, having arrived in Bogotá hours before, Garmon was giving interviews to Israeli television from Colombia shortly after the quake struck on Monday morning. Days earlier, he had been in the city of Cali, where he had once served as rabbi , for the presidential inauguration of Abelardo de la Espriella. De la Espriella has made a rapprochement with Israel a priority. Prior to his inauguration, he had announced his intention to move Colombia’s embassy to Jerusalem and suspend the planned opening of an embassy in Ramallah, in addition to meeting with Israeli Foreign Minister Gideon Sa’ar. He also announced the creation of a bilateral economic commission and a formal visit to the country by a Colombian delegation in October. Under the cover of earthquake response, Colombia has taken its support for Israel to new levels, including in a statement recognizing Israeli sovereignty over the Golan Heights. More substantively, the government took advantage of the natural disaster to revoke two decrees that had banned the export of Colombian coal to Israel. The mandated five-day window to comment on the proposed legislation began the day after the earthquake, when attention was directed elsewhere. These actions have been warmly received by the Israeli government. On Tuesday, the day after the earthquake, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu posted a video thanking Colombia for its recognition of Israel’s claim to the Golan Heights, saying that just as the “people are with the Golan Heights,” today the “people are with Colombia.” The day after, following a request from de la Espriella, Netanyahu ordered the dispatch of a formal humanitarian mission. Israel was one of the few countries granted permission to send a rescue team alongside the United States, Ecuador, and El Salvador. While the government has denied that any ideological considerations are at play — claiming instead that these missions were the only ones that “comply with international protocols” — it is clear that they were only willing to accept desperately needed assistance from their right-wing allies. The government’s rejection of an elite search and rescue team from Mexico, which yesterday morning President Claudia Sheinbaum disclosed was refused entry into the country, made the politicization of aid clear. Israel’s formal aid mission will complement the work of ZAKA, which is already on the ground in affected cities like Cali and Pereira. As they did in Venezuela , Israelis are already sharing stories with the media about the positive reception they are receiving from Colombian citizens. One Israeli businessman who had also attended de la Espriella’s inauguration spoke of Colombians approaching him and asking for his blessings in the aftermath of the earthquakes. It was “the opposite of hatred,” he said. Right-wing Colombian politicians are also publicly praising Israeli organizations like ZAKA, highlighting their technological marvels and the essential contribution they will make to the recovery efforts. Israeli aid may very well be an essential component of saving lives in a situation that remains dangerous; people remain trapped under buildings, and the window for finding survivors among the rubble has all but closed. Likewise, for many Colombians, the belief in Israel’s technological prowess is likely apolitical, a response to an increasingly dire situation. Nevertheless, the government’s rejection of foreign rescue missions like the Mexican brigadistas — a force with far more experience in post-earthquake operations — will surely lead to unnecessary death. The Colombian government’s attention to Israeli matters has stood in stark contrast to the coordination of its own rescue efforts. A Regional Trend Under Gustavo Petro, Colombia was a global leader in fighting to hold Israel accountable for its genocidal crimes. Petro severed diplomatic ties and banned the export of coal to Israel. His outspoken advocacy for the Palestinian cause also placed him firmly in the crosshairs of the Trump administration, which at one point revoked his US visa for his criticisms. Though he went further than any leader in the region, Petro was not alone in criticizing Israel’s genocide. At the time, the leaders of Bolivia , Chile , and Honduras did the same, often joining forces in organizations like the Hague Group, dedicated to finding ways to hold Israel to account. Latin America was at the forefront of the Palestinian solidarity movement: in addition to its outspoken leaders, students led solidarity encampments , trade unions launched innovative campaigns to boycott Israeli goods, and indigenous groups saw their struggles mirrored in the plight of the Palestinians. Nevertheless, in the last year, a series of right-wing electoral victories have drastically changed the picture, as these new leaders quickly walked back any pro-Palestinian policies. In the past year, newly elected presidents like Rodrigo Paz of Bolivia, the far-right José Antonio Kast of Chile , and Nasry Asfura of Honduras (who happens to be of Palestinian descent) reopened or actively strengthened ties with Israel since taking office. A year earlier, with the Israeli government increasingly isolated on the global stage, it turned its attention to Latin America, and its efforts seem to be paying off. At the end of last year, Israeli Foreign Minister Gideon Sa’ar declared that 2026 would be the “year of Latin America” for Israel. He has since worked to make that dream a reality, meeting with allied foreign ministers and visiting the region in person. In the cases of Venezuela and Colombia, natural disaster relief has provided the Israeli state with a vehicle to connect to local populations directly. It has also enabled these governments to legitimize their rapprochements with a state that is widely denounced by the majority of the population. Foreign aid missions have long been understood as an extension of diplomacy. Yet aid driven primarily by political rather than humanitarian objectives — exemplified by the Colombian administration’s rejection of rescue missions from nonaligned countries and Israel’s framing of humanitarian relief as a vehicle to achieve geopolitical goals and secure access to resources — only reflects right-wing leaders’ aversion to diplomatic cooperation in favor of transactionalism.

Help Us Break the Two-Party Stranglehold, James Carville

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Ben Burgis ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-14T14:11:56+00:00

With his bald head, glasses, and Cajun accent, political consultant James Carville was long one of the most iconic representatives of the Democratic Party establishment. In the 1990s, he was often seen as a kind of consigliere to President Bill Clinton. In the 2000s, he was everywhere on cable television offering up his characteristic mix of colorful zingers and boilerplate liberal politics. Now he says he’s considering leaving the Democrats altogether. In multiple interviews, he’s raged against the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) and left-wing influencer Hasan Piker . In an appearance on Fox Business earlier this month, he expressed amazement at “this idea that we’re going to seize the means of production” and urged viewers to “just go look at the DSA platform.” ( I agree .) At one point, he said: I am not going to be in the same party with Hasan Piker, I can tell you that right now. If he becomes a force in the Democratic Party, I’m outta here. I have no intention of ever being in the same political party with that guy. As the segment continued, he said that the “two-party system” might be under “tremendous stress” in the coming months, and that he wasn’t sure what would happen. The one thing he could say “for sure,” though, is that he and Hasan Piker won’t be in the same party. Sadly, this is almost certainly hot air. But if he does turn out to be serious, and he and other centrists start a third party, that would be an excellent thing for American democracy. Ways to “Leave” the Democratic Party When Carville talks about leaving the Democratic Party, his meaning is not entirely clear. For one thing, the Democrats and Republicans aren’t membership organizations like European or Latin American political parties. If you’re a congressman or a senator, you can be a member of the Democratic caucus within one of those institutions, but technically there’s nothing for a citizen like Carville to either join or leave. Instead, “the Democratic Party” is a nebulous concept that vaguely refers to many things — the Democratic caucuses in various elected bodies, the Democratic “brand,” the population of Americans who vote in Democratic primaries or consider themselves Democrats, and so on. Most of all, though, it refers to the Democratic ballot line . America’s half-democratic election system is designed to make it extremely difficult for any candidate who isn’t on either the Democratic or the Republican ballot line to meaningfully compete in a general election. The deck is stacked against third-party candidates on every level, from signature gathering requirements to participation in debates. Additionally, we have what’s called a “first-past-the-post” voting system, where voters can’t rank candidates. Instead of a runoff when no candidate clears 50 percent, whoever wins a plurality is simply declared the winner. This creates strong incentives for voters to vote not for their ideological first choice but simply for the least-bad candidate with a chance of winning. This in turn creates collective action problems that make it extremely difficult to build up a new party to a level where it can win. That’s why the overwhelming majority of the socialist left in the United States has concluded that trying to form a ballot-line third party is a nonstarter under current conditions. The deck is stacked in many ways against insurgents in major-party primaries, but realistically it’s stacked much more against third-party candidates . If we want to actually elect socialists to office rather than simply run symbolic protest candidacies, the only way to do that is to challenge the Democratic establishment for the Democratic ballot line in races where there’s a strong socialist contender. What a Fuller Democracy Might Look Like In the short term, this has been an effective strategy. As of now, it looks like several new socialists will be arriving in Congress after this fall’s general election, a fact that has caused a cascade of rolling meltdowns among centrist commentators and politicians. In the long run, as Carville says, the two-party system will be under increasing stress. Sooner or later, divorce may be inevitable. If Carville “leaves the Democratic Party” in the sense that he starts rhetorically calling himself an independent but nothing else changes, and he still regularly weighs in on Democratic contests, that means nothing. If he leaves to become a Republican, an annoying presence will be removed from those contests (and it’s unlikely that the GOP will benefit much from his presence). But the least likely possibility indicated by his comment about the two-party system is the most interesting. What if Carville convinces some nontrivial subset of his centrist friends to leave and start a third party with its own ballot line? If nothing else changes, the dynamics described above will destroy the new centrist party just as they’ve crushed every attempt to form a third party on the Left. In order to get a different result, at a bare minimum, Carville and his friends would have to push for reforms like ranked-choice voting that would remove many of these hurdles. The good news for them is that, as they fight for election reforms, they can tap into networks of deep-pocketed corporate and individual donors to which the Left has no access. Who knows? Perhaps they can even pull it off. If so, they would be doing the Left a tremendous favor. In 2016 and 2020, polls often showed that Bernie Sanders would cruise to victory over Donald Trump if he reached the general election, but he never did. If Carville and whomever he brought with him truly were able to reform our system to the point where a centrist third party was viable, then the Left would be spared the choice between fighting an uphill battle over a ballot line ultimately controlled by our ideological enemies and fighting a much steeper uphill battle against the basic dynamics of American elections. Instead, there could be one ballot line controlled by socialists, another controlled by centrists, and voters could face a real choice without the distorting effect of “spoiler” worries. It won’t be easy, but if Carville can figure it out, the Left will owe him a debt of gratitude.

Mainstream Economic Myths Are Disastrous for US Policy

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: James K. Galbraith ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-14T13:42:20+00:00

After decades of domination by the neoliberal consensus, Washington has started to break with that paradigm under recent presidential administrations, including Joe Biden’s and Donald Trump’s. Their embrace of tariffs and efforts to rebuild domestic manufacturing capacity are a departure from previous policy orthodoxy, inspired in part by the rise of China as an economic and geopolitical powerhouse and, in Biden’s case, by the challenge of climate change. James K. Galbraith is the Lloyd J. Bentsen chair of government/business relations at the Lyndon B. Johnson School of Public Affairs and a professor in government at the University of Texas at Austin. He previously worked on the House Banking Committee and served as executive director of the Joint Economic Committee of Congress in the early 1980s. An influential heterodox voice on economics, he is the author of several books, including The Predator State: How Conservatives Abandoned the Free Market and Why Liberals Should Too (2008) and Inequality: What Everyone Needs to Know (2016). In his newest release, The Power to Destroy: How Bad Economics Drove America’s Decline , out next month from University of Chicago Press, Galbraith argues that the dominant framework embraced by mainstream economists has had a disastrous effect on US economic policy over the last several decades. In an interview with Jacobin , Galbraith discussed the arguments of The Power to Destroy , including the problems he sees with Biden and Trump’s recent efforts to move beyond the policy orthodoxy and what the future might portend for US financial and military dominance. Ana G. Perez The Power to Destroy is in large part about how the widely accepted neoclassical economics framework — what you call “equilibrium economics” — is empirically discredited and harmful to American economic policymaking. Could you summarize your analysis of equilibrium economics? James K. Galbraith First, let me speak in rather general terms about the mindset that one is brought up in as a trained economist. It is a product of an effort at system-building, which had these two not completely well-aligned dimensions: the so-called micro and macro. The micro dimension, which became dominant during Ronald Reagan’s administration, essentially gives priority to anything that happens to be organized by a market and says the role of the government is just to come in when there are problems with the market. In fact, in the real world, markets only exist because governments provide a regulatory framework within which they can function. The other piece of it, macroeconomics, starts from the same general idea: that the economy as a whole tends toward a steady state of growth. If it goes down, it’s going to come back up again. Built into this framework are such notions as the so-called trade-off between inflation and unemployment and a whole series of concepts that were predominant in the education of economists of my generation. These ideas are still very much influential on important institutions. Those institutions include the Federal Reserve, which has gotten the notion that it has the ability to control the inflation rate and the price level; or the Congressional Budget Office, which has the notion that the federal budget ought to be balanced. These are conceits or prejudices that are brought to the discussion by the background noise of a century’s worth of economists framing the policy discussion for everybody else. It’s a simpleminded but also highly unrealistic portrait of how actual societies work — of how any living system works. A much more successful policy system could be built up from an alternative conception. What I was trying to do in this book was to give a series of cases in which you could pick out where the influence of standard economic thinking had been predominant and point to the consequences. Ana G. Perez One of the case studies you analyze is the inflation that dominated the news in 2021 and 2022. You argue that the actions taken by the Federal Reserve to control inflation by raising interest rates not only failed but also might have been harmful in other ways. James K. Galbraith There has been a reflexive way of speaking about the Federal Reserve when it raises interest rates. It’s almost automatic that the media will report the Federal Reserve raised interest rates “in order to control inflation.” The reality is that the lever the Federal Reserve has, which is the short-term interest rate, has no connection to the inflation rate or the price level. The only way the Federal Reserve can actually knock down prices is by forcing the economy through a long and painful contraction — throwing a lot of people out of work, bankrupting a lot of businesses. That has happened in the past; it happened in the early 1980s. But if you look at the at the financial structure of the American economy today, it’s fundamentally different. Trade unions are much smaller and much weaker. The manufacturing sector is much smaller; employment is largely in services. The public debt is much larger as a share of GDP. And when the interest rate goes up, as it did under Jerome Powell from early 2022 onward, none of the of the sharp contraction that Paul Volcker achieved through impoverishment of the economy in 1979–1980 actually occurred. So one can’t say that the decline in the inflation rate was due to the Fed. It had nothing to do with the Fed, because the only channel that is operative wasn’t there. What was going on was that in the wake of the pandemic, first, oil prices [went up]. Second, supply chains got blocked and jumbled. So new automobiles became scarce, and the price of used cars went up. And then there was some complicated business with housing. These price increases had nothing to do with any kind of general increase in demand. There was then, particularly at the start of the Biden administration, a very substantial increase in federal spending. A lot of people — Lawrence Summers, for example — made a big issue out of that, saying it was going to drive inflation. He was working from a background model or mental framework that was well established fifty or sixty years ago, when we were much younger and starting our careers. But if you look at what happened when American households got relief from COVID-19 — some under Donald Trump and some under Joe Biden — they very sensibly said to themselves, “I’m out of work right now. This is a useful financial buffer. I will store it up and draw it down over time, and that will give me some leverage. I don’t have to go back to work quite so fast; I’m not desperate to get back to my previously held lousy job.” They didn’t go on some kind of spending spree that would, in the imagination of the economist, drive up the inflation rate. This is not what happened at all. American households behaved like relatively prudent, risk-averse entities. They saved a lot of [the relief money]. And then they drew down their savings as they needed it. There’s no mechanism there that’s going to drive up prices. What did drive up prices in certain markets, like real estate, was the conduct of people whose incomes were not disrupted by COVID: in other words, people like me who had salaries and who didn’t get a lot of benefit from COVID relief. What happened to this class, the top 20 or 25 percent of the income distribution, was that we stopped having access to all the things we were spending money on. Restaurants and travel — the whole services economy shut down. So, we piled up money. What did we do? We remodeled kitchens; we built houses. We went out and bought real estate. We drove up the prices of fixed assets. But driving up the price of a house is not an inflationary act. It’s raising the net worth of somebody who owns a house. What I’m urging in this chapter on inflation is for people to get into the weeds and figure out what’s really happening, and then design policies that are relevant to the actual conditions. The person who did that, and who did it quite brilliantly, was Isabella Weber , a young economist at the University of Massachusetts Amherst. She pointed out that the when the system was hit by a really major disorderly event, a lot of the habits that normally constrain the behavior of businesses are broken, and they end up scrambling to try to raise prices as much as possible in order to make sure that they can cover their costs. That’s a competitive phenomenon, which you can observe by the rise of profit margins. That also occurred. The right response to that is to constrain the behavior. It’s not to hit them with a large shock to their sales. Just constrain their behavior so that they can continue to make a reasonable profit, and so they can get back to the sense of reasonable stability of this structured relationship between their prices and their costs. Ana G. Perez You also analyze the COVID response more broadly and then Biden’s big infrastructure bill, the CHIPS Act, and the Inflation Reduction Act. One argument you make is that, even when policymakers are trying to rebuild US industry, the American economy has changed too much, from a manufacturing to a service economy, and state capacity has deteriorated. So these efforts don’t work; policymakers don’t have enough to work with. James K. Galbraith On COVID: like many people, I became essentially locked up, in my third-floor rooms in my house in Austin. I became very active online and very active in publishing and in trying to move the COVID mobilization in ways that I thought were necessary to meet the supply chain problems. That was not the direction that the policy eventually took. The policy essentially took the direction of flooding the system with money. That forced me to consider what was going on in the underlying economy. The first thing was recognizing that the actual capacity of the government to take concrete steps on specific technical problems, like the supply chain issues, was very small. Let’s leave aside whether masks were a good or a bad idea. The reality was that there was a certain amount of capacity, and the private firms making those things were not all that eager to ramp up their capacity, because, quite reasonably, they figured that once this was over, that would be a sunk investment. They’d have to eat the loss. The reaction in China was very different. Around a thousand firms in China switched over from producing whatever it was, any kind of paper-type product, to making surgical masks. And they exported them very rapidly around the world. That tells you that the structure of productive business in China is very different from what it is here: much more flexible, much more responsive. And if you have the Chinese doing it, you don’t really need other people doing it. You just need to get them to ship the masks out, which they did. The other part was: What were the consequences of flooding the American system with money? Passing out money is something the American government is very good at doing. With a few glitches at the beginning in the unemployment compensation systems and so forth, it got a lot of money into the hands of people. Most of those people were working in service jobs that were suspended, and so they were not getting their regular payroll. In some cases, they got more money from the COVID relief than they were previously earning. What one discovered is that this was not a catastrophe, so long as they were able to meet their commitments: rent, utilities, and food. And they’re saving money on gas. Of course, schooling was a big problem. But by and large, when the situation begins to normalize, a lot of people were not all that anxious to go back to the kind of jobs that they had before. Their lives were adjusting in various ways. Many people did not go back into the labor force, did not rejoin the job search, which tells you a lot about how they felt about the jobs that they had previously been in. Ireland’s Left Should Lead the Campaign for Irish Unity

Earthquake elections are rare under Ireland’s unique form of proportional representation. Trends unfold slowly, and elections yield identikit governments, coalitions after coalitions that do little more than tinker with economic and social policy. But two parliamentary by-elections in May this year did capture a clean snapshot of the country’s main electoral dynamics. In Dublin Central and Galway East, Fianna Fáil suffered some of its worst-ever election performances, less than two years after it returned to power as part of the latest coalition government. Stuck on a similar path of secular decline, Fine Gael won the Galway West seat by a slender margin in a rebuff to doomy predictions, but its candidate only defeated the ascendant right-wing party Independent Ireland with the help of vote transfers. Daniel Ennis, a former footballer who entered politics to oppose racism in his inner-city community, took the Dublin Central seat for the Social Democrats, edging past Sinn Féin. His party, known as the Soc Dems, is a rising force in Irish politics and particularly popular with young people. Some jumped to claim this as a scalp for the “Connolly coalition.” This is a loose network of parties, stretching from the radical-left People Before Profit (PBP) to the centrist Green Party, that came together last year to campaign for the presidency of Catherine Connolly. For PBP’s Paul Murphy, the combined 60 percent vote for the broad left showed that a formal transfer pact and preelection agreement on policy — an organized “left alternative” to the century-long governing project of the two conservative parties whose roots go back to the Irish Civil War — will be both feasible and popular come the next general election in 2028. Rituals of Unity Yet there has been a certain ritualistic quality to these overtures and their inevitable rejections. When almost anything happens in Irish politics, PBP can be heard bellowing about the need for transfer pacts to bring about a first “left government.” When Sinn Féin underperforms or fears an underperformance, it reasserts some form of “left-republican cooperation” as a guiding principle, flirting with the idea of a common left platform. Outside of tense election phases, however, the party sweeps the idea neatly under the rug. When Labour ponders its abysmal current standing and even more abysmal likely future, it beckons for a soft-left bloc alongside the Green Party and the Soc Dems, a negotiating vehicle that could enter government talks directly with Fianna Fáil and Fine Gael after the next election, free of the Marxist taint of PBP and the baggage of Sinn Féin’s republican past. But these unprincipled power-seekers would discard even such a bloc-within-a-bloc if a future seat total gave them enough heft to prop up yet another center-right government. And when the Soc Dems, originally formed as a breakaway group from Labour, heed the views of their most radical supporters, they conjure radiant visions of a united front, eager to talk up the prospect of a left government and careful to invite the radical left to meetings from which Labour sought to exclude it. Yet the party leadership has unequivocally ruled out formal cooperation while inconsistently and half-heartedly promoting informal voting agreements. In short, all parties bar PBP treat the concept of left unity as window dressing. The Connolly Coalition One of the buzziest postelection stories — at least for bored political correspondents who enjoy cozy relationships with politicians — has concerned the frailties of the previously vaunted Connolly coalition. There are, we are beginning to be told, unbridgeable differences between its constituent parts. In the wake of a stream of “public criticisms,” a well-briefed reporter even declared the burgeoning alliance dead. PBP, Labour, and the Soc Dems have all repeatedly voiced concerns about Sinn Féin’s opportunistic drift right on immigration. The center-left parties also fret over the party’s “populist” opposition to certain forms of taxation. Tentative signs of unity have, however, been visible at least three occasions. After the latest coalition government was formed in 2024, the parties of the Left spoke as one to vociferously oppose a plan to hand pro-government independents the speaking time of opposition Teachtaí Dála (TDs). They have also united at various points to push back against attacks on Irish neutrality and to support a full ban on trade in both goods and services with Israeli settlements in Palestine. Somewhat miraculously, these parties canvassed and campaigned together as part of the joint presidential campaign for independent socialist Catherine Connolly, who ended up winning 63.4 percent of the vote — the largest vote tally and share for any presidential candidate in the state’s history. Sinn Féin leader Mary Lou McDonald hailed this “historic political effort which, for the first time, brought together the combined Opposition to present a positive alternative to the jaded politics of Fianna Fáil and Fine Gael.” Unwilling to allow the momentum to dissipate, PBP called for “a major conference of the left to discuss the next steps of continuing and deepening left co-operation.” For her part, Soc Dems leader Holly Cairns opted for a vacuous Obamaism: “Change doesn’t stop here.” But the presidential election was not a good bellwether for the promise of a left government. Before long, talk of a unified candidate in the Galway West by-election came to nought. Connolly’s own election campaign had focused on the issue of neutrality, and the coalition behind it was fragile and ramshackle. Sinn Féin appeared to back Connolly only because of a dearth of suitable internal candidates and her increasingly outspoken views on reunification, while Labour only got on board reluctantly due to Sinn Féin’s presence. Despite the kumbaya optics of the Connolly campaign, it is unrealistic to suggest that any of these forces has ever desired an unconditional form of “left unity.” Attempts at unity have foundered on questions of ideology, strategy, personality, and policy. This degree of fractiousness may seem absurd, but behind it lies a crude rationale. The electoral system often rewards small parties with seats, minor influence, and — if you play your cards right — ministerial berths. And perhaps nobody understands this fact better than Sinn Féin, which is desperate to escape the cordon sanitaire it has been placed under by the establishment parties. The All-Ireland Agenda After his party’s disappointing performance in the 2024 general election, Sinn Féin Chairperson Declan Kearney’s postmortem analysis repeated the customary out-of-election plea for, in his Corbynite phrasing, “a political axis representing the many, not the few.” When it was already too late, here was a member of Sinn Féin’s inner sanctum making the case for an opposition manifesto to buttress a “progressive left republican bloc which respects the independence and autonomy of cooperating political parties, and other TDs.” But this kind of intervention was nothing new. Back in 2003, a fresh-faced Eoin Ó Broin, now the party’s erudite spokesperson for housing, made a similar call for his party to target working-class voters as part of a “left/green/republican alliance” that exists both “inside and outside of political institutions.” In the pages of the party’s newspaper, he was responding to a public debate that had erupted over Sinn Féin’s ideological direction, after accusations it had dialed down the socialist component of its self-professed ambition for a thirty-two-county socialist republic. In this context, we should read Kearney’s comments in 2024 not as an attempt to galvanize support for a left-wing government — something his party doesn’t advocate for these days — but as a repackaging of its decades-long reunification agenda. Sinn Féin’s long journey to the center began, after all, early in the peace process, not when it started recycling talking points on immigration from Ireland’s far right in the mid-2020s. A party that once made strident anti-capitalist arguments, proposed a radical decentralized federal reunified state , and called for the revolutionary overthrow of the island’s two partitionist statelets was, in the post-peace-process era, snookered by the White House and the British state into becoming a more familiar — and less dangerous — kind of constitutional nationalist party. Eager to be accepted by the Irish electorate and trusted by capitalists (foreign or domestic), it decommissioned Irish Republican Army weapons, accepted British institutions of law and order, and entered a power-sharing executive with the Democratic Unionist Party in the North. It also supported juryless trials that were used historically to persecute its own militants, condemned modern republican groups as “traitors,” and expunged democratic socialist ideas from its programs. In the early 2000s, the party pursued a fresh constitutional strategy aimed at reuniting Ireland, which became known as the all-Ireland agenda. National unity could be achieved, it felt, through an extension of the various cross-border bodies the Good Friday Agreement had created. Sinn Féin could also erode the border by turning electoral strength into a border poll and using these “republicanized” all-Ireland structures as the foundations for what it grew to call an “Ireland of equals,” a thoroughly liberal vision of a thirty-two-county state. Tactics included campaigning to secure the right for Northerners to vote in Southern presidential elections. But this strategy mainly involved creating the conditions for economic and political integration, in an approach that is best termed as technocratic anti-partitionism . Sinn Féin believed that effective cross-border bodies would pave the way for a thriving all-island economy, which would in turn be the prelude to a united Ireland. In the decades before Brexit, the party consistently claimed (without evidence) that the agreement’s implementation bodies were making the border redundant. Technocratic Reunification Later Sinn Féin made impressive electoral advances in the South when it deployed anti-austerity rhetoric after the 2008 financial crash. In the North, it progressed simply by continuing to exist as the nationalist face in a power-sharing system, patiently reaping the rewards of demographic change. Meanwhile, it proved its managerialist mettle by cutting education and health care funding, endorsing private finance initiatives, and incessantly calling for cuts in corporation tax. By the start of this decade, the party was dominating oppositional politics south of the border, and it became the most popular party in the 2020 general election with one-quarter of the vote. This seemed to mark the arrival of a long-standing objective of the Irish left: a realignment of party competition along a clear Left-Right axis. Sinn Féin’s support rose even higher during the pandemic, with an average polling score of around one-third of the vote from early 2021 until late 2023, at which point it started to lose ground. Its eventual result in 2024 was more than 5 percent lower than its 2020 vote share. One could explain this decline in part by reference to the new salience of immigration as a political issue. However, it was also related to a perception of the party as an already exhausted government-in-waiting, an ineffectual perma-opposition worn down by a prolonged stint on the sidelines, bruised by forces to its left and right. As PBP likes to point out, Sinn Féin’s unwillingness to develop a preelection left-wing compact potentially holds its vote back. But its single-minded focus on a confusingly technocratic vision of reunification further confuses workers in need of systemic change. Despite all the shrill rhetoric from conservative opponents about its supposed threat to the state’s economic model, Sinn Féin’s approach to Southern politics in particular has been characterized by equivocation and triangulation, particularly over abortion, climate change, immigration, and trans rights. In 2022, the Sunday Business Post revealed how the party of aging revolutionaries was beginning to ingratiate itself with various sectors of the Irish state, from corporate lobbyists to senior civil servants. It recounted a charm offensive to the American Chamber of Commerce by Mary Lou McDonald, who had also recently met with senior Silicon Valley executives. By 2024, the investment group Davy was advising its clients, following discussions with Sinn Féin politicians, that its economic program was “more ‘New Labour’ than ‘Corbyn Labour.’” The Contradictions of Left Unity On the radical left, there are broadly two schools of thought on left unity: People Before Profit’s call for a “left government,” and a more revolutionary posture from its Solidarity partners, based on steering clear of a reformist government to focus on building independent working-class power. According to PBP’s Paul Murphy, writing after Catherine Connolly’s victory last autumn, the party should only enter a government that “commits to a people-power strategy of mobilizing from below to overcome the opposition of the powerful capitalist class and deliver ecosocialist change.” Murphy acknowledges that a strategy of that kind is not likely to be on offer from the other parties that supported Connolly and is open to supporting a less radical government from the outside: We want a left government, even on a programme far weaker than the ecosocialist one we would advocate. We want this government and the approach of reforming capitalism to be tested before the masses. Being Pro-Working Class Isn’t Enough: We Need Victories People Can Feel

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Tag: Labor ／ 著者: 不明 ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-13T17:20:21+00:00

What a bizarro time we now live in. Coming off defiant elections in November 2025 and early 2026, Democrats of all stripes seem to be singing the same tune in 2026. Neoliberal scions Rahm Emanuel and James Carville , socialist NYC mayor Zohran Mamdani, DNC Chair Kenneth Martin , labor leader UAW President Shawn Fain, centrist VA governor Abigail Spanberger , and Bernie Sanders are seemingly aligned: we need to be the party of working people again. To win back the working class voters that the Democratic Party has abandoned, we need to focus relentlessly on affordability and cost of living for working people, orienting ourselves towards economic populism in the process. It’s a long-overdue course correction, both morally and tactically. While I know we’re still quite a ways away from everyone in the Democratic Party singing Solidarity Forever together, this is a real victory for us on the Left. After decades of elections that shouldn’t have been lost, there’s finally something nearing a consensus amongst Democrats. For this emerging orientation towards economic populism to represent a real break from the past and to result in sustained political power for the Left, it must advance an ambition that can sincerely match the scale of the crises we’re in. That means our movements have to prioritize demanding economic results, not economic processes. We need to focus on ends, not means. Yet the economic populism I think we’re settling into —and settling for— across the different factions of the Left is a means-focused economic populism, or what I refer to as “Process Populism.” Process Populism merely prioritizes demands that are processes of working-class politics. Three most emergent demands on the Left now found on countless policy platforms that best exemplify Process Populism are (1) supporting unionization, (2) taxing the rich, and (3) electing working-class candidates. What’s odd about us championing and emphasizing process is that process is one of the powerful’s oldest and easiest tools for defusing pressure. When workers demand change, bosses respond with task forces and listening sessions. When students organize, a university president proposes committees and surveys instead of fixing the actual problem. Delay via process is a part of the toolkit to nullify organizing, even when it’s well-intended. As organizers, that’s why we have to apply the same clarity to our politics that we demand from those in power: tools and processes are not material outcomes. Working people are not just out of time and money, they’re out of patience. They deserve to know how their lives will materially and specifically change. Process Populism does not offer that. Process Populism is no doubt an improvement over the neoliberal drift that defined the party for decades, but it stops halfway. One reason Process Populist policies are emergent is because they do poll well: only a small minority doesn’t want higher taxes on billionaires or doesn’t want stronger unions. And precisely because they poll pretty well, even left-leaning pundits and strategists often center them in our collective vision. Yet, there’s a significant difference between popular support and popular will . Popular support reflects what people agree with; popular will reflects what they’re ready to fight for. Process Populism produces the former, not the latter. It may win consensus, but it doesn’t build power nor demand urgency. And urgency is both a moral and strategic imperative. Process Populism isn’t likely to deliver material gains to the working class fast enough for those workers to want to keep its proponents in office. Globally and domestically, there is anti-incumbent bias for elected officials; the signal for us to draw is there’s a healthy chunk of voters who are willing to vote for candidates across a range of ideologies, but they are focused on results. The only way to break out of this “pendulum theory” of politics is to deliver a quickly better life for working families in concrete, economic ways. And if elected officials and movements can’t deliver within the first two to three years of their term? They’ll be voted out. Real economic populism—what I call “Material Populism”—starts where this version ends. Material Populism also engages with a politics of specificity : it names the specific outcomes we’re fighting for: shorter workdays, rent freezes, higher wages, better care, etc. The affirmative economic specificity encouraged by Material Populism can build popular will. Mamdani’s campaign offered one such model of that kind of affirmative materialism. His message focused on what working people could picture immediately: universal childcare, fast and free buses, and rent freezes. Though his campaign had concrete, thoughtful answers for how they were going to achieve those priorities, the process was not the campaign’s calling card. A campaign that emphasized process over results—a campaign that led with raising taxes on the rich or a campaign that focused on appointing the right people to the Rent Guidelines Board—would not have unleashed the same kind of inspiration and sense of possibility. These examples aren’t limited to socialist electoral campaigns: the most notable policy idea emerging out of the New Jersey Governor’s race was Governor-elect Sherill’s commitment to freezing energy rates for at least a year. You see a similar dynamic in Shawn Fain’s “core four” at the UAW : wages, healthcare, retirement, and control over time. Fain knows that workers don’t rally in the political nor bargaining arena around procedures, but that they’ll rally hard around material outcomes they can feel. The beauty of Material Populism is that it doesn’t require uniformity of policy. In one state, the fight might be for free public transit; in another, for debt relief, universal childcare, or a shorter workweek. The specifics can differ from race to race, movement to movement. What matters is that each is grounded in the same mission: to make life materially better for working people in quick, visible, and non-marginal ways. When movements and politicians focus on material ends during campaigning, they also bring clarity and accountability to the task of governing. It keeps us from treating process as a solution and ensures that once in power, politicians are guided by the result we were promised and that our movements pushed for, not the mechanism they used to get there. Being able to distinguish between Process Populism and Material Populism must become a core part of how we analyze and prioritize demands on the Left and in our electoral, labor, and issue-based movements. To more deeply understand the limitations of the emerging consensus, I look at the three key pillars of today’s process populism and why each, even when taken together, remains insufficient: pro-unionism (how workers conceivably gain power), taxing the rich (how elites potentially lose power), and working-class candidates (who we’d want to wield power). All are necessary for shifting power. Examining each, however, also shows how a process-obsessed politics doesn’t unleash nor match the urgency for the material things working people deserve. Of the populist pillars, being “pro-union” has become the easiest and safest, helping stitch an economic coalition from the Left to the center-left. For a clear example, look at the memo from DNC Chair Ken Martin . It, perhaps surprisingly, nailed the diagnosis: Democrats lost in 2024 because the working class abandoned them after years of feeling abandoned themselves. But when it came to solutions, the memo fell back on the same familiar playbook: defend workers’ rights, protect existing benefits, and support unions. Supporting unions and unionization were the extent of the offensive ambition; that’s a defensive posture, not a real vision On the Left, we often treat unionizing as an end in and of itself. The theory goes that unionizing is an important outcome because unions let people experience their workplaces more democratically. But that’s a politics that’s focused more on form than substance. Though unions remain the most powerful vehicle workers have to win dignity and democracy at their workplace, the reality is nothing materially changes simply from being part of a union. I love unions—I work as a union contract negotiator! But unionism that pretends a union automatically guarantees material gains—or that the moral and spiritual value of solidarity can substitute for them—is not an honest unionism. It risks disaffecting the very same workers it hoped to galvanize. The reality is that unionizing, having a strong union, and winning strong contracts take significant time, resources, and courage. And that’s due to both employer-friendly American labor law and the inherent challenges of collective action. Employers can fight tooth and nail to stop unionizing: captive-audience meetings, retaliation, delay tactics, firings, etc. Even when workers win a union election, the road to a first contract can be terribly long. The average length of a first contract negotiation is 465 days, but bosses can drag out contract negotiations for much longer too. The effort to unionize Starbucks has been one of labor’s most visible, high-profile fights in the last few years. While unionization has been broadly successful, actually winning a contract to secure improved working conditions has proved a tougher task. Starbucks workers unionized in 2021, but as of Summer 2026, there is still no collective bargaining agreement . That’s not because workers nor unions have failed. They’ve been sufficiently militant and organized to authorize a strike across 500+ stores , and the sustained pressure of unionization has certainly improved working conditions in numerous ways. But the safety and promise of a collective bargaining agreement which guarantees those material conditions has thus far remained out of reach. And even when workers win a contract, maintaining a strong union that can continue winning strong successor contracts remains a challenge. Changing that system by making it easier to unionize or bargain a first contract (as the Faster Labor Contracts Act recently passed in the House does) is good policy, but we saw through the shortcomings of the pro-worker Biden administration why it may not make for savvy politics. The main way the Biden administration signaled its pro-worker commitment was to be, relative to other presidencies, unabashedly pro-unionization. Biden deserves credit for NLRB reforms, for championing the PRO Act (a bill exclusively focused on accelerating unionization and building union power), and for standing with workers on picket lines. But friendlier processes and moral support didn’t automatically translate into faster or larger gains for workers. A working class that’s living paycheck to paycheck needs solutions faster than unionizing alone can deliver—and solutions more transformative than even many well-intentioned employers or arbitrators (if the Faster Labor Contracts Act were to pass) could ever grant. It’s no surprise that even as union drives surged during the Biden years (“ Hot Labor Summer ” now feels like a distant memory from a bygone era), (1) union density went down and (2) working-class people kept drifting away from the Democratic Party and from the labor movement—perhaps justifiably so given the lack of immediate change in their lives. Even if union density went up under the Biden-style labor reforms and every active organizing campaign succeeded, it could take decades for density to rebound to the post-New Deal era level where unions held economy-wide power. That’s why a pro-union stance, while essential, cannot be the centerpiece of an economically populist agenda. It’s too slow to meet the scale of the crisis, and voters intuit that. It’s why labor—despite support from every notable union in the country—was ultimately unable to build popular will around the PRO Act, their signature legislation during the Biden administration. For all these reasons, we should also be deeply dissatisfied and, frankly, upset with politicians who limit their working-class advocacy to a simple pro-unionism. 70% of Americans support unions. Politicians being simply “pro-union” is lazy politics because it outsources what should be a politician’s political fights in legislatures—wages, health care, retirement, and more time off the clock—to workplace fights led by overworked workers. A politics for working people has to aim, at minimum, to put increased pressure on legislators to deliver specific and material gains for workers via political avenues. Given how long unionization and collective bargaining can take and how hungry the public is for real change in their paychecks, it may even be easier to win bold, universal improvements in the political arena than at the bargaining table. We need leaders willing to fight for those things themselves, not just to say they stand with those who do fight. If pro-unionism is one side of the Process Populism coin, “tax the rich” is the other. While unions build power for working people, taxing the rich reduces elites’ power. It polls well, and it names a villain. It’s a welcome development for more than a small minority of politicians and unions to name who they’re fighting (the billionaires and the oligarchs), but they have to go one step further and name what they’re fighting for . The wealthy should be taxed more—substantially more than they currently pay. (And after news of Elon becoming the first trillionaire , we should also really consider strict legal limits on CEO wealth accumulation via both salaries and stocks.) But “tax the rich” shouldn’t be the centerpiece of an agenda even if it should be the engine for one. A billionaire’s higher tax bill alone doesn’t make childcare cheaper, shorten anyone’s workweek, or lower rent. There must be a clear line between new revenue and new results. In fact, taxing the rich without clearly naming what that money will fund risks unintentionally mimicking the perverse logic of trickle-down economics. The right promises that tax cuts for the wealthy would eventually benefit everyone else. The Left shouldn’t make the mirror-image mistake of assuming that taxing the wealthy will automatically improve ordinary people’s lives. Just as trickle-down economics failed because it never delivered material gains for the working class, a tax-the-rich agenda without clear outcomes risks the same disappointment. Failing to fill that missing connection could present a political problem. Paradoxically, taxing the rich is popular at the same time that cutting government waste and fraud is popular. Inoculating the public against the claims that taxing the rich will fuel more government waste thus requires coupling taxes with an affirmative economic vision. Naming what the tax revenue will fund will make the tax policy more popular. This is because people are more likely to support taxes when they can see what that revenue is buying: free childcare, cheaper housing, debt relief, or time back in their lives. Clarity about ends can build consensus around the means. Some campaigns and movements are already getting this right. The New York DSA’s “Tax the Rich” campaign explicitly connects their tax campaign to clear, material outcomes: universal childcare, emergency rental assistance, and mass public transit. It showed what a Material Populism’s embrace of tax the rich looks like: specific, visual, and immediate. Contrast that with the labor movement’s more generic “ Workers Over Billionaires ” campaign, which channels righteous anger at the rich and at corporations, but stops short of offering an affirmative economic vision. Both campaigns rightfully pit workers against elites, but only one conveys to working people in clear terms how their life will actually change if they join the fight. If taxing the rich names a villain, electing working-class candidates names a hero. It’s the final notable way today’s Process Populism confuses means for ends. It is the most narratively satisfying and culturally memetic form of contemporary populism: representation constituting substantive change. Absent explicit economic commitments however, I’ve found that this emphasis risks collapsing class politics into a type of identity politics. In that framework, class becomes a marker of authenticity rather than a program of redistribution. A candidate’s biography substitutes for their platform; proximity to hardship stands in for a plan to end it. The moral authority derived from working-class identity is treated as self-justifying. This is the aestheticization of class—a politics of identity without a clear politics of delivery. We’re already seeing this pattern emerge over the last few years. Figures like Sen. John Fetterman and Rep. Marie Gluesenkamp Pérez represent a growing cohort of Democrats who lean into the aesthetics of the working-class—small-business ownership, rural rootedness, cultural moderation—while offering little in the way of transformative economic policy. Even a candidate like Graham Platner—who, unlike Fetterman or Gluesenkamp Pérez, actually holds genuinely transformative economic positions—has become known to almost all voters primarily through his biography (both the compelling and controversial parts) rather than through the substance of what he’d do on the economy if he’s in office. The allure of the “working class candidate” has meant that even among candidates who aren’t working class, a working-class aesthetic has emerged: the Carhartt jacket , the pickup truck , the f-bomb. Putting aside the discussion that working class voters are likely to see through these transparent attempts to mimic their culture from candidates who aren’t working class, it’s clear how focusing on class aesthetics can easily shift from meaningful solidarity signalling to an exercise in brand cultivation. As the party (hopefully) becomes increasingly economically populist, this phenomenon of working class politicians with vacuous politics could proliferate. Without affirmative commitments to major economic outcomes, however, this genre of populism risks substituting affect for agenda. Counterintuitively, the two most notable figures at winning working-class support in the 2025 and 2024 election cycles lack any working-class identity at all and don’t hide that. Zohran Mamdani and Donald Trump could not be more different ideologically and both hail from wealthy enclaves in New York, but both speak in the language of ends. They promise visible, near-term, specific outcomes that people can imagine in their own lives. They illustrate that building inter-class coalitions and solidarity is not solely determined by background but by what a candidate persuades people they will do with power. Representation matters. It must, however, be instrumental, not expressive. The presence of working-class candidates in public office is meaningful only insofar as it expands the horizon of economic possibility for working class people. Or else class identity, untethered from affirmative material commitments, risks becoming another form of identity politics. If we’ve learned anything from the last two decades’ misplaced overemphasis on representational politics, it’s that a genuinely working-class politic must demand much more than who holds power. The Democratic Party’s turn toward economic populism is welcome, and it didn’t happen on its own. Nor did it happen easily. Organizers, unions, and activists fought for decades to make pro-worker politics common sense again, and significant electoral losses in 2016 and 2024 expedited the process of Democratic self-reflection. But if we want to win elections and we want to win them for more than a term or two, we must promise outcomes to working people—and then actually deliver them within one term. That’s how the majorities of the New Deal era that persisted for decades were built: through results working people could feel. Voters are impatient and rightfully so. They want leaders with real answers, not just the right mechanisms. Pro-unionism, taxing the rich, and working-class candidates are all necessary components to a working class politic, but they should not be the ultimate agenda. Without a clear economic vision for specific, fast, tangible gains—higher wages, shorter hours, cheaper care, more time—leftist populism risks failing as a governing project. This conversation couldn’t come at a more critical moment. The 2026 midterms, the organizing push toward a 2028 general strike, and the 2028 general election will test whether the Left can build powerful campaigns that can deliver on their promise. Those campaigns’ successes won’t be measured by pro-worker rhetoric or process, but by results that make ordinary life tangibly easier, freer, and more equal. That’s what it means to build power and govern with the working class, and that’s the only kind of politics worth building. Co-published with The Forge .

Housing Inequality Is a Central Plank of Modern Capitalism

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Adam Peggs ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-13T17:14:50+00:00

In recent years, renters’ unions have played an increasingly prominent role in a number of countries , with tenants’ groups exerting influence on national discussion and to some extent on the policies of governments and political parties. Spain, Portugal, Ireland, and Scotland have all implemented some form of rent control since 2021. Against that backdrop, several writers have sought to combine the questions of political strategy raised by tenant struggles with the political economy of housing. Michael Byrne’s book , Beyond Generation Rent , is part of this trend, examining inequality, financialization, and evictions. It’s a welcome and worthwhile contribution that brings a social critique of landlordism into contemporary academic economics. It also fleshes out a much-needed theory of how and why the private rental sector is inherently organized around the pursuit of economic rents, profiting from control of a scarce asset, and it brings the economic debate into the same discussion as the work of renters’ unions and their prospects. Byrne pulls no punches as he discusses the “impunity” of landlords in their relationship with tenants and the chronic insecurity this produces for the latter. He correctly identifies landlordism as a social relationship that depends on a power asymmetry between the asset owner and the resident. This ground has been well covered in recent years, particularly in Nick Bano’s Against Landlords , but by bringing it closer to the political economy debate, Byrne helps us grasp how the private rental sector operates and its role within the wider economic system. A Theory of Rent Since the classical economists of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, there has been a general understanding of landlordism as a form of economic rent. This is the sort of activity that most economic thinkers oppose, although many contemporary mainstream economists would draw a sharp distinction between the investment of landlords in property development or improvement and the passive income that arises merely from owning land or a home. In spite of this broad consensus, there are few explicit frameworks that show how and why the rent paid to landlords constitutes an economic rent. Byrne offers his theory of “home rent” as the value appropriated by landlords, determined by the local area in which the home is located and its value as someone’s home (particular to the sitting tenant). This allows landlords to profit from ownership of housing, including the appropriation of the value the tenant generates by making the place feel like home. Byrne does not attempt to draw conclusions around what home rent represents for the wider political economy of the capitalist system. That would help us answer the question of how important rent is to the functioning of contemporary capitalism and in what ways. However, he does touch on the work of others, including Javier Moreno Zacarés, whose analysis suggests that real estate capital ends up undermining capital accumulation in general by pushing up rents and asset prices. In my view, rent is crucial to the wider economic system. I have argued elsewhere that “real estate” has formed an increasingly central component of neoliberalism, as a source of stable returns in a financialized system. Nonetheless, Byrne’s decision to leave this question open within his own analysis is clear and well explained. Generation or Class? The book’s title is partly a call for analysis that moves beyond the clichés that mar much writing on the crisis, and partly one of its own central claims. According to Byrne, the housing crisis is not so much a divide between affluent, home-owning boomers and asset-poor younger generations as it is one between renters and property owners (especially multiple property owners) of all ages. This is broadly right, although sometimes Byrne (and others) can overstate the limits of the “generation rent” perspective. In most of the Global North (the Global South is a more varied and complex story) postwar capitalism did lead to a massive “cohort effect,” when people born at a certain time had much better prospects of homeownership than subsequent generations. This doesn’t mean there are no working-class baby boomers — there are many, of course — or that no one in the millennial or Zoomer generations are middle class. But in terms of housing prospects at least, there are profound differences between these generations in most cases. In political discourse across much of Europe and the United States, this has led to far too much emphasis on age group as a dividing line, rather than class and tenure. The most useful position is likely to be one that holds a few truths simultaneously. While questions of class and tenure are critical, postwar welfare state capitalism did produce vastly different housing economies than the neoliberal form that succeeded it. Even so, an excessive focus on age as a determining factor is a barrier to class solidarity. Class vs. Tenure Of course, class isn’t the same as tenure status. People’s housing situation may be critical to their standard of living, not to mention their relationship to place. But it says nothing directly about their relationship to work. Although homeownership does offer a degree of meaningful security, owning a home only amounts to owning capital when it is put to use in producing rent or sold for capital gains (of course, it still represents wealth regardless of what you do with it). Homeownership is crucial in modern capitalism. On the one hand, the scale of residential accumulation makes it ever harder to attain, as housing is concentrated in the hands of ever fewer actors. On the other, it still provides just enough people with a material stake in capitalism to help maintain consent for the current system. Byrne refers to the term “late homeownership societies,” which describes a transition from the mass homeownership of the last century to a much more precarious settlement in which more and more groups are locked out. As Byrne observes, the internationalization of landlordism makes the sector increasingly sensitive to faraway decisions. When the European Central Bank or the Federal Reserve hike interest rates, residents all over the world are likely to be affected. Mobilizing Tenants Byrne rightly notes that we need further research on the build-to-rent sector: purpose-built housing from corporate landlords. In some countries, including the UK, this sector appears to be at a crossroads, facing possible limits on its spectacular growth to date in a period of stagnating house-building. Some key insights have already emerged from existing research, including from Common Wealth . In the UK at least, build-to-rent homes skew toward comparatively affluent tenants, with a bias toward either higher-end housing or simply very high rents. Either way, this suggests that the sector’s continued growth as a share of the market spells bad news for accessibility and affordability. There are flaws and blind spots in some parts of the book. An otherwise valuable analysis of homeownership and housing inequality, particularly in the Western anglophone countries, does not take sufficient account of the role of social housing, which sits outside the system of housing wealth. But social housing is a critical factor here. In a system of deep housing wealth inequality, with some owning vast sums of housing wealth while others own nothing, the prospects and security of social housing tenants differ greatly from those of private renters who are locked out of ownership. However, the final sections of Beyond Generation Rent offer a lot of worthwhile analysis. Byrne summarizes the achievements of renters’ unions in Scotland, England, and Ireland, as well as European cities like Madrid, Barcelona, and Berlin. In Scotland, for example, no-fault evictions were effectively banned almost a decade ago, rents were frozen after the pandemic, and long-term rent controls have since been introduced. In England, the campaigning efforts of unions have eventually secured similar gains on no-fault evictions alongside a range of other improvements to rights and a ban on “bidding wars,” though rent hikes remain uncontrolled (leaving the UK an outlier compared to its neighbors). Most interesting is the degree of success arising from Berlin’s “Expropriate Deutsche Wohnen & Co.” (DWE) campaign. This led to a successful referendum vote for socializing the homes owned by corporate landlords, many of which were former public homes. Byrne describes this as proposing “one of, if not the most, radical forms of housing redistribution ever undertaken in a capitalist society,” potentially outstripping postwar housing requisitions in war-torn Europe or the state-backed Dutch squatting policy in the 1970s. However, the referendum vote has not yet been implemented, and a second binding referendum is now expected to be held in order to force the issue. In the meantime, a city government commission has justifiably noted that compensation to the landlords can be given at below market rates. The proposed structure for DWE would combine centralization with democratic, bottom-up elements, providing benefits of scale along with accountability and distribution of power. With nonresidents able to join and participate, the campaign’s configuration also has the potential to sweep more supporters into the movement and fuel more ambitious demands. If tenant organizing can radically expand the horizon, this begins to offer an answer to the question of whether real leverage can be built and in what ways. His program also points in at least roughly the right direction, toward greater security and affordability for tenants but, more importantly, also toward a significant expansion of nonmarket housing, restrictions on private landlordism, and direct redistribution. Cuba after six months of the oil blockade: crisis and the road to recovery

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Vijay Prashad ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-14T03:59:34+00:00

For more than six decades, the United States has attempted to make the Cuban people pay for their decision to pursue an independent, socialist path. The latest phase of this economic war has targeted the island’s most vulnerable artery: its energy supply. Six months after Washington intensified pressure on countries and companies supplying oil to Cuba, the policy has not produced democracy or improved the lives of ordinary Cubans. It has produced darker homes, immobilized buses, interrupted hospitals, and greater difficulty in moving food from farms to cities. The immediate task is to prevent a humanitarian disaster. The larger task is to replace coercion with sovereign cooperation and to help Cuba build an energy system that cannot again be strangled by a foreign power. Six months of deepening crisis On January 29, 2026, US President Donald Trump issued an executive order establishing a mechanism for additional tariffs on goods from any country that directly or indirectly supplied oil to Cuba. Washington presented the measure as a defense of US national security. Its practical purpose was unmistakable: to force third countries to choose between a small, economically distressed Caribbean nation and access to the enormous US market. The tariff actions authorized under this, and several other emergency orders were terminated on February 20. But the declared emergency remained in force, as did the wider sanctions system, threats against suppliers and uncertainty surrounding ships bound for Cuba. The result has been described as an effective oil blockade: not necessarily a permanent line of US warships, but a system of secondary pressure capable of frightening governments, banks, insurers, shipping companies, and traders away from legitimate commerce with Cuba. The blow fell on an economy already under extraordinary strain. Cuba had long depended on imported petroleum, particularly from Venezuela, while producing only part of its requirements. Venezuelan deliveries declined after the US attack on that country on January 3, 2026, Mexican supplies came under US pressure, and Cuba lacked the foreign exchange to buy sufficient fuel elsewhere. Aging thermal plants, delayed maintenance, hurricanes, inflation and the loss of tourism income compounded the damage. Fuel scarcity spread through every part of social life. Technical failures in the fragile grid repeatedly became island-wide blackouts. Electricity cuts stopped water pumps, while refrigerators failed, spoiling scarce food and medicines. Families were forced to cook, study, and sleep in extreme heat without dependable power. Transport was similarly paralyzed. Fewer public buses operated, petrol stations closed or rationed supplies, and workers struggled to reach their jobs. When diesel is unavailable, crops cannot easily be harvested, and food cannot be transported from rural producers to urban consumers. Garbage collection and sanitation deteriorate. Ambulances, hospitals, and clinics must ration generator fuel even as shortages of medicines and basic supplies grow. Flights were canceled or rerouted because Cuban airports could not guarantee aviation fuel, further damaging tourism – one of the country’s principal sources of hard currency. The burden is unequal. Households with dollars can sometimes obtain food, transport, batteries or solar equipment; workers and pensioners dependent on pesos have far fewer options. Women carry much of the additional unpaid labor of finding food and water and caring for relatives. Rural communities, persons with disabilities and patients requiring refrigerated medicines face particular risks. The central victims of the US policy are all the Cuban people. An exit based on peace, sovereignty, and reconstruction The way out must begin with an immediate humanitarian energy arrangement. Cuba requires predictable deliveries, not occasional cargoes dependent on political permission. The United Nations, together with willing Latin American and Caribbean governments, should establish a monitored fuel corridor. Shipments could be earmarked for power generation, hospitals, water and sanitation, food production, public transport, and disaster preparedness. An exemption is meaningless if banks do not process payments, insurers do not cover vessels, and shipping companies fear punishment. Participating governments should provide payment guarantees, insurance and legal protection for humanitarian suppliers. The United States should issue durable, general licenses rather than case-by-case waivers. Second, emergency relief must open the door to negotiated de-escalation. Washington and Havana should begin structured talks, supported where useful by CARICOM, CELAC, Mexico, and other trusted intermediaries. The principle must be reciprocity without coercion. The United States should suspend measures that punish third-country trade and set out a verifiable timetable for lifting sanctions. Negotiation cannot mean demanding regime change as the price of allowing a population to meet its elementary needs. Nor should humanitarian necessities be held hostage to every unresolved dispute between the two governments. Third, Cuba has already begun to diversity its suppliers and energy system. Contracts with several producers, joint purchasing with Caribbean partners and regional storage reserves could reduce costs and cushion sudden interruptions. Technical cooperation could help stabilize old generating equipment while new capacity is built. Over the medium term, the most durable answer lies in renewable and decentralised energy. Cuba has abundant sunlight and significant potential for solar power, biomass and carefully located wind generation. Rooftop solar systems on clinics, schools, farms and apartment buildings, coupled with batteries, would reduce demand on the national grid. Community micro-grids could keep water pumps, refrigeration and communications operating when a major plant fails. Bagasse from the sugar industry and agricultural residues can contribute to generation if projects are managed sustainably. Renewables must not become a slogan that ignores material constraints: projects need finance, spare parts, trained technicians, batteries, and recycling systems. For countries of the Global South, Cuba’s predicament is not a distant bilateral quarrel. Secondary sanctions and tariff threats establish the dangerous principle that a powerful state may dictate the lawful trading partners of every other nation. Defending Cuba’s right to purchase energy is therefore part of defending a democratic international order. The Global South should oppose extraterritorial coercion, support humanitarian supplies and renewable-energy cooperation, and work with other states to insulate legitimate trade from unilateral sanctions. The Cuban people have survived six decades of pressure through social organization, international solidarity, and remarkable endurance. Endurance, however, should not be romanticized. No people should be required to live indefinitely without reliable electricity, transport, food or medicine to prove their commitment to sovereignty. The just exit is neither capitulation nor isolation. It is the lifting of collective punishment and international cooperation based on equality. That path can bring the lights back on while preserving Cuba’s right to determine its own future. Vijay Prashad is an Indian historian and journalist.
