Zohran Mamdani’s Public Grocery Plan Is a Serious One

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Errol Schweizer ／ 公開日時: 2026-07-30T19:55:47+00:00

Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s pledge to open five publicly subsidized grocery stores in New York City is quickly becoming a reality. On July 27, 2026, Mamdani announced details of the plan, including a 30 percent discount to market prices of a “core basket” of healthy groceries such as fruits, vegetables, eggs, and milk; a request for proposals process to select private sector operators; and an operating subsidy that the city will use to fund the lower prices.According to estimates by the New York City Economic Development Corporation (NYC EDC), for which I am currently consulting, shoppers could save over $1,000 a year on groceries. Two locations have already been announced, including sites in the Bronx and East Harlem. City council has approved $70 million in capital funds for store build-outs, while operational funds are still being decided. The city’s annual budget is over $125 billion; the funds needed to open and run NYC Groceries are less than one-tenth of 1 percent of that budget, a literal rounding error.According to a 2025 poll, support for the public grocery idea in the city is overwhelming. A coalition including trade unions, community groups, cooperative businesses, and food security advocates has been working closely with the city to plan and implement the idea. The result is an idiosyncratic model that may point the way forward for other cities.Mamdani’s initiative is a compelling yet imperfect intervention in the food supply. Grocery prices have increased over 35 percent since 2020, with many categories such as beef, coffee, eggs, snacks, and beverages experiencing even higher inflation. Consumer prices continue to climb even though producer prices have stabilized or, in some cases like cocoa, decreased. Along with other Donald Trump–era policies and trends — like the Iran war, which has driven up food and gas prices; immigration crackdowns, which have left food rotting in the fields and increased fresh produce prices; a gutting of the regulatory sector leading to an outbreak of explosive diarrhea; draconian cutbacks to Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) benefits affecting over four million Americans; and an increase in GLP-1 usage — Americans are now buying a lot less food.According to a Bain/Nielsen IQ study, grocery unit volumes were down 1.8 percent in June, a 2 percent deterioration in a single year, adding up to billions in fewer grocery transactions. In New York alone, over 1.5 million residents face food insecurity, while low-income consumers spend upward of 25 percent of their income on groceries.The inspiration for the NYC Groceries idea comes from an unlikely place: The military commissary system, which has been around for over 150 years and fully subsidizes all groceries through public funds for service members and their families at a 23.7 percent discount. The commissary is loved by working-class shoppers and is a well-established channel of retail trade in the grocery industry, serviced by farmers, packaged food brands, and corporate wholesalers.Unlike the commissary system, the stores will be operated by a private sector expert. The stores will not be operated by the city. This could mean a local supermarket chain, a grocery cooperative, a nonprofit operator, a retail services cooperative, or a wholesaler with a retail arm. New York City, unlike most grocery markets, is not monopolized by Walmart or Kroger and has many diverse private sector operators that could run these stores. The public sector grocery stores will also be run as a public service, not as a for-profit, and the typical retail profit margins (1–3 percent) will be folded into consumer savings. They will be open to all New Yorkers, with no means testing for entry.Mamdani’s key proposal, a core basket of pantry items that will be discounted at 30 percent of market prices, also diverges from the commissary model, which discounts all prices to an average of 23.7 percent to the market. This 30 percent discount will be subsidized by the city to cover the gross margin (operating costs such as labor, utilities, general and administrative expenses, etc.), while rent and taxes will be covered by the city. Operators will have the option of selling other, noncore products at market-rate prices to cover their full overhead.The subsidies will be calculated through item-level discounts multiplied by unit volumes sold, the same way promotional discounts are calculated for grocers by their trade partners. This is a straightforward calculus for experienced operators. The subsidies will be approved through the city budget, just like funds for public transit, libraries, sanitation, and water. The stores will also not be food banks or pantries, selling donations or leftover “food waste” downstream from producers. They will offer the freshest, highest-quality products, showing that good food should be a public service.The store employees will get living wages, comprehensive benefits, and have significant labor protections. Operators will be required to ensure card check recognition, an easier and fairer way for employees to unionize should they so choose; to respect employer/management neutrality in union drives, pledging not to fight union efforts; and to not bring on anti-union consultants. The administration’s goal is to make these stores a great place to work and build a career, which is very rare in the grocery industry, which typically sees store labor as a resource to be strip-mined, streamlined, and exploited.The stores will be thoughtfully designed, laid out, and merchandised so that cash-strapped New Yorkers have a premium shopping experience. The stores will sell a mix of branded items, private labels, and locally grown products geared to the specific communities and markets they serve. Products will be familiar and mainstream, and driven by consumer demand and market trends as opposed to particular ideologies of what the working class should be eating.Mamdani’s public groceries plan could be a model and inspiration for other cities to run with. But it is not without flaws. The military commissary is publicly owned, funded, and operated; ironically, this means the Department of Defense’s public sector grocery purchasing practices are closer to communism than a socialist mayor’s. Outsourcing operations separates the risk from the responsibility, and the city will need to work closely with operators to keep the program running well and ensure operators don’t bail if the going gets tough.The upside is that selected operators will come with a ready-made supply chain, with wholesale, manufacturer, grower, and private-label contracts, operational expertise, administrative, accounting, and informational technology systems that the city will not have to invest in and build from scratch. NYC EDC has a lot of experience with the private sector, such as the NYC Ferry system, but grocery is a complicated business.The administration is facing a lawsuit from a coalition of small supermarket operators and bodega owners who oppose the idea out of fear that the city is using tax dollars to hurt their businesses. Many of these store owners already receive enormous subsidies to their operations through public supplemental food programs like SNAP and Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC). But if the city were smart, it could work with store owners near the NYC Grocery sites to increase the affordability goals. If local stores have seen their business impacted, let them receive subsidies on core basket items to lower those prices and be subjected to the same rigorous accounting, reporting, and price controls as the NYC Grocery stores.The city should also create a retail and wholesale services arm through the NYC EDC. This would ensure long term consistency in the program while building in marketing, administration, and supply chain efficiencies, such as cooperative warehousing, wholesaling and private label sourcing, including utilization of the Hunts Point Cooperative Market, regardless of who operates the stores. Such retail service organizations are very common in the grocery industry as a means for independents to stay competitive with larger chains.Mamdani’s idea to only discount a “core basket” of products is also odd, since it leaves the door open for price gouging on noncore products. Operators will want to make higher margins on noncore items and create a two-tiered pricing model that has the potential to stigmatize value-conscious shoppers. The fastest-growing grocers in the United States are deep discounters such as Aldi, Lidl, and Walmart, and are known unequivocally for low prices across the store.The administration has also passed on implementing a “Values-Based Purchasing” framework for the stores and is instead just incentivizing the operators to purchase from local suppliers. Values-Based Purchasing is modeled on the sourcing standards of more upscale chains such as Whole Foods and Erewhon, and encourages subsidized public sector supply chains to prioritize fair labor, animal welfare, environmental sustainability, economic justice, and healthy foods. Values-Based Purchasing is already required in public purchasing by New York City, and via the Good Food NY Bill passed by both New York State legislative bodies, is currently awaiting a signature by Gov. Kathy Hochul to make it state law. So this omission is bizarre and shortsighted, and will mean the city may likely end up selling products made in conditions and with ingredients that aren’t in line with the administration’s professed values.And the biggest risk is how the administration tacks progress to its goal of affordability. A simple calculation of how many discount subsidies are distributed is the easy part, but how the city estimates and communicates how five stores improve the lives of cash-strapped New Yorkers and point the way to a better and more just food system is a much broader and more compelling calculus.Despite its shortcomings, Mamdani’s public grocery sector plan is among the most compelling innovations in a food supply that is increasingly consolidated, expensive, corrupt, and lately, downright dangerous. And it aims to do one thing that much of the grocery sector, especially Mamdani’s critics, have lost the script for really well: to make good food affordable to all.And that should be the whole point.

US Mail Carriers Are Poised to Elect New Union Leadership

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Alexandra Bradbury ／ 公開日時: 2026-07-30T14:59:20+00:00

A leadership change looks likely in the National Association of Letter Carriers (NALC) this fall. Members are mad about low pay and forced overtime, dangerous heat and robberies, the rejected contract imposed last year, the backlog of tens of thousands of grievances, and the president’s secretive approach to bargaining. There’s not just one group pushing for change but a whole ecosystem of reform groups, slates, and podcasters among the five-thousand-plus delegates coming to Los Angeles for the union convention August 3–7. They’ll rub elbows and hopefully build unity at a “ Reformers Rum Party ” on Sunday night, the eve of the convention. Days before the August 5 nominations, it looks like a three-way race for president. The 200,000 members will get the chance to vote this fall — by mail ballot, of course — for ten officers, three trustees, and fifteen regional business agents. Two reform tickets have been campaigning for two years. One is the Concerned Letter Carriers (CLC) slate, headlined by current Vice President James Henry and including a number of branch (local) presidents. The other is Mike Caref , business agent for Illinois, running solo for president. Despite months of speculation about whether President Brian Renfroe would run again, he still hasn’t made any announcment. But a third slate announced July 17 includes many other incumbents, led by Eddie Davidson, current business agent for Florida, Georgia, and the Carolinas. Doomsday Clock The backdrop is ongoing existential threats to the US Postal Service (USPS). Since Trump returned to office, the postal doomsday clock has been minutes from midnight . Though Trump hasn’t acted on his threat to move the Postal Service under the Department of Commerce, his new batch of nominees for the Board of Governors aren’t even from private sector logistics but from finance — so maybe less interested in rationalizing the service than in selling it for parts. Postmaster General David Steiner, who somehow makes his predecessor Louis DeJoy look good, is going along with Trump’s extreme attacks on vote by mail. He says USPS won’t deliver ballots in states that won’t hand over voter lists to the government. Steiner has also been claiming USPS could run out of money within the year, and threatening to close post offices and cut delivery days if its finances aren’t fixed. The next-biggest union, the American Postal Workers Union (APWU), organized rallies July 28 for three reforms that would help in the short term — raise the debt limit, modify the investment strategy for retiree benefit funds (they’re all in Treasury bonds), and change how the retirement obligation is calculated. Longer term, USPS needs to adapt to fewer letters and more packages. Management’s approach has been to cut costs by degrading service. The unions argue it should instead expand revenue by adding services like banking, internet access, hunting licenses, electric vehicle charging, and checking in on seniors. In addition, retired letter carrier and longtime activist Jamie Partridge argues, the government should subsidize rural delivery, as many other countries do. Overlapping Groups A vote-no movement on the 2024 contract was organized by a coalition of forces including Caref, the CLC, the Build a Fighting NALC (BFN) caucus, and influential podcaster Corey Walton, number two on the CLC ticket. Podcasts are big with letter carriers — you can listen while you slog through the workday. One called Next Generation Carriers has interviewed activists from all the vying groups and even Renfroe himself (Labor Notes staffers have also been guests). Cohost Margo O’Neill, a steward of Branch 352 in Des Moines, is also the treasurer for BFN. Her main goals at the convention, she said, are to grow the reach of the reform movement and to build unity across the various existing groups. The Next Gen hosts have been agitating for a contract demand of “30 for 30”: a starting wage of $30 and no more than one manager per thirty carriers, to save money and alleviate micromanagement. New carriers start at about $21, in an entry-level job called “city carrier assistant” where they’re often made to work Sundays delivering Amazon packages. It can take two years to convert to a career position, and even then, everyone hired since 2013 is on second-tier Table 2 pay. In the Massachusetts chapter of BFN, “we’ve been really pushing to get people more active, talking to CCAs about their rights on the job,” said Chris Persampieri, legislative and political director of Branch 18: A lot of people had no idea that if it’s your day off, you’re not on call. You don’t have to pick up the phone. There’s no union steward on Amazon Sundays, so they’re on their own. We show up with flyers about their rights and a box of joe from Dunkin’ Donuts, because it’s Massachusetts, and that’s what we do here. Some reform activists are backing the CLC; some are backing Caref; some like certain members of the Davidson slate. The election may yield a mixed executive council of people who ran on opposing tickets, and the new president will have to “mold that into a solid team,” said Branch 3 President David Grosskopf of Buffalo, who’s running for trustee with the CLC. Contract Debacle What’s difficult to find is anyone with a good word to say about Renfroe. Last convention, he was already in hot water, facing internal charges of dereliction of duty and impaired driving of a union vehicle. Delegates voted to let him off the hook. But they also voted for more member involvement and substantive bargaining updates; Renfroe had been bargaining for nearly two years in near-complete secrecy. Soon afterward, he came out with a tentative agreement featuring a measly 1.3 percent annual raise. Members voted it down. As the union returned to the table in early 2025, Renfroe announced nationwide “Fight Like Hell” rallies — then reversed course over fears of Trump and the Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE), and rushed into binding arbitration. The arbitrator imposed a contract nearly identical to the one members had voted down, sparking more outrage. And the bad deal had taken so long to negotiate that it was more than half over, expiring in May 2026, so NALC was soon back at the table. The union has made incremental improvements in its approach this time. There was a member survey, a national day of rallies, some video updates, and a collective bargaining conference for branch and state leaders. Still, as the sixty-day mediation period expired in July, meaning the union could be moving to arbitration, members had little information. “We have no idea what economic package our union is asking for,” Grosskopf said. “We don’t know if we’re asking for $30 an hour, or $50.” Renfroe has kept even fellow officers at arm’s length from bargaining. The last straw for Mack Julion, assistant secretary-treasurer, was when the president proposed a four-day workweek of ten-hour days, meaning carriers would no longer get overtime pay after eight hours. “Ours is a physically demanding job,” Julion said. “I’m kinda known for my fancy cowboy boots; I wear those because I have a bad case of plantar fasciitis. I’ve known many letter carriers who had hip replacements and knee surgeries. “I had suggested hazard pay,” he said. “If you think a four-by-ten schedule is a higher priority than hazard pay when we’re just a couple years removed from COVID and we have letter carriers literally dying in the heat . . . We are human, and we break down.” Rather than run again with Renfroe, Julion planned to run independently for reelection. He ended up joining the Davidson slate. Democracy in Action The convention has strongly democratic features. There are no major hurdles to getting nominated. Delegates vote on every constitutional amendment or resolution submitted by any branch or state association — this year there are many . Persampieri authored an amendment for ranked-choice voting (where voters can indicate their second- and third-choice candidates and the resulting votes are tabulated in “instant runoffs”) to remove the fear of voting for a spoiler. Branch 3 proposed that the national bargaining team include five officers, not just the president. Delegates may hear internal charges brought against the officers by a San Diego carrier, Michael Rivera, over the imposition of the rejected contract last year. The officers declined to investigate the charges against themselves, but Rivera is appealing to the convention body. They will also elect delegates to the 2030 AFL-CIO convention. A “Stand-Up Slate” of younger carriers is running on themes like universal health care and the abolition of billionaires. Some resolutions were submitted calling on the union to live stream the convention, which officers decided to do. Minneapolis Branch 9 has sent resolutions on political themes, like ending the war in Iran and funding public services. BFN activist Tyler Vasseur warned first-time delegates not to be too disappointed if they don’t pass. “The idea is to force a debate on the issue,” he said. “If we get 20 to 30 percent of the vote, we can identify people who want to fight and get organized on these issues going forward.” Wins Needed Getting members into motion is the name of the game. “If we’re ever going to get the participation up in the union, people have to feel there’s a fight going on, and we have to get some wins,” Caref said. “We’ve been taking a lot of losses, and it’s really demoralizing for the carriers. “You’ve got to start with a clear vision for where you want to be, and a strategy for how you’re gonna get there. You get some little wins, you get more people involved, and you start moving in the right direction. “That’s why I always tell people, I only want to be president if you guys want me to be president,” he said, “because I’m going to give you stuff to do right away.”

Moderation Is Not a Real Political Strategy

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Matt Bruenig ／ 公開日時: 2026-07-30T14:00:34+00:00

One of Matt Yglesias’s defining political positions is that Democrats should be more moderate in order to attract more votes. This is generally invoked as a critique of the Left and so naturally attracts left-wing ire, which Yglesias seems to enjoy. One issue I’ve long had with this moderation-or-not discourse is that it’s a moving target. If we define “moderate” as something like the median of the current distribution of political views (ignoring all the complications with that concept), then there must be people who organically and earnestly hold those specific views. Yet those people are only moderates for as long as their views happen to be at the median. If the underlying distribution of views changes, which it does, then people who were formerly moderates, so defined, no longer are. But the people who advocate moderation, like Matt Yglesias, are almost never moderate in this organic sense. Yglesias’s views — e.g., those in One Billion Americans — are definitely not at the current median. These people are instead strategic moderates who advocate that politicians and other political actors determine what the current median is and then adopt those views, including in their own political advocacy. This sounds sensible until you realize that the median is constantly changing, and that minoritarian views become majoritarian (and thus moderate) views only because people advocate for them in the political sphere broadly defined. If you are constantly sampling the point-in-time median to define your views, you will have very unstable views, and you will end up having taken positions that become wildly unpopular. Yglesias on Israel Israel provides a useful case study on how this sort of strategic moderation can go wrong. After the October 7 attacks, US support for the Israeli military became a hot-button issue that divided the Left from the center-left in the Democratic Party. In the early period of that dispute, public polling was generally pro-Israel. But this completely reversed over the next couple of years ( Gallup , Pew ). This whipsawing of public opinion resulted in strategic moderates like Yglesias recommending taking positions that quickly became toxic, creating a “defund the police”–type problem for all of the politicians and political actors who followed Yglesias’s advice. To see just how bad this advice really was, I used a coding agent to download every Matt Yglesias tweet from October 7, 2023, to present that mentioned “Israel,” “Palestine,” or “Gaza.” I was hoping I could plot some kind of change over time with that data, but Yglesias appears to have deleted all of his tweets from August 2024 through May 2026. This was presumably done through a tweet deletion service that said it would delete all of his tweets but actually only deleted his last 3,200. So I shifted to a second idea, which was to have Claude analyze all 492 Israel-related tweets Yglesias made between October 7 and August 2024 (i.e., before the mass deletion) and to identify the ones in which Yglesias was arguing, directly or implicitly, that it was smart politics to be pro-Israel. Narrowly, on the merits, everything about it is backward. All else equal, sending money to Israel is not good for the American poor, and, as a matter of political rhetoric, it is pretty easy to say we should be spending that money on services at home. In the years since, that particular rhetorical line seems to have become fairly common and effective. But Yglesias was not making a point about the policy merits. He was saying that Ritchie Torres was, whether sincerely or not, being a smart political moderate because he was doing good position-taking. Just two years later, three members of Congress in Torres-adjacent NYC districts were primaried by people who were, to a large extent, running against the pro-Israel positions of the incumbents. Torres did not face a similar sort of challenge this go-around. He may have won even if he did. But it’s hard to believe today that his past pro-Israel position-taking is a political asset that is helping his impoverished constituents. The same is true for other politicians, not just Torres, who shrewdly followed the polls in 2023 and now have a record on Israel that puts them at odds with the majority of Americans and the vast majority of Democrats. Earnest Politics Are Unavoidable The problems with this sort of strategic moderation are not just that you can get burned by a rapid change of public opinion. There is also something more conceptually troubling about it, which is that it is, in a Kantian sense, not universalizable. If everyone were to try to deduce what the median is and then adopt that view, then there would be no median. To have a median, you need people to earnestly engage in political opinion formation and political advocacy that generates a distribution of political views. Yet this sort of earnest political engagement, which is what makes strategic moderation possible at all, is regarded as naive and reckless by strategic moderates. These days, Yglesias has, in the spirit of strategic moderation, said that Democrats should actually bash Israel. That is, after all, what the current median requires. But if everyone took his advice in 2023, then the median would have never moved to the current anti-Israel position. To the extent that Yglesias tries to deal with these paradoxes, it’s by imagining that it’s possible to simultaneously be moving public opinion in your direction without that bleeding over at all into the electoral process. But these two things are obviously intertwined. There is no secret place where you can spread your ideas away from the ears of voters, and winning politicians’ support is part of the process of moving public opinion. Yglesias’s favored example on this point actually proves the opposite. On Twitter and elsewhere, Yglesias likes to point out that gay marriage advocates did not press Barack Obama to run as a gay marriage proponent because they knew it was not yet popular enough at the time, and Obama had given reassurances that he was actually lying about his views on gay marriage. Yet these same gay marriage advocates had, in the 1990s and 2000s, pursued state-level lawsuits aimed at legalizing gay marriage as part of a long-haul strategy of changing public opinion. Some of these suits succeeded, but they also generated public backlash in the form of state-level constitutional amendments banning same-sex marriage. It was in this back-and-forth that a durable public consensus in favor of gay marriage was forged. Free riding off of earnest politics by having others do it and then, as if floating above it all, putting your finger on the median and going “there” can be very psychologically satisfying. It makes you feel like you are one of the only serious people in politics. But what it really does is turn yourself into a non-factor, someone whose political views twist in the wind based on how persuasive other political actors — the ones who don’t engage in strategic moderation — end up being.

He is the world’s richest man and it is nearly impossible to watch an interview with him for not flinching. He is desperately emotionally needy for something the world’s richest man can never get – approval. No one is his equal and so he can not be truly respected and so he desperately seeks the approval of our demented old patriarch. Put him on a PIP for all our sakes. The Saudi-Emirati Rivalry Is Fueling a Series of Wars

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Jean Montagne ／ 公開日時: 2026-07-30T13:01:58+00:00

The feud between the rulers of Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates (UAE) has ramifications well beyond these two capital-rich, autocratic states in the Arabian Peninsula. It directly affects the long-suffering peoples of Sudan, South Sudan, Yemen, and Somalia who are among the poorest and most conflict-burdened populations of the world. Long simmering under the surface, the Saudi-Emirati rivalry blew up on December 30, 2025, when the Saudis bombed two Emirati ships in the port of Mukalla in Yemen. This was a response to the takeover of the country’s easternmost governorates by the UAE-supported Yemeni separatist Southern Transitional Council (STC) in previous weeks. The Saudi bombing prompted the evacuation of all Emirati personnel and equipment from Yemen and the rapid defeat of the STC throughout the country. Yemen remains the most visible site of the worsening Saudi-Emirati relationship, but there are many others both in foreign and domestic issues. History of Rivalry In the days before the United States and Iran agreed to a nemorandum of understanding that has now disintegrated, the UAE joined Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and other Gulf states in appealing to Donald Trump to end the war. But Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) monarchs are unlikely to forget that the UAE was the only regional state that had earlier encouraged Israel and the US to pursue attacks on Iran and even actively participated in them while others were struggling to end the conflict. As the GCC rulers had long predicted, their territories were the main targets of Iranian attacks. In coming years, joint action and cooperation between Saudi Arabia and the UAE will continue when circumstances demand it, but their rivalry is now a fundamental feature of regional politics and will worsen, regardless of a meaningless exchange of friendly messages between two officials on July 21. Decades of pretense that the GCC states are a cohesive group of like-minded autocracies are misleading. Going back to the nineteenth century, rival families ruling little more than villages were riven by intrafamily disputes, with their leaders killing each other to control the then-scarce resources of the region. This sequence was interrupted when Great Britain fossilized the relationships between them as imperial overlord. Saudi Arabia was, of course, the exception, but it is barely a century since the Al Saud finally took over Hejaz and established the country along its current borders. Had it not been for British domination of the coastal statelets, there is little doubt that, with the exception of Oman and Yemen, the entire peninsula would now be part of Saudi Arabia. Lip Service Relations between Saudi Arabia and the UAE have been tense more often than not. The long-lasting conflict over the Buraimi Oasis was only solved in 1974 when the kingdom recognized the UAE diplomatically. Particularly since the beginning of the twenty-first century, under the leadership of Mohammed bin Zayed (MBZ), the country has used its massive hydrocarbon export income to increase its power and challenge Saudi dominance of the GCC states and beyond. As early as 2009, the Emirati leadership considered Saudi Arabia a serious security threat, as revealed in a US Wikileaks cable from that year: While the UAE pays lip service to the idea of GCC unity, the reality is that Abu Dhabi is deeply skeptical of multilateral approaches particularly on military matter [sic]. And while publicly expressing close ties with Riyadh, the UAE privately regards the Kingdom as its second greatest security threat after Iran (Israel is not on the list). After King Salman’s advent to the Saudi throne in 2015, his son Mohammed bin Salman (MBS) soon ascended to very senior positions. To become crown prince, he needed support from Washington, which favored the then-incumbent, his cousin Mohammed bin Naif, who had been a close collaborator of US intelligence agencies for decades. The UAE’s influential ambassador Yousef al Otaiba made the change possible by persuading the US authorities to support MBS. For a couple of years, observers generally described the relationship between MBZ and MBS as one of mentor and mentee. The high point of their cooperation, which was also to be the root of their future divergence, was the blockade of Qatar between 2017 and 2021. Initiated by the Emiratis, it was at first supported by MBS. However, the Saudi-initiated reconciliation with Qatar was imposed on a reluctant UAE by 2021. By now, the relationship had already significantly deteriorated as a result of this and other issues. Yemen was the first space where their rivalry was visible as early as the summer of 2017, when UAE officials did not hesitate, in private at least, to insult the Saudi leadership. Both Saudi Arabia and the UAE aspire to middle-ranking world power status with international influence. To achieve its targets, Saudi Arabia must develop many sectors in which the UAE is decades ahead, including tourism, entertainment, sports, hospitality, and luxury infrastructure. Domestic Strategies The development strategies of the two states are similar if not identical, based on the export of hydrocarbons, a close alliance with the United States and other Global North states, and neoliberal economic policies. Both are designing their economies for a post-oil future on the basis of advanced technologies, tourism, and grandiose projects. Politically, they are both absolute monarchies unwilling to accept any form of challenge, or even any hint of alternative political views, repressing the mildest forms of dissent. In the long run, the size and financial clout of the Saudi regime are advantages it possesses. The population balance favors the kingdom, three times that of the UAE. Moreover, if we only include nationals of the two countries, disregarding migrant workers, the UAE has barely 10 percent of Saudi Arabia’s population. The UAE has a much higher per capita income for its nationals and is way ahead of the kingdom in its development model: for example, the Saudis only introduced tourism visas in 2019. The UAE’s strategic geographic position, about halfway between East Asia and Europe, is a major asset for trade and manufacturing. Disagreements and forms of competition are growing. Taking early advantage of the GCC Free Trade Agreement, the UAE established numerous productive and other enterprises in a series of “free zones,” mostly in Dubai. In July 2021, the Saudi regime ended free trade for outputs from free zones and imposed import tariffs on these products, targeting the UAE, which is most affected. The same year, it decreed that all major international companies operating in the kingdom must have their HQ there. This prompted more than 660 companies to move to Riyadh, although its living conditions are not yet quite as relaxed as those in Dubai. Various incentives have been provided to support this move. Although the UAE has a long head start, the Saudis are catching up, largely thanks to two factors: improved standards of entertainment and comfort in the kingdom, alongside the size and scope of potential profits for the companies concerned, which far outstrip anything the UAE can offer. This month, the Financial Times reported systematic delays in financial transfers between the two countries. Another difference is the UAE’s focus on military interventionism. Since 1990, its forces have been involved in US-led operations in Afghanistan, Somalia, Kosovo, Libya, and Iraq and received the moniker of “little Sparta” from senior US military staff. By contrast, the Saudi military are widely reputed to be little more than a “parade” army. This has been one reason that few Saudi ground troops have been active in the fighting in Yemen, though the UAE suffered significant losses there. International Relations Saudi and Emirati policies toward Russia and China are similar, ignoring the US-led sanctions and embargoes in favor of deepening economic relations. The Saudis pioneered a prisoner exchange between Ukraine and Russia in 2022. They also hosted an international Ukraine conference in August 2023 as well as meetings involving Ukraine, Russia, and the United States in March 2025. However, the UAE has mediated more prisoner exchanges, and the US-sponsored Ukraine-Russia negotiations in late 2025 took place in Abu Dhabi, suggesting that on this front, it is unclear who has Washington’s favor. In Yemen, the two states were pursuing incompatible policies as early as 2017, and the gap steadily widened until the open breach six months ago. The UAE supports Southern separatists, while the Saudis are in favor of retaining a unified state. A major point of divergence emerged in Sudan when war broke out in 2023 between the Sudanese Armed Forces under Abdel Fattah al-Burhan and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) led by Hemedti (officially Mohammed Hamdan Dagalo), which have been responsible for horrific massacres. Saudi Arabia supports the “official” armed forces, while the Emiratis continue actively arming and financing the RSF despite worldwide outrage at their atrocities in Darfur. In 2025, the UAE supported the formation of a RSF parallel government, while the Saudis established a coordinating council with the Burhan government. Following the eruption of the rivalry in Yemen in December 2025, disagreements on Sudan flared up publicly once again. Tensions are also noticeable around the Red Sea. Saudi Arabia has facilities in Djibouti, whose government expelled the UAE in 2017. The Emiratis then set up a new base in Berbera in the unrecognized state of Somaliland, with additional facilities in Eritrea. Following Israel’s recognition of Somaliland, the UAE is now building military facilities inland to facilitate Israeli and US activities in the area. Meanwhile, the Saudis are deepening their relations with Somalia. They have kept the UAE out of the Red Sea Council, a regional body created in 2020, although it must be said that this remains little more than a paper organization at time of writing. A Deep Split Differences over oil exports and production first became prominent in 2021, when the Saudis compromised with the Emirati position. However, the UAE went on to leave OPEC on May 1 this year, a move that will deeply affect finances in Saudi Arabia. When the long-term impact of constraints on the Strait of Hormuz are solved, oil prices will drop thanks to a glut and the increase in renewable alternatives. This context explains why rivalry, even explicit hostility, dominates the relationship between the Saudi Kingdom and the UAE, with their two leaders hardly on speaking terms. As early as late 2022, MBS was said to have told reporters that the UAE “stabbed us in the back,” for which he threatened to retaliate. Although it is highly unlikely that this situation will lead to lasting armed confrontation between the two states, there is little doubt that the rivalry will intensify and manifest in other situations. There is now a deep split between the UAE and Israel on the one hand and a larger number of states that include Saudi Arabia, other GCC members, and Egypt, alongside Turkey and Pakistan. This is likely to be a feature of regional politics for years to come. Yemen may remain the most visible litmus test of their relationship, though the Red Sea and surrounding states are also likely to become prominent. As the RSF prepares more atrocities in Sudan, now in Kordofan, the UAE shows no sign of reducing its support. Conflict continues in Yemen, with the Emirati leadership undermining efforts to solve the crisis. The main victims of this rivalry are the people of these countries.

India’s Cockroach Rebellion and what comes next

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Atul Chandra ／ 公開日時: 2026-07-30T23:28:32+00:00

They came to Jantar Mantar in cockroach masks, throwing back at power a word that power had used against them. On July 25, 2026, after weeks of protest, a twenty-six-day hunger strike by Sonam Wangchuk, baton charges, tear gas and the closure of 18 Delhi Metro stations, Union Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan submitted his resignation . By late afternoon on July 25, India’s students and young people had forced one of the cabinet’s most powerful figures from office. Who are the cockroaches? On May 15, 2026, during a Supreme Court session, the Chief Justice of India, Surya Kant, described the unemployed youth who ‘attack institutions’ as “youngsters like cockroaches”. The comment sparked anger amongst young people, and a creative response: the formation of the Cockroach Janta Party, a place for the young “cockroaches” to come together and organize their rejection of the system. Within twenty-four hours of its formation, the Cockroach Janta Party generated unprecedented momentum. Founded on May 16, 2026 as a digital clapback to the judge’s jibe, the Cockroach Janta Party drew over twenty million online followers within a fortnight. But, it did not just accumulate followers. It published manifestos on different aspects of Indian political life, such as how Chief Justices are appointed, what electoral integrity should be, demands for media that is free and independent from corporate houses like Adani and Ambani, and on the national examination system. The CJP ran a nationwide “Pradhan Go Back” campaign centered around an indefinite sit-in at Jantar Mantar in New Delhi. As part of the sit-in, several activists were on hunger strike, including Sonam Wangchuk who sustained his hunger strike for 26 days. The youth and student movement also organized a mass mobilization of tens of thousands to march to Parliament on July 20, 2026. In just two months, it forced a breakthrough that had eluded even the most resilient peasant and farmers movements, human rights advocates, independent journalists, and opposition groups over a decade of struggle. The weight of that should be neither understated nor misread. For twelve years the Modi government has treated concessions as weakness. It outlasted the largest farmers’ mobilization in the country’s history, and when it repealed the three farm laws in November 2021 it kept its agriculture minister in place to pilot the repeal himself. It absorbed the anti-CAA protests, the female wrestlers’ sit-in ,and the agony of Manipur without losing a minister. Pradhan, only weeks earlier, had called the students “a B-team of terrorists” out to “divide the country”, a charge sharpened by his own past: he entered politics protesting a paper leak in 1997. That a government so practiced in refusal has given up a cabinet post marks a genuine shift in the balance of forces. The long apprenticeship of Indian democracy The young have carried this burden before. India’s students have repeatedly tested whether the republic’s promises mean anything, and each time expanded them. When the colonial state partitioned Bengal in 1905, students tore up the circular threatening them, walked out of government colleges, and built a parallel “national education” that survives today. When Gandhi called for non-cooperation in 1920, they emptied the Raj’s classrooms and founded Jamia Millia Islamia. In 1942, with the Congress leadership in jail, it was students who hoisted flags over secretariats and ran Usha Mehta’s underground Congress Radio. After independence, the pattern deepened into something more than protest. In 1965, students across Tamil Nadu rose up against the imposition of Hindi. The state killed scores of them, then reversed its language policy and lost two Union ministers from the region. In the winter of 1973 to 1974, a hostel fee rise in Ahmedabad became the Nav Nirman Andolan, which forced out Gujarat’s chief minister Chimanbhai Patel and dissolved the assembly. That current ran to Bihar, where a student agitation drew in Jayaprakash Narayan, hardened into the movement Indira Gandhi answered with the Emergency, and returned in 1977 to remove her. A student union led the Assam agitation of 1979 to 1985 and compelled a national government to sign an accord ; Osmania University’s students were central to the birth of Telangana in 2014. None of this was won by decorum but by pressure, by forcing the state to answer to people it preferred to ignore. That is what deepening a democracy means. The visible concession and the structural test Here the sober reading begins. Across all these cases runs a single pattern: the state gives up the visible while the structural becomes the real terrain of contest. The anti-CAA and NRC protests of 2019 and 2020 imposed a real cost. The nationwide National Register of Citizens was shelved and has not been developed, while the government is still telling Parliament that no decision has been taken. Yet the Citizenship Amendment Act itself was passed and its rules notified in March 2024. The movement won a pause, not a repeal. Pradhan’s exit sits inside this same logic, and the government has moved quickly to be seen taking action. In the days around the resignation, the Union Cabinet cleared a tougher anti-paper-leak bill, doubling prison terms and raising fines to ten crore rupees, with fast-track trials promised within three months, while the National Testing Agency dismissed forty-seven officials and announced an overhaul of its outsourcing model. So far, the measures taken are focused on punishment and personnel, aimed at those who breach the system rather than the system that produces the breach. Whether the promised overhaul delivers a meaningfully new structure, or refurbishes the same commodified examination process that turns the futures of millions into a market of coaching fees and leaked papers, is precisely what remains to be seen. While student leaders have now called off the protests at Jantar Mantar after securing government commitments to drop police cases and compensate affected families, the retreat from the streets is best understood not as the final settlement of a grievance, but as a conditional pause – leaving the broader account of structural reform still waiting to be drawn up. Unity is the real target What made the victory possible also marks its point of greatest vulnerability. Its strength lay in a solidarity that cut across the lines ordinarily used to fragment popular anger, a constituency defined not by caste, faith or region but by a shared material condition: the cheated aspirant, the graduate without work, the family that mortgaged land for a coaching fee. Grievances of that kind belong to everyone at once, which is why they unsettle a government fluent in communal arithmetic. The record shows how such unity dissolves. In 1990 the mobilization against the Mandal Commission’s reservations (also known as Other Backward Caste reservations) split the student body along caste lines, upper-caste youth against the quota, Bahujan youth for it, and the common antagonist, an unaccountable state, slipped from view. The Assam movement built a formidable unity, but on an ethnic and exclusionary basis that ended in the massacre of some 2,000 Bengali Muslims at Nellie in Assam. The instrument that breaks a movement of this kind is rarely the baton. It is the reminder to students that they are Hindu or Muslim, forward or backward, before they are the exploited youth. Pradhan’s charge that the protesters sought to “divide the country” inverts the real danger, which is that the country’s rulers divide the protest. The apparatus that met them remains where it was. The lathis, the tear gas, the internet shutdown near Parliament, the facial-recognition cameras, and the automatic weapons at its gates were withdrawn only when the political cost of using them grew too high. A concession extracted under pressure lasts only as long as the pressure stays credible. None of this diminishes the achievement. For the first time in twelve years an administration built on the appearance of invulnerability has been made to retreat by ordinary people standing together. But a retreat is not a rout, and a resignation is not a reckoning. The students have shown that the state can be moved. Whether it can be changed will be settled not at the moment of a minister’s exit but in the far longer contest over the structures left behind. Keiko Fujimori takes office as Peru’s new president

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Pablo Meriguet ／ 公開日時: 2026-07-30T21:26:17+00:00

On July 28, right-wing candidate Keiko Fujimori took office as president of Peru. After being defeated three times in a row (2016, 2021, and 2022), Fujimori managed to secure a victory that had eluded her until now due to the significant resistance caused by the figure of her father, Alberto Fujimori , who ruled Peru through a combination of an iron fist and the rapid implementation of the most predatory neoliberalism throughout the 1990s and proclaimed himself a dictator. Paradoxically, Keiko Fujimori has simultaneously used her father’s legacy as a political springboard; for while it provokes widespread rejection, it also guarantees a loyal voter base known as “Fujimorism,” which has enabled her to reach the runoff election four times, including in 2026. A recent history of instability But this year, things seem to have finally gone her way. After a vote count marred by controversy, allegations of electoral fraud, rule changes, and heated exchanges, Fujimori was declared president-elect over Roberto Sánchez. The leftist candidate was backed by Pedro Castillo, Peru’s former president, who is currently in prison for attempting to convene a Constituent Assembly. The opposition, including Fujimori’s supporters, labeled the move an attempted coup. Following this, Peru was plunged into a wave of protests demanding the resignation of Dina Boluarte’s government. Boluarte, the country’s new president, did not hesitate to harshly suppress the months-long demonstrations, leaving more than 50 dead; as a result, Boluarte is now facing a criminal investigation for alleged human rights violations. Boluarte was subsequently succeeded by several interim presidents whose brief tenures merely underscored the clear political crisis the country is facing. And it is, in fact, a crisis of legitimacy that Fujimori must now overcome. In the first round, her vote share did not exceed 17.19%. In the runoff, she secured a (highly contested) victory with just 50.14% to 49.86%. Her victory was by a margin of barely 50,000 votes. Sánchez managed to secure nearly 50% of the vote share thanks to the support of part of the center and even many right-wingers who were willing to negotiate with the left rather than face a government led by a new Fujimori. Unity and neoliberalism However, beyond the allegations of electoral fraud and the civil resistance that Sánchez has promised, the fact is that Fujimori was sworn in on July 28. She is the new president of a country that, despite its constant political crises, remains economically stable. However, many critics of the current neoliberal system argue that it is a system that benefits international capital (which holds a strong position in the Peruvian economy) and the country’s wealthiest but keeps the vast majority of Peruvians in poverty, informal employment, and material insecurity. Fujimori, 51, as she stated in her inaugural address , did not promise to transform Peru’s neoliberal model, but rather to reinforce it. To do so, the new president does not appear to wish to disrupt the economic functioning of Peruvian neoliberalism, but rather to secure the other leg of the table needed to maintain stability: the political situation. At least that is what she hinted at in her speech: “I am committed to achieving the goals of order and national reconciliation, in perfectly compatible terms. The real enemies are Peru’s major and serious problems.” Clearly, Keiko is seeking to secure, as soon as possible, a sort of truce among Peruvian political groups that, in recent years, have been marked by relentless conflict. To this end, Fujimori has established two areas of focus that she hopes will earn her greater legitimacy from her opponents: crime and natural disasters. “The fight will not be limited to common criminals. We will go after the national and international mafias that finance extortion, contract killings, drug trafficking, and illegal mining,” said Fujimori , while also stating that the Armed Forces will assume new responsibilities for internal security and promising to improve the National Police’s equipment. According to the president , nearly 24% of companies in Peru have been subjected to extortion by criminal groups. She also promised to tackle the upcoming El Niño phenomenon with intelligence and planning; according to some experts, it could become the most devastating natural disaster of its kind in decades. For this reason, Fujimori announced that she is elevating this issue to a “national emergency,” on par with the fight against crime. Likewise, Fujimori, like the other newly elected presidents, kept a trump card up her sleeve in case she needed it. She stated that Peru’s situation is complex and painful. “We inherited a state weakened by corruption.” She even went so far as to describe the country’s situation as “catastrophic” due to inadequate basic services, thousands of stalled public works projects, and a “chaotic” transportation system – all resulting from “decades of governments that shirked their responsibilities.” Thus, Fujimori decided to begin her term with a somewhat cautious speech, yet one that signaled her readiness to launch a new phase in the development of neoliberalism in Peru. And this cannot be separated from the region’s current geopolitical context. With the victories of Fujimori in Peru and De la Espriella in Colombia, the ideological balance of these governments is clearly shifting toward a right wing aligned with Washington’s agenda. A clear yet complex shift to the right It comes as no surprise to see right-wing and far-right presidents in the front row during Fujimori’s speech: Nasry Asfura of Honduras, Daniel Noboa of Ecuador, José Raúl Mulino of Panama, Santiago Peña of Paraguay, Rodrigo Paz of Bolivia, José Antonio Kast of Chile, and Javier Milei of Argentina – all of whom have signed the Shield of the Americas, the new hemispheric strategy led by the Trump administration. Thus, with only Yamandú Orsi, Uruguay’s president, representing the center-left in attendance at the presidential inauguration in Lima, the region’s clear shift to the right was evident. Perhaps the most notable absence was that of Brazil’s center-left President Lula da Silva (a country with which Peru shares a border of more than 2,800 kilometers) who declined to attend both Fujimori’s inauguration and that of De la Espriella, scheduled for next month. However, Fujimori will face a dilemma similar to that of Kast. While they may align politically with Washington, Peru’s main trading partner is China, a country that recently inaugurated the massive Chancay seaport, which promises to become the primary gateway for Chinese products into South America via the Pacific Ocean. For now, Fujimori has maintained a pragmatic stance on economic matters and has instead focused on restoring the legitimacy of a political system whose state of near-perpetual crisis has become the norm – a situation that could cause significant difficulties for a newly inaugurated government, which marks the ninth administration in just 10 years. To that end, she announced an increase in the minimum wage to $381 (1,300 soles) and compensation for companies, signaling that her administration is kicking off with executive orders. “From this moment on, an irreversible countdown begins. We have 1,826 days of administration ahead of us, during which every ministry, every region, and every public official will have to work intensely, with measurable goals and constant accountability.” Keiko Fujimori is Peru’s first female president, a fact that has been celebrated by many. However, her most important challenge now will be to prove that she can be the first female president to successfully complete her term since 2016. For now, she has Congress’s support, although if recent Peruvian political history has taught us anything, it is that nothing is more fragile than the apparent certainty of power.

Five years after Saied’s power grab, economic crisis and democratic decline fuel discontent in Tunisia

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Nicholas Mwangi ／ 公開日時: 2026-07-30T15:20:19+00:00

Tunisians took to the streets on July 26 to mark the fifth anniversary of President Kais Saied’s consolidation of power, calling for his resignation and expressing opposition to increasing restrictions on political freedoms, worsening economic hardship, and declining living conditions. Protests mark five years since Saied’s power grab Saied came to power on July 25, 2021, following which, he suspended parliament, dismissed then-Prime Minister Hichem Mechichi, lifted parliamentary immunity, and assumed broad executive powers. At the time, Saied defended the move as a necessary step to end years of political paralysis, corruption, and economic stagnation. However, opposition parties, movements, and civil society groups characterized the measures as a coup, arguing that they undermined the democratic institutions established after Tunisia’s 2011 revolution. Since then, Saied has continued to rule with expanded presidential authority . He introduced a new constitution in 2022 that significantly strengthened the powers of the presidency while reducing the role of parliament. In 2024, he secured a second presidential term in an election marked by turnout of approximately 28% , one of the lowest levels since the revolution. Several leading opposition figures were imprisoned or prevented from participating, while major opposition parties boycotted the vote, questioning its credibility. The anniversary protests reflected growing public frustration over Tunisia’s political direction and economic challenges. Opposition groups argue that the government has failed to deliver on promises of reform and economic recovery made in 2021. Workers’ Party says Tunisia has “no water, no light, no freedom” The Workers’ Party of Tunisia, whose leader had recently received death threats online , released a statement which described the current political situation as a continuation of authoritarian rule and declared that Tunisia had become a country of “no water, no light, no freedom.” The party says that the republican aspirations proclaimed at Tunisia’s independence had been undermined by successive governments and that the situation had worsened since what it called the “populist coup” of July 25, 2021. According to the Workers’ Party, the promises of “reform,” “rescue,” “serving the interests of the people and the nation,” and “protecting national sovereignty” made by Saied’s government had been exposed as “empty slogans.” It argued that, instead of resolving the country’s problems, the past five years had produced what it described as “an even more horrific form” of authoritarian rule. The party said Tunisia’s economic crisis had deepened across multiple sectors, pointing to rising public debt, growing dependence on foreign financing, persistent budget and trade deficits, declining productive capacity, and pressures on energy and food security. It argued that these economic challenges had intensified social problems, contributing to higher unemployment, poverty, inflation, and declining purchasing power as prices increased while wages remained under pressure. The statement also highlighted problems affecting public services, saying shortages of essential goods and medicines, alongside “frequent power and water outages,” had become major sources of public frustration. Beyond economic concerns, the Workers’ Party accused the authorities of expanding political repression. It alleged that the government had responded to criticism through “more political repression, security escalation, vindictiveness and trials,” claiming that the judiciary had been transformed into “a functional tool” serving executive interests. The party also accused authorities of restricting political activity, trade unions, civil society organizations, and independent media. Opposition sees momentum building alongside deepening crisis Although it acknowledged that mass mobilization has declined in recent years, the Workers’ Party argued that opposition to the government has not disappeared. “The struggle movement has experienced a significant decline … but its embers have not been completely extinguished,” the statement said, pointing to renewed activity among people, movements and other social groups. It urged progressive organizations and democratic forces to unite around demands for the release of political prisoners, the return of exiled opposition figures, greater judicial independence, media freedoms, and improvements in wages, healthcare, transportation, housing, water, and electricity services. Five years after Saied’s consolidation of power, Tunisia remains deeply polarized. While the president continues to exercise sweeping executive authority, opposition parties and social movements appear to be regaining momentum amid growing public frustration over the government’s inability to resolve the country’s persistent economic and political crises.

Brazilian public health experts see de-privatization, regional collaboration as keys to strengthen health system

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Outra Saude ／ 公開日時: 2026-07-30T15:20:19+00:00

On Wednesday, July 22, public health experts Ligia Bahia and Gonzalo Vecina met at the São Paulo School of Sociology and Politics Foundation (FESPSP) to discuss ways to strengthen Brazil’s Unified Health System (SUS) amid the challenges posed by the 2026 elections. The discussion was moderated by Aparecida Pimenta, current coordinator of FESPSP’s Walter Leser Institute. The panel was part of the seminar series Challenges for Another Brazil, organized by FESPSP and Outras Palavras . The six previous sessions addressed other strategic issues for the country’s structural transformations, such as the public budget, Social Security, labor, public safety, energy sovereignty, and industrialization. All recordings are available online . Ligia Bahia, a professor at the Federal University of Rio de Janeiro (UFRJ) and coordinator of the Group for Research and Documentation of the Commercialization of Health (GPEDS), dedicated her remarks to calling for a sober assessment of the current state of the SUS, which is increasingly overtaken by public-private relationships. In this context, she argued it is time to adopt a strategy of “de-privatization” of the healthcare system. “If we don’t move toward this, there will be no more SUS,” she emphasized. For his part, Gonzalo Vecina, professor emeritus at the School of Public Health of the University of São Paulo and founder of Anvisa, highlighted five key areas where efforts must be focused to strengthen the SUS – including the Health Economic-Industrial Complex, funding, and the establishment of new regional management structures. “We must not confuse incremental changes with reforms” Ligia Bahia opened her remarks by noting that four decades have passed since the SUS was established by the 1988 Federal Constitution and called for a thorough reassessment. She argued that despite the health movement’s courage and the importance of public healthcare, gains have been modest: “We have a social protection system in Brazil that is incomplete, very fragile, and full of holes. It is totally privatized, more so in health than in education.” Read more: 8th National Health Conference in Brazil: two opposing projects, 40 years later Bahia added: “Inequality must be the starting point for any discussion on health. This also connects us to other sectors. Today, we are isolated: we have little dialogue with Science and Technology, Transportation, or Housing. Thus, the concept of social determinants of health is neglected – while it is precisely through this lens that we see inequality as what kills.” “We must ask: have the SUS and public health policies reduced inequality? The answer may well be no, just as economic inequalities have worsened. This contrasts with the apologetic view of the SUS as the largest and best system, but as scholars, we must clearly state that public policies must be geared towards reducing inequalities.” Her assessment also highlighted that the SUS has effectively become a public-private system. “It is private in its financing, private in its workforce, and private in the provision of medical procedures. In a country with such inequality, we have a fully privatized health system that is inadequate and turns its back on the population’s health needs,” she explained. In her view, funding is one of the most critical aspects of this process. “We have to fund the SUS, but how? What’s happening in practice is that private funding is increasing while public funding is decreasing. The alligator’s mouth is wide open.” For Bahia, even congressional appropriations are less significant than tax subsidies for private healthcare. Read more: SUS: de-privatization is possible – and necessary If the growing dominance of private interests over the SUS lies at the heart of its difficulties in adequately serving the population, Bahia pointed out, this contradiction must be the focus of the political struggle in defense of the right to health. This is where the cause of de-privatizing the health system comes in. The Observatory for the De-privatization of Health , coordinated by Bahia’s research group, states that the concept refers to “processes and movements aimed at expanding or reclaiming public and democratic control over essential services and common goods in order to meet social demands and collective needs.” “In the elections, we’ll need to have a very consistent health policy agenda,” Bahia concluded. Five points for revitalizing the SUS In a concise speech, Gonzalo Vecina outlined five key issues to address within the SUS during the next political cycle. Echoing Bahia’s concerns, his first point was healthcare financing. Beyond rejecting funding cuts, Vecina advocated ending tax exemptions for private healthcare and revising monetary policy. “We have to rethink this economic equation that only serves to tax people to pay interest to capitalists. We must stop this voracious drain on funds that creates enormous inefficiency,” he criticized. Second was healthcare system management. Vecina argued that regional consortia – formed between municipalities or between states and municipalities – can help resolve current bottlenecks, particularly in supply procurement, staffing, and waiting list management. Third, closely related to management, was primary care. Vecina is critical of the Agora Tem Especialistas (“Now There Are Specialists”) program, which has been positioned as the flagship initiative of Health Minister Alexandre Padilha. “This program won’t solve any problems; it won’t increase the supply of care in the long term. It will provide some temporary relief here and there, but it isn’t the solution. Primary care may not treat cancer, but it can diagnose it if properly trained. The problem is that people need to be referred somewhere – and that somewhere doesn’t exist yet. We need to create specialized care networks that are accessible through primary care, that’s how we’ll be able to solve the problem that the Agora Tem Especialistas program seeks to address,” he argued. In his view, regional consortia are the tool that can make this expansion of the SUS feasible. Read more: Jaime Breilh: Health is incompatible with capital Fourth, Vecina highlighted the Health Economic-Industrial Complex, advocating for a focus on manufacturing “essential” products. “We need to produce vaccines, because we are a country that consumes 200 million doses when needed. I believe the experience with the Partnerships for Productive Development (PDPs) has been positive and is yielding results. With the strides that Fiocruz, the Butantan Institute, and Lafepe are making, they will be able to achieve even more. The other laboratories still need to make further progress,” he said. Finally, Vecina stressed the need to strengthen social oversight within the SUS. “It’s difficult – very difficult – but it’s a tool that transformed the quality of what we were able to achieve in government. We have to strengthen it,” he concluded. The report was written by Guilherme Arruda and published in Portuguese on Outra Saúde . It was lightly edited for length. Ecuador: Noboa’s semi-dictatorship seeks to win by eliminating opponents, not by votes

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Pilar Troya Fernández ／ 公開日時: 2026-07-30T15:20:19+00:00

President Daniel Noboa governs Ecuador with increasingly authoritarian methods . Elected in 2023 and reelected in 2025, he has concentrated power in the executive branch, clashed with the Constitutional Court, abused states of emergency, and turned the justice system into a tool against his opponents. Analysts and human rights organizations describe a drift toward a dictatorial edge: a leader who preserves the forms of democracy while eroding its substance. The run-up to the local elections on November 29, 2026, offers the clearest example of this strategy, aimed at securing victory by preventing the opposition from fielding candidates. A two-part maneuver The maneuver combines two elements that can only be understood together. The first: on March 6, 2026, following a complaint filed by the Attorney General’s Office, a judge from the Contentious-Electoral Tribunal (TCE) suspended the Citizen Revolution (RC) – the largest opposition party and movement led by former President Rafael Correa – for nine months. The measure was upheld weeks later and, in July, endorsed by the Constitutional Court. At the same time, Unidad Popular, a left-wing party, and Construye, a right-wing movement for which Fernando Villavicencio – who was assassinated in 2023 – ran for president, were removed from the registry of political organizations by the National Electoral Council (CNE) on the grounds that they did not meet the minimum number of members required by law. The appeal was denied in the case of Construye, which denounced “judicial persecution of critics and opponents” and described the decision as democratic fraud. Unidad Popular managed to prove that the CNE acted outside the legal time limit (23 days late) and that the cancellation lacked sufficient evidence regarding the actual number of members. The second piece is the calendar. In March, the National Electoral Council (CNE) moved up the local government elections (originally scheduled for February 2027) to November 29, 2026, citing the risk of rains associated with the El Niño phenomenon; neither the 1998 El Niño nor the pandemic had previously justified such an early date. The effect of fitting these two pieces together is clear: the new candidate registration period (from August 2 to 17, 2026) falls within the nine-month period during which the opposition is suspended. Moving the elections forward to coincide with the suspension prevents the government’s main rival (RC) from submitting slates under its own party name. The alternative party, also shut down Faced with this deadlock, the Correístas sought a legal workaround: registering their candidates under the allied Amigo movement. The alternative was short-lived. On July 16, the Attorney General’s Office informed the TCE of an investigation into alleged money laundering against that movement; the following day, the Court also suspended it for nine months, and the CNE implemented the measure immediately, just two weeks before the registration deadline. The Executive Branch’s conduct fuels suspicions. On July 23, Noboa stated that pre-candidates could register through “any other party that has not been involved in money laundering,” mentioning the Socialist Party in the same breath – that is, presenting an alleged crime that is still under investigation as a fait accompli. His secretary of public administration, José Julio Neira – former director of the government agency that manages confidential financial information – publicly detailed the origin of the suspicions. Legal expert Mauricio Alarcón warned that what is alarming is not the investigation itself, but rather that the government appears to have access to confidential files “that are not even in the hands of the parties involved,” and he questioned “the selectivity” of the investigation and its timing in relation to the electoral calendar. If the RC candidates register through the Socialist Party, it would come as no surprise if that party were also disbanded or suspended. Torture, disappearances, and activists killed All of this is unfolding against a sinister backdrop. In June 2026, Ecuador was included for the first time in the World Organization Against Torture’s Global Torture Index, in the “high-risk” category: the organization documented 369 reports of torture and at least 51 enforced disappearances attributed to state agents between 2024 and 2026, within the context of the “internal armed conflict” declared by the government, which has imposed 21 states of emergency, 9 curfews, and 272 days of movement restrictions – measures that have had virtually no effect on reducing crime and have led to an increase in internal displacement due to threats, extortion, and pressure from criminal groups. Two deaths occurred amid this climate. Robinson del Pezo, who was covering land conflicts in the province of Santa Elena, was murdered in November 2025. Anti-corruption activist Mónica Silva, of Polish origin and a resident of the country for over a decade, who had demanded justice for that crime and reported threats against her life, was found dead on June 8, 2026. The Minister of the Interior suggested suicide, but the autopsy revealed a blow to the head and strangulation. Silva was investigating alleged cocaine shipments hidden in banana containers belonging to a company linked to the president’s family, as well as a land-trafficking network. The UN Special Rapporteur on human rights defenders called on Ecuador to “cease the persecution” and protect activists. Corruption at home The official narrative presents the cases against the opposition as an anti-corruption crusade. However, the biggest scandal of the period involves the executive branch itself: in the Progen case, the State Comptroller General’s Office estimated the loss to the state at approximately $104 million due to emergency thermal power generation contracts signed in 2024 with that US company, whose equipment never became operational. There are also the eight shipments of cocaine found in the Noboa family’s banana exports. The result is an election whose contenders are being decided in the courts rather than at the polls. The government insists that these cases are unrelated to the executive branch; the opposition and part of the legal community counter that the selectivity of the proceedings and their timing point in another direction. US sanctions top Chinese universities and research institutes, citing national security threat

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Abdul Rahman ／ 公開日時: 2026-07-29T21:59:19+00:00

Last week, the US Department of “War” added China’s leading universities and dozens of other research organizations into a list of foreign institutions considered to be a potential “threat to its national security” over their alleged links with the Chinese armed forces. China has voiced strong opposition to the latest US sanctions, claiming the US is weaponizing its research for political reasons and argued that such moves ultimately harm global collaboration in scientific and technological developments. The list, released on July 23, includes a total of 130 institutions, 88 of which are Chinese, including top universities Fudan and Shanghai Jiao Tong, and other entities located in Russia and Iran. “These institutions have been confirmed to engage in activities that increase the likelihood of US government funded research and development efforts being misappropriated,” the Pentagon claimed in the release. It further claimed that such “misappropriation” enables adverse external interventions in science and technology “that directly threatens America’s national security and scientific integrity.” Their inclusion on the list means that no US institution can have collaborative research or technological exchanges with these institutions. The Section 1286 list, as it is formally known, was created under the 2019 National Defense Authorization Act to curb the transfer of “sensitive technologies” or collaboration with institutes linked with foreign military or intelligence agencies. The US has long accused China of “stealing” its technological innovations. Similar allegations were repeated by Trump’s science and technology advisor Michael Kratsios last week when he alleged a Chinese AI company was stealing US AI models. A reflection of Cold war mentality Last week’s sanctions and repeated allegations of stealing are widely seen as a part of Washington’s attempts to curb China’s advances in science and technology and undermine its achievements. China has rejected such allegations and maintained that such moves are part of the US plan to block its economic development and a reflection of the Cold War mentality of its ruling class. The list is part of a series of anti-China steps taken by successive US administrations in the last decade. The first Trump administration started a trade war while the Joe Biden administration tried to restrict Chinese access to advanced semiconductors. Trump in his second term has reenergized economic warfare. Last month China reciprocated US attempts to restrict the operations of dozens of its top companies, including BYD, Alibaba, and Baidu, by adding 10 US firms to its export ban list. Earlier this month, the US National Science Foundation (NSF) expressed its intention to ban all future collaboration with any research institute or their employees included in any restricted entity lists issued by the government mostly impacting the Chinese institutes. In response to last week’s sanctions, the Chinese Ministry of Commerce issued a statement on Wednesday, July 29, expressing its strong dissatisfaction and firm opposition. “The US has continuously overstretched the concept of national security, erected discriminatory barriers, and politicized, weaponized and instrumentalized scientific research cooperation,” Chinese Ministry of Commerce said in a statement. It asserted that China will take all necessary measures to “safeguard the legitimate rights and interests of Chinese research institutions and ensure a sound environment for normal scientific and technological exchanges and cooperation. However, it warned that the US move “undermines the exchange and cooperation between the two countries’ scientific communities and runs counter to the global trend of international collaboration in scientific and technological innovations.”
