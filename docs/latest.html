The Blue Collar Brigade Has the Right Messengers

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Jared Abbott ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-02T13:51:36+00:00

When Brian Poindexter made his first fundraising calls in his campaign for Congress (OH-7), he phoned a fellow ironworker and asked for fifty dollars. His buddy said he couldn’t help, since he’d been laid off. It’s hard to imagine Poindexter’s opponent, Republican Congressman Max Miller, having a conversation like that — Miller, an heir to one of Cleveland’s wealthiest families , has millions at his disposal. That contrast is the whole idea behind the Blue Collar Brigade, the joint fundraising alliance Poindexter launched this summer with four other working-class Democrats running for Congress. They pooled their money because none of them had access to the wealthy donor networks traditional candidates rely on. They are betting that a firefighter (Bob Brooks, PA-7), a flight attendant (Kaela Berg, MN-2), a smokejumper (Sam Forstag, MT-1), an ironworker (Brian Poindexter, OH-7), and a son of Central Valley California farmworkers (Randy Villegas, CA-22) are Democrats’ best shot at winning back critically important, working-class-heavy swing districts. The Brigade’s gamble on working-class, economic-populist candidates is a smart one. Our research at the Center for Working-Class Politics has shown again and again that non-elite candidates are a genuine asset with working-class voters, and that an upper-class background is a serious liability. In turn, we’ve consistently found that running on a populist platform that puts working people at the center of America’s story and calling out economic elites and corrupt Washington politicians (ideally from both parties) is a winning formula. The Brigade is typifying this approach. Poindexter, for instance, believes that “an economy that works can’t be built from the penthouse down. It has to be built from the foundation up, and workers are that foundation.” Meanwhile, firefighter and union leader Bob Brooks bluntly concludes that “Washington’s a dumpster fire, and working people are the ones getting burned.” What’s more, all five of these candidates are union members. Our research has shown both that union candidates’ campaigns are more consistently aimed at winning working-class votes, and that union members in office are more effective tribunes of working-class interests than other candidates (even other progressives). We at the CWCP have been exhorting candidates for years to focus like a laser on bread-and-butter economic issues. These candidates not only do that, but their economic platforms are genuinely ambitious. From raising taxes on billionaires and big corporations, to strengthening workers’ power on the job, to letting every American buy into the same trusted and high-quality coverage Medicare already provides, to creating millions of well-paid jobs to rebuild America’s crumbling infrastructure, the Brigade is running on economic programs big enough to convince working-class voters that someone is actually fighting for them. Every one of these policies points to the same problem. “There was a time,” Brooks explains , “when a blue-collar job could make you a decent living. It doesn’t anymore.” The Blue Collar Brigade is refreshing in its single-minded obsession with fixing that problem. Their shared goal is to ensure, as Brian Poindexter puts it , that “if you work hard, you should be able to afford to live with dignity, support your family, and plan for the future. That’s not asking for special treatment — it’s asking for a fair deal.” In recent research I conducted with Joan C. Williams, we found that effective working-class messaging also requires speaking to core working-class values. The Blue Collar Brigade largely excels on this score. Poindexter ties pride and loyalty to country directly to jobs, for example. “If we believe in America, we should build it here,” he has said . “I believe in ‘Made in America’ in real, practical ways: in the jobs we create, the products we buy, and the communities we invest in.” Brooks’s campaign makes hard work the through line of his biography, describing a candidate who, “whether plowing driveways at 4 a.m. or mowing lawns after a firefighting shift. . . . has never stopped hustling to provide for his family.” Likewise, Brooks treats gun ownership as an inherited tradition, describing himself as “a firefighter who owns guns and grew up around guns” and respects the Second Amendment, only then making the case for universal background checks. Sam Forstag, meanwhile, takes working-class Americans’ concerns about public safety seriously rather than dismissing them out of hand, promising a system that “keeps violent criminals and dangerous drugs off our streets” while also championing criminal justice reform and accountability for law enforcement overreach, including Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE). The common thread is that these candidates don’t map contested issues onto the usual red-blue divide and demand party loyalty. They speak the language of working-class values instead — a strategy with real potential to stem the Democratic Party’s exodus of working-class voters. Differences in the Ranks Beyond their points of unity, there are important differences in how the Blue Collar Brigade candidates speak and where they stand on social issues. The first difference is one of register: how effectively each candidate connects to working-class values and how well they avoid activist and technocratic language that can alienate working-class voters. Brooks and Poindexter are the strongest in this regard, using plainspoken language that reads as unmistakably working class. By contrast, while Kaela Berg’s personal story is as compelling as anyone’s, her campaign statements read at a college level, and her packaged messaging sometimes drifts into the language of a policy memo rather than everyday speech. There are also clear differences around how the candidates navigate social issues. Brooks presents himself as the most culturally moderate, a gun owner who supports universal background checks and pairs border security with a path to earned citizenship. Poindexter says almost nothing about culture at all and keeps his campaign focused relentlessly on class. By contrast, though generally disciplined in how they discuss social issues to ensure the economic-populist core of their message remains front and center, Forstag and Villegas nonetheless come across as more culturally progressive than Brooks or Poindexter. Forstag was a paid lobbyist for the American Civil Liberties Union and Planned Parenthood who describes that record as a fight to “keep government out of your business.” Villegas takes a different approach than Brooks and Poindexter on immigration, centering that discussion on the human cost of ICE raids in his heavily Latino Central Valley district. Berg, the only Brigade member who still needs to clear the primary stage, is the only one of the five candidates who openly holds full-throated left-wing positions on immigration, transgender issues, and Israel-Palestine. Whether any of these differences will prove electorally meaningful is an open question, since Democratic candidates in swing districts blending working-class biography and economic-populist messaging is so rare that we have little precedent to judge by. But it is worth noting that Villegas’s positions on immigration are unlikely to be out of step with his overwhelmingly working-class Latino constituents, whose communities were among the first in the country to endure large-scale immigration raids in 2025. Likewise, Berg is running in a more Democratic-leaning district with a smaller share of working-class voters than any of the others, in Minnesota, a state that bore the brunt of some of ICE’s worst excesses earlier this year. A Broader Brigade While the Brigade has banded together under the same umbrella, they’re not the only candidates running the playbook. There’s Rebecca Cooke , a dairy farmer’s daughter who can be found waiting tables when not on the campaign trail. And Nate Powell , a Spokane firefighter and marine running as an independent in eastern Washington. There are also Senate hopefuls with similarly powerful working-class, economic-populist credentials, such as independent Dan Osborn in Nebraska and Democrat Troy Jackson in Maine. And though they are imperfect parallels, since the Brigade generally brings more firsthand working-class experience and runs on bolder progressive economic platforms, a handful of sitting Democrats in tough districts offer important precedents. Marie Gluesenkamp Perez (WA-3), who owns a rural auto repair shop and campaigns in a mechanic’s jumpsuit, has twice held a Donald Trump–won district in southwest Washington, running on a small-business-focused version of economic populism. Marcy Kaptur has held her Toledo-anchored, Trump-won district (OH-9) for decades, and Sherrod Brown — a fiery opponent of deindustrialization and bad trade deals — won statewide in an increasingly Republican Ohio until his narrow 2024 loss. Brown is now running for Ohio’s other Senate seat against incumbent Republican Jon Husted, and the race is a toss-up . And Gabe Vasquez (NM-2) — who comes, like Villegas, from a working-class immigrant family with deep roots in his district but who also built a professional career before running for office, has won repeatedly in a majority Latino, Trump-trending swing district in southern New Mexico by keeping his message plainspoken and focused squarely on kitchen-table economics. For all their differences, the Blue Collar Brigade candidates share far more than divides them. Even one or two wins in November would set a significant precedent, proving that the Bernie Sanders coalition of 2016 can travel into the swing districts any lasting working-class realignment of American politics will require.

Mahmoud Khalil Is Suing the Trump Administration

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Mahmoud Khalil ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-02T13:51:36+00:00

Since October 7, 2023, critics of Israel in Europe and the United States have faced serious repression. In the United States, college student protests against the unfolding genocide of Palestinians in Gaza were met with a heavy-handed response by police and university administrations. Repression of pro-Palestine activists intensified with Donald Trump’s return to the White House in 2025, as the Trump administration began detaining and deporting noncitizen residents for their criticisms of the United States’ policy toward Israel. Mahmoud Khalil , a green card holder and former Columbia University graduate student, was an early victim of the crackdown. Though Khalil has been freed from detention, the government is still seeking his deportation; Khalil is now suing the Trump administration in turn for alleged violations of his rights. In an interview for The Jacobin Show , Khalil spoke to David Griscom about his lawsuits against the administration, the repression of pro-Palestine voices, and the state of the movement for Palestine in the United States today. You can watch the full interview here . This transcript has been edited for length and clarity. David Griscom You have this new lawsuit against the Trump administration. What’s being argued in this suit? Mahmoud Khalil I now have multiple lawsuits against the Trump administration. The first one, which we consider the biggest one, is the conspiracy lawsuit. We’ve already filed a lawsuit against members of the Trump administration — Stephen Miller, Marco Rubio, the attorney general and a couple others, as well as the Heritage Foundation, Canary Mission, Betar, these right-wing Zionist hate groups. We’re suing them for conspiring together to deprive me of my civil rights under the Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871. That act was passed by Congress during the Reconstruction era, more than a century ago, to prevent local governments from conspiring with the Ku Klux Klan to deprive newly freed black people of their rights. Today, unfortunately, we’re seeing ourselves in similar situations. You have private and public actors conspiring together to make public examples of Palestinians and their supporters, to send a message to others that if you speak, then they will come after you — the same way the Klan sought to do during Reconstruction. In this lawsuit, we’re saying the federal government should not be colluding with private groups to target and persecute people for their First Amendment–protected rights, whether it’s about supporting Palestine or not. It’s not only about Palestine; it’s about this plot between private actors and public actors. David Griscom Could you talk a little bit about the Canary Mission? This organization’s operations are pretty offensive to free speech. Mahmoud Khalil Canary Mission is a shadowy, unaccountable group. Newly reported investigations have uncovered that it’s actually being run from Israel by American Israeli citizens there. Its main function used to be to intimidate those who speak out in support of Palestine by posting their profiles online, contacting their employers, doxing them, smearing them. [Being targeted by Canary Mission] was a risk I knew about when I got into Palestine advocacy. A lot of pro-Palestine people use it like a dating app, if you have a profile there . . . David Griscom I got my start on the Left doing Students for Justice in Palestine back in the day. And I remember these organizations. People did kind of see it like, “Oh, it’s nice to be noticed!” But this is gallows humor. The repercussions from this can be quite severe. Mahmoud Khalil The most dangerous thing is they use it as an intimidation tactic. They use it to smear people so they stop advocating for Palestine. I made that calculation: If an employer wouldn’t hire me for my advocacy for Palestine, why would I work for them? But I never imagined that the anti-Palestinian bigotry would become institutionalized. That the government would go to Canary Mission’s website and would communicate with Canary Mission to actually target and deport people. It’s not only happening to noncitizens but also to citizens. Court records have uncovered that the government used and communicated with Canary Mission to target people. They got five thousand names from Canary Mission to surveil and to go after. I was one of those people whose name was communicated by Canary Mission and Betar and others to the administration. [These organizations have had] no accountability whatsoever. They’re like a shell company. The same with Betar. Betar is known to be a very racist right-wing Israeli organization that adopts a very extreme pro-Israeli position. Recently, New York actually banned Betar from operating there, because of the pervasive intimidation and threats coming from this group against activists and advocates in the country. David Griscom What is Project Esther, and how is the Heritage Foundation rolled up into this conspiracy? Mahmoud Khalil The Heritage Foundation — we know that since Trump got into office the first time — they’ve been advising him on how to change American politics, change American society. They issued Project 2025, which they are famous for. But they’re less famous for Project Esther, which was issued the year after October 7, after they saw the amount of support that Palestine received on college campuses and in the streets. They issued Project Esther, which is a blueprint for the government to follow. It talks about private-public partnerships to intimidate those speaking out for Palestine, so that you can make an example out of them, and then no one would speak out. For international students, they said you have to deport them, then other international students in the country wouldn’t speak out for Palestine. This was one of the things in Project Esther. And because it was issued before Trump was in power [a second time], it said that we need an administration willing to collaborate with these private institutions. So they already had a very well-established network of these shady groups like Betar and Canary Mission, but they were missing the willing administration to support them. Once Trump was in power, he was more than willing to collaborate with them. Project Esther is about criminalizing support for Palestine in a way that would intimidate people, would chill speech across the country. That’s the main purpose of it, because they know they can never win on the merits. They can never win on the argument of supporting a genocidal apartheid state like Israel. David Griscom I live in Texas, and I hear all the time from Republicans about how people are trying to indoctrinate the kids, and that we need to protect young people. But here we have, at the highest level of government, people acting at the behest of a foreign government trying to shut down free speech at our college campuses and using serious threats to achieve those ends. If you’re worried about what’s being forced upon the next generation, this is something that you probably should be worried about. Mahmoud Khalil That’s the base of it. Because what they’re trying to do is to stop any argument, any free inquiry, any academic freedom. They’re trying to push Anti-Defamation League trainings into school curricula. What we’re saying is it’s freedom of speech. The First Amendment is there to protect that. America is the land of the free; they can’t employ and inflict such violence on people for speaking out on their ideas. For so many years, Israel and its lobby have criminalized the Boycott, Divestment, and Sanctions campaign in a lot of states. Although that’s very First Amendment: I don’t like something, I want to boycott it. They’ve been working on this for so many years. What’s happening to me is only the tip of the iceberg of what these groups are willing to do. I’m always baffled by politicians and institutions that are willing to sacrifice the Constitution of the United States. They are willing to sacrifice the rule of law, the safety of their own people, just to protect Israel. David Griscom You wrote a piece a while ago where you asked the question: Who has the right to have rights? I think about this in your case but also in many other cases. For my friends who do labor organizing, for example, the question of citizenship comes up. Like, if I join a union, I might lose my legal status in this country. You see the violence in the streets all across the country, with Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), and all these areas where this country makes exceptions. It says, “We are the land of the free, we have all these rights,” and then, in this circumstance, that doesn’t count anymore. Mahmoud Khalil I’m still struggling with that question, of who has the right to have rights. The first thing I said upon my release from detention is, “No human being is illegal.” We’re all equal. We were all born equal, and we should continue to be equal. This government is trying to divide people into tiers: first is an American-born citizen, then a naturalized citizen, then a permanent resident, then an undocumented person. [They want to say] what rights you have are based on these things. This government is trying to take all these rights that generations in this country, since its founding, [have been working to achieve]. That’s my problem: that the state thinks that it grants these rights and people accept them. When they say a green card is a privilege or a visa is a privilege, I’m like, “Yeah, it is a privilege of the law — it’s a right . It’s not a privilege of Trump or a privilege of this administration.” But this is what this administration is trying to do: to make everything a privilege. Federal funding is a privilege. Medicare is a privilege. Freedom of speech is a privilege. They’re trying to turn everything into a privilege, while in reality they are rights. And they are rights that everyone in this country deserves and should claim. Now I don’t want to be naive about what citizenship is, or borders. We don’t live in utopia [where we can] say, we don’t need any borders. But what’s happening is dehumanization; it’s divide and conquer. David Griscom We’re seeing it across the board. Regularly we have candidates in Texas, who are very likely to be elected to office, who have called not just for deportations of migrants, or of people who were born in other countries and got citizenship, but of people who just have political opinions that they disagree with. I want to turn toward the movement for human rights through the movement for Palestinian people in this country, and to hear your thoughts on where we’re at in this moment. In many ways, being able to talk openly about this subject, to speak the truth about Israel, has become more accepted than it ever was before. We are seeing major public officials, like Zohran Mamdani, say things that a lot of others wouldn’t in the past. Yet we are still watching horrors in Gaza; we are still watching horrors in the West Bank. Mahmoud Khalil At the outset, I would say we shouldn’t take for granted that now more people are speaking their minds on Palestine or on other issues. Because we fought for this — whether it’s the students who got suspended or expelled, whether it is the individuals who were sacked from their jobs, fired for speaking out for Palestine. I don’t want to take that for granted, because again, that’s the thing about rights being taken away from us. Public memory is usually short, and that’s what Israel is banking on. [A lot of people think the genocide in Gaza] is only a Benjamin Netanyahu phenomenon. This is what Israel has been since its founding. Since 1948, Israel has been a genocidal state. Since ’48, Israel has been an apartheid state. It’s nothing new; it’s not about Netanyahu. That’s why I wouldn’t take anything for granted and would continue to push more and more. The movement, in the past few years, has made huge successes in shifting public opinion in the country. If we’re talking about the American public as a whole, we have the majority of them opposing sending arms to Israel, a lot of them favoring Palestinian human rights. Which is great. But also, at what expense that came — it came at the expense of over one hundred thousand people killed in Palestine and Syria and Lebanon and Iran, all across the region. A huge cost was paid to get to this level of public opinion. Now we’re cashing in on this, thankfully, with the election of Zohran, with the election of Darializa Avila Chevalier , of Claire Valdez , of others. But it’s not enough. We have to realize that the movement for Palestine in this country is facing an incredible amount of suppression of intimidation, and it’s not unique to this administration. Everything that Trump is doing now, Joe Biden paved the way for it. It’s not like Trump is coming up with all these ideas or was able to do all of what he has done in one day. The previous presidential administrations really paved the way for this administration to be able to do what it’s doing right now. And to think about the amount of violence that was inflicted on this movement in this country — whether through sham congressional hearings, whether through ICE and weaponization of the immigration system, or the weaponization of antisemitism, or through expelling hundreds of students to make examples out of them. . . . What I really look forward to, in terms of accountability — accountability comes not only through courts, not only through winning lawsuits, it comes also from the public punishing Rep. Adriano Espaillat for not standing up for his constituents and the students at Columbia University, for punishing Dan Goldman for being a staunch supporter of Israel. David Griscom We are going into the midterms right now. What should these basic demands be, of anyone who wants to earn a vote? What does accountability look like here? Mahmoud Khalil I speak as first as a Palestinian but also a father of an American citizen and a husband of an American citizen. Really, it’s about human rights first. And even when they say, “America First,” to me that wouldn’t change much: when you put human rights first, America would be first, but all other human beings would be first as well. I paid thousands of dollars this year for taxes. Why am I spending that money on Israel and not for my son to have a better playground? I think the most important thing is — that’s what I like about the candidates I mentioned, that they were very unequivocal and unapologetic about the principles they stand for. Because what we’re seeing now with a lot of Democrats, especially those who [only recently signed a bill] to stop arming Israel, they only did that because their constituents were mad at them, not because they’re principled. That’s what Palestine exposed about people. A lot of people did not vote for Zohran just because of his views on Palestine. But when you’re wrong on Palestine, that exposes how rotten your politics are. To miss that very clear principle says a lot about who you are and how you would represent your own people.

Monthly Newsletter of the Rochester Chapter of Democratic Socialists of America Brett A., Secretary . We talk about “solidarity” a lot as socialists, whether standing in it, or singing our favorite songs about it. It’s a simple concept that can seem somewhat mystified in our current political climate. We are conditioned to think “Everyone for themselves”, looking out for our own self-interest only, selling our skills on the market as a single economic unit, casting a single vote within limits already marked out by elites. Solidarity is the antidote to the chill of this lonesome individualism. And it does not entail a hive-mind-like unity as if we were a colony of ants or bees. Solidarity is not unity, but it is cohesion of diversity across differences. We may disagree on specific tactics, but we know to come together and support one another, basking in the warmth of shared struggle. What’s crucial is that solidarity is not merely a feeling, it is made real in action. There is nothing more empowering and more indicative of our shared humanity than fighting for your neighbor, on the picket line, at city hall, on the streets or whatever terrain we call home. As socialists, these bonds need to be reconstructed, and I encourage you to spread solidarity wherever you go. Our work will require us to organize with those whose politics are close to ours to extreme opposites, yet we know that all humans are siblings and that is the power we must harness to achieve our goals. * * * Interested in contributing? What Jimothy Can Teach Vermont About Dignity

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Champlain Valley DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-02T10:24:42+00:00

Note: posts by individual GMDSA members do not necessarily reflect the views of the broader membership or of its leadership and should not be regarded as official statements by the chapter. Photo Credit: Kiana Hall For the past few weeks this summer, a round and lanky raccoon named Jimothy stole the hearts of everyone on the internet. Millions of people shared videos of his unusual body. Artists drew him. People made plushies, memes, and some people even got tattoos of the unlikely internet celebrity. He was showered in compliments, described as adorable and perfect. His difference wasn't something to hide, it became the very reason people celebrated him. Wildlife experts believe Jimothy may have a rare congenital condition known as short spine syndrome, which causes the spine to be shortened or compressed, giving affected animals their distinctive appearance. He doesn't look like or move like other raccoons. Yet the overwhelming reaction wasn't disgust or ridicule. It was affection. Watching the world embrace Jimothy has been heartwarming. It has also left me wondering why we find it so much easier to celebrate differences in animals than in humans. One in four Vermonters lives with a disability. They don't become internet celebrities when they move differently, speak differently, or navigate the world with mobility aids. More often, they encounter inaccessible buildings, employers that fail to provide reasonable accommodations, housing that doesn't meet their needs, transportation systems that leave them behind, and public policies that treat disability as an afterthought. This is not to compare disabled people to animals. Disabled people are our coworkers, neighbors, classmates, friends, and family members. They have dreams, ambitions, frustrations, talents, and opinions as varied as anyone else's. They do not need to become symbols of inspiration or objects of pity. They deserve something much more meaningful. They deserve dignity. Dignity is more than being treated politely. It means having the freedom to participate fully in your community: to work if you choose, to find housing you can access, to participate in public life, to visit friends, to raise a family, and to live independently. Those freedoms depend not only on personal kindness, but on a society we build together. Too often, discussions about disability begin and end with kindness. Kindness matters, but kindness alone cannot build a wheelchair-accessible apartment. It cannot guarantee reliable public transportation. It cannot eliminate policies that force people to choose between accepting a better-paying job and losing the benefits that allow them to survive. Those are political questions. The disability rights movement has long argued that many of the greatest barriers disabled people face are not created by their bodies but by the society built around them. Disability is a natural part of the human experience. Any one of us can become disabled through illness, injury, or simply living long enough. The question is not whether disability exists. The question is whether we choose to build communities where everyone can participate fully. Here in Vermont, we like to think of ourselves as good neighbors. Often, we are. But being a good neighbor means more than holding the door open. It means building towns where every neighbor can actually get through the door. Across our state, disabled Vermonters continue to encounter inaccessible housing, gaps in transportation, limited employment opportunities, and public spaces that too often exclude rather than include. These are not inevitable realities. They are the result of public choices about funding, infrastructure, housing policy, transportation, education, and labor. The same is true of poverty. Many disabled Vermonters live with the constant fear of the "benefits cliff," where earning a little more money can mean losing healthcare or other essential supports before wages are high enough to replace them. A promotion, an extra shift, or a better-paying job should be a path toward greater independence, not a financial trap. Instead of encouraging growth, these policies can punish people for succeeding. Housing tells a similar story. People experiencing disability are disproportionately vulnerable to housing insecurity and homelessness. As Vermont continues to confront its housing crisis, accessibility cannot be treated as an optional feature added after the fact. Safe, affordable, accessible housing is not a luxury. It is a prerequisite for participating in community life. Jimothy reminds us of something beautiful about ourselves. Our first instinct was compassion. No one demanded that he change before he deserved affection. No one suggested his body made him less worthy of existing. People simply accepted that he belonged. Imagine extending that instinct beyond a viral raccoon. Imagine a Vermont where every sidewalk, school, apartment building, workplace, and public meeting was designed with disabled people in mind from the beginning instead of retrofitted later. A Vermont where nobody had to choose between employment and healthcare. A Vermont where disability was treated not as an individual burden but as a shared responsibility to remove unnecessary barriers. That vision requires more than goodwill. It requires investing in accessible housing, reliable public transportation, universal healthcare, strong labor protections, and public services that allow everyone to participate fully in civic life. It requires recognizing that dignity is not something earned through productivity or independence. It belongs to every person simply because they are a person. Loving Jimothy costs us nothing. Building a Vermont where disabled people can live with security, independence, and dignity will require resources, political courage, and collective action. One is a viral moment. Viral moments fade, the values they reveal don’t have to. The compassion we showed Jimothy is worth celebrating. The challenge now is extending that same instinct to our neighbors and building a Vermont that reflects it.

Book Review: Grief and resistance for Palestine

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: Mondoweiss ／ 著者: Kirk Campbell ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-01T15:37:21+00:00

Ramsey Hanhan's book, "Palestine Bleeds for You," is a sensitive and astute expression of grief and resistance to oppression.
