Smoke, Heat, Storms: How Climate Change is Making Bad Working Conditions Even Worse

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: In These Times ／ 著者: 不明 ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-13T19:50:42+00:00

One morning, when Jacob started his 9 a.m. shift as a UPS driver in Minnesota, he noticed it was hazy outside. It was July 16 , and wildfires raging across northeastern Minnesota and southern Canada had unleashed acrid, hazardous smoke across much of the state. The Minnesota Pollution Control Agency was urging people to stay indoors. But Jacob was expected to work an estimated 9 - or 10 -hour shift, getting in and out of his vehicle delivering packages. Management hadn’t provided any protective measures, like masks, he says, and he was inhaling the toxic smoke for hours. ​ “ The worst part about driving and working outside is we don’t have air conditioning or anything,” he says over the phone. ​ “ On that day it was extremely hot as well as humid, so we drove with both doors open on the highway or just in our delivery area. We were exposed to the air.” (Jacob is a pseudonym, and we are not identifying his specific work location within Minnesota, to protect him from retaliation.) Three hours into his shift, he felt a tightness in his chest. ​ “ I didn’t pay it no mind until I started to get light-headed,” he tells me. Determining it was unsafe to keep driving, Jacob sought medical attention. After hours of observation and tests, he was eventually cleared for release, but the experience left him unsettled. Wildfires are growing larger and more destructive due to human-made climate change. ​ “ It’s a little scary knowing that you’re breathing in toxic air and still needing to service your community, and that there isn’t anything in place that protects you or your wellbeing,” he says. As climate change intensifies , worsening fires and extreme weather are creating increasingly hostile conditions for workers like Jacob. Alongside fighting for healthcare, wages and better treatment, these workers now find themselves fighting for protections from dangerous heat, smoke and storms — the severity of which is only expected to worsen if the status quo continues. Our warming planet, in other words, is defining the terrain in which workers must organize. ​ “ In Minnesota, every summer, we now experience wildfire smoke in these hot months,” says Scott Wilkie, the president of Teamsters Local 638 , the union that represents Jacob. ​ “ It’s been more intense, especially in the northern parts of Minnesota,” he adds. ​ “ You’re dealing with heat, already tough working conditions and then, on top of that, now you’ve got an air quality index that’s at an unhealthy level.” At the Affy Tapple food processing plant in Niles, Ill., workers describe sweltering indoor temperatures and cooling systems so insufficient that, during the summer, it’s often hotter inside than out. “​​If the temperature is 85 [degrees Fahrenheit] outside, I can bet the temperature inside is at least 95 [degrees],” one worker told me. Like many labor issues, workers say the heat is the result of a combination of factors, including management shortcomings. But they worry about what their workplace will be like as climate change intensifies. Inside the factory, workers cook caramel and dip and prepare caramel apples on assembly lines, before they’re packed and sent out for delivery. During candy season, which starts in August and goes through the fall, up to 200 people work at the facility, many of them hired through temporary staffing agencies. Outside of this busy season, around 50 workers work at the facility, workers say. Workers say their uniforms add to the discomfort of the heat. ​ “ We have to use plastic sleeves covering our entire arms,” a worker tells me. ​ “ The plastic sticks to our skin because of sweat, and it’s incredibly uncomfortable. It’s the same thing with the glove. You are sweating and can’t pull up your gloves comfortably because of the sweat.” “ I’m thirsty all the time but can’t leave the line and go get water,” a worker told me. ​ “ Maybe every hour you have a chance to get a drink.” Another worker put it this way: ​ “ I get tired very quickly. I’m sweating and want water. Your body gets exhausted.” To protect them from retaliation, workers’ identities and jobs are anonymous. They don’t have a union, and heat, they say, is only one of several issues they face. Health insurance and pay are inadequate, they tell me, and they are concerned about preventable workplace injuries, including severe burns. ​ “ The focus is on the product,” one worker told me. ​ “ If the workers melt or the workers freeze, nobody gives a damn. The apples have to come out perfect.” Smoke from the Camp Fire settles over Moose Lake as several wildfires burning in the Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness forced its closure and evacuation on Tuesday, July 14, 2026, in Ely, Minn. Photo by Anthony Soufflé/The Minnesota Star Tribune via Getty Images There is overwhelming evidence that rising temperatures increase heat-related illnesses among workers, especially those in fields like construction and agriculture. But rising temperatures also affect indoor workers, particularly those who face ​ “ confounding factors,” such as ​ “ heat from machinery and surroundings” and ​ “ wearing of personal protective equipment,” according to a 2025 report from the World Meteorological Organization and the World Health Organization. In 2023 , heat contributed to about 28 , 000 workplace injuries in the United States, according to one estimate. Workers depend on their employers to create hazard-free work environments. Affy Tapple is owned by private equity, an opaque industry associated with cost-cutting and poor labor conditions. Affy Tapple is owned by Beavers Holdings, which is owned by Pritzker Private Capital, which is private equity, according to Matt Parr, communications director for the Private Equity Stakeholder Project. “ It would not surprise me to see a correlation there between private equity ownership and poor working conditions,” Parr says. ​ “ It’s a scenario we’ve seen play out across different types of sectors with cost-cutting or laying workers off or not having good working conditions.” Affy Tapple did not respond to a request for comment submitted through a contact form on their website. The same day Jacob, the UPS driver, was sickened by the smoke, his union, Teamsters Local 638 , issued a statement declaring, ​ “ Our guidance to members at this time in simplest form is, take care of your health needs no matter what — especially if your employer won’t. Seek medical attention immediately if needed.” Wildfire smoke is more dangerous than other forms of air pollution caused by traffic and agriculture, because wildfires burn far hotter, which means the fine particles they release ​ “ are much more chemically active and can be very, very toxic,” according to University of California – San Diego environmental epidemiologist Tarik Benmarhnia. A 2022 study looking at California’s wildfire seasons from 2004 to 2009 found that smoky days are associated with a 10 . 3 % increase in hospital visits for asthma, with children five and under most severely affected. Another study from June found that chronic exposure to fine particulate matter in wildfire smoke increases stroke risk in older adults. A UPS driver drapes a towel over his head while driving along Broadway during a heat wave on July 6, 2012, in New York City. Forecasts at the time predicted temperatures near 100 degrees Fahrenheit, with a heat index as high as 106 degrees due to humidity, according to the National Weather Service. The severe heat wave gripping much of the Midwest also damaged crops and kept residents indoors. Photo by Spencer Platt via Getty Images Section 5 of the federal Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 says bosses must provide workplaces that are ​ “ free from recognized hazards that are causing or are likely to cause death or serious physical harm to his employees.” But Wilkie says he’s concerned that UPS management failed to ensure a hazard-free workplace — despite reported warnings from the union. Wilkie says he reached out to regional UPS management on July 15 , urging them to take proactive action in response to the worsening air quality index, but said they did nothing of substance. The next day, Wilkie says, ​ “ it was just business as usual, and they didn’t provide any mask options.” Management did encourage workers to run their fans during the day and take lunch breaks inside, he says, but did little beyond those suggestions. According to Wilkie, ​ “ They didn’t say, ​ ‘ Hey, if you have concerns about working under the conditions, let us know. You don’t have to work today because it’s hazardous conditions.’” In some cases, climate change leads to degraded conditions not only for workers but for the communities they serve. In late September 2024 , Hurricane Helene hit North Carolina as a Category 4 storm, unleashing flash floods, deluges of rain and overwhelming winds, and killing 107 people in the state, and more than 200 people overall. Front-yard ditches turned into raging rivers, felled trees crushed houses and blocked roads , and mass landslides cut off access to roads. It was the deadliest storm to hit the U.S. mainland since Hurricane Katrina in 2005 . Most of the deaths occurred in areas considered low risk for hurricanes — in some cases hundreds of miles from the coast. Hurricane Helene portends what’s to come as the globe warms: scientists say the deadly rains and wind it brought are likely to grow more frequent and severe due to human-made climate change. Flood-damaged cars sit outside a restaurant in the aftermath of Hurricane Helene on October 1, 2024, in Asheville, N.C. The Category 4 storm killed more than 200 people across the southeastern U.S. and left millions without power. Photo by Melissa Sue Gerrits via Getty Images For workers at Asheville’s Mission Hospital, the climate crisis became a workplace crisis. The main medical facility in the area, it stayed open even as the hospital lost power for several days and had to switch to backup generators, prioritizing critical equipment. The hospital also lost running water for nearly 11 weeks and had to rely on water from trucks and wells. ​ “ Patients had wounds, patients had been exposed to chemicals, fuel and other toxins in the water, patients had been trapped under debris and wreckage, patients were continuing to have strokes and heart attacks, patients with ongoing medical needs, like dialysis, were in bad shape,” according to an October 2024 dispatch from National Nurses United, which represents more than 1 , 500 nurses at the hospital. ​ “ And of course many of the patients were traumatized by the flooding and the horror of watching others die, or their family homes being swept away by the angry, seemingly ever-rising river.” Kerri Wilson is a nurse at the hospital’s cardiology step-down unit, which treats patients with heart conditions. During the first couple of days of the storm, she was off work and at home with her then eight-year-old son. ​ “ When I came back several days after the storm hit, we were still using buckets to flush toilets,” she told me over the phone. ​ “ We had the Army Corps of Engineers who came in and figured out a way to drill a well, so we had tanker trucks with water, so we could have water to flush toilets and wash our hands and things. They would deliver these laundry push carts full of water, and we would take our buckets and fill up. Every two hours we were going through, trying to flush toilets, to keep things as sanitary as we could. Just finding somewhere to wash your hands with soap and water was difficult because the sinks weren’t running.” Storm damage in Asheville, N.C., where the western part of the state was devastated by heavy rains and flooding in the aftermath of Hurricane Helene on September 30, 2024 Photo by Peter Zay/Anadolu via Getty Images Susan Coppin works as a case management nurse at the hospital, covering all of its units. She lives in Hendersonville, 23 miles south of the hospital, and had already taken off of work when the storm hit, which was a good thing, she says, because her house was badly damaged. Around five days after the hurricane hit, she went back to work. ​ “ I was on a heart unit, I remember, and I had heard that they couldn’t even use the toilets. In patients’ rooms, they put bedside commodes and lined them with garbage bags.” “ I opened up our own bathroom, the employees’ bathroom, and instead of the commode, there’s this bedside commode in our employee bathroom,” she says. National Nurses United reported that to decontaminate patients, they filled garbage cans with water and poured it over them. Some urinated in buckets. In the immediate aftermath, they say, the ER ran out of food for the workers, though the community stepped in with meals. At one point, the ER was at double its usual capacity, workers reported . Making the situation more challenging for workers, the hospital became a place of respite for people facing weather-related emergencies. ​ “ People from the community who didn’t actually need hospital care had to utilize the hospital because of their chronic health conditions, maybe they were on oxygen at home, or they had an LVAD — a left ventricular assistive device, which is basically a surgically implanted battery pack that helps their heart pump. They need a power source, so if they’re without power at home, they have no way to recharge their batteries,” Wilson says. ​ “ And that is truly what keeps them alive. So they were having to utilize the hospital just to keep them alive.” “ For a long time, nursing in particular has been looked at as, ​ ‘ They’re this profession of martyrs,’ because most people who go into nursing do want to help people,” Wilson says. ​ “ And for many years, it was seen as a calling more than a profession. … These corporations like HCA try to exploit that when you have these natural disasters and things related to climate change.” HCA Healthcare bought Mission Hospital in 2019 . “ Nurses tend to sacrifice themselves for their patients, and they exploit that in every way they can. That’s why, when we’re short-staffed, our patients suffer, but we don’t get a lunch break because we’re going to work through it to make sure our patients get everything they need. It’s the same kind of situation when you’re working in the aftermath of Helene and you don’t have appropriate nutrition or water. You just keep going. You put yourself last and do what you can for your patients.” In response to a request for comment, a spokesperson for HCA Healthcare said, ​ “ The interviews and accounts referenced in your questions present an incomplete picture of what occurred at Mission Hospital during and after Hurricane Helene.” The spokesperson also sent an HCA press release titled, ​ “ HCA Healthcare to Donate $ 1 Million Towards Hurricane Helene Disaster Relief Efforts in North Carolina.” In his book Or Something Worse: Why We Need to Disrupt the Climate Transitio n, Nicholas Beuret writes that ​ “ climate change is creating an increasingly hostile working environment for millions of people.” The transition society is undergoing as a result of climate change, in fact, ​ “ is the terrain of contemporary class and social conflict, one that is reshaping our everyday lives and radically transforming political strategy and action,” he writes. Regular people tend to grasp that there are profound changes taking place. ​ “ Overall, 65 % of U.S. adults expressed concern that climate change will harm their health,” according to a study published in Health Affairs Scholar in May. Yet, alongside the political failure to curb fossil fuel emissions, the United States does not even offer the most basic worker protections. ​ “ Despite clear scientific evidence and well-established solutions that are low-cost and relatively easy to implement, the United States lacks a comprehensive federal standard to protect workers from heat exposure, leaving worker safety dependent on employer discretion and uneven state policies,” states a May 2026 report by Groundwork Collaborative, Workshop, and the Center for Labor and a Just Economy. The United Electrical, Radio and Machine Workers of America has a Green Locomotives campaign aimed at promoting electric rail and reducing filthy locomotive emissions that hurt workers and communities near railyards. And United Auto Workers Region 6 has been involved in efforts to pass legislation in California aimed at decarbonizing the economy and creating green jobs. UPS drivers in Minnesota face the ongoing challenge of trying to protect the health of workers. Teamsters won some heat protections in their 2023 contract, but when it’s time to negotiate the new deal,” Wilkie says, ​ “ we will have to have language in there to protect our drivers from smoke. I don’t think the wildfire problem is going to go away.” Jacob was just one of several workers who reported troubling health consequences. ​ “ I had UPS drivers calling me throughout the day saying, ​ ‘ I need to come off route because I’m feeling sick,’” Wilkie says. When wildfire smoke meets climbing temperatures, it creates new levels of workplace hazards. “ On this day, even with caution given by the Teamsters union beforehand, UPS, once again, did not uphold that commitment and failed their staff,” Wilkie says. This isn’t just a film review On a Wednesday afternoon, I walked into the Music Box Theater, ready to watch Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey in 70mm. Nolan’s film is carried by an ensemble cast, beautiful cinematography, and of course, the use of IMAX cameras, a first for a blockbuster film. Yet after leaving the showing, Nolan’s interpretation of Homer’s iconic work wasn’t the first thing that came to my mind. Instead, I concluded that the film was a response to the United States’ war and invasion of Iraq. Warning: the following contains spoilers ahead for Nolan’s The Odyssey Baghdad is Troy, Troy is Baghdad Nolan’s film opens on Odysseus’s flashback, like Homer’s epic, to the Trojan War. This opening scene depicts Greek soldiers hiding in the Trojan Horse, a ‘gift’ to the Trojans and their gods. Sinon, played by Elliot Page, sacrifices himself before the Trojans to ensure that the horse makes it through Troy’s walls. At night, after being dragged in, the Greek soldiers descend from the wooden horse and unleash chaos throughout the city of Troy. This is the first direct comparison to the invasion of Iraq, specifically to the fall of Baghdad. The looting and arson besieging Troy is a direct link to how the United States and its allies (most notably the United Kingdom) besieged Baghdad in April of 2003. The Greek soldiers created chaos while panicked Trojan civilians tried to escape, with statues of the gods themselves beheaded or broken during the siege. One might recall how U.S. soldiers helped Iraqi civilians destroy statues of Saddam Hussein. It is clear Nolan is connecting the Greeks’ savagery to the United States and its actions in Iraq. As with the fall of Troy, with the capture of Baghdad, all of Iraq fell under U.S. occupation. The Bush administration’s Trojan horse was the promise that they would bring about democracy for the Iraqi people after Saddam Hussein’s rule. However, after two decades (which is how long it takes for Odysseus to get home), the U.S. occupation decimated the Iraqi population and left puppet leaders throughout the region, not to mention human rights violations committed by the U.S. and its allies (see Abu Ghraib ). The final part of the film, similar to the original epic, reflects on the hero’s journey – specifically Odysseus’s regret for his actions against Troy and the fate of the men under his command. It mirrors that of veterans of the Iraq War who regret their actions in Iraq, how they were just ‘following orders’ (Agemmon and Odysseus play the roles of the Bush administration and the U.S. military). They bear the scars of war both for themselves and the people they have killed or hurt along the way. In the end, just like the fall of Baghdad, the fall of Troy reflects on how decades of bloodshed and the mad decline of human civilization have unfolded amid promises of so-called democracy. “…To burn the walls of Troy was to burn the world entirely, including his home…” — Odysseus , disguised as a beggar, talking to Penelope ( The Odyssey , 2026) Trump’s “Golden Age” of America While this film is more or less a direct analog to the Iraq War, Nolan takes a slight jab at the current Trump regime near the end. Being Pro-Working Class Isn’t Enough: We Need Victories People Can Feel

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Tag: Labor ／ 著者: 不明 ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-13T17:20:21+00:00

What a bizarro time we now live in. Coming off defiant elections in November 2025 and early 2026, Democrats of all stripes seem to be singing the same tune in 2026. Neoliberal scions Rahm Emanuel and James Carville , socialist NYC mayor Zohran Mamdani, DNC Chair Kenneth Martin , labor leader UAW President Shawn Fain, centrist VA governor Abigail Spanberger , and Bernie Sanders are seemingly aligned: we need to be the party of working people again. To win back the working class voters that the Democratic Party has abandoned, we need to focus relentlessly on affordability and cost of living for working people, orienting ourselves towards economic populism in the process. It’s a long-overdue course correction, both morally and tactically. While I know we’re still quite a ways away from everyone in the Democratic Party singing Solidarity Forever together, this is a real victory for us on the Left. After decades of elections that shouldn’t have been lost, there’s finally something nearing a consensus amongst Democrats. For this emerging orientation towards economic populism to represent a real break from the past and to result in sustained political power for the Left, it must advance an ambition that can sincerely match the scale of the crises we’re in. That means our movements have to prioritize demanding economic results, not economic processes. We need to focus on ends, not means. Yet the economic populism I think we’re settling into —and settling for— across the different factions of the Left is a means-focused economic populism, or what I refer to as “Process Populism.” Process Populism merely prioritizes demands that are processes of working-class politics. Three most emergent demands on the Left now found on countless policy platforms that best exemplify Process Populism are (1) supporting unionization, (2) taxing the rich, and (3) electing working-class candidates. What’s odd about us championing and emphasizing process is that process is one of the powerful’s oldest and easiest tools for defusing pressure. When workers demand change, bosses respond with task forces and listening sessions. When students organize, a university president proposes committees and surveys instead of fixing the actual problem. Delay via process is a part of the toolkit to nullify organizing, even when it’s well-intended. As organizers, that’s why we have to apply the same clarity to our politics that we demand from those in power: tools and processes are not material outcomes. Working people are not just out of time and money, they’re out of patience. They deserve to know how their lives will materially and specifically change. Process Populism does not offer that. Process Populism is no doubt an improvement over the neoliberal drift that defined the party for decades, but it stops halfway. One reason Process Populist policies are emergent is because they do poll well: only a small minority doesn’t want higher taxes on billionaires or doesn’t want stronger unions. And precisely because they poll pretty well, even left-leaning pundits and strategists often center them in our collective vision. Yet, there’s a significant difference between popular support and popular will . Popular support reflects what people agree with; popular will reflects what they’re ready to fight for. Process Populism produces the former, not the latter. It may win consensus, but it doesn’t build power nor demand urgency. And urgency is both a moral and strategic imperative. Process Populism isn’t likely to deliver material gains to the working class fast enough for those workers to want to keep its proponents in office. Globally and domestically, there is anti-incumbent bias for elected officials; the signal for us to draw is there’s a healthy chunk of voters who are willing to vote for candidates across a range of ideologies, but they are focused on results. The only way to break out of this “pendulum theory” of politics is to deliver a quickly better life for working families in concrete, economic ways. And if elected officials and movements can’t deliver within the first two to three years of their term? They’ll be voted out. Real economic populism—what I call “Material Populism”—starts where this version ends. Material Populism also engages with a politics of specificity : it names the specific outcomes we’re fighting for: shorter workdays, rent freezes, higher wages, better care, etc. The affirmative economic specificity encouraged by Material Populism can build popular will. Mamdani’s campaign offered one such model of that kind of affirmative materialism. His message focused on what working people could picture immediately: universal childcare, fast and free buses, and rent freezes. Though his campaign had concrete, thoughtful answers for how they were going to achieve those priorities, the process was not the campaign’s calling card. A campaign that emphasized process over results—a campaign that led with raising taxes on the rich or a campaign that focused on appointing the right people to the Rent Guidelines Board—would not have unleashed the same kind of inspiration and sense of possibility. These examples aren’t limited to socialist electoral campaigns: the most notable policy idea emerging out of the New Jersey Governor’s race was Governor-elect Sherill’s commitment to freezing energy rates for at least a year. You see a similar dynamic in Shawn Fain’s “core four” at the UAW : wages, healthcare, retirement, and control over time. Fain knows that workers don’t rally in the political nor bargaining arena around procedures, but that they’ll rally hard around material outcomes they can feel. The beauty of Material Populism is that it doesn’t require uniformity of policy. In one state, the fight might be for free public transit; in another, for debt relief, universal childcare, or a shorter workweek. The specifics can differ from race to race, movement to movement. What matters is that each is grounded in the same mission: to make life materially better for working people in quick, visible, and non-marginal ways. When movements and politicians focus on material ends during campaigning, they also bring clarity and accountability to the task of governing. It keeps us from treating process as a solution and ensures that once in power, politicians are guided by the result we were promised and that our movements pushed for, not the mechanism they used to get there. Being able to distinguish between Process Populism and Material Populism must become a core part of how we analyze and prioritize demands on the Left and in our electoral, labor, and issue-based movements. To more deeply understand the limitations of the emerging consensus, I look at the three key pillars of today’s process populism and why each, even when taken together, remains insufficient: pro-unionism (how workers conceivably gain power), taxing the rich (how elites potentially lose power), and working-class candidates (who we’d want to wield power). All are necessary for shifting power. Examining each, however, also shows how a process-obsessed politics doesn’t unleash nor match the urgency for the material things working people deserve. Of the populist pillars, being “pro-union” has become the easiest and safest, helping stitch an economic coalition from the Left to the center-left. For a clear example, look at the memo from DNC Chair Ken Martin . It, perhaps surprisingly, nailed the diagnosis: Democrats lost in 2024 because the working class abandoned them after years of feeling abandoned themselves. But when it came to solutions, the memo fell back on the same familiar playbook: defend workers’ rights, protect existing benefits, and support unions. Supporting unions and unionization were the extent of the offensive ambition; that’s a defensive posture, not a real vision On the Left, we often treat unionizing as an end in and of itself. The theory goes that unionizing is an important outcome because unions let people experience their workplaces more democratically. But that’s a politics that’s focused more on form than substance. Though unions remain the most powerful vehicle workers have to win dignity and democracy at their workplace, the reality is nothing materially changes simply from being part of a union. I love unions—I work as a union contract negotiator! But unionism that pretends a union automatically guarantees material gains—or that the moral and spiritual value of solidarity can substitute for them—is not an honest unionism. It risks disaffecting the very same workers it hoped to galvanize. The reality is that unionizing, having a strong union, and winning strong contracts take significant time, resources, and courage. And that’s due to both employer-friendly American labor law and the inherent challenges of collective action. Employers can fight tooth and nail to stop unionizing: captive-audience meetings, retaliation, delay tactics, firings, etc. Even when workers win a union election, the road to a first contract can be terribly long. The average length of a first contract negotiation is 465 days, but bosses can drag out contract negotiations for much longer too. The effort to unionize Starbucks has been one of labor’s most visible, high-profile fights in the last few years. While unionization has been broadly successful, actually winning a contract to secure improved working conditions has proved a tougher task. Starbucks workers unionized in 2021, but as of Summer 2026, there is still no collective bargaining agreement . That’s not because workers nor unions have failed. They’ve been sufficiently militant and organized to authorize a strike across 500+ stores , and the sustained pressure of unionization has certainly improved working conditions in numerous ways. But the safety and promise of a collective bargaining agreement which guarantees those material conditions has thus far remained out of reach. And even when workers win a contract, maintaining a strong union that can continue winning strong successor contracts remains a challenge. Changing that system by making it easier to unionize or bargain a first contract (as the Faster Labor Contracts Act recently passed in the House does) is good policy, but we saw through the shortcomings of the pro-worker Biden administration why it may not make for savvy politics. The main way the Biden administration signaled its pro-worker commitment was to be, relative to other presidencies, unabashedly pro-unionization. Biden deserves credit for NLRB reforms, for championing the PRO Act (a bill exclusively focused on accelerating unionization and building union power), and for standing with workers on picket lines. But friendlier processes and moral support didn’t automatically translate into faster or larger gains for workers. A working class that’s living paycheck to paycheck needs solutions faster than unionizing alone can deliver—and solutions more transformative than even many well-intentioned employers or arbitrators (if the Faster Labor Contracts Act were to pass) could ever grant. It’s no surprise that even as union drives surged during the Biden years (“ Hot Labor Summer ” now feels like a distant memory from a bygone era), (1) union density went down and (2) working-class people kept drifting away from the Democratic Party and from the labor movement—perhaps justifiably so given the lack of immediate change in their lives. Even if union density went up under the Biden-style labor reforms and every active organizing campaign succeeded, it could take decades for density to rebound to the post-New Deal era level where unions held economy-wide power. That’s why a pro-union stance, while essential, cannot be the centerpiece of an economically populist agenda. It’s too slow to meet the scale of the crisis, and voters intuit that. It’s why labor—despite support from every notable union in the country—was ultimately unable to build popular will around the PRO Act, their signature legislation during the Biden administration. For all these reasons, we should also be deeply dissatisfied and, frankly, upset with politicians who limit their working-class advocacy to a simple pro-unionism. 70% of Americans support unions. Politicians being simply “pro-union” is lazy politics because it outsources what should be a politician’s political fights in legislatures—wages, health care, retirement, and more time off the clock—to workplace fights led by overworked workers. A politics for working people has to aim, at minimum, to put increased pressure on legislators to deliver specific and material gains for workers via political avenues. Given how long unionization and collective bargaining can take and how hungry the public is for real change in their paychecks, it may even be easier to win bold, universal improvements in the political arena than at the bargaining table. We need leaders willing to fight for those things themselves, not just to say they stand with those who do fight. If pro-unionism is one side of the Process Populism coin, “tax the rich” is the other. While unions build power for working people, taxing the rich reduces elites’ power. It polls well, and it names a villain. It’s a welcome development for more than a small minority of politicians and unions to name who they’re fighting (the billionaires and the oligarchs), but they have to go one step further and name what they’re fighting for . The wealthy should be taxed more—substantially more than they currently pay. (And after news of Elon becoming the first trillionaire , we should also really consider strict legal limits on CEO wealth accumulation via both salaries and stocks.) But “tax the rich” shouldn’t be the centerpiece of an agenda even if it should be the engine for one. A billionaire’s higher tax bill alone doesn’t make childcare cheaper, shorten anyone’s workweek, or lower rent. There must be a clear line between new revenue and new results. In fact, taxing the rich without clearly naming what that money will fund risks unintentionally mimicking the perverse logic of trickle-down economics. The right promises that tax cuts for the wealthy would eventually benefit everyone else. The Left shouldn’t make the mirror-image mistake of assuming that taxing the wealthy will automatically improve ordinary people’s lives. Just as trickle-down economics failed because it never delivered material gains for the working class, a tax-the-rich agenda without clear outcomes risks the same disappointment. Failing to fill that missing connection could present a political problem. Paradoxically, taxing the rich is popular at the same time that cutting government waste and fraud is popular. Inoculating the public against the claims that taxing the rich will fuel more government waste thus requires coupling taxes with an affirmative economic vision. Naming what the tax revenue will fund will make the tax policy more popular. This is because people are more likely to support taxes when they can see what that revenue is buying: free childcare, cheaper housing, debt relief, or time back in their lives. Clarity about ends can build consensus around the means. Some campaigns and movements are already getting this right. The New York DSA’s “Tax the Rich” campaign explicitly connects their tax campaign to clear, material outcomes: universal childcare, emergency rental assistance, and mass public transit. It showed what a Material Populism’s embrace of tax the rich looks like: specific, visual, and immediate. Contrast that with the labor movement’s more generic “ Workers Over Billionaires ” campaign, which channels righteous anger at the rich and at corporations, but stops short of offering an affirmative economic vision. Both campaigns rightfully pit workers against elites, but only one conveys to working people in clear terms how their life will actually change if they join the fight. If taxing the rich names a villain, electing working-class candidates names a hero. It’s the final notable way today’s Process Populism confuses means for ends. It is the most narratively satisfying and culturally memetic form of contemporary populism: representation constituting substantive change. Absent explicit economic commitments however, I’ve found that this emphasis risks collapsing class politics into a type of identity politics. In that framework, class becomes a marker of authenticity rather than a program of redistribution. A candidate’s biography substitutes for their platform; proximity to hardship stands in for a plan to end it. The moral authority derived from working-class identity is treated as self-justifying. This is the aestheticization of class—a politics of identity without a clear politics of delivery. We’re already seeing this pattern emerge over the last few years. Figures like Sen. John Fetterman and Rep. Marie Gluesenkamp Pérez represent a growing cohort of Democrats who lean into the aesthetics of the working-class—small-business ownership, rural rootedness, cultural moderation—while offering little in the way of transformative economic policy. Even a candidate like Graham Platner—who, unlike Fetterman or Gluesenkamp Pérez, actually holds genuinely transformative economic positions—has become known to almost all voters primarily through his biography (both the compelling and controversial parts) rather than through the substance of what he’d do on the economy if he’s in office. The allure of the “working class candidate” has meant that even among candidates who aren’t working class, a working-class aesthetic has emerged: the Carhartt jacket , the pickup truck , the f-bomb. Putting aside the discussion that working class voters are likely to see through these transparent attempts to mimic their culture from candidates who aren’t working class, it’s clear how focusing on class aesthetics can easily shift from meaningful solidarity signalling to an exercise in brand cultivation. As the party (hopefully) becomes increasingly economically populist, this phenomenon of working class politicians with vacuous politics could proliferate. Without affirmative commitments to major economic outcomes, however, this genre of populism risks substituting affect for agenda. Counterintuitively, the two most notable figures at winning working-class support in the 2025 and 2024 election cycles lack any working-class identity at all and don’t hide that. Zohran Mamdani and Donald Trump could not be more different ideologically and both hail from wealthy enclaves in New York, but both speak in the language of ends. They promise visible, near-term, specific outcomes that people can imagine in their own lives. They illustrate that building inter-class coalitions and solidarity is not solely determined by background but by what a candidate persuades people they will do with power. Representation matters. It must, however, be instrumental, not expressive. The presence of working-class candidates in public office is meaningful only insofar as it expands the horizon of economic possibility for working class people. Or else class identity, untethered from affirmative material commitments, risks becoming another form of identity politics. If we’ve learned anything from the last two decades’ misplaced overemphasis on representational politics, it’s that a genuinely working-class politic must demand much more than who holds power. The Democratic Party’s turn toward economic populism is welcome, and it didn’t happen on its own. Nor did it happen easily. Organizers, unions, and activists fought for decades to make pro-worker politics common sense again, and significant electoral losses in 2016 and 2024 expedited the process of Democratic self-reflection. But if we want to win elections and we want to win them for more than a term or two, we must promise outcomes to working people—and then actually deliver them within one term. That’s how the majorities of the New Deal era that persisted for decades were built: through results working people could feel. Voters are impatient and rightfully so. They want leaders with real answers, not just the right mechanisms. Pro-unionism, taxing the rich, and working-class candidates are all necessary components to a working class politic, but they should not be the ultimate agenda. Without a clear economic vision for specific, fast, tangible gains—higher wages, shorter hours, cheaper care, more time—leftist populism risks failing as a governing project. This conversation couldn’t come at a more critical moment. The 2026 midterms, the organizing push toward a 2028 general strike, and the 2028 general election will test whether the Left can build powerful campaigns that can deliver on their promise. Those campaigns’ successes won’t be measured by pro-worker rhetoric or process, but by results that make ordinary life tangibly easier, freer, and more equal. That’s what it means to build power and govern with the working class, and that’s the only kind of politics worth building. Co-published with The Forge .

Housing Inequality Is a Central Plank of Modern Capitalism

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Adam Peggs ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-13T17:14:50+00:00

In recent years, renters’ unions have played an increasingly prominent role in a number of countries , with tenants’ groups exerting influence on national discussion and to some extent on the policies of governments and political parties. Spain, Portugal, Ireland, and Scotland have all implemented some form of rent control since 2021. Against that backdrop, several writers have sought to combine the questions of political strategy raised by tenant struggles with the political economy of housing. Michael Byrne’s book , Beyond Generation Rent , is part of this trend, examining inequality, financialization, and evictions. It’s a welcome and worthwhile contribution that brings a social critique of landlordism into contemporary academic economics. It also fleshes out a much-needed theory of how and why the private rental sector is inherently organized around the pursuit of economic rents, profiting from control of a scarce asset, and it brings the economic debate into the same discussion as the work of renters’ unions and their prospects. Byrne pulls no punches as he discusses the “impunity” of landlords in their relationship with tenants and the chronic insecurity this produces for the latter. He correctly identifies landlordism as a social relationship that depends on a power asymmetry between the asset owner and the resident. This ground has been well covered in recent years, particularly in Nick Bano’s Against Landlords , but by bringing it closer to the political economy debate, Byrne helps us grasp how the private rental sector operates and its role within the wider economic system. A Theory of Rent Since the classical economists of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, there has been a general understanding of landlordism as a form of economic rent. This is the sort of activity that most economic thinkers oppose, although many contemporary mainstream economists would draw a sharp distinction between the investment of landlords in property development or improvement and the passive income that arises merely from owning land or a home. In spite of this broad consensus, there are few explicit frameworks that show how and why the rent paid to landlords constitutes an economic rent. Byrne offers his theory of “home rent” as the value appropriated by landlords, determined by the local area in which the home is located and its value as someone’s home (particular to the sitting tenant). This allows landlords to profit from ownership of housing, including the appropriation of the value the tenant generates by making the place feel like home. Byrne does not attempt to draw conclusions around what home rent represents for the wider political economy of the capitalist system. That would help us answer the question of how important rent is to the functioning of contemporary capitalism and in what ways. However, he does touch on the work of others, including Javier Moreno Zacarés, whose analysis suggests that real estate capital ends up undermining capital accumulation in general by pushing up rents and asset prices. In my view, rent is crucial to the wider economic system. I have argued elsewhere that “real estate” has formed an increasingly central component of neoliberalism, as a source of stable returns in a financialized system. Nonetheless, Byrne’s decision to leave this question open within his own analysis is clear and well explained. Generation or Class? The book’s title is partly a call for analysis that moves beyond the clichés that mar much writing on the crisis, and partly one of its own central claims. According to Byrne, the housing crisis is not so much a divide between affluent, home-owning boomers and asset-poor younger generations as it is one between renters and property owners (especially multiple property owners) of all ages. This is broadly right, although sometimes Byrne (and others) can overstate the limits of the “generation rent” perspective. In most of the Global North (the Global South is a more varied and complex story) postwar capitalism did lead to a massive “cohort effect,” when people born at a certain time had much better prospects of homeownership than subsequent generations. This doesn’t mean there are no working-class baby boomers — there are many, of course — or that no one in the millennial or Zoomer generations are middle class. But in terms of housing prospects at least, there are profound differences between these generations in most cases. In political discourse across much of Europe and the United States, this has led to far too much emphasis on age group as a dividing line, rather than class and tenure. The most useful position is likely to be one that holds a few truths simultaneously. While questions of class and tenure are critical, postwar welfare state capitalism did produce vastly different housing economies than the neoliberal form that succeeded it. Even so, an excessive focus on age as a determining factor is a barrier to class solidarity. Class vs. Tenure Of course, class isn’t the same as tenure status. People’s housing situation may be critical to their standard of living, not to mention their relationship to place. But it says nothing directly about their relationship to work. Although homeownership does offer a degree of meaningful security, owning a home only amounts to owning capital when it is put to use in producing rent or sold for capital gains (of course, it still represents wealth regardless of what you do with it). Homeownership is crucial in modern capitalism. On the one hand, the scale of residential accumulation makes it ever harder to attain, as housing is concentrated in the hands of ever fewer actors. On the other, it still provides just enough people with a material stake in capitalism to help maintain consent for the current system. Byrne refers to the term “late homeownership societies,” which describes a transition from the mass homeownership of the last century to a much more precarious settlement in which more and more groups are locked out. As Byrne observes, the internationalization of landlordism makes the sector increasingly sensitive to faraway decisions. When the European Central Bank or the Federal Reserve hike interest rates, residents all over the world are likely to be affected. Mobilizing Tenants Byrne rightly notes that we need further research on the build-to-rent sector: purpose-built housing from corporate landlords. In some countries, including the UK, this sector appears to be at a crossroads, facing possible limits on its spectacular growth to date in a period of stagnating house-building. Some key insights have already emerged from existing research, including from Common Wealth . In the UK at least, build-to-rent homes skew toward comparatively affluent tenants, with a bias toward either higher-end housing or simply very high rents. Either way, this suggests that the sector’s continued growth as a share of the market spells bad news for accessibility and affordability. There are flaws and blind spots in some parts of the book. An otherwise valuable analysis of homeownership and housing inequality, particularly in the Western anglophone countries, does not take sufficient account of the role of social housing, which sits outside the system of housing wealth. But social housing is a critical factor here. In a system of deep housing wealth inequality, with some owning vast sums of housing wealth while others own nothing, the prospects and security of social housing tenants differ greatly from those of private renters who are locked out of ownership. However, the final sections of Beyond Generation Rent offer a lot of worthwhile analysis. Byrne summarizes the achievements of renters’ unions in Scotland, England, and Ireland, as well as European cities like Madrid, Barcelona, and Berlin. In Scotland, for example, no-fault evictions were effectively banned almost a decade ago, rents were frozen after the pandemic, and long-term rent controls have since been introduced. In England, the campaigning efforts of unions have eventually secured similar gains on no-fault evictions alongside a range of other improvements to rights and a ban on “bidding wars,” though rent hikes remain uncontrolled (leaving the UK an outlier compared to its neighbors). Most interesting is the degree of success arising from Berlin’s “Expropriate Deutsche Wohnen & Co.” (DWE) campaign. This led to a successful referendum vote for socializing the homes owned by corporate landlords, many of which were former public homes. Byrne describes this as proposing “one of, if not the most, radical forms of housing redistribution ever undertaken in a capitalist society,” potentially outstripping postwar housing requisitions in war-torn Europe or the state-backed Dutch squatting policy in the 1970s. However, the referendum vote has not yet been implemented, and a second binding referendum is now expected to be held in order to force the issue. In the meantime, a city government commission has justifiably noted that compensation to the landlords can be given at below market rates. The proposed structure for DWE would combine centralization with democratic, bottom-up elements, providing benefits of scale along with accountability and distribution of power. With nonresidents able to join and participate, the campaign’s configuration also has the potential to sweep more supporters into the movement and fuel more ambitious demands. If tenant organizing can radically expand the horizon, this begins to offer an answer to the question of whether real leverage can be built and in what ways. Byrne’s claim that the rise of corporate landlords offers an easier base for tenants to organize around, as they face a smaller and more recognizable adversary, is convincing (renters’ movements in some countries and regions still face a more diffuse market of landlords, including Britain). His program also points in at least roughly the right direction, toward greater security and affordability for tenants but, more importantly, also toward a significant expansion of nonmarket housing, restrictions on private landlordism, and direct redistribution. Here we might add the importance of asserting democratic control over development and urban planning, which is implicit in at least some forms of socializing the rental sector. But this non-prescriptive agenda is a decent starting point.

The Jeju Massacre Was a Hidden Crime of America’s Cold War

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Kap Seol ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-13T15:14:04+00:00

South Koreans tend to memorialize the tragedies of their modern history by date. The Korean War is 6.25 for June 25, 1950, the day it broke out; the Gwangju Uprising is 5.18, for May 18, 1980, when a ten-day popular revolt began before the military drowned it in blood. The same kind of shorthand exists elsewhere in East Asia — 6.4 for the Tiananmen massacre of 1989 and 3.11 for the Great East Japan Earthquake of 2011 — but nowhere is it as deeply embedded in collective memory as in South Korea. Each pair of digits separated by a dot compresses, and even conceals, the memory of mass death and the story behind it. About three million civilians were killed in the war South Koreans call 6.25. The number of civilians felled by government bullets in Gwangju or at Tiananmen remains contested, while the full nuclear toll of Fukushima may not be known for many more decades. To many South Koreans, 4.3 is another such numerical memory, the oldest of them all, predating and outlasting 6.25 and the founding of the republic itself. It refers to April 3, 1948, the moment when protests on Jeju Island prompted a counterinsurgency campaign of extraordinary brutality and swiftness. Over the following six years, 25,000 to 30,000 civilians were killed — one islander in every ten. South Korea’s largest and most gorgeous island was, in the exact and original sense of the word, decimated. Hallan For more than four decades of dictatorship, from the 1950s to the late 1980s, the massacre remained virtually unknown on mainland South Korea. Every year, a fresh army of newlyweds and tourists landed at Jeju’s airport, built atop a mass grave of victims, to enjoy a scenic subtropical island where winter snow still shrouds palm trees. Only after democracy was restored in the 1990s were the mass graves excavated and an official inquiry launched. The 4.3 massacre came to the attention of a wider English-speaking readership in early 2025 when Han Kang’s We Do Not Part appeared in English on the heels of her Nobel Prize in Literature. Han is a chronicler of pain, capable of casting light on the universal dimensions of historically rooted personal traumas through the suffering of individual protagonists. Almost invariably, her work depicts individual anguish infused with the repercussions of historical violence, from the 4.3 massacre in We Do Not Part and the Gwangju Uprising in Human Acts to the more oblique echoes of the Vietnam War in The Vegetarian . Hallan , a Korean independent film that had its US premiere at the New York Asian Film Festival last month, is a cinematic inversion of We Do Not Part . Han’s novel keeps the massacre at an archival distance through a survivor’s lifetime of documents and newspaper clippings, uncovered by the next generation. The film stands inside the event itself, with a mother and her six-year-old daughter fleeing the killings. The mother of Han’s protagonist chronicled what she had lived through in the hope it would someday come to light. The film shows what there was to remember, before the memory had found a home. Set on Jeju in 1948, the second year of the six-year massacre, barely three years after liberation from Japanese colonial rule, Hallan confers moral authority on neither armed side: not the left-wing guerrillas who fled into the fastness of Mountain Halla and not the Korean constabulary that hunted them, with the benefit of US training and advice, and outgunned and outnumbered them badly. Like many other islanders, protagonist A-jin and her daughter Haeseng were caught in the middle of spontaneous protests over economic hardship tinged with aspirations for national sovereignty that were met with deadly suppression before evolving into a form of internecine violence and ultimately a massacre. As the situation evolved, it became increasingly detached from the populace and increasingly deadly to it. General Strike In August 1945, Koreans received their liberation and their partition in the same month. They had longed for an independent, sovereign state. Instead, the United States and the Soviet Union carved the peninsula in two as they divided the postwar world. Their respective militaries ruled what would become North and South Korea for three years before installing their respective protégés in power: in the North, Kim Il-Sung, an anti-Japanese guerilla leader who sat out World War II after fleeing to Soviet Russia from the Manchurian theater; in the South, Syngman Rhee, a longtime independence campaigner and a Presbyterian who had taken refuge in the United States. In the South, the lives of working people deteriorated in many respects, in some cases becoming even harder than in colonial times. The US military government’s mismanagement of the economy sent rice prices soaring while the return of tens of thousands of Koreans from overseas flooded an already fragile labor market. The People’s Committees, an autonomous body that had sprung up across the country to fill the administrative vacuum left by the Japanese withdrawal, came into conflict with the US military government. Instead of dismantling the colonial state, the occupation authorities largely repurposed its administrative apparatus, staffing it with the same Korean bureaucrats and police officers who had served under Japanese rule. On Jeju, these grievances erupted into mass protests on March 1, 1947, the anniversary of the nationwide campaign against Japanese rule in 1919. During the demonstration, a mounted police officer rode over a child, further inflaming the protestors. Police then opened fire on the crowd, killing six civilians. Less than two weeks later, on March 10, much of the island ground to a halt as workers launched a general strike, joined even by local cops and low-ranking civil servants. The US military government swiftly crushed the strike, bringing in officials from the mainland to replace much of the island’s bureaucracy and deploying additional constabulary and police troops. It also relied on far-right youth organizations from the mainland to terrorize the island’s inhabitants. Taking Up Arms By April 1948, the island’s communists came to believe the time was ripe for an armed uprising. It may have been a premature decision. The general strike had ended in defeat, and much of their underground network was destroyed in the process. However, they believed conditions both on Jeju and across the peninsula had grown dire enough to justify taking up arms. Far-right reactionaries were terrorizing the island, and an election scheduled for the next month threatened to establish a separate South Korean state, turning what had been promised as only a temporary partition into a permanent one. On April 3, 1948, about 350 communist guerillas rose up, raiding twelve police stations and the barracks of far-right groups from the mainland. The bloody fight escalated into a vicious cycle of brutal retaliation, with violence increasingly taking the form of a personal vendetta. Although state forces and far-right militias carried out the overwhelming majority of the violence, left-wing guerrillas also committed atrocities, including the killing of the families of police officers and soldiers, whose relatives and comrades often responded with retaliatory attacks against guerilla families. Yet the insurgency could have ended at an early stage. During May, about ten days ahead of the national elections, the US military government and the guerilla group began dialogue over a possible ceasefire. However, far-right terrorists torched a village, blaming guerillas for the fire, to break up the negotiations. In response, the US military government carried out a large-scale sweep against the insurgents, who retaliated in kind. Through a combination of coercion and persuasion, guerillas drove thousands of villagers from their villages deep into Mount Halla, successfully disrupting the election on Jeju. Draining the Sea The standoff might still have been defused if the US military government had not singled out Jeju as a “red island” and treated much of its population as communists or communist sympathizers. In October 1948, seeking to crush already weakened guerilla units, the US military ordered that anyone found outside a five-kilometer coastal zone be treated as a guerilla, regardless of age or sex. Many, including children and the elderly, were summarily executed, while others were rounded up and brought before military tribunals that offered little semblance of due process. This became one of the earliest scorched-earth operations under the emerging rationale of the Truman doctrine of 1947, whose declared aim was to contain Soviet communist expansion. Harry Truman’s generals in South Korea and their Korean junior associates appeared to embrace their own interpretation of Mao Zedong’s famous metaphor on guerilla warfare: “The guerilla must move amongst the people as fish swim in the sea.” To annihilate a guerrilla force that never numbered more than three hundred fighters at its peak, the US military government and the subsequent South Korean government deployed a motley force of five thousand soldiers, police officers, and far-right paramilitaries, ultimately wiping out an estimated 10 percent of Jeju’s population — as if they were draining the sea to catch a few fish. In Hallan , A-jin and Haeseng find themselves caught at the edge of the five-kilometer coastal zone. A-jin’s mother-in-law has been executed with other elderly villagers by soldiers motivated by their assigned kill quotas. Later, while searching for Haeseng after the death of her grandmother, A-jin encounters a band of guerillas who hide the truth about her husband’s death: they had summarily executed him for abandoning their hideout. The idea of “a better world” as preached by the partisans no longer holds any appeal for A-jin. Her vision of “a better world” has become far more modest yet no less unattainable: a world where her daughter can learn to read and write so that one day she may record, in her words, what they endured in 1948. In her feature debut, director and screenwriter Ha Myung-mi has weaved sadness, desperation, tension, and courage into the stunning late-summer beauty of Jeju. Kim Hyang-gi, best known internationally for her role in the cult favorite Along With Gods series, plays the young mother with a blend of strength and vulnerability. The two protagonists are composite characters drawn from 4.3 and Women , a five-volume collection of interviews of female survivors published by a Jeju-based nonprofit research institute, according to director Ha. English-speaking viewers with no knowledge of Korean are not the only ones who have to rely on subtitles: Hallan is so rich in the Jeju vernacular that even native Korean speakers required subtitles when it was premiered in Seoul last year. To Name 4.3 Jeju activists and scholars have spent more than five decades campaigning to bring the truth of 4.3 to light. One of their great accomplishments has been to unite islanders around the memory of atrocities instead of dividing them over who was to blame. Bereaved families of partisans and local police and soldiers have since reconciled, moving beyond the past in which they held one another responsible for the deaths of their next of kin. Together they now pay their respects at each other’s family graves each year — a meaningful ritual in South Korea, where regular visits to ancestral graves remain an important part of remembrance. Nevertheless, Jeju islanders still call it simply 4.3. Any available label falls short of defining what happened. It was not an “incident,” a word too small to capture the scale of the killings. It was not an “uprising” because only a fraction of population directly took part. Nor was it a conventional “insurgency” given the movement’s limited organization. Yet even the term “massacre” cannot fully capture what really happened. From 4.3 to the Korean War, much of the first postwar decade was marked by some of the bloodiest and most turbulent years in the country’s modern history — years that many Koreans remember through a series of dates. What remains clearest to many of them is that the violence could have been far less devastating and far less prolonged had Korea’s future been left fully in Korean hands, without the rampant US intervention that began on Jeju.

Gen Z Isn’t Apolitical. It’s Lost Faith in Capitalism.

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Dora Mengüç ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-13T13:41:25+00:00

Generation Z is often described as distracted, cynical, politically inconsistent, or allergic to institutions. But this description misses the deeper rupture. What distinguishes this generation is not a lack of politics but the collapse of faith in the institutions and rules that once gave politics, work, and adulthood a recognizable structure. For decades, capitalism promised young people a sequence: education would lead to work; work would lead to stability; stability would lead to a home, a family, and a life with some measure of dignity. This promise was never distributed equally. For many, it was always fragile. But for Generation Z, it has now become almost impossible to believe in. Across very different societies, young people are entering adulthood through the same narrow door. Behind that door lie precarious jobs, unstable housing, student and consumer debt, algorithmically managed labor markets, climate anxiety, and political institutions that appear either too weak or too captured to change anything meaningful. This generation does not trust parties, leaders, or institutions. Yet it also votes, boycotts, protests, organizes online, quits jobs, and channels its anger through digital platforms. Most importantly, it can turn everyday life into a political battleground. Its politics is fragmented, fast moving, leaderless, and often reabsorbed by the very markets it rejects. But it is politics nonetheless. In interviews conducted for this article, Guy Standing, Emre Erdoğan, Christian Fuchs, and Anu Muhammad described different parts of the same crisis: the making of a generation with no secure occupational future, no stable social contract, and no convincing reason to believe that capitalism can still deliver on its promises. A nineteen-year-old university student living in Istanbul puts it more bluntly: “I don’t have an ideology. I just have a world order that I hate.” That sentence captures something larger than youthful anger. What we are witnessing is a generational rupture. Generation Z has not withdrawn from politics. It is simply disillusioned with the old language and model of politics. And it has grown up in a world where old political vocabularies can no longer make sense of the insecurity it faces. Generation Z does not exist outside class relations. Changes in capitalism have reshaped the class structure and the conditions of work, and Gen Z has come of age within these new relations. What we describe as “Gen Z politics” should not be understood as the politics of a homogeneous generation but as a generational expression of a broader transformation in class relations. A Generation Without an Occupational Future The British economist and labor theorist Guy Standing has spent years describing the rise of a new class: the “precariat.” This is not simply a group of people with bad jobs. It is a class shaped by insecurity itself: unstable employment, irregular income, debt, the absence of occupational identity, and the constant requirement to retrain, rebrand, and remain available for work that may never become secure. Standing understands the contemporary economy as “a globalized rentier age”: “We are living in the globalized rentier age of capitalism; income, wealth, and power are increasingly flowing to those who own wealth and to elites who own the main forms of property,” he says. “The vast majority of young adults will face unstable and insecure jobs. They will live with low and uncertain wages and will be in almost constant debt.” For Standing, the issue is not merely that young people have worse labor conditions than previous generations. The issue is that each new generation is being drawn more deeply into the precariat than the one before it. As he puts it, “Each new generation is more involved in this class than the previous one.” For standing, the work possibilities for young people are so bleak that they have “no occupational future.” “Individuals in the precariat are also forced to constantly search for jobs, retrain themselves, and find new sources of income,” Standing says. “Most of these activities are unpaid and invisible. Therefore, people cannot develop an occupational identity and cannot position themselves within a stable career line.” This is the collapse of one of capitalism’s classic promises. The old story about work in liberal democracies was that it would give young people not only wages but direction, belonging, and a future. In the new labor market, work often gives them only exhaustion, uncertainty, and debt. Standing also emphasizes that this condition produces bodily and psychological consequences. Chronic uncertainty does not remain outside the body. It turns into stress, illness, and fear. “Especially young people, but everyone in the precariat generally, has to cope with chronic uncertainty,” he says. “This means facing ‘unknown unknowns’; that is, there is neither robustness to withstand shocks nor resilience to recover. The individual consequences of this are debt, stress, and increasing illnesses.” This is not a temporary labor market problem. It is a political condition. When young people can no longer imagine a stable future through work, they also begin to lose faith in institutions that still speak as if such a future exists. The Rules No Longer Work Economic insecurity alone does not explain Generation Z’s politics. What makes the present rupture deeper is the collapse of the belief that society has clear and legitimate rules. Political scientist Emre Erdoğan, whose research focuses on social trust, youth, and political behavior, describes this as a broader crisis of the postwar social order. Erdoğan argues that young people’s insecurity reflects the breakdown of the institutions that once gave capitalism legitimacy. “The post-1945 consensus has ended,” Erdoğan says. “The United Nations order has ended. The system established by the World Bank, IMF, and World Trade Organization has collapsed. The welfare state has collapsed. What we call the safety net no longer exists. When you fall, who will hold you? The answer is: family, relatives, fellow countrymen. Not the state.” Standing’s analyses explain the economic condition, and Erdoğan explains why it becomes political. Young people are not only struggling to find work. They are also struggling to understand which rules they are supposed to follow. “You can no longer say to young people, ‘If you work hard, you will succeed,’” Erdoğan says. “We do not know how to succeed. People increasingly do not believe in the rules. There is no consensus on what is required to be successful in this society. Young people struggle not only to find jobs but to understand what rules they should follow. This uncertainty, over time, turns into a perception of structural injustice rather than a feeling of individual failure. And this perception feeds anger.” This distinction matters. If young people believed their problems were merely personal failures, the result might be shame, withdrawal, or quiet despair. But when insecurity is understood as structural injustice, anger becomes political. A Greek Erasmus student in Istanbul describes this distrust starkly: “If I ever vote, it will probably be in protest or to destroy something, not because I trust some politician.” This is not merely cynicism. It is an expression of anomie: a condition in which the rules of social life lose their legitimacy. The young person may still vote. They may still participate. But the act is no longer rooted in trust. It becomes an act of refusal. Erdoğan has a single word for this condition: “resentment.” In Turkey, this resentment appeared powerfully during the Gezi Park protests in 2013, when an environmental protest grew into a mass anti-government movement. While Gezi predates the political world of Gen Z, the resentments that Erdoğan identifies have since reappeared in more recent forms of protests: student movements, urban protests, online campaigns, consumer boycotts, and leaderless acts of refusal. Around the world, Generation Z has repeatedly been at the forefront of political upheaval. This is not due to a shared generational ideology; it is a result of the shared experience of precarity and institutional distrust. From climate strikes to the women-led uprising in Iran to youth mobilizations in Bangladesh and Nepal to India’s recent “Cockroach” protests , Gen Z has repeatedly emerged as a disruptive force in moments of political crisis. Anger is not new. What is new is its speed, its mobility, and the way it travels across streets, screens, workplaces, campuses, and markets. Digital Capitalism and the New Terrain of Struggle It is tempting to say that social media is the driver of Gen Z’s politics. But that would be too simple. Digital platforms did not create exploitation, debt, insecurity, or police violence. But they have changed the terrain on which these conflicts are seen, organized, accelerated, and monetized. Media theorist Christian Fuchs, whose work focuses on digital labor and capitalism, argues that platforms should be understood not as the cause of contemporary uprisings but as mediators of social struggle under digital capitalism: Social media is not the cause of contemporary protests, rebellions, and revolutions. Given that young people are highly proficient in technology, it is natural that they use all kinds of digital media in protest communication, coordination, and organization. Precarious labor is certainly an important factor in some Generation Z protests. However, we cannot say that these protests are ‘created’ by social media platforms. In digital capitalism, these platforms do not create exploitation and social struggles but rather mediate them. The politics that have emerged among many members of Gen Z cannot be reduced to TikTok, Instagram, X, or any other platform. But they also cannot be understood outside them. Generational cohorts do not erase class. Rather, generational experience shapes the conditions under which class relations are experienced. Digital media has become one of the main spaces where Gen Z’s anger circulates, where protest is organized, where identities are formed, and where even opposition can be turned into content, data, and profit. The contradiction lies in the conditions under which many young people now work. Even those who reject the system can be drawn into speculative digital markets, influencer economies, platform labor, and algorithmic competition. They may protest capitalism in the morning and be forced to perform for its platforms at night. Digital capitalism does not stand outside young people’s lives. It structures their work, their communication, their attention, and increasingly their sense of self. Fuchs describes digital capital as global, while digital workers are local, isolated, and fragmented. The problem is not only that platform labor is poorly paid. It is also that it is organized in ways that make collective power harder to build. “Digital labor is often individualized and fragmented,” Fuchs says. “Workers operate disconnected from each other, and the possibilities for collective organization are weak. This makes them both more fragile and more easily exploitable.” In other words, the digital economy creates a workforce that is constantly connected but politically separated. Millions are online; everyone is visible; everyone is producing something. Yet workers often face the market alone. Fuchs argues that if capital has globalized, labor must also organize globally. “We need a transnational and global digital labor union where digital workers around the world unite against digital capital.” This is where the discussion moves beyond youth culture. The question is not whether young people are more ironic, anxious, online, or impatient than previous generations. The question is this: Are new forms of labor and communication reshaping class formations? And if so, can these new forms of class politics escape being absorbed by the platforms through which they circulate? Youth as Victim and Political Force Anu Muhammad, a Bangladeshi economist known for his critiques of capitalism, sees unemployment and precarious work as inseparable from the capitalist market economy. But he also emphasizes that these conditions do not produce a single political outcome. “The ruling classes see this situation as an opportunity to use young people,” Muhammad says. “Young people face uncertainty, precariousness, and hopelessness from an early age. While these conditions turn some into part of the system, they push others to question and oppose this order.” This is one of the central contradictions of Generation Z politics, which mirrors the broader crisis of capitalism itself. Conditions of insecurity do not produce a single politics. They can produce resignation, nihilism, or adaptation — or opposition and revolt. One part of youth may become an apparatus of the system, while another becomes part of the resistance against it. The 2024 uprising in Bangladesh showed the volatility of youth discontent. Similar patterns can be seen elsewhere. Young people are not always organized through parties. They are not always guided by coherent ideologies. Their movements are often unstable and internally contradictory. Yet they repeatedly appear at the center of political ruptures. These dynamics are disconcerting to older political institutions. Parties often expect young people to enter politics through familiar channels: membership, campaigns, elections, ideological loyalty. But Generation Z often enters politics through crisis: a housing struggle, a climate disaster, a corruption scandal, a campus protest, or a viral video that crystallizes a broader injustice. The result is a politics that is difficult to contain. It can be fast and morally intense. It can be amorphous. It can disappear and reappear. It can refuse leaders. It can distrust negotiation. But it cannot simply be dismissed as apolitical. No Trust, but No Withdrawal One of the paradoxes of Generation Z is this: distrust does not always lead to withdrawal. Young people may distrust institutions and still vote. They may hate the political system and still take to the streets. They may mock traditional politics and still develop strong moral judgments about work, consumption, identity, climate, war, and inequality. One young person I spoke with described the collapse of the old career promise with brutal clarity: “Climbing the career ladder? I am just trying not to stay under the ladder. The house and car that previous generations had at the age of twenty-five are beyond even a dream for me.” This is not merely a complaint about money. It expresses the breakdown of an entire moral economy. The old promise said that effort would be rewarded, that adulthood would become more stable over time, and that the future would be something one could reasonably plan. For many young people, that promise now looks like a historical relic. Yet this loss of faith does not mean that young people have stopped acting. On the contrary, their politics often appears in places older generations do not recognize as political. Politically active members of Gen Z boycott brands. They quit jobs. They build mutual aid networks. They amplify hashtags. They expose employers. They join street protests without joining parties. They use irony as a weapon. They turn consumption into judgment and digital visibility into pressure. Some of this is easily commodified. Some of it is shallow. Some of it disappears as quickly as it appears. But the fact that it is contradictory does not mean that it is meaningless. Standing sees in this process the possibility that the precariat — of which Gen Z makes up a disproportionate share — is slowly beginning to recognize itself as a class. “We see that the precariat is slowly becoming ‘a class for itself’ worldwide,” he says. “In other words, being in the precariat is no longer seen as a sign of shame or failure but is increasingly recognized as a result of today’s global capitalism.” This recognition is politically explosive. Once insecurity is no longer experienced as private failure, it can galvanize collective action. A New Political Imagination Generation Z is more than a demographic cohort. It is also an outcome: of decades of neoliberal restructuring, declining labor protections, rising debt, unaffordable housing, weakened welfare states, digital exploitation, and political systems that increasingly fail to represent social reality. Its anger is contradictory, fragmented, and often co-opted by the market it rejects. It can become protest, but it can also become content. The young people who want to refuse the system are nevertheless often integrated into the platform economy. But this does not make Generation Z apolitical. It shows that political expression is emerging in forms that older institutions struggle to read. This generation is not outside politics. But it increasingly feels itself to be outside the old institutions of politics. Its politics do not necessarily begin with parties, programs, or ideological traditions. It begins with insecurity, humiliation, blocked futures, and the feeling that the rules are rigged — or no longer work at all. The old language of politics asks whether these young people are left wing, liberal, conservative, populist, radical, or apathetic. But the deeper question may be different: What happens when a generation no longer believes that the system can offer it a future? Capitalism’s crisis today is not only that it produces inequality. It is that its central promise has lost credibility. The old story — work hard and you can succeed — no longer convinces the generation expected to inherit the world. Whether Gen Z’s rage and disillusionment produce resignation, reaction, or solidarity remains to be seen. What is clear is that more and more young people no longer see insecurity as a private failing but as a shared condition. Recognizing what appears to be a private misfortune as a shared public condition is where politics begins. It is what propels collective action and demands for structural change.

Affordability's Key Ingredient Is Union Power

Affordability — or the lack thereof — has dominated the recent political debate in the U.S. And for good reason. Across the country, too many families are struggling to make ends meet. However, almost every conversation about affordability focuses entirely on prices, as if the only way to make life more affordable is to make things cheaper. But the actual driver of today’s affordability squeeze is suppressed pay — a consequence of decades of policy choices that weakened workers’ bargaining power and shifted income away from working people. Had pay for typical workers kept pace with productivity over the past 45 years, their paychecks today would be roughly 40 % larger. If policymakers are serious about addressing affordability, they would champion one institution that has consistently proven capable of raising pay: unions. Through collective bargaining, unions are the most effective mechanism for workers to raise their wages and secure their fair share of the wealth they produce. Our new report at the Economic Policy Institute quantifies how transformative it would be to rebuild union power. Specifically, we examine what we stand to gain if we tripled current union membership to 30 % — similar to its peak in the U.S. before decades of relentless attacks on unions and collective bargaining eroded it, and just shy of the current rate in Canada. We find that tripling union membership would raise pay for the typical worker by more than $ 7 , 700 every year, or nearly $ 270 , 000 over a 35 -year career. This would be life-changing for a working family — nearly covering the cost of raising a child from birth through age 17 , for example. And those increases aren’t just for unionized workers — strong unions also benefit nonunion workers by establishing broad standards that employers must follow to get and keep the workers they need. The gains from tripling union density would be especially significant for Black and Hispanic workers, who get a larger boost from unionization than white workers. This is because unions provide transparency and other guardrails that help protect workers from employer discrimination, and because unions deliver larger wage gains for lower-wage jobs, where Black and Hispanic workers are disproportionately concentrated due to the pervasive effects of racism on labor market outcomes. Tripling union density would close one-third of today’s racial wage gaps. If you add up the impact across the workforce, tripling union density would shift $ 1 . 2 trillion annually to working people and away from corporate elites — reversing one-third of the rise in inequality since 1979 . The benefits of achieving 30 % union density extend far beyond wages. Since unions often win better benefits, including health insurance, tripling union membership would result in 25 % fewer uninsured Americans. By educating workers and boosting voter turnout, strong unions also protect democracy and give workers a voice in shaping the social and political fabric of their communities. States with high union density invest more in public education, deliver unemployment benefits to a far greater share of jobless workers, and have all expanded Medicaid. The world our report imagines is not out of reach. Unions are more popular now than they’ve been in decades, especially among younger workers. And survey data show that 43 % of nonunion workers would vote to unionize if they could — well above our goal of 30 %. It's high time lawmakers translate the public’s near-record support for unions into policy. Meeting this moment will require not just sustained organizing by workers and unions across the country, but also bold reforms to reverse decades of attacks on unions and collective bargaining. It’s high time lawmakers translate the public’s near-record support for unions into policy. Policymakers have a clear roadmap to rebuilding union power. Congress can start by passing two existing bipartisan bills that would reform U.S. labor law and strengthen workers’ right to unionize: the Protecting the Right to Organize (PRO) Act and the Public Service Freedom to Negotiate Act. Congress should also consider bold new ideas alongside the labor law reform bills already on the table. We offer a few such proposals in our report. One guarantees that newly unionized workers can get a first contract that includes a raise. Another proposal requires collective bargaining at any company where CEOs earn 100 times more than typical workers in their industry — allowing workers to negotiate a fairer share of company profits. States have a role to play as well. By repealing anti-union ​ “ right-to-work” laws and ensuring collective bargaining rights for all public-sector workers and other workers not currently covered by federal labor law, states can take their own steps to grow union power. Rebuilding union membership would raise wages, shrink racial wage gaps, expand health coverage, strengthen communities, and protect our democracy — all at the same time. There is no other policy lever that does all of that. It’s what workers want. And it’s entirely within our power to achieve.

Last night was a sobering one for the Left. After months of exhilarating victories, including Abdul El-Sayed’s stunning Michigan Senate primary win just last week, the Left’s hopes for another high-profile Midwest triumph crashed in Wisconsin, where socialist gubernatorial candidate Francesca Hong saw her double-digit polling lead evaporate into a 0.5-point defeat to the Democratic establishment’s centrist choice, David Crowley. That result is already being used to create the impression that this was a wholesale rejection of socialists up and down the ballot, which is not the case. Five of eight candidates endorsed by Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) chapters in Wisconsin and Minnesota won their races last night, while one more has advanced without a primary, and a further five of seven DSA-backed candidates are moving on to the general election in Connecticut and Vermont. And one of those losing DSA-backed candidates lost to another, non-DSA-endorsed socialist. But there is no doubt Hong’s race was by far the most coveted prize for the Left last night and the most important electoral test for socialists in particular, given it was a statewide race in the Midwest and that unlike the victorious El-Sayed in Michigan, Hong actually identifies as a socialist. What explains such a bitter, if narrow, defeat only one week after the broad Left was riding high in a neighboring state? Was it the money? If Michigan proved that big money can be overcome, Wisconsin is a reminder that it still often wins out. Pro-Hong forces were massively outspent , their less than half a million dollars on ad spending dwarfed by the $4 million spent by the pro-Crowley camp, a significant chunk of which went toward an attack ad in the home stretch casting Hong as a risky candidate in the general election. She was also the only candidate to not spend a single dollar on traditional TV and focus instead on streaming services and other digital ads, a deliberate strategy her campaign spokesperson boasted about on the eve of the election. In the recent Democratic primary in Missouri’s first congressional district, which includes all of St Louis and much of northern St Louis County, including Ferguson, the American Israel Public Affairs Committee (AIPAC)–funded incumbent Wesley Bell bucked national trends. Whereas AIPAC support has become an albatross for many candidates running in Democratic primaries, Bell easily defeated former congresswoman and Israel critic Cori Bush. Although the money AIPAC funneled into the primary indicates Middle East politics played an outsize role in the struggle to control a black-majority Missouri district, deeply rooted local concerns seemed weightier. This is not surprising: the political careers of both Bell and Bush began in 2014 with the protests that engulfed Ferguson in response to the police murder of Michael Brown. Bush supporters, thus, not only brought attention to Bell’s ties to AIPAC, but also to his Deputy Chief of Staff Jordan Blase’s controversial past as a Veiled Prophet debutante. As a local activist said on Instagram, after spotting an album of photos on Blase’s Facebook page dedicated to the annual Veiled Prophet Ball, Blase and her family “have been attending their white supremacist ball for decades.” What in the hell is a “Veiled Prophet,” you might ask? And why is Blase’s history with it controversial (though not controversial enough to tilt the race in favor of Bush)? For answers to these questions and many more about the history of St Louis elite society, we turn to Devin Thomas O’Shea’s fascinating new book, The Veiled Prophet: Secret Societies, White Supremacy, and the Struggle for St Louis . Reconstruction and the Great Railroad Strike The Veiled Prophet Society, a purportedly secret organization with a highly exclusive membership that hosts an annual parade and a debutante ball, was founded in 1877 for the explicit purpose of shielding the local bourgeoisie from an increasingly militant proletariat. It was no coincidence that the Veiled Prophet Society was created in the smoldering aftermath of the Great Railroad Strikes of 1877, or that it was established in St Louis, where workers not only walked off the job but also organized to take control of the city. The Veiled Prophet Society was formed to destroy the specter of working-class solidarity that momentarily sprang to life in what contemporaries called the St Louis Commune, comparing it to the more famous 1871 Paris Commune. The St Louis Commune, led by the short-lived Workingmen’s Party of the United States (WPUS) — the first Marxist party in the United States — briefly took power in East St Louis, the section of the city on the east side of the Mississippi and a rail hub and industrial loading dock. The Great Railroad Strike, which began in West Virginia and fanned out across multiple states, disrupting the nation’s circulation of goods, was a violent affair. In response to workers burning down railroad facilities, the companies, enlisting private militias, local and state police, and federal troops, put down the strike with malice, killing about 150 workers. Unlike strikers elsewhere, St Louis workers refrained from destroying property, since their objective was to expropriate it for their own use. They were successful for about a week, before the forces of capital took back control of the city. The title page for the program of the 1883 Veiled Prophet Festival. To protect their interests in the face of an assertive proletariat, the St Louis bourgeoisie created the ironically named Committee for Public Safety, which, unlike the original French revolutionary committee of the same name, was designed to crush rather than defend hard-won republican rights. “The strike,” O’Shea writes, “was an effort to crown the workers as the masters” of the enormous wealth that flowed through St Louis in the nineteenth century, such abundance owing to the city sitting along the largest inland waterway on the continent, at the crossroads of both the nation’s east–west and north–south frontiers. “The city’s financial interests used racism, and the barrel of a gun, to quash that effort.” The racism of St Louis elites is central to O’Shea’s story. One of the keys to the brief success of the St Louis working class in 1877 was its interracial solidarity. And one of the lasting features of the Veiled Prophet Society, which was formed out of the Committee for Public Safety and has been in existence ever since, has been its white supremacy. The founder of the Veiled Prophet Society, St Louis native Alonzo Slayback, is prima facie evidence of O’Shea’s argument about white supremacy being the establishing principle of the organization. Slayback was an officer in the Confederate Army and a true believer in the cause of the South, slavery. After the United States defeated the Confederacy, Slayback followed notorious Confederate General Jo Shelby south to Mexico City, where they hoped to escape punishment for their treason. A portrait of Alonzo W. Slayback, circa 1882. Shelby, Slayback, and company solicited help from fellow reactionary elite, Emperor Maximilian I, the Austrian archduke reigning over the so-called Second Mexican Empire. As a member of the European ruling class, Maximilian was closely aligned with the potentate of the Second French Empire, Napoleon III (known to posterity as Karl Marx’s object of mockery — in The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte , Marx famously described Napoleon III’s ascension as a farce). The account of defeated Confederates in Mexico City seeking help from a soon-to-be executed European monarch (Maximilian met his death by firing squad in 1867) is one of many stories of unhinged conduct common to the rich, powerful, and reactionary men of the Veiled Prophet Society. Such elite vulgarity will seem familiar to twenty-first-century readers. From Elon Musk’s (alleged) ketamine-induced displays of neurotic Nazism to Peter Thiel’s (confirmed) obsession with the Antichrist, repugnant behavior is a timeless characteristic of rich, powerful, and reactionary men. We can only wish our current oligarchs were, like their Confederate predecessors, on the run, seeking shelter in a foreign capital. Little did Slayback know when he traveled south of the border, that a full pardon awaited him upon his homecoming. (One of the biggest political mistakes in US history is letting those who committed treason in defense of slavery off the hook so easily.) Slayback returned to St Louis in 1866 and started a law practice. He had rejoined an elite class of Americans that by 1877 wished to turn the page on the Civil War and Reconstruction and instead focus its attention on capital accumulation. The ultimate objective of the Veiled Prophet Society was protecting its members’ wealth. Upholding racial hierarchy was a secondary, though interconnected, goal. The Veiled Prophet, the society’s mysterious mascot annually paraded before the St Louis rabble, wears a Ku Klux Klan–inspired mask and robe that double as odes to the Irish poet Thomas Moore’s orientalist tale, Lalla Rookh . The Veiled Prophet was intended to evoke awe of the local elite among the working-class masses who lined the streets of St Louis each year and watched as the wealthy paraded by them. O’Shea describes what motivated Slayback and his fellow elites to concoct such an absurdity: The Veiled Prophet Society would be a Mardi Gras club for white men to parade their power in the streets of St Louis, communicating their message to the proletariat — that Americans are white and civilized and that the rest of the world is barbaric and Black; that the threat of Southern plantation violence was not dead, but lay dormant in everyday life; but most of all, that commerce reigns supreme and should operate freely without checks or restraints. The mascot often stimulated ridicule, even anger, especially among black locals who rejected the parade’s nostalgia for plantation slavery. The Veiled Prophet was frequently the target of various projectiles along the parade route. Yet despite bouts of mostly harmless resistance from local proles, the Veiled Prophet Society was apparently successful in reversing the republicanism that had made its home in St Louis during the Civil War and Reconstruction. Elites overturned a widespread radical sensibility, nicely expressed by Marx’s German ’48er comrade Joseph Weydemeyer, a colonel in the Union Army who was placed in command of forces defending St Louis during the war. In an 1866 essay promoting black suffrage, Weydemeyer argued that, in the struggle against capital, workers needed to join forces “regardless of what ancestry they have to thank for their skin color.” Weydemeyer advocated for the very same multiracial working-class formation that W. E. B. DuBois later mourned in his monumental 1935 book, Black Reconstruction in America . Into the 20th Century In the book’s interregnum of sorts, O’Shea narrates a gripping tale that turns out to be a serious plot twist. On October 13, 1882, Slayback barged into St Louis Post-Dispatch editor John A. Cockerill’s office. The former Confederate general was outraged over the rough treatment the newspaper had been dishing out to his law partner and fellow Veiled Prophet Society member James Broadhead, who was running for local office. An angry Slayback arrived armed and loaded, but Cockerill got the jump on him, shooting and killing him. Convinced the newspaperman had acted in self-defense, local authorities never charged Cockerill with a crime. The founder of the Veiled Prophet Society was pronounced dead at the age of forty-four. But the secret organization did not perish alongside him. The king is dead, long live the king. As The Veiled Prophet Society moves into the twentieth century, we learn about the growing prestige of the group during the era of its close kin, the second Ku Klux Klan, when the “club commanded the attention of presidents, and robber barons like Jay Gould were compelled to attend the ball.” We also learn about the larger history of St Louis and its segregated and easily exploited industrial workforce, part and parcel of ongoing racial violence which at times exploded, such as in a 1917 racial pogrom that left around 150 black residents dead. Resistance to the Veiled Prophet Society was sporadic at best, only truly gaining momentum in the 1930s, a time when the organization was more “severely out of touch” than usual. Until then, the club had acted as a symbol of elite unification and working-class fracture. But in the age of the Congress of Industrial Organizations , when even the normally taboo Communist Party USA experienced rapid growth, it was more difficult for an older, venal bourgeoisie — the exact type that constituted the Veiled Prophet Society — to preserve its hegemonic grip on the political culture. Yet in St Louis, as elsewhere in the United States, the ruling class adjusted to new circumstances. One of the better indicators that the old elite remained dominant, even during the postwar age of high Fordism, was in how it dictated the terms of urban renewal, which was all the rage in postwar American cities. Urban renewal policies transformed St Louis, but not for the better, especially for the black working class. A 1947 portrait of then-adviser to Harry S. Truman, Clark Clifford. Several newly constructed highways carved up the heart of the city, clearing out historically black neighborhoods. A massive housing project, Pruitt-Igoe, opened its doors in 1954 to thousands of displaced and impoverished residents, only to be demolished after two decades of severe neglect. None of which is to say that a public housing project is an inherently bad idea. But public goods must be nurtured if they are to be, well, good . Meanwhile, as Pruitt-Igoe was starved to death, the Veiled Prophet Society resumed celebrating high society, emphasizing its annual ball, where its luckiest daughters were honored as brides of the Veiled Prophet. Another sign that the Veiled Prophet Society, an inherently reactionary entity, retained its mojo even at the high point of American liberalism was when President Harry Truman, a Missouri man, brought a Veiled Prophet contingent with him to Washington, DC. Most notable among them was Clark Clifford, who helped establish the Central Intelligence Agency in 1947. Clifford went on to serve in several Democratic administrations, including briefly as Lyndon B. Johnson’s secretary of defense. Another Veiled Prophet Society member, Tom Dooley, operated as a spy in Southeast Asia prior to the Vietnam War. Dooley gained notoriety as author of the anti-communist screed Deliver Us from Evil , an ostensible dismantling of Ho Chi Minh that was in fact a total fabrication. During the Cold War, the Veiled Prophet Society had joined what C. Wright Mills labeled “the power elite,” the overlapping network of government, business, and military brokers who ruled the United States and, by extension, much of the world. Does the Ruling Class Need Secret Societies? The Veiled Prophet Society is a fun and informative book, especially for sickos like me who never tire of reading about the American class struggle, even though the struggle almost never tilts in favor of the working class. But O’Shea’s overarching analysis is too heavily reliant on assuming the historical agency of the Veiled Prophet Society. The not-so-secret club is a window onto elite capitalist society and how it operates to protect its interests. It is not a mechanism, or at least not the mechanism, for defending modern capitalist hierarchy. O’Shea’s book is excellent in describing the strange manifestations of the American class struggle from the perspective of the elites who had a lot to gain from maintaining capitalist arrangements. But the existence of a secret society itself, even one so embedded in a specific location, is not a smoking gun. There is no conspiracy other than the grand conspiracy of capitalism that has been known to us since Marx and Friedrich Engels wrote about the “new conditions of oppression” in The Communist Manifesto . O’Shea’s skill in narrating a tale, alongside his somewhat upside-down analysis, is evident in his half-hearted conjecture that the Veiled Prophet Society was possibly responsible for the assassination of Martin Luther King, Jr — a possibility that was investigated by the House Select Committee on Assassinations in the late 1970s and dismissed. O’Shea begrudgingly admits that the evidence connecting the club to the murderer, James Earl Ray, who spent a significant amount of time in St Louis and was born nearby, is at best speculative. But he also implies that the connection is true in the way good fiction is true. “It makes sense,” he writes, “that a narrative developed around King and the Prophet. One is royalty of the African American struggle, the other is the monarch of the cut-throat white business classes; two entities at war with one another for the soul of America.” The civil rights movement in St Louis forced the Veiled Prophet Society on its heels with a campaign of constant harassment. The elite club responded by shutting down its parade, which had become an easy target of backlash, and by instead hosting an annual fair under the shadow of the Gateway Arch. Anodyne music acts, generic food vendors, and plenty of Budweiser replaced the Veiled Prophet and its racist baggage. Eventually organizers even ditched the Veiled Prophet Society moniker, as the club tried to hide its controversial identity as sponsor of the fair. The Veiled Prophet Society’s efforts to clean up its image also led to incremental efforts to diversify its membership. The first black member was admitted to Alonzo Slayback’s club in the early 1980s. By 2014, the year of the Ferguson uprising, the Veiled Prophet Society was a shell of its former self. And yet the elite are in firm control of St Louis and almost every other American city. The Left is doing better than it has in a while, but capitalism is far from threatened. Secret societies and similar such elite clubs are hardly necessary for continued ruling class domination. One question that O’Shea’s book raises: Were they ever?

Five Takeaways From Francesca Hong’s Near-Win in Wisconsin

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Paul Heideman ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-12T16:08:56+00:00

Francesca Hong, the democratic socialist running in the Democratic primary for the Wisconsin governor’s race, lost last night in an extremely close election. She came within half a percentage point of beating David Crowley, the executive of Milwaukee County. Preelection polling showed Hong with a commanding lead, and many assumed she would coast to victory. As such, her result is being spun as a hard defeat for the socialist left. The truth is more complicated. Hong accomplished a great deal in her campaign, and even her loss contains valuable lessons for future attempts. 1. Horse-Race Polling Warps the Narrative Perhaps the biggest lesson from this race is that a focus on the horse-race polling makes for bad politics. Since serious polling for this race began, Hong held a solid lead. She led current Lieutenant Governor Sara Rodriguez by 10 points even before Rodriguez had to drop out because of campaign finance mishaps. Crowley, who ultimately won, hadn’t come within 20 points of Hong since March. Because of this, Hong’s victory was widely viewed as all but inevitable. But the polls were wrong. Turnout was up significantly from the last statewide Democratic primary in 2022. And large numbers of voters were clearly waiting to make up their minds. Hong’s lead in the polls lent itself to a narrative of her as a prohibitive favorite. This was clearly a mistake. And it obscures how far the campaign came. When Hong launched her campaign back in September of last year, she described herself as a wild card in the race and was largely treated as a fringe candidate. The vast majority of voters in the state had never heard of her. She was a state assembly member (a part-time position in Wisconsin), and worked in restaurants. But through dedicated campaigning across the state, Hong built up a real profile and won the trust of hundreds of thousands of voters. Coming within half a percent of victory from such a starting point is an objectively impressive achievement. The Democratic establishment in Wisconsin can hardly be thrilled that it was only able to defeat a novice candidate like Hong by the skin of its teeth. But the focus on polling obscures all of this. 2. Subcultural Leftism Hurts Socialists Hong accomplished a tremendous amount in this race. But it is impossible to avoid the conclusion that she could have accomplished much more if she hadn’t had to carry the load of past statements she made exemplifying insular leftist subcultures. A post she had made in 2020 calling for scrapping Thanksgiving for “celebrating colonialism” and “a time that’s incredibly painful for many people in our communities” received a great deal of attention in the last weeks of the race, for example. So did her endorsement of abolishing the police, a slogan I’ve recently argued is a barrier to left success and desperately needed criminal justice reform. And while it’s unlikely that many Democratic primary voters decided not to endorse Hong because they were afraid of being deprived of turkey in November, the sheer number of statements Hong had to walk back in the last few weeks convinced many voters that she would have a hard time defeating the Republican Tom Tiffany in the general election. In a race with margins this tight, it’s at least plausible that all this cost her the race. Hong isn’t the only socialist who got on board with self-marginalizing politics during the “Great Awokening” of the early 2020s. Zohran Mamdani also embraced defunding the police. But by frankly repudiating his earlier position in the campaign and maintaining iron discipline in his focus on affordability in all campaign appearances, he was able to convince voters that he really had changed his positions. Hong’s disavowals, coming out late in the race and made far less forcefully, were not as impactful. For candidates who are freighted with this kind of baggage, extraordinary message discipline is necessary. 3. Socialists Need to Be Able to Make the Case They’ll Defeat Republicans This leads directly into the next point. It should have been clear after Joe Biden’s victory over Bernie Sanders in 2020 that the ability to defeat Republicans in general elections is nonnegotiable for Democratic primary voters. In places like New York City, where the GOP runs a local mascot like Curtis Sliwa for mayor rather than an actual candidate, this is less of a concern. But in a state like Wisconsin, where voters remember the devastation wrought by eight years of Scott Walker’s austerity-crazed, viciously antiunion Republican administration, Democratic primary voters were unwilling to take a chance with someone they viewed as less likely to win the general election. Socialist candidates need to embrace the work of making this case, and need to campaign in a way that demonstrates to voters that we take beating Republicans every bit as seriously as they do. 4. Campaign on What Matters to Voters Hong achieved as much as she did in this race because of her focus on what mattered to voters. She campaigned on free public childcare, taxing the rich, and instituting a one-year moratorium on data center construction. These issues resonated deeply with voters and convinced thousands and thousands to support a candidate they had not even heard of when the race began. To appreciate the way her policies made Hong stand out, compare her plan for free public childcare with Lieutenant Governor Sara Rodriguez’s childcare plan. Rodriguez proposed a means-tested program that would cap childcare expenses at 7 percent of annual income. Given a choice between the two policies, it’s not hard to see why so many voters preferred Hong. Similarly, data centers are deeply unpopular in Wisconsin, and Hong was the only candidate to come out for a moratorium on their construction. Simple, clear policies like these are a strong foundation for outsider candidates. 5. The Space for Socialist Politics, Even Outside of Major Cities Like New York, Is Massive After Zohran Mamdani won the New York City mayoral race in 2025, a common refrain from the pundit choir was that Mamdani’s approach could work in big cities like New York or Chicago but stood no chance in purple states. Hong showed that even in Wisconsin, there is a huge appetite for a break from politics as usual. Her campaign began much like Mamdani’s, with virtually no funding, support, or media visibility. But through hard campaigning across the state, Hong built up a profile, at the same time that the nonsocialist Democrats in the race all failed to inspire much excitement. Much of the commentariat would like to declare that Hong’s loss shows the ceiling on democratic socialist politics in purple states. But given Hong’s burden of subcultural leftism and the reported disorganization of her campaign, it would be more apt to conclude that a more disciplined candidate and campaign with the same platform could have achieved much more. Hong’s campaign both showed the potential that exists for the socialist left in purple states and what we need to do to reach it.

Rarely do the legions of armchair observers move beyond such caricature, because they are reliant on an ecosystem of opinion consensus by measurement, which is itself partly incorporated into Putinism’s media management. Pollsters present Russians with the correct framing: “The conduct of the armed forces in Ukraine — do you support it?” And dutifully a majority in favor is produced. Even on its own terms, though, cracks begin to show, such as a recent poll that found only 30 percent favor continuing the war — even within what is obviously a self-selected sample of mainly loyalist citizens willing to invite a pollster into their home. Opinion polls and interpretation of elite discussion: that’s the extent of the current Kremlinology. There is a strong allergy to field data and field experts, partly as a result of the retreat of area expertise in the United States. As a result, the Washington-centric common sense about Russia is hegemonic. In June–July, two worthy think tanks, Carnegie and Kennan , both published questionable versions of an argument about a renewed rally-’round-the-flag effect in response to the step change in Ukrainian attacks. To me, as one of the last of a breed of anthropologists examining politics from below, there is a disturbing groupthink around the idea that Russians are enthusiastic for the war and its aims, even as it becomes impossible to ignore the costs within Russia. Speaking to any selection of ordinary people, it’s clear that a large majority want the war to end now, on any terms short of having to pay reparations. But arguments about public sentiment merely reinforce the sense that we are not even able to articulate a language that would facilitate a discussion about the longer-term crisis in Russia, of which the war is just a symptom. The invasion of Ukraine is not a departure from the normality of Putinism, but rather the morbid culmination of a socioeconomic crisis inherent to authoritarian neoliberal governance. For over two decades, the regime operated as a distillation of unbridled political power bent on accumulation of economic rents, insider ownership, and constellations of patron-client control. It did this while starving the social state: a system characterized by astute observers like Nick Trickett as an “empire of austerity.” One reason for the catastrophic outcome of the security elite’s decision to go to war is that this same elite failed to understand how weak Russian society had become because of a systematic refusal to invest in public goods beyond the bare minimum required to mitigate immediate crisis. This war is the gangrenous result of a system that has long prioritized the protection of a tiny rapacious elite over the basic survival of its population. As I argue in my recent book on the war and its less proximate causes, Russia has become a state-of-the-art laboratory for the production of poverty and the imposition of neoliberal rationality — one that is now admired especially by a similar breed of political capitalists in the orbit of Donald Trump and beyond it. Unlike the caricatures in Western media, the Russian state is not a throwback to Soviet planning. Rather, it drives market-oriented subjectivities where the individual is forced to become a contestant in a nightmarish game of social reproduction, in an atmosphere of cynicism and disregard for human costs. This system has successfully depoliticized social inequality, framing the divide between winners and losers as a matter of personal failure rather than structural dispossession. Putinism’s legitimacy has rested on a hollowed-out social compact: the state provides the veneer of a convenient consumer culture, symbolized by the platform-capitalism world of ubiquitous brands like Wildberries, in exchange for political passivity. Yet this convenience masks a brutal socioeconomic Darwinism where representatives of the elite and government, including those who oversaw the deindustrialization of the country, the legalized theft of its remaining assets, and the casting into poverty of tens of millions in the 1990s, like to hold forth about Russians’ inherent bovine mentality. They are called mindless munchers of coarse feed, inured to suffering and privation, who expect handouts and a paternal state while not understanding that “man is wolf to man,” as the saying goes, and that the government “owes you nothing.” Since the 2008 financial crisis, Russians have faced some of the worst developed-world income stagnation and a wealth concentration that is significantly higher than in the United States or China. Putinism has responded to this long-term decay not with reinvestment of its petrochemical bounty, but with a neoconservative “cultural war” against Western “permissiveness,” using “sovereign democracy” as a shield to protect the ill-gotten gains of a security-state elite. Observers often rely on a poorly fitting translation from a very different cultural and historical context — the United States after 9/11 and a rally-’round-the-flag narrative — to explain what seems to be Russians’ lack of resistance to the war, or even enthusiasm for it. By contrast, I prefer to think in terms of a defensive consolidation. Russians have not “rallied” to patriotic fervor; rather, they have retreated into comforting narratives of encirclement and civilizational peril, some of which align with propaganda but others which do not. They do this to manage both long-term socioeconomic trauma and a sense of helplessness exacerbated by the war and its resulting stresses. After the invasion in 2022, many consolidated behind the Putinite authorities out of a desperate unspoken hope: that the war would finally break the dismal neoliberal compact and force the state to return to a more prosocial collectivist project reminiscent of the Soviet era’s social guarantees. Military Keynesianism? However, this hope for a more protective state has proven illusory. The “military Keynesianism” touted by both the Kremlin and certain segments of the Left who claim that the working classes are “winning” from the war economy is a fantasy. While there was short-lived growth in real incomes, peaking in 2024, this “multiplier effect” was geographically delimited and has already been devoured by inflation, the resulting massive hike in bank rates, and the hard capacity limits of an economy plagued by a lack of investment. The “social elevator” of war money is reaching its limit, and the structural power of labor remains suppressed by a legal environment that makes defending workers’ rights nearly impossible. The true unraveling of the Putin era will not be found in the dream come true of a revolutionary democratizing subject, which is what Alexei Navalny embodied until his murder by the state. The emotional investment by the West, by the Russian liberals, and by the pundit class in figures like Navalny has led to an analytical myopia. Rather than the smart and charismatic leader featured in Forbes fifteen times between his arrest in 2021 and death in 2024, a much more likely figure to exploit a future crisis, or even precipitate it, will be a composite character embodying features more reminiscent of the 1990s: a red-brown politician from within the existing security elite, promising to resolve the contradiction between spectacular elite wealth and everyday insecurity. This is because the reality of rising inequality meeting austerity and the inability of a state to ensure basic human needs like adequate health care remains more “real” to Russians than the war itself. Despite the “military Keynesian” rhetoric, measurable inequality remains as stark as ever, with the top 1 percent holding 70 percent of all assets. Russia is an outlier among countries rated as high on the human development index, with 10 percent of the population receiving half of all income, and 96 percent of savers have negligible savings. The tax system is a masterpiece of regressive redistribution. The state funds its war through sales taxes and fees as much as by selling oil and gas. These taxes fall disproportionately on the poor, while a much-lauded flat income tax ensures that even those in extreme poverty pay a higher relative burden than those with assets. There is no wealth tax, and inheritance tax was abolished nearly twenty years ago to help protect the transition of wealth within the plutocracy. This is the context in which we must view the recent attacks on Wildberries infrastructure. These attacks strike at the heart of the convenient consumer culture that substituted for political legitimacy among the urban middle class. When these logistics hubs are targeted, it strips away the last remaining mask of the neoliberal compact. Putinism cannot provide security, it cannot provide social mobility, and now it can no longer guarantee the frictionless consumption economy that was built on the backs of an exploited, micro-proletarianized workforce. Conditions of Protest Periods of apparently immovable political order have often seen growing reservoirs of popular grievance that unexpectedly emerge when existing ways of accommodating to the system fail. Catherine II confronted enormous peasant rebellion despite presiding over one of the strongest imperial states in Europe. The revolutions of 1905 and 1917 emerged from societies long considered politically pacified. The Novocherkassk massacre of 1962 reminded Soviet authorities that workers remained capable of seemingly spontaneous collective resistance despite extensive brutalization. The coal miners’ strikes of 1989 helped destabilize a system that had appeared institutionally impregnable only a few years earlier. More recently, the labor disputes, social protests against loss of benefits and pensions, environmental campaigns, and local civic mobilizations against housing demolitions of the 2000s and 2010s demonstrated that collective action never disappeared beneath Putinism; it simply operated in fragmented and localized forms typical of what might be called a refined and relatively polite form of classic authoritarianism. Before the war, labor-related protests were at a record high despite legal repression of unions and, essentially, a ban on legal industrial actions. Labor actions alone are unlikely to develop into political protests. Yet they might, as the leading researcher Pyotr Bizyukov argues, create conditions for other forms of political resistance to emerge. Today micropolitical resistance is already fermenting, from the sabotage of military draft offices to online activists showing people how to avoid mobilization. Indeed, it is hard to keep track of the many groups still organizing, albeit clandestinely or online and abroad. As I argue in my book, the memory of a future-orientated “commonality” (in Russian: obshchnost ) and the ideal of an achievable, if flawed, postcapitalist society that provided public goods to all remain alive, represents a lasting political stumbling block to the regime’s largely empty rhetoric and inability to offer Russians better prospects. Even Vladimir Putin seemed to confront this fact recently when asked an “astonishing” question about what positive roles Russians will be able to look forward to after the war. He clearly found it surprising, when confronted by an ordinary person with the age-old Russian question: “what is to be done”? He interpreted this as about people’s need to be incorporated into a “great cause.” As the country enters a period of stagflation and illusions in the war economy dissolve, the refusal of the state to recognize that its main purpose is to ensure the social reproduction of its people may finally meet the historical limit of Russians’ patience. In its great laboratory of dispossession, exploitation, and now battlefield disposal, the vanguard of authoritarian neoliberalism has long treated ordinary Russians as biopolitical waste. Now, four years into a war even Russia’s leaders can no longer explain, this elite is entering into sharper confrontation with this same people.
