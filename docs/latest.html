The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. The United States can always find money for war. But when Americans need healthcare, education, housing, or investments in their communities, the familiar refrain is that there simply isn't enough money. That contradiction framed Our Bloated Military Budget , a session at the 2026 Left Coast Forum, held October 2–4 at California State University, Los Angeles. Featuring military veteran and peace advocate Matthew Hoh and foreign policy analyst Ben Freeman , the discussion examined the human and moral consequences of American militarism and the political culture that allows it to continue. Freeman, director of the Democratizing Foreign Policy program at the Quincy Institute, is co-author with William Hartung of The Trillion Dollar War Machine: How Runaway Military Spending Drives America into Foreign Wars and Bankrupts Us at Home . Hoh, a former Marine Corps officer and State Department official who resigned in protest of the Afghanistan War in 2009, brought a deeply personal perspective to the discussion. Rather than focusing exclusively on Pentagon expenditures, the conversation explored something more fundamental: What does perpetual warfare do to the people who fight it, the communities caught in its path, and the society that finances it? The War Comes Home Hoh began by describing the consequences of his military service, which continue nearly two decades after his final deployments to Iraq and Afghanistan. He spoke candidly about traumatic brain injury, psychological suffering, substance abuse, and the effects of medications prescribed to treat his conditions. His weight, he explained, had fluctuated dramatically over the years. But these physical struggles represented only part of the damage. Hoh described the concept of moral injury —the psychological and spiritual suffering that can arise when individuals participate in actions that violate their deepest ethical convictions. Unlike physical wounds, moral injury can be invisible, even while fundamentally altering a person's relationship with themselves and the world. Hoh argued that the connection between combat, guilt, and suicide among veterans receives insufficient attention, particularly when discussing the consequences of killing. He recalled undergoing years of Veterans Affairs therapy without being asked directly about his participation in wartime violence. For Hoh, this omission reflects a larger institutional reluctance to confront what warfare actually requires of those sent to fight. Related: 9/11, Blowback, and the History America Refuses to Discuss "War Makes You Its Agent" One of the discussion's most powerful moments centered on Hoh's observation that "war makes you its agent." He explained that many service members enter combat believing they can retain control over their moral choices. They imagine that they will act conscientiously, protect civilians, and avoid participating in atrocities. But warfare, Hoh argued, operates according to forces that overwhelm individual intentions. To illustrate his point, he referenced a scene from the television series Generation Kill , in which Marines attempt to avoid shooting innocent civilians by firing a warning smoke grenade toward an approaching vehicle. The grenade accidentally strikes and kills an elderly man. Even an attempt to prevent violence produces another death. For Hoh, the lesson was unmistakable: Individuals cannot reliably control the consequences of their actions within the machinery of war. His reflections also challenged the conventional American distinction between heroic soldiers and the people designated as enemies. In Iraq and Afghanistan, he argued, many of those fighting American forces were resisting foreign military occupation. "We invaded them. We occupied them," he said, emphasizing that American intervention helped create the very resistance it then sought to defeat. The People Left Behind Hoh's account became particularly poignant when he described the Iraqis with whom he had worked during the occupation. Among them was Mona, an Iraqi woman who helped care for the American personnel and became something of a mother figure to him. Hoh has a tattoo honoring her. He does not know what happened to Mona after he left Iraq. He similarly lost contact with other Iraqi colleagues, including a deputy whose community was devastated by the Islamic State. These relationships remain central to his understanding of his responsibilities as a veteran and peace advocate. Hoh acknowledged that much of his activism is driven by anger and guilt. He feels an obligation to speak for people whose suffering has been forgotten or dismissed, while those responsible for devastating wars have largely escaped accountability. His white poppy, which commemorates all victims of war rather than only military personnel, symbolizes that commitment. The suffering of American veterans, he emphasized, cannot be separated from the much greater devastation experienced by populations subjected to American military intervention. Why Doesn't America Learn? Freeman raised a question that goes to the heart of antiwar organizing: How can the experiences of veterans and military families become part of the national conversation before another war begins? He described conversations with Gold Star mothers—women who had lost children in military service—and efforts to amplify their experiences as warnings against future military interventions. Hoh's response was sobering. "Every generation thinks they're exempt from history," he observed. Despite the repeated failures of American military interventions, each generation persuades itself that the next conflict will be different. Hoh recounted speaking with students enrolled in military officer training programs after his resignation from the State Department. Although some acknowledged his arguments, they continued into military service. Only afterward did some tell him that his warnings had been correct. Yet Hoh also identified a source of hope. Independent journalism, alternative media, and social platforms have weakened the ability of political and military elites to monopolize public understanding of war. This changing information environment, he suggested, gives opponents of militarism opportunities that earlier generations did not possess. A Military Machine That Cannot Stop The discussion also turned toward the dangers of confrontation with Iran and the possibility of nuclear escalation. Hoh warned about efforts to develop lower-yield nuclear weapons and promote the idea that nuclear war could be limited or controlled. He questioned whether American leaders fully appreciate the risks associated with the country's continuing nuclear modernization program. These dangers underscore the broader critique presented in the panel's description: The military-industrial complex is not merely a collection of weapons manufacturers. It is an interconnected system of political power, institutional interests, military infrastructure, and economic priorities that perpetuates warfare. Meanwhile, public investments are routinely subjected to austerity arguments that rarely constrain military spending. Related: American Way of War: A Required Reading List What Kind of Security Are We Buying? Ultimately, the panel challenged the assumption that ever-increasing military expenditures necessarily make Americans safer. A nation can possess extraordinary destructive power while leaving its own communities underfunded, its veterans struggling, and populations overseas devastated by wars undertaken in the name of national security. Hoh's testimony gave that contradiction a profoundly human dimension. Freeman's questions connected those individual experiences to the larger challenge of changing American foreign policy before another generation suffers the same consequences. The issue is not simply whether the United States spends too much on its military. It is whether a political system that consistently prioritizes warfare over human needs can genuinely claim to be protecting its people. The question Americans must confront is not whether we can afford peace, but how much longer we can afford the human, moral, and economic costs of endless war.

One day in 1964, at the age of nineteen, I rode around Nagasaki on a tour bus with a Japanese friend. Profoundly ignorant of Japan, I was ill equipped to process the sights we saw—the broken ruins of Urakami Cathedral, near the hypocenter, and the peace park with its monumental statue sitting atop the hill, right hand pointing to the sky as if warning of danger from above, left hand extended palm down as if urging peace and harmony, eyes closed as if in prayer. Nor was I prepared for the city’s A-bomb museum. In one room, there was a section of the wooden outer wall of a building, unremarkable except for the human silhouette burned into it. As I stood there trying to take in what I was seeing, I realized I was surrounded by Japanese people speaking softly to each other. I had no idea what they were saying, but it dawned on me that I was the only American in a room full of Japanese looking at exhibits of the horrors other Americans had inflicted on their city. Terrified, I fled and cowered in the back of the bus, knowing that all those Japanese would be getting back on, that my friend would be getting back on, and wondering how I would face them. I realized, eventually, that I was not personally responsible for what other Americans had done. But I realized also that I was not off the hook, that it was my responsibility to do whatever I could to help make sure nothing like that ever happened again. In a single night in Tokyo, incendiary bombs caused a fire storm that killed a hundred thousand. For many years, I taught at a college where we took K-12 educators to Japan, and we always took them to Hiroshima. When they emerged from the museum, some were white-faced and silent, others weeping openly. Others will equivocate, unwilling to accept the reality of what ordinary and fundamentally decent people are capable of doing to each other. Most Americans these days know little or nothing about the American destruction of Japan. It's not part of their lived experience, and more recent horrors have taken stage front: the devastation of 9/11, the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq, the death toll from the pandemic, the seizure of Venezuela, the ongoing war with Iran, and the president’s cavalier trivialization of the suffering he has caused. A plaza just outside 780 Third Avenue in Midtown Manhattan, brimming with well-tended flowers in warm weather and furnished with elegant stone benches, looks like an inviting place to sit down and enjoy a slice of pizza. But POPS violations, in which the public are denied access to these spaces by the companies who received the legal clearance to build additional floors, are common. Much of the real estate industry bitterly resents having to provide any public goods despite having legally agreed to do so. They prefer to take up space in our skies without providing anything in return. Phillips, particularly incensed after carrying out his legal research and learning he was in the right to sit on the bench, went back to 780 Third Avenue the next day and told the security guard that the plaza was a public park. The evening of my visit, as we sit in the sunken plaza, a security guard watches us carefully and keeps talking into his phone. Just two days earlier, at the same spot, Phillips was removed by security. Rabid Anti-Communism Still Shapes the Politics of California

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Michael G. Vann ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-01T15:16:29+00:00

Donald Trump’s political vocabulary is not especially original. When he calls Democrats “radical left,” “Marxists,” or “hardcore godless communists,” he recycles one of the oldest weapons in American political life: the charge that political opponents are not merely wrong but fundamentally un-American because of their alleged leftist ideology. In his rambling social media posts and ham-fisted rants, Trump uses anti-communism as a blunt political instrument. He labels proposals for expanded health care, rent control, stronger unions, and higher taxes on the wealthy as evidence of some sort of communist conspiracy. Obviously, this is not analytical precision but brutish intimidation. California, home of coastal elites who pride themselves on leading a progressive bastion of resistance to MAGA, should not dismiss this rhetoric as something imported from the reactionary red states of flyover country. Anti-communism has deep roots in the Golden State’s political history. In California, the legal and cultural architecture of the Cold War survives today. California’s Cold War The irony is as beautiful as a Malibu sunset. Trump and other right-wing ideologues routinely describe California as a “communist” state, an absurd insult to direct at one of the most heavily capitalist jurisdictions on Earth. California is home to Silicon Valley, Hollywood, immense agricultural corporations, vast real estate fortunes, and some of the most concentrated private wealth in the United States if not the world. Calling California “communist” is not political analysis; it is a silly denunciation ritual. But it is a ritual with a history behind it. In the late 1940s and early 1950s, California was a laboratory for Cold War repression. With the 1950 Levering Act, the state legislature required public employees to take a loyalty oath. The law reflected a climate where politicians claimed Communist Party members were an internal enemy seeking to infiltrate public institutions such as schools and universities. University of California professors who refused to sign the oath lost their positions. As a graduate student teaching assistant at the University of California, Santa Cruz, in 1990, I was required to sign an iteration of the oath in my onboarding paperwork. Californian anti-communism produced the Cold War’s most consequential conservative presidents. Richard Nixon began his career as a California congressman by pursuing Alger Hiss through the House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC). This red-baiting made him a nationally recognized figure. A middling Hollywood actor who had been elected as president of the Screen Actors Guild in 1947, Ronald Reagan willingly collaborated with HUAC. In the 1950s, he starred on a weekly television show called “General Electric Theater.” As well as introducing each episode, Reagan traveled across the country to tour GE plants and deliver free-market, anti-communist speeches. He then starred in 1962’s “The Truth About Communism,” a propaganda film whose IMDb page includes a memorable line: “Top Cast: Adolf Hitler — Aleksandr Kerensky — Ronald Reagan.” After he retired from acting, Reagan entered politics by attacking the University of California, especially its Berkeley campus, as a symbol of decadence and decline. During his 1966 gubernatorial campaign, he conflated student protests, antiwar activism, drugs, and sexual freedom with alleged communist influence. Promising to “clean up the mess at Berkeley,” he converted hostility to campus dissent into a central conservative political issue. Governor Reagan’s contempt for the Berkeley Free Speech Movement is the direct ancestor of Trump’s vilification of Columbia’s student encampment for Gaza. There is a line from Sproul Plaza to Hind Hall. Legacies of Anti-Communism Both Reagan and Nixon understood the domestic potential of anti-communism to discredit liberal institutions, discipline the Left, and mobilize conservative voters. Their California roots led to presidencies defined by military escalation, repression, covert intervention, and the assault on New Deal and Great Society liberalism. With the Cold War now a fading memory, California’s anti-communist laws seemed increasingly anachronistic when State Senator Alan Lowenthal introduced Senate Bill 1322 in 2008. This would have repealed provisions that allowed the dismissal of public employees for Communist Party membership and removed related Cold War language from the state codes. The bill passed both legislative chambers but then ran into a successful countermobilization by the Vietnamese American community. Vietnamese American lobbyists and politicians campaigned against it. Activists, including lawyer Nguyễn Quốc Lân, urged Republican Governor Arnold Schwarzenegger to veto the bill, for fear that it would permit communists to teach in California schools. Schwarzenegger answered their call, invoking the experience of Californians who had fled communist regimes and arguing that the state should keep the existing laws in place. The episode demonstrates how anti-communism survives in the Golden State. Senate Bill 1322 would hardly have legalized violent subversion or served as a pathway to revolution. It would merely have removed archaic penalties for political affiliation while preserving the state’s power to punish advocacy of its violent overthrow. But refugees from one of the most successful revolutions in world history transformed a narrow civil liberties reform into a supposed threat to public safety. The law’s legacy remains embedded in California’s legal bureaucracy. Government Code sections 3100–3109 still require public employees to take an oath before entering state employment. Currently the oath requires employees to support and defend the US and California constitutions, disavowing violent, unlawful revolutionary organizations. Today California employees are not required to utter the clichéd declaration, “I am not nor have I ever been a member of the Communist Party.” Explicit Cold War anti-communism has given way to a broader prohibition on organizations advocating the overthrow of the government. But the relevant provisions flow directly from laws designed specifically to target Communist affiliation, while other sections of the Government Code still refer explicitly to Communist Party membership in background checks. Diaspora Politics Californian anti-communism also lives on in diaspora politics. After the collapse of the US-backed South Vietnamese regime in 1975, hundreds of thousands of Vietnamese refugees arrived in the Golden State. Southern California became the center of the largest Vietnamese community outside Vietnam. Those who fled the revolution consider April 30, 1975, to have been the date of the “fall of Saigon.” The victorious Hanoi government prefers to speak of the city’s “liberation” (before it was promptly renamed Ho Chi Minh City). These new Californians refused to celebrate April 30 as the achievement of national unification. For them, “Black April” is a moment of catastrophe. For these refugees and their descendants, the Republic of Vietnam’s yellow flag with three red stripes represents a lost homeland, family sacrifice, and displacement, but above all a mythologized resistance to communist tyranny in the name of freedom. Just as Miami’s Little Havana recalls pre–Fidel Castro Cuba with nostalgia, and West LA’s “Tehrangeles” reimagines the shah’s Iran as an era of progressive modernity, Orange County’s Little Saigon and San Jose’s Vietnamese neighborhoods invoke the alleged heroism of the Army of the Republic of Vietnam. In these circles, discussion of endemic corruption, rigged elections, and bustling torture rooms under US client dictatorships is not welcome. These exiles silence the memory of Fulgencio Batista’s gangsterism, the brutality of Iran’s Bureau for Intelligence and for Security of the State (SAVAK), and the tiger cages of the Saigon regime. These newly enfranchised Southeast Asian Californians made common cause with established white anti-communist Californians. In the reactionary Reagan era of the 1980s, firebrand conservative and Vietnam combat veteran Bob Dornan moved from liberal West LA to Orange County. Behind the Orange Curtain, Dornan exploited the trauma of Westminster’s Vietnamese refugees for his political career. In the House of Representatives, while the Californian Republican was not particularly effective in pursuing a legislative agenda, he proved to be one of the most vocal cheerleaders for Reagan’s global anti-communist crusade. As these Vietnamese émigrés passed their rabid anti-communism onto their children, the American-born generation continued to fight their lost cause. Despite his white nationalist anti-immigrant rhetoric and policies, Trump found a pool of MAGA loyalists in Little Saigon. In some of the most surreal images from January 6, 2021, Californian Vietnamese Americans marched on the US Capitol waving the South Vietnamese flag. A Bipartisan Effort In California, anti-communism is a bipartisan effort. In 2024, Democratic State Assembly member Stephanie Nguyen and her Republican colleague Tri Ta took issue with Los Angeles County’s decision to declare April 30 “Jane Fonda Day” in honor of her decades of progressive activism. Nguyen and Ta argued that celebrating the woman dubbed “Hanoi Jane” by her detractors on April 30 was deeply hurtful to the Vietnamese community, which claims a monopoly of the date for their anti-communist rituals. Right-wing veterans and their supporters, who had never forgiven the young Fonda’s 1972 peace mission to North Vietnam, supported the protest. California anti-communism can also take on much cruder forms. At a Trump-inspired anti-communist demonstration on Huntington Beach in 2017, a group of white men chanted , “You can’t run, you can’t hide, you’re going to get a helicopter ride,” while wearing shirts that depicted bodies falling from helicopters, invoking a global history of anti-communist state terror. While Trump’s anti-communism is opportunistic, vulgar, and intellectually empty, it is effective because it draws upon institutions and memories that long predate him. Of course, California is not and has never been communist, but it has been and remains deeply shaped by anti-communism. Democratic voters overwhelmingly support the public ownership and operation of several key industries. At least one of the Democratic politicians who want to win the party’s next presidential primary should take note. A poll I recently commissioned from the Center for Strategic Politics found that a supermajority of Democratic voters support public ownership and operation of health care and the electrical grid. A slight majority supports public ownership of the nation’s railroads. And far more voters said they would support public ownership and operation of artificial intelligence than opposed it. Per other polling conducted back in June, Bernie Sanders’s proposal to partially nationalize AI companies is also incredibly popular across the political spectrum. Maybe it’s the number of socialists holding public office now, or maybe it’s the Trump administration’s habit of demanding stakes in private companies, but public ownership isn’t all that taboo anymore. Not if you trust the polls. And at least in American politics, polling has been having a moment for the last few years. Political consultants and talking heads are paid handsomely to tell politicians and partisans of both parties that they really need to consult the latest polls before deciding how they’ll campaign. Besides the inherent issues with such an approach, polling is usually a poor way to find a novel policy proposal or a topic that candidates could win a campaign by bearing down on. Ergo, we don’t see many polls that directly ask whether voters would support nationalizing parts of the economy, aside from questions about Medicare for All. But we do know that nationalization can be very popular. Across gender, age group, ethnicity, and political leaning, and across polls, the economy or the cost of living is by far the issue cited most frequently as deciding Americans’ votes. The poll I commissioned showed that grocery stores are one of the industries Democrats are least interested in taking into public ownership, but piloting municipally owned groceries was a key platform plank of the wildly successful Zohran Mamdani mayoral campaign. Calls for a larger public stake in or full public ownership of other industries are far more likely to be political winners: thanks to the Hugging Face hack and similar fiascos, the public is increasingly concerned about recent developments in artificial intelligence. The war with Iran and increased demand are driving up electricity prices across the United States. And private health care and health insurance are just inherently disastrous. Right now, Democratic voters still haven’t fully lined up behind any of the major presidential hopefuls. The largest bloc across almost every poll says it will support Kamala Harris, but a half-dozen other candidates are also polling relatively well. Most notably, democratic socialist Congresswoman Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez usually ends up in the top tier of serious contenders. The poll results suggest that a properly motivated political entrepreneur could benefit from the American cost-of-living crisis quite easily. Just call to nationalize the industries that Americans want to see nationalized. If, as Substacker Matthew Yglesias suggests , the politically minded “should encourage candidates to embrace popular progressive causes,” then we should encourage candidates to embrace nationalization. Now, maybe ambitious Democrats should not call for the nationalization of restaurants, but at a minimum, the government taking a leading role in the rapidly developing AI sector and large swaths of the private health care system appears overwhelmingly popular. As a vision to rally around, it would at least help secure them the nomination. Harold Cruse, the Loneliest Black Radical Intellectual

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Alan Wald ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-01T15:16:29+00:00

Harold Wright Cruse (1916–2005), my colleague at the University of Michigan for decades, may have been the most flamboyantly paradoxical radical intellectual ever to roam our institution’s halls. An astonishing autodidact with a wealth of knowledge about cultural and political history, Cruse authored a foundational work in black studies: The Crisis of the Negro Intellectual: A Historical Analysis of the Failure of Black Leadership (1967). Now, two decades after his death, a short, pacy biography by Villanova University theologian and Africana studies scholar Vincent W. Lloyd titled Harold Cruse: Critic, Writer, Teacher captures many of Cruse’s idiosyncrasies to present a riot of contradictions. Cruse, a high school dropout with no college degrees, was recruited to the Michigan history department one year after the publication of Crisis , which quickly became a standard classroom text, often cited alongside Frantz Fanon’s The Wretched of the Earth (1961) and The Autobiography of Malcolm X (1965). Cruse’s book was a six-hundred-page tome bursting with insight and erudition but also ornery opinions about racial integration, black nationalism, and communism, not to mention uncharitable generalizations about women, Jews, and Caribbean immigrants. These were voiced, on and off the page, with absolute certainty and self-righteousness. His most scathing criticisms were aimed at leading African American activists and thinkers, and his style stood out for his penchant for hurling rhetorical grenades. His targets included civil rights organizations such as the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) and Martin Luther King Jr’s Southern Christian Leadership Conference, which he saw as “motivated by selfish class interests, because the main objective of the Negro middle class is a status and a social position approximating . . . the great Anglo-American middle class.” But more often, such “Cruse missiles” were launched at the very black leftists I was teaching and admired. Richard Wright, for example, my cultural lodestar since high school, was depicted, in language redolent of far-right Judeo-Bolshevik conspiracy theories, as “so ideologically blinded by the smog of Jewish-Marxist nationalism that he was unable to see his own clearly.” Lorraine Hansberry, James Baldwin, and Paul Robeson were part of a long list under fire for naiveté and a lack of independent, sophisticated thought. Hansberry, Cruse said, was the “Joan of Arc of People’s Integration,” and Baldwin “a rather innocent and provincial intellectual.” Robeson’s 1958 autobiographical manifesto, Here I Stand , was described as “a weak, muddled, sentimental book, suffused with innocent idealism and lacking a single paragraph of original thought on Negro problems.” Cruse’s overall literary legacy, consisting of three books and numerous essays, left an erratic track record: a baffling mix of acuity, wide reading, personal grudges, and dubious hot takes. He used Marxist terminology but was not a Marxist, for he saw the United States as a battleground of competing racial and ethnic groups more than a conflict between economic classes. He advocated black self-determination, but at the center was culture, not land, and his notion of culture was black art, theater, literature, and media, not the less elitist anthropological definition. For Cruse, Harlem was to be the center of black autonomy, even as over 90 percent of the black population lived outside Harlem with a vastly different cultural experience. Most problematic, as the late Trinity College Professor Jerry Gafio Watts observed in Heroism and the Black Intellectual (1994), is that he “wanted to frame the issues of black intellectual life and politics along a rather simpleminded axis between black nationalism and integrationism.” It was a questionable either-or version of the old race and class debate that still roils the Left in new forms. Cruse’s belief that black Americans should operate as an organized ethnic interest group to bargain for power failed to challenge the capitalist foundation of American society. Was there no way for a fight for cultural self-determination, racial pride, and democratic rights against a racially oppressive system to blend with class struggle, aiming at a society with economic and social justice for all? Lloyd even speculates that, until the end of his life, Cruse wrestled with the question: “What if separate but equal was not such a bad thing?” Portrait of a Person Lloyd’s approach to Cruse is more that of a storyteller than a historian. His focus is on the portrait of a person, not on defending or disputing Cruse’s political theories. Through a narrative based on numerous interviews and some original documentation, we soon learn that there were many Harold Cruses. That’s because Lloyd makes every effort “to allow the people who knew Cruse to speak for themselves.” People’s perceptions of memorable individuals vary by age and context, and the man who ultimately emerges from Lloyd’s portrait was not to everyone’s taste. As an authoritative spokesperson for a particular brand of African American self-determination in the 1960s and ’70s, Cruse was both adored by acolytes and despised by those with whom he differed or whom he had offended. Many, including me, admired his ambition, his ability to pose challenging questions, and his desire to promote African American intellectual thought. We liked that he didn’t pitch softballs but were flummoxed by shortcomings in his thinking — too many grandiose claims followed by superficial analysis. Lloyd himself is less drawn by any agreement with Cruse’s stances than by what he sees as the “critical dimension” and “spirit” of Cruse’s thought. Nevertheless, for those of us who knew Cruse, all this was complicated by how to negotiate a personal relationship with an imposing figure marked by a titanic but fragile ego. In five chapters, Lloyd offers a rather chatty survey that begins with Cruse’s working-class family background in Virginia, his hardscrabble youth with relatives in Harlem, and his military service in World War II. Then come a half-dozen years in the Communist Party USA (CPUSA) as a journalist and librarian, followed by life as a Greenwich Village bohemian with literary aspirations in theater and fiction. Cruse next shifts into black nationalist activism and social criticism with the Liberator Magazine and the Freedom Now Party. When Crisis becomes a national publishing sensation, he is hired as a visiting black studies instructor at the University of Michigan. He retires fifteen years later as a full professor, an “elder,” and something of a legend. Lloyd’s is a kind of magpie method, with proleptic leaps from the outset, but it proves surprisingly helpful in certain areas because, previously, only uneven source material had been available for many facets of his subject’s life. Lloyd knows how to distill the essential from the inessential and never allows archival minutiae to submerge the core concern. Moreover, Lloyd is no slouch in the revelation department. Cruse was ostentatious about name-dropping and telling tales of his army experiences in North Africa and Italy, as well as his 1960 trip to Cuba sponsored by the Fair Play for Cuba Committee. But he was mostly tight-lipped about his personal and emotional past. This left some of us wondering how dark his secrets might be. As it turns out, most of Cruse’s closeted skeletons are the familiar human ones — a love life Lloyd can’t untangle and some problematic episodes with alcohol. On the other hand, there is the revelation from Freedom of Information Act records that Cruse “named about a dozen of his former [Communist] party associates as Communist Party members” to the FBI, even as he would later deny any such cooperation. Sometimes not all the facts in a person’s life are convenient, and often the lies we tell about the past seem necessary to live in the present. Still, this is a whopper that will leave many of his acquaintances with the taste of ashes in their mouths. Otherwise, what Lloyd unravels is a long life with many loops, interludes, and repetitions. At times, it’s something of a detective story as he tries to figure out whether Cruse was ever legally married, whether he abandoned a son in Europe, and whether the sweep of Cruse’s career reads more like a rise and slide than a rise and fall. After a twenty-year wait and many false starts, his final book, Plural but Equal: A Critical Study of Blacks and Minorities and America’s Plural Society , appeared in 1987. Instead of a late-life powerhouse performance, it was judged to add little to his thesis that the United States was a society of competing ethnic blocs, and it has been effectively out of print for nearly four decades. We learn about many elements of Cruse’s baroque personality — highly complex, dramatic, extravagant, and emotionally intense. With ramrod posture and a face often set in a dignified pose, he projected brooding bitterness and the aura of a man who felt personally wronged. At Michigan, he seemed to swing between episodes of immense energy and passion and periods of existential rumination, with students both benefiting and suffering. Through it all, Lloyd is remarkably nonjudgmental. He rightly seems to accept the mysteries of the shadowlands of human motivations. He has no desire to canonize a saint and recognizes that Cruse was somewhat intoxicated with his own sense of self-importance. I would also add that, intellectually, Cruse was the type who always needed to have a foil. The Boundary Crosser For me, the arc of Cruse’s life felt awe-inspiring. A true boundary crosser, he was catapulted from plebeian marginality to superstar fame, mainly through ulcer-producing hard work and an authentic, self-authored identity that I associated with my own Marxist-existentialist aspirations. Lloyd, however, shows that certain fortuitous friendships (a Jewish couple with publishing connections) and political coincidences (a black student revolt at the University of Michigan) also helped his rise. Yet despite our fundamentally different political orientations, some of us on the Left were attracted to Cruse’s intellectual stridency, not to mention the exciting charge of his transgressive opinions. Attacking orthodoxies and stomping on taboos — what’s not to like? After all, I, too, was working on a book-length call to arms for a new approach based on a critical reassessment of the Left’s past record on culture and intellectuals. That my focus was on Jews, and that I was Jewish, never seemed to be a problem for him, despite his oft-expressed wariness of Jewish Marxist intellectuals. Moreover, having read much C. L. R. James and George Breitman’s 1967 book, The Last Year of Malcolm X , I was open to the idea of an independent black liberation movement as a vanguard catalyst that did not need to subordinate itself to a conservatized white working class. To be sure, I was hardly an expert on the chameleon ideology of nationalism, but I sympathized with anti-colonialism and opposed expansionist and chauvinistic nationalism. One could be suspicious of any claims of nationalist belonging and yet reject the facile stereotype of all varieties of nationalism as equally essentialist and ethnocentric. I felt most politically at odds with Cruse on the centrality of intellectuals, ultimate economic goals, and the black movement’s relationship to the white working class. As for his treatment of the CPUSA, my own research had convinced me that one can be correct about its Stalinist politics but wrong about almost everything else. Cruse had grossly misrepresented both the sincerity and the impact of the CPUSA members’ commitment to anti-racism, and in this regard Crisis read too much like a pulpy novel of revenge. Lloyd’s interview findings match my own experiences with Cruse. I first met him at events sponsored by Michigan’s Center for Afroamerican and African Studies in 1975, just after his brief term as an early director. After several discussions about his CPUSA experiences, he gave me an autographed copy of a long and remarkable course description for “Marxism and the Negro Question,” a class he took at the CPUSA’s Jefferson School in the late 1940s. Then, in the fall of 1979, he requested a favor: Would I take over some reviewing assignments he had promised the socialist newspaper In These Times ? This would start with James Baldwin’s new novel Just Above My Head (1979), which he found “unreadable.” As an untenured nobody, I was quite flattered, and this review led to eight more, on writers such as Hansberry, W. E. B. Du Bois, and my fellow English department member Gayl Jones. For the next fifteen years, during which time he officially retired in 1984, we continued our association, although I mainly queried him for information on his CPUSA comrades with little reciprocal curiosity. Then, in the spring of 1992, I invited the emeritus Cruse to address two sessions of a seminar I had organized on the cultural left in the 1950s; the plan was to juxtapose chapters of Crisis against one of Cruse’s objects of criticism — the novel Youngblood (1954) by the pro-Communist leader of the Harlem Writers Guild, John Oliver Killens. Cruse seemed pleased, and I opened the first class with a fulsome introduction, including a biographical sketch with highlights of his career that I thought he might have been too modest to mention himself. To my surprise, the discussion that ensued around Killens was stilted and relatively unproductive. When I met with Cruse afterward, he gave me a sharp dressing-down; nothing I had said was inaccurate or disrespectful, but he had wanted to represent himself, not have me do it for him. This was followed by a letter explaining that he would not return to the seminar, but for a different reason: “To be honest, I never finished reading Youngblood after it was published. For me, the novel possessed no literary merit.” Sheesh , I thought, why didn’t he say that in the first place ? Lloyd’s biography, however, discloses a history of similar behavior — walking offstage, hanging up mid-conversation, and precipitously dumping a graduate student — and I likely got off easy. Just four months later, we were back in dialogue. He wanted to learn what I had heard about Van Gosse’s book in progress about the Fair Play for Cuba Committee, although he did manage to stick in an admonishment about my allegedly teaching Youngblood as a “masterpiece.” The point is that Cruse had an unsleeping vigilance for any possible slight, which was a significant element in his alienation from the CPUSA. As Lloyd reveals, Hansberry and others he saw as an insider group under CPUSA control rebuffed his aspirations to write for the theater in the 1950s. And as Lloyd convincingly argues, “Cruse’s ideas . . . grew out of Cruse’s life.” But too much of it was a synthetic certainty about the motives and achievements of a bricolage of black communist cultural dupes: “White intellectuals are unoriginal, imitative, and puerile, and black intellectuals are brainwashed by the whites.” Throughout his writing, he hammers this theme like Vulcan, rolling over it relentlessly from new angles — casting would-be African American radical intellectuals as betrayed by West Indians, manipulated by Jews, and undermined by black women. No wonder one of Cruse’s most impressive legacies has been highly sophisticated, well-researched refutations, starting with the concluding section of Winston James’s Holding Aloft the Banner of Ethiopia: Caribbean Radicalism in Early Twentieth Century America (1999). The longer one ponders Cruse, the more enigmatic his beguiling strangeness becomes. Yes, he was always available for discussion and consultation, yet this seemed tethered to a thirst for adulation. His inner life is not my story to tell, and he had a perpetually inscrutable aura. I quickly saw I would never be the yin to his yang — far from it. It was hard not to sense that his personality contained many combustible elements that, mixed at high velocity, might explode. I, and probably many others, handled him with kid gloves. But did I ever actually see him for who he was? Perhaps the crucial insight comes from a private letter to his mother that Lloyd quotes early in his biography: “I have grown up mostly alone. I have always been forced to be reliant on no one but myself.” Simone de Beauvoir, among others, has written about the deep emotional isolation that comes from complete self-reliance, but surely the price is tenfold for a working-class African American like Cruse aspiring to literary fame. His may superficially resemble the familiar radical individualism of those of us who reject what we believe are prepackaged identities handed down by others. But for Cruse, it was a new and self-owned identity agonizingly forged in the crucible of a white-racist and class-divided society. Cruse was no doubt as guilty as the rest of us at times in acquiescing to opportunistic acts of self-preservation. At Michigan, although he taught pioneering and successful classes, he was no ally in the activist campaigns against South African apartheid and US intervention in Latin America. He sneered at most campus activism. If he was not the leading man, he wasn’t going to play a supporting role. Moreover, his anti-communism fed Cold War myths, and many of his attitudes about gender were retrograde even by the standards of his generation. Although commonly regarded as a black cultural nationalist, he was hypercritical of the movement’s leaders and a splinter in its toe as well. Nevertheless, he is hardly alone among his generation in being disoriented by the Stalinist experience, and I see his obsessive writing as testimony to an undying struggle to define his own life and create his own meaning. The loneliness of the black radical intellectual was perhaps the most direct price he paid for this autonomy. He identified five major expenses undermining household finances: childcare, higher education, housing, health care, and high-interest debt. Reducing these costs is essential, he argued, but it addresses only half the problem. The other half is increasing what working people earn. "Democrats [are] running around the country and talking about affordability," he said. He points to prosperous democracies that combine market economies with strong labor protections, public services, and government responsibility for economic security. First, progressives must organize within the party's existing institutions, seeking influence over local organizations, state parties, national committees, and the rules governing electoral contests. Second, they must win primary elections, replacing establishment politicians with candidates committed to working-class economic policies. These efforts must reinforce one another. Progressive victories in primary elections can alter the direction of the party's elected leadership, while changes to internal party structures can make future progressive campaigns more competitive. Minsky also emphasized the importance of coordinated electoral organizing. Electing a few progressive members of Congress, he argued, is insufficient when meaningful legislative power requires a governing majority. "You got to get to 218," he observed, referring to the number of seats needed for a majority in the House of Representatives. Progressive organizations must therefore think beyond isolated victories and develop the capacity to win power nationally. Democracy Under a Stress Test Hovering over the discussion was Minsky's concern that the American constitutional system faces an extraordinary challenge. He described the 2026 midterm elections as a potential turning point in whether democratic institutions can withstand authoritarian pressure. Minsky argued that Americans must organize to defeat Republican candidates while continuing to challenge establishment Democrats within their own party. Importantly, he acknowledged that his strategy depends on the continued viability of democratic elections. If electoral outcomes cease to be respected, he suggested, the left may need to reconsider its political methods altogether. That possibility gave the discussion its particular urgency. From Resistance to Political Power Minsky's presentation reflected the central theme of Left Coast Forum 2026: "Building Power in an Age of Crisis: From Resistance to Reconstruction." His message was that opposition to authoritarianism cannot remain simply an exercise in protest. Movements must develop the organizational strength to win elections, enact legislation, and fundamentally reshape the economy. The discussion also demonstrated that progressives remain deeply divided about the best path forward. Some see the Democratic Party as an indispensable instrument for building political power. Others regard it as an obstacle that must ultimately be replaced. Minsky does not dismiss the party's failures. Indeed, he considers those failures central to explaining the country's current political crisis. But he believes the possibility of transforming the Democratic Party remains real, and that the opportunity must be seized before it disappears. The challenge he left with the audience was not simply whether progressives could win more elections. It was whether the American left could build a political movement strong enough to govern—and deliver the fundamental changes that ordinary Americans have been waiting for.
