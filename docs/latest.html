With Anti-DSA Bill, Congress Is Red-Baiting Like It’s 1955

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Ben Burgis ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-03T17:50:59+00:00

As summer turns into fall, the United States remains mired in a wildly unpopular war in Iran. On the home front, inflation is stubbornly persistent. All indications point to the president’s party suffering major losses in the November midterms — if, that is, the various Republican attempts to interfere with the integrity of the election fall short.Even with all this going on, though, the House found time on Tuesday to pass a resolution symbolically condemning the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) and “all forms of socialism,” quite obviously inspired by Zohran Mamdani’s mayoralty and the primary victories of several DSA-backed congressional candidates.The anti-DSA bill is a strange document. It starts so similarly to the other congressional resolutions condemning socialism over the last two years that I initially wondered whether the authors had simply cut and pasted an earlier one.Whereas socialist ideology necessitates a concentration of power that has, time and time again, collapsed into communist regimes, totalitarian rule, and repressive dictatorships;Whereas socialism has repeatedly led to famine and mass murders, and the killing of over 100,000,000 people worldwide;Then it cuts to up-to-the-minute complaints about DSA and the socialist mayor of New York.Whereas, since the November 2025 New York City mayoral election of a Democratic Socialist, candidates for public office affiliated with the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) have run in over one hundred elections in at least half the states;Whereas the DSA’s ideological tenet labeled “Democracy for All” calls for noncitizens and incarcerated criminals to vote in American elections;Whereas the DSA’s ideological tenet labeled “A Democratic Congress” calls for abolishing the U.S. Senate, implementing ranked choice voting in all elections, and replacing the two-party system;Whereas, on February 19, 2026, the DSA stated “[t]he United States of America has never been a democracy”;Whereas the DSA’s ideology and socialism, in any form, are incompatible with American values and the United States Constitution;In the final “resolved” section, the House “condemns and denounces socialism in all its forms, including the Democratic Socialists of America, and opposes the implementation of socialist policies in the United States.” But between here and there are hundreds of words of phantasmagoric Trumpian fearmongering about noncitizens illegally registering to vote in American elections. The only tenuous connection is that DSA’s platform calls for changing the laws so that everyone who lives in the United States is allowed to vote. But it reads as if whoever drafted the resolution had a particularly hysterical Fox News segment about noncitizen voting playing in the background, got distracted, and switched topics halfway through.The resolution’s nominal argument is a mess. The first two “whereas” clauses are claims about the crimes of Stalinist regimes. But democratic socialists have always been critical of such regimes, even then. Condemning democratic socialists with claims about the crimes of Stalinist authoritarianism makes about as much sense as an atheist bringing up the Spanish Inquisition to condemn the Southern Baptists.Even rhetorically, the resolution’s strategy of lumping democratic socialists with authoritarian states is dramatically undercut by clauses about DSA’s calls to expand voting rights, abolish the Senate, and enact ranked-choice voting to make it easier for third parties to compete. Letting more people vote, making political representation proportional to the number of voters in each state, and expanding the options on Americans’ ballots would take us much further from anything resembling an authoritarian one-party state.So too little democracy or too much — which is it? One suspects that the latter is the more frightening option for signatories.Playing up crimes committed by Stalinist dictators (whether real ones, Cold War exaggerations, or some messy mixture of the two) is a time-tested way of making socialism sound scary. But ironically, the fearmongering gestures here at the notion that America needs a powerful right-wing authoritarian government to protect us from the socialist menace, which seeks to introduce more democracy at the nation’s peril. Why else would a resolution condemning “all forms of socialism” include hundreds of words on the alleged plague of noncitizen voting and the importance of making it harder to vote?The good news is that the propaganda doesn’t seem to be working on Americans. Far from rallying behind President Donald Trump as an American Julius Caesar who will save the republic from itself by taking on authoritarian powers, the public has made him historically unpopular. Far from DSA being exiled to the margins of American politics, a recent Pew poll found that Democrats and Democratic-leaning independents are far likelier to like leaders who identify as democratic socialists (32 percent) than to dislike them (11 percent). And centrist Democrats in Congress were leery enough of alienating their own constituents that only eight House Democrats joined the 212 House Republicans in voting for it.The Republican-dominated Congress thinks that the mention of the S-word will inspire terror in the hearts of patriotic Americans, as though this were 1955. But it’s wrong to assume the American people are on board.

The Rise of the Billionaire Lobby

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Ben Case ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-03T16:24:02+00:00

You’ve heard of the oil lobby, the tobacco lobby, the Israel lobby, the charter school lobby. Behind all of these are billionaires who benefit from the underlying industries or sectors, and whose corporations use their resources to lobby the government for favorable policies. Now, in reaction to a California ballot initiative for the world’s first billionaire tax, we have an innovation: a lobby for billionaires themselves. Self-advocacy by wealthy people is nothing new, of course. But faced with the prospect of persuading voters to reject Proposition 40 — a temporary tax on roughly two hundred unfathomably rich individuals to fund health care for millions of people — billionaires must make the case to the public that forcing them to share a small amount of their fortunes would do more harm than good. At a time when half of American families can’t afford basic necessities, and 95 percent of the country understands that we’re in an affordability crisis, that’s going to be a tough sell. A handful of billionaires led by Google cofounder Sergey Brin are out to prove that money can buy anything. They are pouring unprecedented resources into a political campaign to convince — or if that doesn’t work, to trick — California voters into prioritizing billionaires’ wealth over their own livelihoods. When Billionaires Don’t Get Their Way In October 2025, a labor union of California hospital and health care workers announced their intention to qualify a billionaire tax initiative for the state ballot. Written in collaboration with leading progressive economists like Emmanuel Saez and Robert Reich , it would levy a onetime 5 percent tax on the net assets of billionaires residing in California as of January 1, 2026. The tax would raise $100 billion in one shot, $90 billion of which would be used to fund Medi-Cal, the state’s Medicaid program, which is otherwise facing a dire fiscal cliff thanks to Donald Trump’s One Big Beautiful Bill. The remaining $10 billion would go into public education. By December, before the campaign was even circulating petitions, billionaires like Peter Thiel, Sergey Brin, and Larry Page were rushing to move assets out of state. Whether or not they’d end up avoiding the tax (or actually stop spending time and money in California), they’d jump-start the narrative that taxing billionaires would chase them away — a story the billionaire lobby would lean heavily on. A small cohort of billionaires began organizing to undermine the billionaire tax and keep it from reaching voters. They flooded the market for paid signature-gatherers (which all large initiative campaigns use even if volunteers do the bulk of the work) and allegedly even attempted to buy out a key canvassing firm. These efforts multiplied costs for the billionaire tax campaign, while the glut of clipboard-wielding canvassers on the streets made the job of billionaire tax volunteers significantly more difficult. The billionaires also enlisted the help of Governor Gavin Newsom. Shortly after chatting with Brin about the matter at a crypto mogul’s holiday party, Newsom took a stand against the billionaire tax, vowing he’d defeat the campaign one way or another. Following the lead of Newsom and billionaire donors, a handful of liberal nonprofits and labor unions came out against the billionaire tax as well, joining forces with Republicans and business groups to defeat it — all without an alternative plan to fund Medi-Cal once Trump’s cuts kick in next year. So far, all these efforts have come to naught. The billionaire tax qualified by submitting over 1.6 million signatures, more than double the required number, becoming Proposition 40 on November’s ballot. It’s since been endorsed by the California Federation of Labor Unions and a host of other labor groups, the California Nurses Association, and recently the state Democratic Party. Now Brin and his billionaires must convince a majority of voters that closing hospitals, clinics, and emergency rooms is preferable to taxing billionaires who won’t miss the money. Behold a Billionaire Lobby Brin’s anti–Proposition 40 organization is called Building a Better California. It’s the kind of innocuous and unmemorable name given to so many political nonprofits, in this case also seeming to hint at Joe Biden’s bygone Build Back Better infrastructure bill, perhaps to remind us of the billionaire lobby’s bipartisan nature. Building a Better California has already raised more than $150 million, most of it from Brin himself. The group recently dropped its first major advertisement buy, a working-class-centered commercial that also highlights Newsom’s opposition to Prop 40. The ad mentions neither the billionaires themselves nor the fact that Newsom is a longtime personal friend of Brin’s. Brin’s group is expected to spend upward of $90 million on ads, an airtime spending advantage eleven thousand times that of Prop 40’s current budget. The group has already blanketed the terrain with mailers, sending colorful foldouts to every California household warning that the billionaire tax will destroy the state. The purpose, it seems, is to spread confusion and misinformation, and most of all to divert attention from the jaw-dropping profits billionaires have been raking in as day-to-day life gets harder for most Americans. Brin alone, who theoretically would owe something like $9 billion should Prop 40 pass, grew his net wealth by around $100 billion in the past year alone . Billionaires do themselves few favors when they pull back the curtain, giving the public a glimpse of their personalities and priorities. Take, for example, Peter Thiel’s obsession with the Antichrist and Elon Musk’s “transhumanist” desire to implant AI in your brain. Consequently, rather than appealing to the public outright, they know it’s smarter to throw the public off the scent, which is Building a Better California’s raison d’être . The most rational argument against a billionaire tax is that the superrich will relocate to other states, as Thiel, Brin, and Page claim to have done (though some argue the moves were only on paper), depriving California of both the jobs and the taxes these individuals supposedly supply. But this idea has been roundly debunked, from academic studies to recent examples like the 2022 Massachusetts millionaire’s tax, after which the number of millionaires in the Bay State actually increased , with the tax generating billions more in revenue than anticipated. The fact that the billionaire flight argument is still rolled out makes it clear that it is not an evidence-based statement but a threat, albeit a relatively empty one. Texas billionaire Mark Cuban voiced this directly when he said he’d refuse to invest in any California company if Prop 40 passes. The billionaires are essentially saying to the public: “You can’t tax us, otherwise we’ll leave, and then you won’t be able to tax us.” The inherent flimsiness of this argument enhances the need to keep fearmongering , sans credible evidence or details, that the tax will collapse the economy. Evasions abound. Interviewed about Prop 40 on California Insider , Stanford economics professor Joshua Rauh began with the capital flight argument, but before long his commentary took a revealing turn. “There is a principle here of protecting private property,” he said. “It’s a very slippery slope, because who is actually wealthy?” The question was asked with an air of Socratic profundity, even though Prop 40 very clearly defines the taxable group as individuals who own more than $1 billion in assets. “From a philosophical standpoint,” Rauh went on, “would you want the same treatment as what we’re doing to billionaires?” The billionaire lobby needs to convince voters to identify with them — despite the fact that, aside from cellular-level processes like the Krebs cycle, a working person has nothing in common with a tech oligarch worth $258 billion. But Rauh came dangerously close to saying the quiet part out loud. The very point of redistributive proposals like the billionaire tax is that everyone else wants some of the same treatment given to billionaires. Most people pay a much higher real percentage of taxes than billionaires do and yet still struggle to get by. Most people can’t afford to pursue their dreams and passions like billionaires can. And politicians don’t prioritize most people’s needs the way they do billionaires’. Perhaps anticipating their persuasion falling short with the public, even with the money they’re planning to burn on it, the billionaire lobby has a backup plan. Brin personally donated over $100 million to qualify two poison pill initiatives, Propositions 41 and 42, which at a glance appear unrelated to Prop 40 but would nullify the billionaire tax if passed with a higher vote count. To the billionaire class, voters are nuisances to be interfaced with only when strictly necessary. If an unwanted issue can be kept off the ballot, all the better. If not, try to convince people it will spell destruction. Failing that, simply trick them into voting contrary to their own wishes. A Mask-Off Moment For the Superrich The billionaire lobby as it’s being constructed in California is a reaction to Proposition 40, but it’s also reflective of a moment when there are more billionaires in and around the White House than ever before. Billionaires have grown their personal wealth by orders of magnitude as daily life grinds down just about everyone else. The recent acceleration is part of a half-century-long class war offensive in which nearly $80 trillion has been siphoned from the working and middle classes to the superrich. The California billionaire tax is an attempt to protect health care for millions and health care sector jobs for hundreds of thousands. It also says, in clear and concrete terms, that billionaires have taken too much, and that it’s time to use some of that wealth for the public good. And because it is being put up for a majority vote as a ballot initiative, the billionaires are being forced to have a public conversation about their wealth. The billionaire lobby’s purpose, then, is not only to defeat Prop 40 but to justify billionaires’ existence and their place as society’s ultimate decision-makers.

We Must Talk about Denuclearizing Israel

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 / 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Socialist Forum ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-03T13:39:45+00:00

There is a simple action DSA can take to dramatically change the relationship between the United States and the genocidal regime in Israel. Start talking about their nukes. Openly, aggressively. Denounce Israel as a rogue nuclear state. Since Israel began developing its nuclear program in the 1950s, its nuclear capabilities have remained the subject of longstanding secrecy and diplomatic silence. The U.S. government has refused to acknowledge Israeli weapons for a variety of reasons; a big one is that a Presidential determination would trigger the stiff sanctions in Sec. 102(B) of the Arms Export Control Act. By acknowledging and denouncing Israel’s nuclear arsenal, DSA can force political recognition of these weapons and inspire action, such as imposing sanctions on Israel. Furthermore, the non-recognition of Israeli nuclear weapons creates a political asymmetry in which Iranians become the “bad” guys for wanting to deter Israeli nuclear aggression. If Iran wanted to acquire such weapons, it would not be doing so in a vacuum as though a bunch of mavericks had suddenly introduced nuclear weapons into the Middle East for the first time. Israel already possesses them and is obsessed with destroying the Iranian state. In other words, the public logic of the aggression against Iran, which was launched with the atomically thin justification of denying Iran nuclear weapons, depends upon the continued non-recognition of Israel’s own nuclear arsenal. The imperialist line is even more absurd given that at least until the February 2026 U.S. assassination of Ayatollah Khamenei, Iran had a fatwa (a religious ruling) against building such weapons. Moreover, what is known of Israel’s nuclear policy is that it is alone in the world in continuing to contemplate weapons use against its non-nuclear neighbors explicitly. Yet, this is not the half of it. Israel’s nuclear arsenal helps explain several otherwise puzzling aspects of the U.S.-Israel “special relationship.” Why does the genocidal regime in Tel Aviv exercise such extraordinary influence over U.S. policy? Why do countries around the world, despite widespread disgust at Israeli and American genocide and aggression, have been unwilling or unable to forcefully intervene? Why do the weaker nations of the world allow international law to be discarded so casually? What would be needed to stop Israel for good? The Israeli nuclear arsenal is a key under-discussed factor. The U.S. socialist movement, including our elected leaders, is not anywhere near where we need to be in terms of formal influence over public policy around these issues, yet this shouldn’t mean total silence. We have a bully pulpit from which to speak, and we must engage all levers of power to stop the genocidal Israeli grab for lebensraum. U.S. support for Israel cannot simply be explained by some timeless affinity with Jewish people, given the history of U.S. antisemitism. There are sticky factors that are tying American imperialism to this specific colonial outpost, and a big one is the bomb. Nuclear containment with flowers The American state consists of both a rational part and an ideological part. There must be something that is firmly convincing the rational actors to let the ideologues have their way. Israel is a tiny nuclear state with a huge victim complex that is highly militarily vulnerable should its subsidies be cut off. It is unique in that it is the only nuclear state that is not formally acknowledged. A formal admission by the U.S. that Israel is nuclear-armed would trigger domestic sanctions and begin major reorientations in the region. Of all the countries that have acquired nukes after the 1967 Non-Proliferation Treaty cutoff (the DPRK, India, Israel, and Pakistan), Pakistan and the DPRK are subject to sanctions. India was subject to stricter sanctions, but craftily cut a deal with the U.S. in 2008, and more of those sanctions are being removed . Israel alone has avoided sanctions entirely. Apartheid South Africa, a nuclear collaborator with Israel, also attempted this trick, but when the Afrikaner regime finally collapsed, they dismantled their small stockpile of nuclear weapons to avoid letting them fall into the hands of Black Africans. Notwithstanding the occasional slip-up, U.S. politicians do not acknowledge Israel’s nuclear status, and Israeli politicians are usually ( but not always ) oblique about it. This suppresses most official discussion of nuclear policy around Israel, though academics and journalists will sometimes speak up on the margins. Congresspeople do not discuss the issue publicly since it goes against the U.S.’s unofficial but rigid nuclear policy position of strategic ambiguity regarding Israel’s weapons program. It is a strange game where everyone knows the truth but does not speak it out loud, leaving Israel’s status suspended in the air. It is thought that Israel has boosted fission nuclear weapons and potentially untested thermonuclear and neutron bomb designs based on research data from its collaborations with France and South Africa. Modern boosted fission weapons are an order of magnitude more powerful than the two comparatively simple and morally shocking 15-20 kiloton city killers that America detonated in Japan. To give a sense of proportion, while American nuclear arsenals have had bombs up to 25 megatons in the 1960s, our modern strategy focuses on a greater number of lower-yield weapons in the hundred kiloton range. For the most part, this strategy is more efficient at destroying a given area than a larger bomb of equivalent strength (though the big ones are still useful when used with inaccurate missiles). In order for a research program to develop reliable weapon designs, it is crucial to obtain accurate explosive test data. Despite U.S. denials, it is likely that Israel collected test data in partnership with South Africa; a part of a test sequence was observed in the 1979 VELA satellite incident when a break in the cloud cover revealed a tell-tale double flash over the Southern Indian Ocean. Seymour Hersh wrote there were three tests, and the one that was caught by VELA was a low-yield nuclear artillery shell according to multiple unnamed Israeli government sources in his 1991 book “The Samson Option.” (Hersh, p. 271) Said French nuclear physicist Francis Perrin, who participated in discussions about building the reactor, “We thought the Israeli bomb was aimed at the Americans…. Not to launch it against America but to say, ‘If you don’t want to help us in a critical situation, we will require you to help us. Otherwise we will use our bombs.'” (Hersh, p. 40). There is an unfortunate realist logic to Israel’s nuclear policy. Since the country is so small, it cannot absorb a ground invasion force the way Russia, for example, has been able to historically. Therefore, since it is nuclear-armed, Israel has implied it will use its nuclear weapons in a first strike against non-nuclear states should it fear for the life of the state. A nuclear attack in the region would slaughter innocent people in the hundreds of thousands, millions, or tens of millions in a horrific manner . Additionally, Israel is located in an energy-rich region with critical shipping lanes. Such an attack would likely target economic targets like oil fields across the Middle East, obliterate them, and contaminate them with radioactive fallout. Shipping lanes would be disrupted, with ports destroyed or even tight geographic features like the Suez Canal could be damaged. Firestorms started by nuclear explosions could smoke into the stratosphere , which would linger for some time. The reduced sunlight would degrade agricultural output over a wide area (an effect termed “nuclear autumn”), creating famines that could be even more deadly than direct war damage. This is the kind of power the Tel Aviv Reich wields. It helps explain why the international community has barely lifted a finger to help the Palestinian people in the midst of a genocide where one 2025 projection estimates 680,000 Palestinians may have already perished from all causes. In addition to all the direct sticks and carrots American diplomats have undoubtedly deployed behind closed doors, they can also appeal to reason: You wouldn’t want them to go nuclear, would you? Such a situation as outlined above would be much worse for the economy than the current self-induced crisis in the Strait of Hormuz. Therefore, one might think that American planners would attempt to mitigate the risk of nuclear war—and mitigate it they do. You can see this in their harsh and unrelenting treatment of Iran, where they constantly talk about preventing an Iranian nuclear weapon. While I don’t believe this is their only motivating factor, nor that their treatment is justifiable on a moral or legal basis, it is true that they routinely set back Iranian nuclear chemistry capabilities with varying degrees of effectiveness. Iran bears American aggression because they do not possess a nuclear weapon. During the Korean War, North Korea had about 20% of its population wiped out in the 1950s by American aggression and was constantly antagonized until they acquired a nuclear deterrent and now appear to be safe. By contrast, Libya surrendered its nuclear program, and its leader Gaddafi was sodomized with a bayonet after America secured the safety of a militant insurgency from the air. Three different countries contradicting American interests, three different nuclear statuses, three different outcomes. Iran has nuclear potential and is resisting; Libya surrendered and was defeated; the DPRK succeeded and is secure. That doesn’t mean acquiring a nuclear weapon is some unalloyed good. It defers one immediate state-wide risk for a potentially wide-area or world-wide one that gets bigger with each country that acquires them. Israel offers a mirror perspective to examine. They already possess nuclear weapons. If America treated Israel like we do Iran, such as by routinely assassinating their citizens and invading their airspace, Israel would probably perform a nuclear strike, so that approach is ruled out. The Sewer Socialists Were Pragmatic Class Warriors

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Eric Blanc ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-03T13:27:58+00:00

Milwaukee, Wisconsin, was a Socialist Party stronghold for much of the first half of the twentieth century. Socialists there built a strong presence in the labor movement and city council and held the mayor’s office for nearly forty years between 1910 and 1960. The Milwaukee socialists’ focus on good governance and municipal policy achievements like clean water earned them the ire of some fellow socialists, who derisively dubbed them “sewer socialists.” Yet New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani has cited them as an inspiration for his own administration, and, as Jacobin contributor Eric Blanc argued in an essay for Catalyst earlier this year, the sewer socialists’ pragmatic approach to building working-class power has much to teach the Left today. David Griscom recently interviewed Blanc for The Jacobin Show about his research on the Milwaukee socialists, discussing their strategy and potential takeaways for Mayor Mamdani and American socialists in the current moment. You can watch the full interview here. This transcript has been edited for length and clarity. David Griscom Who were the sewer socialists? Eric Blanc The sewer socialists were the most important socialist party in the history of the United States. They ran Milwaukee for most years between 1910 and 1960. They had more members per capita than anywhere else in the United States. They were as strong in Milwaukee as the German Social Democratic Party was in Germany. They ran the town. They led the labor movement. They were electorally successful. They were deeply rooted across the working class. And they delivered. They pioneered a lot of the things that now we take for granted, or have been taken away from us, that came to fruition in the best parts of the New Deal, and they showed the rest of the socialist movement at the time — and I think still today — what mass democratic socialist politics can look like and deliver. David Griscom What did they achieve for working people, beyond electoral victories? Eric Blanc For those of us who are on the Left today and are trying to think about how we could reconnect socialist politics with the working class and what that looks like, the sewer socialists show that it’s possible. And at a moment when the Democratic Party is in such a crisis and Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) has an opening. I actually think that our best model for replicating some sort of mass majoritarian politics is the democratic practice and politics of the sewer socialists. What they were able to deliver was remarkable for a local level. They dramatically expanded public services. They improved people’s health. This is a moment, remember, when things like cholera are really widespread. For us, saying they expanded the sewers might sound like small change, but if your kids are dying from preventable diseases, it’s actually a big deal if you can prevent these needless deaths by providing basic public services. They [also] made people’s lives more fun. It wasn’t just the bread; it was the roses as well. I think Zohran Mamdani in New York City is living up to that spirit. They had free public concerts; they built a ton of parks; they had parades. They did that over and over and over again, both through bottom-up struggle and by using the power of the state. Building a Socialist Culture David Griscom People who have read my book and have heard me talk about the Texas Socialist Party — one of the major things that I find really interesting about that moment in time is that, in Texas, they’d have these massive encampments where thousands of people would meet up together in the summer and they would hear speeches from figures like Eugene Debs. They would dance together, eat together, and it was an incredible social experience that I would love to see replicated today. A huge part of the reason for their success lies in the culture, if you want to think about it that way — they built this culture of socializing and community and purpose. It was almost like a church at its best, where you have the music, you have the community. I think that’s something we can look toward, especially right now when people are so atomized and stuck at home on their phones, and there’s something truly transformative about singing “Solidarity Forever” with a bunch of other comrades or being out at a picnic all day — these types of things that back then were less rare and a little more par for the course in politics. Today, when we look at what the sewer socialists did, or what they did in Texas, that’s maybe the lowest-hanging fruit for being able to replicate their success. David Griscom That’s something that’s been exciting about watching Zohran, all of these little events and quests. Even during the World Cup, having the lottery to give people tickets to go to one of the big games. . . . I think that’s a creative way to use state power that isn’t directly like, “This is class struggle,” but is showing people that we can make society work for everyday people in a way that really benefits you. Not just putting butter on your bread but giving you a joyful and fulfilled life. Eric Blanc That’s so crucial because the strategy of the sewer socialists, and I think Zohran as well, wasn’t limited to this vision of a better welfare state. They understood that you were never going to be able to get a majority of working-class people to go further to demand a radical transformation of society, economic democracy, socialism, if you couldn’t show that the public sector and the government works better than the private sector. You had to provide some proof of concept. You also needed to help them get better organized. One of the big things that the sewer socialists did was that they created an environment for unions to flourish. I think the union movement in Milwaukee got stronger than anywhere else. They had something like 60 percent union density, which is astronomical, because they would have the state literally intervene when the workers were on strike. Can you imagine if that was today? Workers go on strike, and you have the state, in one case, literally shutting down the company — forcing it to close and prevent scabs from coming in. These types of direct intervention on the side of the workers from the state is something that I think should open our vistas for the possibilities of creating bottom-up organization, not just through organizing but leaning on our elected officials as well. Delivering Material Improvements David Griscom There was a serious commitment to achievement, right? Not just to having the right slogans or the right theoretical positions, but this understanding that, yes, socialism is an idea of a better future, but it is also a practical vehicle to achieve that better future. Could you talk about the orientation of these socialists to other socialists in the United States, in the past or today, or internationally? Eric Blanc A lot of the debates we still have on the Left were paralleled back then. It’s funny because before I started doing the research for this, I had a lot of myths in my head about what the sewer socialists were that turned out not to be accurate. I had thought, these are just moderate, milquetoast reformists who weren’t real socialists; they just wanted to expand the sewer system or whatever. Far from that being the case, these were committed socialists. They were what we call democratic socialists today. But their goal was to overcome capitalism. They educated people about it; that was very central to what drove their volunteer base. If you’re getting up at 5:30 in the morning to distribute the newspaper to all of your neighbors, what drives you is a vision of that long-term society. But they were also extremely pragmatic. They understood that you had to base your politics on an accurate assessment where the majority of working-class people were. You couldn’t go too far ahead of them. You had to really gauge how far you could go: What demands are plausible right now? They also understood that the main way you get to socialism isn’t just going to be spreading the good word. There was a lot of sectarianism and what I think you can call ultraleftism in the Socialist Party at the time. The Milwaukee socialists were more the exception than the norm. I think a lot of times people misread the moment that that we’re in: what everyday people need is not to be woken up from a capitalist mythology that keeps them in chains, but rather for socialists to provide a viable vehicle, a viable vision of a future — not in the distant future, but actually something that is achievable today. Eric Blanc There’s a lot of lessons there. When I was eighteen, I just wanted to go to the barricades the next day. Consciousness is different; the political conditions are different. Ultimately, that acknowledgment that we don’t yet know what’s going to work and we have to learn through experience — I think that kind of pragmatic approach is the only viable way if we’re going to rebuild a mass socialist politics in the United States today. Sewer Socialism Inside and Outside the State David Griscom Let’s get specific here. Again, “sewer socialism” is often presented as just, “We’ll make public amenities work well.” That doesn’t seem very controversial as a principle. What are the lessons from the sewer socialists that we might not be following today, or that certain sections of the Left maybe need to take more seriously? Eric Blanc One important thing we haven’t talked about as much is that they had a robust vision and practice for working inside and outside of the state, as we would put it today. They didn’t believe what those who we might call social democrats believe today, in some parts of the world, that you can just elect people and that’s going to be how change happens. They purposefully and consciously said that’s not going to be enough, because they understood you’re going you’re going up against important corporations and the political establishment, even the best politicians getting elected aren’t going to be able to win their program. So they consciously and consistently combined electing people and doing mass organizing in neighborhoods and in workplaces. That was the sweet spot, because without that bottom-up power, there’s no way that the sewer socialists — who, it’s worth saying, very rarely had a majority on their city council, and never had a majority statewide — they ended up passing all of this stuff despite being in a minority within the state for most of the time. They tended to have the mayorship, but they didn’t have some sort of supermajority through which they could have done everything. The way they were able to pressure all these establishment politicians and overcome the capitalists who were funding them and trying to stop these policies was by going and talking to the people: constantly going and talking to your neighbors, talking to your coworkers, leading the union movement. They didn’t just project what their program would be and hope people would come around. They listened. It turned out that what people wanted was same thing they want today. They want to be able to get by. You want decent wages, decent housing, to be able to afford health care for your kids, be able to afford enough to enjoy life. In Defense of Victor Serge

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Claudio Albertani ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-03T12:58:07+00:00

For decades, anyone writing about Victor Serge had to begin by explaining who he was and why we should care. Despite an enormous literary legacy, he had been literally airbrushed from Soviet history, a forgotten figure even on much of the Left. History is rarely kind to the vanquished, and Serge stood on the side of the defeated, with those who refused to compromise with capitalism or surrender to Stalinism. The cost was high, but Serge’s eloquent voice and mighty pen could not prevent his being sidelined by history, ignored or poorly understood, consigned to a life of poverty. Four of his books were confiscated by the Soviet Censorship ( Glavlit ), and other writings were left unpublished, though they establish him as a man who belongs to our future. Who was Serge? A worker, a militant, an intellectual, an internationalist by experience and conviction, and always poor, Serge lived from 1890 to 1947. He took part in three revolutions, spent a decade in various prisons, and was politically active in seven countries. He published more than thirty books and left behind thousands of pages of unpublished work. He was born Victor Kibalchich in Belgium, to Russian anarcho-populist parents in exile, and died in exile himself in Mexico as an independent, nondoctrinaire socialist thinker and writer. He spent the years 1919–1936 for the most part in the Soviet Union, part of a revolutionary generation that sought to create a socialist society. They were outmaneuvered and defeated by Joseph Stalin, who established a bureaucratic authoritarian dictatorship ruling in the name of communism. Serge devoted what remained of his life to understanding the Soviet experiment’s promise and its failure — not to renounce socialism but to rescue it from the catastrophe of Stalinism. He opposed capitalism first as an anarchist, then as a Bolshevik. He opposed Bolshevism’s undemocratic practices and then opposed Stalin as a Left Oppositionist. He argued with Leon Trotsky from within the anti-Stalinist left, and he opposed both fascism and the emerging Cold War as an unrepentant revolutionary Marxist. His perceptions often put him at odds with his co-thinkers in the West, causing him much grief, isolating him from the very movement he had devoted so many years to, at such great risk. Yet he did not succumb to pessimism. He never wavered in his commitment to the creation of a society that defends human freedom, enhances human dignity, and improves the human condition. To Serge, democracy was not an accessory to socialism but its indispensable condition. Through his essays, memoirs, notebooks, novels, and journalism, he sought to understand the defeats of the twentieth century in order to illuminate the possibilities of the future, calling for a renewal of socialist thought and practice. Writing from exile in Mexico in the final years of his life, he offered a warning to the “fossilized dogmatists” who believed European revolution was inevitable after the war. A dark age is opening up before Europe and the world. The best revolutionaries have been destroyed by past defeats and the war. Time will have to elapse before new cadres are formed. The old socialist programs and routines have been superseded and must be renewed. Stalinism, victorious thanks to the unconditional support and concessions made by the Allies, will be more dangerous than ever. . . . If we want to save Europe we have to start by bringing together all free democratic forces in order simply to practice the art of not dying away. Serge’s admonition captures the revolutionary but resolutely independent thought of a man who insisted, above all, that socialism without liberty and democracy is not and cannot be socialism. As he put it in 1942: We want to participate in the construction of a socialism restored to its dignity and real aims, which can only be through an organization of free men. We want honest and clear ideas within a healthy workers’ movement invigorated by fraternal competition and free debate. From the heart of threatened democracy, of socialism and the workers’ movement, above all we defend freedom of opinion and the human dignity of the militant, the rights of minorities and a critical spirit. We fight relentlessly, and we will not cease to fight, against controlled thought, the cult of the leader, passive obedience and the despicable ploys of parties subject to blind discipline as well as the systematic use of lies, slander and assassinations. Rediscovering Serge is no longer a rescue operation. The logjam has broken: the publication of his Carnets ( Notebooks, 1936–1947 ) by Agone, the new editions of the Memoirs of a Revolutionary and his novels by New York Review Books, and now two major new biographies — Mitchell Abidor’s Victor Serge: Unruly Revolutionary and Claudio Albertani’s Le jeune Victor Serge , the first volume of a biography not yet translated into English — mark something of a Serge renaissance. We have entered a period in which the task is no longer simply to introduce his literary and revolutionary legacy but to defend it. We write from different political traditions — anarchism, the Left Opposition, revolutionary socialism — but we are united by a shared conviction that Abidor’s biography, for all its archival depth, misreads its subject. Serge’s legacy is not that of a man who abandoned socialism for Cold War anti-communism. It is that of a socialist thinker who refused every capitulation — to Stalinism, to liberalism, to despair — and who paid the full price of that refusal. Don’t Pigeonhole Serge Coming from distinct political and intellectual traditions frees us from the temptation to confine Victor Serge within a single, tendentious interpretation and from subjecting him to the verdict of hindsight, as though every stage of his life inevitably pointed toward his supposed later anti-communism. Whether we come from anarchism, Trotskyism, revolutionary socialism, or other currents of the Left, one thing should remain common: the refusal to transform a life defined by struggles, defeats, and resistance into a trial by innuendo. It is from this shared concern — not from agreement on every political question — that we approach Abidor’s biography. Yet this is precisely what Abidor does all too often. The book contains valuable material, including important documents and moving accounts — for example, of Serge’s years in France. The problem is not that Abidor is harsh on Serge; it is that his central interpretive approach strikes us as profoundly flawed. He psychologizes Serge, casts doubt on his sincerity and moral character, and exaggerates anecdotes and phrases taken out of context. The partisan use of real material ends up producing a distorted image of Serge. Falsification does not necessarily imply inventing facts; it can consist of arranging them in such a way that they no longer correspond to their original context. As an anarchist, one might reasonably have expected Abidor’s criticism to focus on Serge’s decision to join the Bolshevik Party in 1919, marking his break with the anarchism of his youth. But this is not the case. In his account of Serge’s youth, Abidor treats the anarcho-individualist movement, in which Serge was an active participant for nearly a decade, with scant sympathy. More seriously, in discussing Serge’s role in the Liabeuf affair, the 1910 political campaign surrounding the execution of the worker Jean-Jacques Liabeuf. Abidor notes that the socialist journalist Gustave Hervé was prosecuted for public statements, whereas Serge was not. Although he acknowledges that no documentary evidence explains why Serge escaped prosecution, he nevertheless invites the reader to infer that his immunity reflected dubious dealings with the police. The lack of evidence functions less as a reason for historical caution than as an invitation to suspicion. Abidor reserves his sharpest criticism of Serge for the final years of Serge’s life. By then, working in increasing isolation, Serge had deepened his analysis of Stalinism. Some of the judgments expressed in his correspondence, notebook entries, and later writings are open to debate and bear the unmistakable imprint of the extraordinary circumstances in which they were written. The violence of Stalinism, the memory of the Moscow Trials, the catastrophe of World War II, Trotsky’s assassination, and Serge’s own political isolation during his final exile in Mexico all weighed heavily on his thinking. Recognizing this hardening of tone, however, does not vindicate Abidor’s interpretation. Does it amount to a conversion on Serge’s part to crude, vulgar anti-communism? We do not believe it does. Abidor reads Serge through his own lens — he calls it his “inner paradigm” — and suspects him of insincerity, as if Serge were expressing opinions he didn’t hold. His central argument is that Serge became a staunch anti-communist in his later years. He bases this on a flawed premise: the idea that the Soviet Union under Stalin still represented, in however distorted a fashion, the socialist project. Indeed, Abidor accepts that the USSR was a communist state — a surprising position for someone who professes an affinity with the anarchist tradition. He even calls it “the only socialist state” without seriously considering Serge’s analysis that Stalinism represented a system antithetical to socialism, ultimately anti-socialist and antihuman. In the final pages of his biography, Abidor writes that Serge’s insistence on the protection of political freedom and democracy “turned him into an uncompromising enemy of communism.” There could be no clearer statement of Abidor’s acceptance of Stalin’s own definition of his system as communist — a definition that would make Karl Marx shudder, and one that Serge spent his life refuting. Fortunately, we need not speculate: Serge’s ideas are clearly documented in his essays and articles, notably in “For a Renewal of Socialism” ( Pour une rénovation du socialisme ), written in Mexico in the 1940s and published in French in Masses: Socialisme et Liberté (June 1946). There, Serge asserts that “collectivism is not, as one might have been tempted to assume, synonymous with socialism, and can even take on anti-socialist forms of exploitation of labor and contempt for humanity.” Serge’s definition of socialism places less emphasis on the economic structure of society than on its political and legal organization — that is, on human rights, freedom, and democracy. In his unpublished manuscript “Économie Dirigée et Démocratie” ( Directed Economy and Democracy ), Serge analyzes Soviet “planning” as the antithesis of democracy, and of planning itself: not the conscious regulation of society by freely associated producers, as Marx defined it, but peremptory commands ( ukases ) issued from above, impossible to fulfill except on paper. He describes the USSR for what it was: a system operating through terror against its own population, run by Stalin’s dictatorship of the secretariat and secret police. It had nothing in common with the ideals of October. A letter from Serge to René Lefeuvre, included in the 1984 edition of 16 Fusillés à Moscou ( 16 Shot in Moscow ), shows a man preoccupied with Lefeuvre’s publication of a report that is insufficiently critical of American imperialism — a stark contrast to Abidor’s reductive portrayal: “I understand that the Stalinist threat alarms you. But it must not make us lose sight of our overall vision. We must not play into the hands of the anti-communist bloc; and, judging by the early issues of Masses , we deserve to be criticized for doing so.” A similar sentiment appears in Serge’s Notebooks . In September 1944, he wrote that “the battle is open between the totalitarian Communist Party and socialist democracy.” He immediately clarified that the decisive opposition was no longer situated, as it was in 1917–1918, between socialist revolution and capitalist reaction, but also between Stalinist totalitarianism and democratic socialism. In 1942, regarding Spain, he writes that the objectives of the democratic revolution “can only be achieved by the socialist masses” and must be transcended by sweeping measures of “nationalization that entail planning.” In September 1944, when contrasting the “totalitarian Communist Party” with “socialist democracy,” Serge formulates the alternative in terms of “Stalinist totalitarianism” versus “democratic socialism,” making no concessions whatsoever to Western liberalism. In an interview given to Protean Magazine in December 2025, Abidor declares: “[Serge] wanted to be a New York intellectual. At the end of his life, if he could have been anything in the world, he would have been a New York Jewish intellectual.” This is a forced and unprovable construct. Serge was not navigating the trajectory of the New York intellectual milieu — the Max Eastman and Max Shachtman arc, the familiar story of ex-Trotskyists moving rightward toward the Congress for Cultural Freedom. That is a real historical trajectory, but it is not Serge’s. Enriching Socialism, Not Denouncing It Tellingly, Abidor sees Serge’s long-held respect for the individual as anti-Marxist, which is to misread both Marx and Serge, who took this value as both starting point and conclusion. Marx argued that human freedom and self-realization could only be possible with the abolition of capitalism, which reduces the individual to a commodity. Rosa Luxemburg insisted on freedom of individual thought, especially for one’s enemies. So did Serge. Serge’s political experience did not lead him to renounce socialism after Stalin’s triumph but to enrich it with a declaration of human rights. In “For a Renewal of Socialism,” published in Masses: Socialisme et Liberté (June 1946) Serge calls for updating Marxist thought in light of psychology, science, and new social formations. This was a renewal, not a rejection. Working in isolation, deprived of the revolutionary generation who had understood both Marxism and Stalinism from the inside, Serge was grappling with the contours of the postwar world, attempting to analyze a formation in process of becoming. He could see the tendencies but died just as the Cold War was taking shape, before its implications were fully evident. Serge died in November 1947. The Truman Doctrine had been proclaimed in March of that year; the Congress for Cultural Freedom, the CIA’s cultural apparatus, dates from 1950. The full ideological architecture of postwar anti-communism did not emerge until after his death. Projecting that framework onto Serge is not interpretation. It is tendentious anachronism, just as it is ahistorical to suppose, as Abidor does sotto voce in the Protean interview, that Serge “would have” supported the Americans in Vietnam. Abidor attaches great significance to the fact that Serge served as Mexico correspondent for The New Leader , viewing it as proof of his ideological convergence with right-wing social democracy. The truth is more prosaic: Serge had no money and almost no means to earn a living in Mexico. He lived by his pen, but the Soviet state systematically closed off his publishing options. Manuel Alemán, future president of Mexico, admitted there was intense Soviet pressure to deny Serge any means of public expression. So Serge wrote wherever he could get published and, in the case of The New Leader , paid. He also refused to submit to censorship: he told Nancy and Dwight Macdonald that the magazine permitted a pluralism the Trotskyist press itself did not tolerate. At the same time, Serge wrote for Politics , the libertarian socialist journal edited by Dwight Macdonald. Here then, is the Serge of his final years: not an anti-communist nor a cold warrior but a socialist thinker seeking to renew the tradition under extremely adverse conditions. Above all, Serge was a great writer. He evokes the atmosphere of the 1920s and ’30s inside the Soviet Union and the Communist movement, and spells out the dilemmas of the 1940s with a sense of immediacy and clarity. It is striking that Abidor fails to address the importance of Serge’s novels and poems for understanding the tragedy of a revolution that devours itself. Five months before his death, in a letter dated June 22, 1947, addressed to “Podoliak,” the pseudonym of Ukrainian writer Hryhory Kostiuk, Serge declared: “I remain — unshakeably — a socialist, a partisan of democratic socialism. The system against which I have fought and continue to fight — and which you know well — I regard as a form of totalitarianism; that is to say, something new, yet profoundly inhumane and anti-socialist.” Kostiuk was neither a Western liberal nor a Cold War anti-communist; he was a Ukrainian revolutionary intellectual who had experienced Soviet terror firsthand, the editor of Vaplite , a journal in which Serge had published. This is not the portrait of a man settling into Cold War anti-communism. It is that of a man fighting to make his voice heard against isolation, unwaveringly affirming the necessity of socialist commitment against Stalinist totalitarianism. We were surprised by Ian Birchall’s review in Jacobin , given the high regard we have for him as a historian. Undoubtedly, his review is more measured than Abidor’s text, but it remains troubling that Birchall finds convincing Abidor’s conclusion that “in his last years, Serge saw communism as the main enemy.” Birchall acknowledges that one can only speculate about what Serge would have thought regarding Korea or Vietnam, but nonetheless suggests a political trajectory for Serge that his writings do not support. This point is all the more striking given that Birchall himself had previously cited Serge’s letter to Lefeuvre as evidence to the contrary. That letter reveals a Serge alarmed by Stalinism but explicitly refusing to play into the hands of an anti-communist bloc. It compels us to resist overly simplistic readings: Serge does not cease to be a socialist because he identifies Stalinism as a central threat. He attempts, under tragic circumstances, to maintain an independent revolutionary position between Stalinist bureaucratic authoritarian rule and the Western capitalist bloc. Abidor, for his part, conflates rhetoric with analysis. The polemics of an exile do not constitute a political program. The bitterness of an outcast is not a doctrine. A man hunted by Stalinism in Mexico City, living in a world of assassinations, threats, and settling of scores may produce terrible pronouncements. But nothing is gained by transforming these statements into a certificate of belonging to a definitively established political family. Victor Serge was a man of passion and complexity, a political maverick but never a renegade. A biography should neither condemn nor absolve him but rather seek to understand him, to read his life and work on their own terms. There is a difference between writing history and conducting a prosecution. Serge, like any human being, was capable of error and contradiction. Tested by so many adversities, trying to think freshly about what socialist democracy could mean in the postwar world, Serge was a critical Left Oppositionist who never abandoned socialism, unlike those who succumbed to the rhetoric of the “god that failed.” He did not convert the failure of Stalinism into the failure of socialism. He did not renounce collective emancipation. On the contrary, he sought to liberate socialism from its bureaucratic and totalitarian deformation. For this reason, we must, in conclusion, oppose the inquisitorial logic of Unruly Revolutionary with the testimony of the poet Octavio Paz, who met Serge in Mexico in 1942: I was immediately drawn to Serge. I spoke at length with him, and I still have two of his letters.. . . . Nothing could be further removed from the pedantry of dialecticians than Serge’s human warmth, his simplicity and his generosity. A sensitive, living intelligence. Despite his sufferings, his setbacks, and his long years of arid political disputes, he had managed to retain his humanity. He owed this, undoubtedly, to his anarchist roots; also to his great heart.. . . . For me, Victor Serge was the epitome of the fusion of two opposing qualities: moral and intellectual intransigence combined with tolerance and compassion.

Coalitions Make the Left Stronger

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Midwestern Socialist -- Chicago DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-02T19:37:10+00:00

DSA has had a string of spectacular electoral victories. With success comes greater media attention, growing membership, and new challenges. One of the most important questions is how to build broad unity that can achieve transformative policies. And how do we criticize each other and political allies without falling into sectarian purism where differences overshadow what we hold in common and disrupt working together? The specter haunting DSA seems to be the possibility of socialist congresswoman Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (AOC) joining the presidential primary. Positions within the organization range from enthusiastic support, to begrudging inevitability, to outright opposition. But the debate over AOC is just the tip of the iceberg. As DSA grows and fields more and more candidates, the question of which progressive and socialist voices to support will turn into a constant question. In this article, I want to draw on personal organizing experience that helped develop my ideas on coalition building. This account comes from my years as a labor activist, first as a steelworker, then as a teacher. Politics in the Steelworkers Union I worked at the U.S. Steel South Works Mill in South Chicago in the late 1970s and early 1980s until deindustrialization hit with full force. At the time I belonged to the October League, a Maoist formation with cadre concentrations in five major mills in the Chicago and Indiana region, and other mills throughout the country. Ed Sadlowski, a militant leader and head of the Midwest District 31, was running an insurgent campaign for union president. The national union leadership were typical conservative and corrupt bureaucrats. Sadlowski had created a lot of energy and excitement, although he eventually lost the election. But the October League had refused to support him because he wasn’t a “true revolutionary leader.” We defined him as a social democrat, and social democrats had to be exposed and removed so communist leadership could emerge. This type of sectarian and ultra-left purism led to our isolation within the entire militant wing of the union. At the time I was working in a tobacco factory in Louisville, but a year later when I got a job at South Works I saw our organizing efforts were in shambles. Cadre had even stopped going to union meetings, which they called a “circus of opportunists.” There were about eight different communist organizations at South Works, but only one militant left caucus, which was led by Alice Puerala. Alice was widely respected throughout the mill where she had been working for about 20 years, first as a Socialist Workers Party cadre, and now as an independent leftist. For sectarian reasons none of the eight communist groups were part of her caucus. I began supporting her shortly after going to work. We won the next election and she became union president. At that time, she was the only woman president of a basic mill with over 6,000 workers. I became editor of the union paper, convinced a fellow cadre to join efforts to organize with her, and Alice served two terms as president. None of the other groups joined in. I had some differences with Alice, most practically with her rocky relationship with a new militant Black caucus, with whom we had forged an alliance. And of course we had our feet in different historic left camps: Trotsky and Mao. But we built a solid and warm working relationship based in the fight for better working conditions, a broader anti-corporate militancy, and the need for multi-racial solidarity. These same conditions were present in the Sadlowski campaign, though our purism and drawing red lines over sectarian issues prevented unity. Each of the other communist groups had their own red lines that kept them at arms-length from Alice, resulting in little to no influence in the union for most of those organizations. When I look at DSA today with its many caucuses, some with their own red lines when it comes to AOC, I think back to the mill and the difference between being isolated or effective mass organizers. The key to this was understanding how the majority of steelworkers viewed Alice as a militant leader as opposed to our own narrow view based on our own tendencies. Today I see the same disconnect between how millions see AOC and the complaints held dear by some in the DSA. As for the October League, the persistence of ultra-leftism in other areas of organizing eventually lead to its demise. The Faculty Association at DeVry University After a stint in the Harold Washington administration, I, along with others, were quicky purged when Daley became mayor. I went on to be a teacher at DeVry University. There had been a faculty association at DeVry that had fallen into hibernation but was being revived, and I decided to run for Chair. There were only fifty faculty members, and I lost the election by four votes. But the count found that our ballot box had been stuffed with more ballots than there were faculty. So we held the election again, and it ended in a 25-to-25 tie. The humanities department teachers were solidly progressive/Left, majorities in the other departments were conservative, male and white. Instead of fighting for Left dominance and splitting the faculty, I went to my opponent (Lowell) and suggested we act as co-chairs. Lowell was an older white engineer living in a small town 40 miles west of Chicago, at the top of the seniority list, and probably a Republican. But we both wanted the faculty to have a stronger organized voice, and we had different goals that didn’t conflict. Lowell wanted formal bylaws and a more structured faculty association; I was interested in putting out a newsletter focused on working conditions and school issues. We agreed to work together, and it united the faculty. Unity resulted in a number of important victories. We reduced class sizes, reduced the number of classes faculty had to teach each semester, won sabbaticals, got the administration to expand the library by letting teachers order thousands of new books, and equalized wages between departments, which meant a raise for humanity teachers by thousands of dollars. When the administration met with the faculty, we were able to present a united front and coordinate demands between departments. DeVry Chicago quickly gained a reputation as the most militant campus in the DeVvry network, and one which national administrators dreaded to visit. I was openly known as a leftist and to many a Marxist. An administrator told me they knew I was a communist. But they never succeeded in isolating me or other Left faculty members because we stayed grounded in the concrete demands of teachers and the concerns of students. Left leadership successfully set a tone of confrontation and militancy based on achievable demands that benefited all teachers. We could have taken the path of defining conservative teachers as our opponents, creating red lines and purity tests, and emphasizing differences. Instead, we built a solid coalition, and won significant changes, respect for the Left, and room to bring innovative programs to the school. These included faculty workshops on Freire pedagogy, bringing in Left academics for guest lectures, and inviting anti-war Iraq veterans to speak to students. The challenge for the Left is to unite broadly, to aim our main blow at the principal enemy. The Left has a sad history of sectarianism towards each other and towards our progressive and liberal allies. We are coming to a period in which the DSA may have the possibility to grow into a mass party of the working class. To do so I believe we must broaden our politics, build our coalitions, avoid abstract slogans, and welcome all friends and allies in the struggle to transform our country. This is the best manner to popularize our political vision, win people to a socialist platform, and expand our membership. Coalitions don’t undercut our message, they magnify it. We thrive in the soil of mass mobilization and movement building when we stay rooted to the urgent demands of the working class and consciously build our organization. Under Israeli Settler Attacks, I Feel I Am Not Alive

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Miriame Mazid ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-02T16:26:09+00:00

In the West Bank village of At-Tuwani this July 19, Ruqayya Rabe’i, three months pregnant, lay in her room beside her four sleeping daughters. At 12:30 a.m., the first stones hit her home. She rushed to check the front door. There in the living room, she found her fifteen-year-old son, Usayd, sleeping directly below a window where, according to Ruqayya, several settlers were armed with guns and knives and trying to force their way in. She tried to shake her son awake, but he didn’t stir. When the settlers noticed her, she fled, certain that if they saw him, they would shoot. The window was shattered, a chemical compound poured through it, and the frame went up in flames, Ruqayya said. She called her husband, who was working in the nearby city of Yatta, frantic, believing at that moment that her son had been killed. Her son, meanwhile, woke up to the flames and ran to find his mother. At the sight of him alive, Ruqayya collapsed from shock, unconscious. By the time an ambulance arrived, she could hardly crawl to the street — one hand on the ground, the other on her belly. The attack was over in ten minutes. The perpetrators abruptly left the scene, leaving behind burned vehicles , vandalized homes with the Hebrew words for “revenge” and “house arrest,” and a destroyed mosque . But the effects on this family will be felt for a lifetime. Numbering near forty according to residents, the mob of settlers had descended from an illegal outpost, Havat Ma’on, located just southeast of the Palestinian village. The settlers there have been terrorizing the Palestinians of At-Tuwani since the outpost’s establishment over two decades ago. Impunity by Design Havat Ma’on is one of over five hundred settlements and outposts across the West Bank, more than 165 of which have been established since October 2023 and all of which are illegal under international law. Settlers like those who attacked At-Tuwani are not acting in isolation but instead are instruments in a broader Israeli strategy of land-grabbing by force, backed by a state that encourages them every step of the way. Outposts such as Havat Ma’on are established illegally even under Israeli law yet are routinely legalized after their formation, rewarding settlers for occupying Palestinian land instead of punishing them for stealing it. Settlers then face little to no legal accountability for their crimes against Palestinians and are further encouraged through extreme armament campaigns. Of the eighteen Palestinians killed by Israeli settlers and forces in the West Bank during the first half of 2026, no murderers have been convicted. According to a study by Israeli rights group Yesh Din, 93.6 percent of police investigations into settler crimes in the West Bank are closed without charge, and only 3 percent of cases lead to any actual conviction. Hundreds of thousands of Israeli settlers have received state-issued guns, in large part thanks to Israeli National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir’s massive armament campaign since October 2023, relaxing already loose permit laws for Israeli citizens. Critics argue this “green-lights” the intensification of violence against Palestinians, using settlers as “private militias” to advance state land-grabbing goals under the pretext of civilian self-defense. More than just a byproduct of Israeli policy in the West Bank, this violence is a weapon of the broader Zionist colonial project aimed at displacing Palestinians and consolidating de facto annexation. An Accelerating Crisis Amid unchecked impunity, accelerating settlement expansion and armament, and mounting crackdowns on Palestinians since October 2023, settler violence has reached an all-time high. A June 2026 United Nations Commission of Inquiry found that settler attacks had risen by 130 percent since 2023. The UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) recorded more than 1,330 attacks causing casualties or property damage across roughly 250 Palestinian communities in the first seven months of 2026, with attacks now occurring at a rate of around six per day. Around 880 Palestinians have been injured in the context of settler attacks this year, while eighteen have been killed — already surpassing the seventeen fatalities recorded throughout the full year of 2025. Yet these figures capture only documented incidents. Much of the everyday violence shaping Palestinian life in the West Bank never enters official statistics. A 2026 report by Israeli settlement-monitoring groups Kerem Navot and Peace Now found that the vast majority of incidents, ranging from harassment and threats to physical violence, are never documented or counted by any authority. For communities living alongside expanding outposts, this intimidation and constant threats of attack have become part of daily life, and the recorded figures offer only a limited reflection of the reality they face. “They do these attacks on the pretext of land defense,” Mohammad Hurani, an At-Tuwani resident and neighbor of Ruqayya, told us. “Every twenty-four hours something happens, and if something doesn’t happen, the people remain on edge.” During the July 19 attack on At-Tuwani, settlers attempted to set Mohammad’s house on fire and spray-painted the Hebrew word for “revenge” on an exterior wall. “We tried to defend ourselves, but they outnumbered us ten to one, so we couldn’t do much.” Hurani explains that there has been a severe increase not only in the frequency of attacks but also in their intensity. Prior to October 2023, “settlers used to come in groups of three or four but now come in mobs of several dozen.” In documented cases, the Israeli military has protected settlers during attacks, joined them in raiding villages, or arrived at the scene only after the settlers have fled, as documented by various Israeli and international human rights groups. In At-Tuwani, Israeli military forces arrived approximately forty minutes after the settlers left, according to Mohammad. “[The military] began yelling, ‘You don’t belong here,’” he recalls. “Without taking any statements from us, they proceeded to Havat Ma’on to collect statements from the Israelis.” OCHA estimates that 56 percent of total Palestinian injuries in the West Bank this year occurred in the context of settler attacks. Over 1,100 Palestinians have been killed in the West Bank by settlers and Israeli forces since 2023, according to the UN Relief and Works Agency for Palestinian Refugees in the Near East (UNRWA). Almost all perpetrators walk free. But the true cost of these attacks cannot be measured in numbers. Following the attack in At-Tuwani, Ruqayya was transferred to Yatta Public Hospital, where despite doctors finding that the fetus had a weak heartbeat, she was discharged after an hour without treatment. Public hospitals across the West Bank have been operating under severe strain as the Israeli government continues to withhold over $5 billion worth of tax revenues from the Palestinian Authority, which would otherwise pay public sector employees and keep hospitals running, according to Deutsche Welle. Suffering from emotional trauma and painful cramping through the following day, Ruqayya was brought to a private hospital in Yatta for testing. At 5 p.m. on July 20, the fetus was pronounced dead. “I feel I am not alive,” says Ruqayya. “He left before he could see the good things in this world, before we could even see him. Now he is gone.” The attacks leave communities in a permanent state of vigilance. Ruqayya’s daughters still sleep in a room with no protective barriers, leaving her constantly worried about future attacks. “The sound of broken glass is still ringing in my ears. I’m afraid for my daughters — I can’t sleep.” Yet in At-Tuwani, as across Masafer Yatta, fear has not erased resistance. Residents continue to rebuild damaged homes, accompany shepherds and farmers to their land, document attacks, and support families targeted by settlers. In communities where violence and intimidation aim not only to injure individuals but to drive Palestinians from their homes, remaining on the land has become an act of resistance. An Election Without a Clear Break The months ahead may bring even greater pressure. Israel’s upcoming election is not the cause of the surge in settler violence, but it may intensify the forces already driving it. With Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s bloc facing the possibility of losing power, settler leaders and their political allies have incentives to consolidate territorial gains before any political transition. Meanwhile, neither of Netanyahu’s leading opposition rivals are currently positioned to meaningfully challenge the structures enabling settler violence. While their policies differ in intensity, neither offers a clear break with the broader trajectory of settlement expansion and the displacement of Palestinians. Although Gadi Eisenkot, a former Israel Defense Forces chief of staff who has surged in recent polling, has publicly condemned the “ violent lawbreaking by an extremist minority” of settlers in the West Bank, he has centered his campaign on security and governance rather than settlement and settler policy. Naftali Bennett, a former Israeli prime minister running in alliance with centrist leader Yair Lapid under the Together (Beyachad) party, has traded the lead with Eisenkot throughout the campaign. As a former head of the settler movement’s main umbrella organization, Bennett opposes a Palestinian state outright and has publicly advocated that Area C, which comprises more than 60 percent of the West Bank, should “ultimately be part of the State of Israel.” Electoral competition may therefore accelerate settlers’ efforts to establish further “facts on the ground,” with little indication of meaningful policy shift. Beyond Condemnation Ultimately, protecting Palestinian communities requires moving beyond condemnation and imposing material costs on those enabling their displacement. The targeted sanctions already imposed by the European Union and several Western governments are an important step, but they remain largely insufficient given the scale of ongoing settlement expansion and state-enabled violence. Governments should expand sanctions beyond individual violent settlers to the organizations financing illegal outposts and the government officials implicated in facilitating settler violence or forced displacement, including through asset freezes and travel bans. They should also restrict trade, investment, and financial activity that sustains illegal settlements and halt arms transfers, given the inherent risk that they may contribute to serious violations. The UN Human Rights Office has identified 158 companies involved in settlement-related activities. Settlement expansion is sustained not only politically and militarily but through international economic networks that governments clearly have the power to change. Still, pressure cannot come from governments alone. Individuals and civil society can also increase pressure through political organizing, divestment, and targeted boycotts. The Palestinian-led Boycott, Divestment, and Sanctions movement publishes regularly updated guidance identifying priority companies and institutions to boycott, divest from, or pressure based on their involvement in violations of Palestinian rights. For Ruqayya, such measures would come too late. For other Palestinian families, they could decide whether Israeli violence and displacement continue, with almost no political or economic cost for the perpetrators.

Trump’s Oil Deal Is a Return to Venezuela’s Colonial Past

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Logan McMillen ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-02T16:26:09+00:00

On Friday evening, US President Donald Trump announced — via Truth Social post — the “biggest oil deal in world history,” which would see the United States take partial control of a shocking sixty-five billion barrels of Venezuelan oil, more than doubling the United States’ current reserves. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, who negotiated the deal with interim Venezuelan President Delcy Rodríguez, hailed the agreement as “a huge win for both the American and Venezuelan people,” dangling the scintillating prospect of “nearly $100 billion in private investment” for Venezuela and the creation of “thousands of high-paying jobs.” Details of the deal were already leaking Thursday afternoon, with sources suggesting that it would take the form of a one hundred-year lease across seventeen oil fields — including undeveloped heavy crude sites in the Orinoco Belt as well as developed light crude sites in Lake Maracaibo. The deal was also announced amid rumors that Venezuela may be leaving the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries. Reactions to the deal were swift. Institutionalists of both neoconservative and liberal stripes were united in disbelief and anger that Rubio had made a long-term deal with Delcy. Leftists of all tendencies in the United States largely opposed the deal for its clearly imperial designs and for further muddying the legacy of Hugo Chávez. The Wall Street Journal compared it to The Godfather: Part II , and energy and business experts were skeptical of its potential impact on markets — rightly pointing out that details were murky. It also didn’t help public perception when reports surfaced that Venezuelan oil oligarch Alejandro Betancourt would be in charge of operations under the deal. But perhaps most shockingly, the deal includes a provision, unprecedented in US history, that allows the Pentagon’s Office of Strategic Capital to take a 35 percent ownership stake in the newly formed private extraction entity, along with the rights to buy 20 percent of the output at cost. Miami hard-liners, the nexus of American foreign policy on Venezuela, didn’t know what to think. For years they had championed “maximum pressure” policies, anticipating that the collapse of the Venezuelan government would pave the way for the American exile lobby to manage the country’s privatization. Instead, Rubio, the most powerful Miami hard-liner on earth, bypassed them entirely. In one notable incident , Congresswoman Maria Elvira Salazar deleted her initial negative response to the deal on X and then replaced it with a glowing review. On Saturday evening, Delcy Rodríguez walked through the agreement in a brief televised address, touting its “ endless benefits ” to the Venezuelan people while reiterating the core premise of her presidency that, despite all appearances to the contrary, the Venezuelan government remained sovereign. Crucially, Rodríguez mentioned a twenty-five-year lease, contradicting earlier reports of a one-hundred-year concession. Her royalties calculation also assumed a baseline price of $65 per barrel of oil, which some suggested was severely undervalued. Far from clarifying anything, the address heightened the sense of confusion surrounding the deal. “We do not have written documents,” notes political economist Antulio Rosales. “We have Truth Social posts and a five-minute speech from Delcy Rodríguez. This is a masterclass in authoritarian governance; it lacks transparency, it lacks political legitimacy, it has not been debated in the Venezuelan parliament or American Congress.” Within Venezuela, the deal has resulted in a political outcome that would’ve been unthinkable months ago: conservatives are angry with the Trump administration. “This deal offends right-wingers who assumed they would have an intermediary role in resource management,” explains historian Miguel Tinker Salas. “Now they have no role.” This is creating a small measure of consensus in a country that is widely perceived as politically dysfunctional and polarized. “There’s greater indignation in Venezuela from both the Right and the Left, who feel that the country has been sold out,” Salas added. “Ironically, it has united them.” Venezuelan Oil Nationalism’s Deep Roots In the early twentieth century, Venezuelan dictator Juan Vicente Gómez granted sweeping, low-rent concessions to foreign oil trusts to guarantee international backing for his autocratic regime. Tinker Salas draws a direct parallel between Trump’s deal and this era: “The contract announced by Trump takes us back to the era when oil was first discovered. An era when dictators such as Juan Vicente Gómez made decisions on behalf of the country.” A popular misconception about Venezuela’s resource politics — perpetuated by Washington and the international press — is that resource nationalism in Venezuela was the ideological invention of Hugo Chávez. In reality, this consensus was built over decades through broad, cross-ideological political movements. “Oil nationalism evolved from the early twentieth century and reached its peak in the 1960s and when the industry was nationalized in the 1970s,” explains Rosales. “It was centered on the idea that they needed to control the resource within the ground (rents and royalties), as well as the industry above ground (through monopolizing extraction with a national company).” From the passage of the Ley de Hidrocarburos in 1943 to the historic 1976 nationalization under social democrat Carlos Andrés Pérez, the principles of modern Venezuelan energy politics have remained consistent. The 1999 Constitution, the foundational document of the Bolivarian Fifth Republic, explicitly codified these. “The Venezuelan Constitution is clear: the oil belongs to the Venezuelan people,” Tinker Salas emphasizes. “This deal strikes against every normative legal and political process that Venezuela has experienced over the past seventy years.” This helps explain why the reaction to this deal has been so uniformly negative across the political spectrum. For years, the ruling Partido Socialista Unido de Venezuela (PSUV) warned that if right-wing opposition figures like María Corina Machado took power, they would execute a fire sale of Venezuela’s oil wealth to the Americans. “Many Venezuelans were concerned that MCM would return and agree to the very deal that Delcy has just signed,” observes Salas. “There is a simmering discontent in Venezuela, like a volcano,” Salas warns. “Oil no longer generates the expectation of social and political change.” In the wake of the announcement, a small but notable political discourse has emerged on Latin American social media, suggesting that this deal marks the end of the Fifth Republic. The social contract that defined modern Venezuela, the promise that oil rents and production would serve as an engine for national development and social redistribution, has been fundamentally broken. Crooked Dealings We know that the deal itself will be governed by “productive participation contracts,” a recent opaque concession framework wherein private, US-backed entities assume total operational control. As Ricardo Vaz, editor at Venezuelanalysis , explains, this arrangement strips Petróleos de Venezuela, the national oil company, of its core function. It “turns over prime and promising oil fields, further consolidating Venezuela’s status as a mere oil resource owner as opposed to an oil producer.” PSUV is doing everything in its power to contradict this characterization. “Delcy is trying to say that her deal is compatible with the basic tenets of oil nationalism, and the minimum 16 percent rent is an important detail,” Rosales notes. “It is the bare minimum that any oil nationalist would consider to be sufficient. This number is symbolic of a long struggle against autocrats, such as the despot Juan Vicente Gómez, who provided concessions under this figure.” And while it’s true that this deal would not have been possible under the constitutional framework that existed up until January 2026 , the ground had already been cleared through an internal restructuring of the state apparatus. “The current oil dynamic is a prolonged crisis that finds its roots in mismanagement, corruption, and the crisis of the Venezuelan economy — which drove Venezuelan oil workers out of the country — as well as prolonged American sanctions,” Rosales details. Those sanctions are critical, because they starved Venezuela of foreign investment and led to the passage of the 2020 Anti-Blockade Law , in which productive participation contracts were first conceptualized. To facilitate foreign investment, the Venezuelan government stripped the legislative branch of its oversight function and shifted approval and regulatory control to the executive. While the Trump administration demanded further changes on behalf of US corporations, Rosales notes that the internal groundwork had already been laid: “It would be a mistake to say this was only desired by the United States. The Venezuelan political elite also wanted to change the locus of approval, oversight, and control from the parliament to the executive. They gave the green light for a more authoritarian approach to oil management.” “I would not necessarily call it ‘oil trade’ because it suggests a relation between equals,” Vaz observes. “What we have right now, amid very opaque conditions, is a Venezuelan energy sector that is completely subordinated to US interests . . . Venezuelan oil revenues are currently under White House control. Funds are deposited in a US Treasury account before US officials decide the timings and amounts to disburse back to Caracas.” Another profound tragedy of the deal lies in its implications for the environment and for the future development of both the United States and Venezuela, chaining both countries to the oil economy at the precise moment that both should be investing in renewable alternatives. “Venezuela was at the forefront of the hydrocarbons industry’s rise. Now it is at the forefront of its afterlife, but this time without the consent of Venezuelan society,” Rosales reflects. “The United States and Venezuela are tied at the hip to each other. And they are tied at the hip to the fossil fuel industry in a world that desperately needs green energy alternatives. . . . Diversification of energy is the only thing that makes sense for the continued development of the global economy, and both countries are turning their back on it.” A Privatization to Disappoint All Parties Within Venezuela, the legacy of the Bolivarian Revolution is now contested not just by popular social movements but by government forces. “What we have seen thus far has been a remarkable [party] discipline even as the project sharply turns away from its core principles,” explains Vaz. “In the case of the National Assembly, there has been zero dissent in terms of approving new laws that directly dismantle the advances made under Chávez.” Over the past few months, it’s become increasingly clear that the PSUV’s abandonment of long-held internationalist principles are part of a desperate bid to ensure regime survival. “There is no sugarcoating this. Right now Venezuela is fully subordinated to US interests both in its domestic and foreign policies,” Vaz says. “Simply put, Rodríguez and the leadership reached the conclusion that their best bet to stay in power is to fully cater to Trump’s agenda.” The government’s top-down consensus has not fully suppressed discontent. High-profile figures, including former Vice President Elías Jaua , have openly condemned the deal, making calls to recover national sovereignty alongside the Popular Anti-Imperialist Front and non-Chavista leftist organizations. For now, the Venezuelan government has managed to contain a wider uprising. “When it comes to the popular movements within Chavismo, most of them, unfortunately, have been silent or offered some vague, uncritical backing for Rodríguez,” Vaz notes. However, as living conditions deteriorate under stagnant wages, eviscerated social services, botched disaster responses, and chronic power blackouts, this patronage model will face increasing financial strain. “My overall expectation is that major deals like this one will eventually be folded into the debt restructuring process,” Vaz warns. “As we all know, debt is the prime mechanism to maintain Global South countries mired in dependency and underdevelopment.” “From the left perspective, there are surely many necessary discussions on where the Bolivarian Revolution could have taken a different path, to make the gains more irreversible,” Vaz reflects. “I think it was a concrete path toward socialism that, under imperialist pressure and aggression, was gradually and then completely abandoned.” But perhaps the greatest shock was felt by the traditional Venezuelan right. For years, the US-aligned opposition viewed privatization as their exclusive political domain. “What is a major shock for the opposition in Venezuelan society is that, in their mind, this was a thing that only they could do,” Rosales explains. “They surrendered agency to a foreign country thinking their political power would be returned.” Instead, they are now realizing that the American empire’s demand for resources will always override its rhetorical commitments to liberal democracy. “From the reaction of the Venezuelan opposition there is clear disbelief, illusions shattered over what they felt was the US empire’s role in Venezuela,” Vaz explains. “In their worldview, it was only natural for Venezuela to be incorporated into US interests, but they did not expect it to such an extent, and they certainly did not expect that it would be done with other people in power.” Neocolonial Redux The deal has stripped away the polite fictions of US foreign policy. For decades, the neoliberal consensus insisted that the Cold War in the Americas was over, and that US engagement in the western hemisphere was driven by a commitment to human rights, rules-based markets, and the promotion of democracy. But by seizing sixty-five billion barrels of oil as part of a backroom deal with an authoritarian president hated by both the Left and the Right, Trump and Rubio have disabused Venezuelans, both within the country and in the diaspora, of any remaining illusions about US empire. Clearly the Trump administration views this deal as a diplomatic and economic triumph. “Venezuela has unarguably been an early success for the ‘Donroe Doctrine’,” Vaz notes. “Trump managed to trample international law, now has a powerful lever for energy security, a market to export US products, . . . and a pliant ally on international matters. And this in turn has meant driving away geopolitical rivals — China, Iran, and Russia — which had in Venezuela their most significant partner in the western hemisphere.” Yet the path forward for the United States is fraught with peril. The night Nicolás Maduro was captured by US forces, Trump and Rubio, with Pete Hegseth, were too consumed with triumphalism to realize they had set a new status quo. Every new failure of the Venezuelan government would no longer be an abstract consequence of mismanagement; they would now be partially owned by the United States. When social services are dismantled to feed the margins of foreign operators, when the state fails to deliver aid in the aftermath of devastating earthquakes, when the national grid collapses and plunges millions into darkness, and when the domestic economy is hollowed out by food imports and suppressed wages, the Venezuelan public will no longer look solely at the PSUV. They will also look at the US Treasury accounts holding their oil revenues in escrow, and to the foreign companies extracting their wealth under threat of military intervention. “Whether this is sustainable in the medium to long term is a tougher question,” Vaz argues. “Certainly, going back to neocolonial relations with the United States is not going to result in improved living conditions for the majority, and there’s only so much the US and local elites can contain with puppet presidents, repression, and electoral fraud.
