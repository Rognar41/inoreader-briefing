Tim Curry Was Never Not Having Fun

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Eileen Jones ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-26T21:53:50+00:00

It’s our miserable fate to lose Dolly Parton and Tim Curry in a one-two punch at the end of summer 2026. Both died at eighty years old, and it seems damned unfair that both didn’t live to be 120 and die mid-performance, which was Parton’s stated goal.What unites Parton and Curry is their joy. Two happier people would be hard to identify in the notoriously tragic world of showbiz fame, where behind-the-scenes lives tend toward grim pathos. Dolly Parton was known worldwide for her almost incredible levels of wide-smiling, life-loving élan that she managed to communicate to virtually everyone she met, and to everyone she didn’t, via wildly popular songs and film and television performances radiating ecstasy.Tim Curry’s Joker-esque grin was equally wide and contained something else, an almost salacious glee in performance. Nobody was going to have to ask him twice to get up there and provide some splendidly extroverted entertainment that would give the public something to cheer about. Like so many British actors, he approached his career with alacrity, eager to show up for almost anything. Theater, movies, television, music videos, audiobook and animated film and TV voice work — you name it, you’ll find Tim Curry there. He even cut a few albums.And his celebrity impersonations? Chef’s kiss. He did a fond but unsparing take on Mick Jagger’s pop-eyed, twitchy, mouthy, chicken-dance strutting to “Satisfaction” that’s a thing of beauty and a joy forever.Stage-trained, with a huge, expressive voice, he first made his mark in the 1968 London production of the landmark hippie musical Hair, and from there went directly into his breakthrough role of mad scientist and übersexy “sweet transvestite from Transylvania,” Dr Frank-N-Furter, in the legendary camp-rock musical The Rocky Horror Show. The show was such a smash, it went on to tour America from New York City to Los Angeles and made Tim Curry a household name. The 1975 Rocky Horror film version became a cult hit that was considered such required viewing among young people, it kicked off the “Midnight Movie” phenomenon on college campuses across the United States.It was in LA that Curry found himself most feted, where, as he put it, he went from “grubbing around” trying to pay the rent to “sitting by Jack Nicholson’s swimming pool.”But it was also where he found himself having to come to terms with the thorny experience of sustaining a showbiz career and negotiating media fame. His response was typically wholesome and practical: he began reading biographies of famous bygone movie actors to see how they’d managed it.Typecasting in Hollywood is especially pernicious, and Curry found himself constantly asked to reprise the Dr Frank-N-Furter effect by playing exuberant roles in drag. An effortless wit, Curry joked that he countered this tendency once and for all by aging in order to become “chubby and plain.”And he also discovered there were pitfalls to being a British actor in Hollywood generally, in that there were only a few roles on constant offer for Brits. This observation led to a three-word greeting always exchanged when encountering former Monty Python member Eric Idle: “Butlers and villains.”However, cast Curry as a butler, and you’ll inevitably get the loudest, funniest, most garrulous, and overly self-dramatizing English butler that ever lived, and his delightfully braying Wadsworth in Clue (1985) is the result.Curry was always such a big presence, it was deemed impossible to cast him as a self-effacing introvert or any kind of wallflower. If he’s onstage in The Pirates of Penzance, he’s playing the Pirate King and earning a 1982 Laurence Olivier Award nomination for Best Actor in a Musical. If he’s starting his voice-acting career in a Hanna–Barbera cartoon depicting the Garden of Eden, he’s playing the snake and hissing out all the best lines. And he’s never not employed doing cartoon voice work thereafter. He won a Daytime Emmy for the cartoon series Peter Pan & the Pirates (1990–91), playing — you guessed it — the colorfully craven Captain Hook.“I’m a toon type of guy,” he said cheerfully.Onstage in the 1980 Broadway production of Amadeus, he’s nominated for a Tony Award playing the obnoxious young genius Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart. In Clive Donner’s 1982 TV movie Oliver Twist, he’s the terrifying, murderous villain Bill Sikes. In Ridley Scott’s Legend (1985), he’s Darkness. In Muppet Treasure Island (1996), he’s “the sweet scoundrel pirate” Long John Silver. And in the first screen version of Stephen King’s It (1990), he’s inevitably the fanged clown Pennywise terrorizing children and guffawing with evil glee over it.So many consistently, unabashedly oversize performances won a lot of affection from the public over the decades. Because, to put it brutally, we live in an ever-duller era when it comes to actors, especially, but also performers in general. There’s so much tiresome ego and artistic pretension and, underneath all that mess, cowardly fear of risk-taking that it’s a real thrill when someone like Tim Curry practically prances out front — he’s so glad to give his all for entertainment.Curry titled his 2025 memoir Vagabond because of his own roving life and the fact that, as he explained, “in the 17th century, in the criminal code, actors were lumped together with thieves and vagabonds.” He was a heartening throwback to much more vivid days of yore when it came to performance, and with his usual congenial self-awareness, he was happy to announce the fact.He had a rather rough childhood, constantly on the move as the son of a naval chaplain who spent most of his time at sea. At age twelve, Curry lost his kindly, humorous father — who was only forty-five — to an untimely stroke. After he became an actor, Curry channeled “his mercurial mother’s rages” into a range of dark, daunting characters.In 2012, Curry nearly died of a stroke that partially paralyzed him. Terrified he might be confronting his father’s fate, confined to a wheelchair, and only gradually recovering his powers of speech, Curry still couldn’t imagine a life of inactivity. With his typical jaunty courage, he pivoted to memoir writing, then narrated the audiobook version.It’s heartwarming to read that after many years of high-spirited partying, in later adulthood Curry had come to love his domestic life extravagantly. His idea of a great evening had become reading books in the company of his dog, and avid gardening — his father’s hobby when ashore. “If you’re English, somewhere around thirty a trowel appears in your hand, sort of like a prosthetic device,” he said.Even after his stroke, Curry said he couldn’t imagine retiring. So it’s a bitter blow that forces outside himself have put an end to Tim Curry’s performing joys. Still, keep in mind that The Rocky Horror Picture Show is always playing somewhere in the world.

The Increased Cost of Living: An Analysis of Some of the Factors That Cause the Mysterious Jumps in Prices (1911)

by Gustavus Meyers Gustavus Meyers (1872 – 1942) was a journalist and historian most known for his study on the History of the Great American Fortunes , detailing the corruption and criminality behind their accumulation of wealth. The Masses was a socialist monthly that ran from 1911 until 1917, when the magazine was shuttered by the federal government for violating the Espionage Act by publishing criticism of conscription during the First World War. In the following essay, Meyers decries the weakened buying power of the working class due to rapid inflation, which he attributes to overvalued “watered stocks.” Perhaps readers will find resonance in the rising prices and looming AI bubble of our own time. – GLH This, of all times, is the time when wage-earners should support any economic movement in their behalf which promises to conserve the power of their wages. Of this there is the most imperative need. The purchasing power of money is not only constantly falling, but a gigantic conspiracy is already successfully under way to impose upon the workers a fiat currency which has not even an artificial basis of value, as capitalists reckon value in relation to money. Of the whole number of workers, few realize that the money that capitalists pay them in wages is a depreciated, swindling currency in more than one sense. A dollar to-day brings less than half in commodities of what it could purchase ten years ago. The enormous, exorbitant advances in the prices of every necessity have so reduced the buying power of a dollar, that it is equal in value to less than what half a dollar was a decade ago. In no case have wages been adequately increased to meet this increase in the cost of living. The strongest labor unions have not been able to get more than a fifteen per cent increase in wages, and such an increase is exceptional, at that. What is a fifteen per cent increase compared to an increase in the cost of living which approximates more than fifty per cent of what it was ten years ago? The worker, who has by great difficulties amassed a few hundred dollars in a savings bank as as a security for sickness or being out of work, has been robbed of more than one-half the amount of his deposit. If, let us say, he has $200 to his credit in bank for the last few years, he really has not $200 but less than $100. If he drew the $200 out of bank, he would find out that it would buy considerably less than what $100 did a few years ago. The only value of money is its buying capacity; a dollar may still nominally be a dollar; but if its purchasing power is cut in half, it actually is only a half dollar. Thus billions of dollars are being indirectly robbed from the meager wages of the workers to pay dividends on enormous issues of watered stocks put out by every trust and other capitalist concern. But this is not all. Under a recent act passed by Congress at the bidding of the great financial interests, the national banks are allowed to turn out currency against these very watered stocks. Hotherto, the United States Government allowed currency – that is, bank bills – to be issued against deposits of United States bonds only. But the Aldrich-Vreeland bill, passed recently, contained this “little joker,” “or any other securities.” The result has been that immense quantities of watered stocks have been deposited with the United States Treasury, and many millions of currency have been issued against them. It is announced that $500,000,000 more will be issued against these watered stocks. All of this currency is wild-cat currency, having nothing but a fiat value, which means no basis value, even as capitalist standards go. This is the spurious stuff that is now being paid out to the workers, while the banknotes or Treasury notes representing gold, silver, or United States bonds are being carefully gathered in by the great financial interests. A smash-up is sure to come. The dice are loaded against the working class, and pending the time when the whole capitalist regime will be overthrown, the workers should realize that they must give their support to co-operative movements. Only by this means, with the present odds so fearfully against them, can they hope to conserve something of the buying power of their scant wages, now being largely paid in wild-cat currency. If, instead of depositing their money in banks where it is cut in half and used against them, the workers should become partners in co-operative concerns, they would have the benefit of their wages, which they do not have now. The Daycare Wars Are a Trap

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Elliot Haspel ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-26T16:21:20+00:00

On a policy level, much of the debate about childcare comes down to how best to shore up a failing system. While there is tremendous disagreement about what role government should play, everyone from democratic socialists to the US Chamber of Commerce to Republican senators acknowledges that childcare isn’t working for too many households and that it’s having negative ripple effects on both families and the economy. From the reddest states to the bluest, lawmakers are actively wrestling with that reality.On an individual parent level, however, much of the debate is still stuck on whether sending a child to a daycare center is going to harm them for life. That argument — along with its counterpoint, that formal childcare is an unalloyed positive — gets in the way of a productive conversation about making sure every parent has the care they need to raise their kids the way they want while providing for their families.It was helpful, then, when Phoebe Arslanagic-Little and Ellen Pasternack wrote a recent piece titled “Daycare pseudoscience” for Works in Progress, an outlet generally aligned with the “abundance” movement. Much of their ire is directed at daycare critics like the prominent psychoanalyst Erica Komisar, who regularly claims that formal childcare disrupts children’s normal brain development — claims that Arslanagic-Little and Pasternack dismiss as “pure speculation.”As enjoyable as that takedown may be, the pair are just as hard on liberal leaders, who routinely treat early care and education as a near-miracle intervention capable of transforming children’s educational and later-life outcomes. Enough research has been done, the authors counter, that “if there were large, lasting impacts on children, either positive or negative, we would probably know by now.”I study chidcare for a living, and Arslanagic-Little and Pasternack do make some valid critiques. Out of a well-intentioned zeal for helping young children, the early childhood sector has at times gotten out over its evidentiary skis; there has been a tendency, for instance, to lean too heavily on small-sample studies of unusually well-funded and rigorous programs from the 1960s and ’70s. And while early childhood is indeed a sensitive period for brain development, when many foundational neural connections are formed, the brain remains fairly plastic throughout middle childhood and well into adolescence.That said, Arslanagic-Little and Pasternack also miss important points. First and foremost, it’s vital to remember that there is always a counterfactual at play: Where would the child in question be getting their early care and learning if they weren’t enrolled in a given childcare program? While the authors scoff at how hard global development groups push for early care and education investments in low-income countries, the alternative for young kids in many of those cases is neglect — if not outright danger.Most US studies compare children who are generally getting adequate care and learning in one setting to children who are generally getting adequate care and learning in a different setting. There is certainly such a thing as high-quality early care and low-quality early care, and we should be pushing for as much of the former as possible. But it’s not shocking — especially given that healthy child development seems to require sufficiency, not optimization — that adequate versions of various formal and informal care settings don’t lead to fundamentally different outcomes on a population level (even while they can absolutely lead to different outcomes on an individual level).That’s not evidence, however, that childcare writ large has no large and lasting impacts, and the counterfactual is also likely why the largest positive results of childcare accumulate for lower-income children.It’s also worth noting here that a lot of the early childhood research in America relies on very old data. There has not been a major childcare-focused study that follows a cohort of babies through adolescence and beyond since the federal Study of Early Child Care and Youth Development, which recruited babies born in 1991. Yes, the kids in one of the most-cited studies we’ve got on childcare impacts were in care during the George H. W. Bush administration! If anyone would like to get on funding another one — especially after the Trump administration canceled a cohort study of kindergartners that was already underway, which was the closest we were going to get for a while — that would be very welcome.Back to the topic at hand, many of the positive impacts of a well-funded childcare system seem to flow through the parents and not the child. Because young children develop in a family ecosystem, the stability of the family is paramount — both in terms of material stability, like having secure housing and food, and relational stability, like having parents who are able to be warm and responsive.For instance, a study of extended-day pre-K in New Haven, Connecticut, found that parents who won a lottery to get a slot saw their earnings go up by nearly 22 percent on average thanks to the extra care access, with those gains persisting for at least six years. Even though researchers didn’t detect much lasting academic impact compared to the lottery losers (perhaps not stunning given the state of New Haven schools), it’s hard to imagine that level of improvement to family financial stability wouldn’t have a salutary impact on child well-being.Similarly, a population-level scan misses all the ways in which childcare programs act as social infrastructure, facilitating friendships and creating vital neighborhood networks that can frequently be life-changing or even lifesaving.But networks aside, those benefits aren’t unique to formal childcare programs: families can gain just as much from having real options to use family, friends, or neighbors as caregivers or to be financially supported to provide care themselves.Starting from the principle that society does better when parents have viable childcare choices — and the ability to move between them as needs and preferences change — leads to a very different place than the quicksand of the daycare wars. This is a common arrangement internationally, as in several Nordic nations, where families can choose between a heavily subsidized (often free) licensed program and a monthly “home care stipend.” The key, of course, is that for licensed childcare programs to actually be viable and not constantly closing or paying skilled early educators a pittance that leads to sky-high turnover, they need permanent public funding.That’s why it’s encouraging to see Democrats moving in the direction of a more pluralistic, inclusive, fully funded system, as with Project 2029’s “Child Care Choice Guarantee,” written by veterans of the last several Democratic administrations. So let critics like Komisar keep fearmongering; everyone else would do well to ignore the distraction and focus on building a system where parents can choose the care situations that work best for their families — without the irrational fear that picking wrong will cause permanent damage.

For The Economist, Mentioning Workers’ Interests Is Heresy

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Alberto Garzón ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-26T14:54:02+00:00

Daron Acemoglu, winner in 2024 of the so-called Nobel Prize in economics, has just published a new book, What Happened to Liberal Democracy ? Now The Economist — the paper, founded in 1843, that stands as the epitome of free trade and economic liberalism — has seized on the opportunity to settle a few scores . In a piece that has taken everyone by surprise, it leveled a scattering of theoretical criticisms at Acemoglu’s work and, above all, called into question the standing of a highly regarded economist. Perhaps the clearest evidence is that The Economist declares in its headline that Acemoglu is “oddly unconvincing,” and that his arguments about AI are pessimistic to the point of losing credibility. Small wonder that the target of the attack took it personally and felt obliged to respond in public. Everything suggests that the ultimate cause of the falling-out is that Acemoglu’s academic work, which revolves around technological change and the role of artificial intelligence, offers a proposal to strengthen the working class. This is one of the points The Economist goes after, labeling his approach pessimistic and unspecific. The economist Mònica Martínez Bravo captured the tension well when she wrote of “what curious things happen when a Nobel laureate in economics writes a book arguing for putting the working class back at the center of economic policy.” In what follows, I want to examine in what sense, and how far, Martínez Bravo is right. And along the way I want to head into the fascinating and worrying world of the socioeconomic consequences of artificial intelligence. The Role of Automation One of the great open questions in economics concerns the effects of automation, that is, of the replacement of human workers by machines. Does it destroy jobs? Does it depress wages? Does it alter the balance of forces between capital and labor? Two hundred years on, the debate has produced no consensus at all, only a range of approaches for thinking about a highly complex process. And to understand it properly, we have to go back to the early nineteenth century. David Ricardo is regarded as one of the founding fathers of political economy. In the first two editions of his major work, the Principles , however, Ricardo had little in particular to say about automation, since he shared the conventional optimistic view of the economists of his day. It is important to grasp this way of thinking about automation because, in broad terms, it still dominates the profession. On this view, when machines replace workers in a production process, two effects follow. The first is the obvious one: a certain number of people lose their jobs. The second is that introducing the new machinery can create new jobs that absorb the previously displaced labor. In other words, for economists there is nothing here to worry about, because the economy regulates itself in such a way that the introduction of machines — the incorporation of new technology — is able to sustain the level of employment. And Ricardo thought more or less this, but only until 1821. In the famous third edition of the Principles , Ricardo added a new chapter in which he judged the conventional view to be mistaken and acknowledged that “the substitution of machinery for human labour is often very injurious to the interests of the class of labourers.” The impact of this shift was enormous, and his disciple John Ramsay McCulloch complained to him bitterly on the grounds that it gave encouragement to the anti-machine workers’ movements. It was a time of enormous social unrest: the Industrial Revolution was unfolding across the United Kingdom and radically transforming the working class’s way of life. In a 2024 academic paper , Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson addressed this historical moment precisely. In their account, Ricardo’s change of mind about machinery had to do with his election to the House of Commons. His seat allowed him to see firsthand what was actually happening in the cotton industry and so correct his earlier views, because what was happening bore no resemblance to the economists’ optimistic picture: the Industrial Revolution was severely degrading the living conditions of the working class. The paper, incidentally, leans heavily on the work of two giants of British Marxist historiography, E. P. Thompson and Eric Hobsbawm (though it also cites, by way of complement, the eminent mainstream historian Joel Mokyr). Acemoglu’s figures indicate that, with automation, the real wages of handloom weavers fell by at least 25 percent, with no possibility for those workers of moving into new, emerging sectors. Their living conditions were simply flattened by the introduction of machinery into industry. The paradox is that, because productivity was rising and wages were not, employers reaped enormous profits that pushed inequality upward. In a phrase which Acemoglu returns to again and again: prosperity was not shared. The logic of this critical reading of automation had been built into Acemoglu’s work long before. It appears most clearly, in a form aimed at the general reader, in his 2023 book Power and Progress , cowritten with Simon Johnson, where they stress that introducing new technology is not automatically beneficial for society. Whether productivity gains are shared, they argue, depends on factors such as the type of technology, the institutions (norms, customs, laws), and the direction society chooses to give technological advances. Significantly, Acemoglu and Johnson accept that prosperity is shared only when those advances depart from elites’ self-interest. Hence their account of the improvements in working-class living standards during the second half of the nineteenth century, which they attribute to the democratization of the political system, the growth of trade unions and legislation protecting workers’ rights; all of them factors that forced technical change in a direction other than that of the elites’ narrow economic interests. Anyone familiar with the history of the labor movement will recognize a clearly subversive political element in this line of reasoning. It would be a mistake to think that all this is merely economic history or nineteenth-century political economy. Acemoglu’s point of reference is always present-day technological change, and more specifically the adoption of artificial intelligence in today’s production processes. But he uses economic history to think about the contemporary process, asking (in Power and Progress ) what happens if the mainstream economists are wrong: if AI fundamentally generates labor market distortions that widen inequalities in wages and employment; if its impact redistributes power toward elites; if it impoverishes billions of people in the developing world; if it entrenches ethnic or social prejudice; or if it destroys democratic institutions. His conclusion is that contemporary society needs what emerged in the nineteenth century: the growth of counterarguments and organizations capable of pushing back against conventional thinking. To give a fuller sense of where he stands, it is enough to note that one of his most recent papers , coauthored with David Autor and Simon Johnson, is entitled “Building Pro-Worker Artificial Intelligence.” Not a Heterodox Economist Although some have found him too much of a dissenter, it is worth remembering that Daron Acemoglu is no revolutionary in the classical sense, even within his own discipline. The first book of his to reach my shelves was not an essay but his 2009 textbook Introduction to Modern Economic Growth : a graduate text of formidable algebraic complexity that surveys the models of economic growth from a single vantage point, namely, neoclassical theory. This is the dominant theory in economics faculties, and other schools of thought have historically been ranged against it, such as the post-Keynesian and the Marxist, which are for that reason regarded as heterodox . Acemoglu belongs to the dominant school, and indeed he recently described debate about capitalism as “mindless.” In the worldview underlying his models, there are no relations of production; power enters as a category of bargaining strength and regulatory capture; and the view of institutions is thin and never structural. His concept of the working class is consequently not a Marxist one, but it leads to what he calls “ working-class liberalism .” Yet all this is exactly what makes him genuinely dangerous in the eyes of mainstream economists: Acemoglu reaches his “radical” conclusions from inside the profession and inside the mainstream. His entire academic output is a lucid and algebraically demanding reformulation of the neoclassical models of technical progress. And every step he has taken over the past twenty years, such as treating the division of income between capital and labor as endogenous to technical change, he has taken without drawing on the vast body of heterodox work that trod that path long before him. Acemoglu today holds a view of trade that would not seem strange to Marxist authors, and a view of income distribution that post-Keynesians such as Joan Robinson or Nicholas Kaldor would recognize. And we have already seen that his reading of certain aspects of nineteenth-century economic history has striking points of contact with Marxist historiography. The crucial point is that Acemoglu has arrived at conclusions very close to those of the post-Keynesian and even Marxist schools but through different concepts and methods. Mainstream economists cannot fault him for want of technical brilliance, which is amply established. But they are frightened by the political conclusions that follow from his deviation. The maneuver had already been rehearsed with Joseph Stiglitz. The US economist received the Nobel Prize in 2001 for his contributions to neoclassical theory, in particular his analyses of asymmetric information, and during the 1990s he was known, among other things, for his critique of the hard version of ecological economics. All of this made him chief economist of the World Bank, one of the institutions charged with promoting the “Washington Consensus” among developing countries — that is, with imposing neoliberal policies. Before his term was up, Stiglitz left his post, and in 2002 he published Globalization and Its Discontents , in which he called much of neoliberal policy into question. It was a harsh diagnosis, and one that those of us who were then postgraduate students of international and development economics always found instructive: a neoclassical economist attacking the conclusions of his own old paradigm. As now, The Economist was the spearhead against what struck it as an inadmissible turn, remarking drily that Stiglitz could have written a good book about globalization, but perhaps next time, and conceding that he was a brilliant economist before proceeding to demolish his standing altogether. Nor was it the only example: Kenneth Rogoff, then director of research at the International Monetary Fund (the other neoliberal pillar of the international system), attacked Stiglitz fiercely and entrenched what became the settled view among mainstream economists that as a scholar Stiglitz was a genius with a wonderful mind, but as a politician he was far less impressive. It is the clearest precedent for The Economist ’s article on Daron Acemoglu. What we can expect over the coming years is a concerted effort, led by part of the mainstream community of which The Economist is merely the vanguard, to devalue Acemoglu’s reputation, not so much on technical grounds as on account of his political “eccentricities.” As we have seen, this is not the first case and probably will not be the last, but the underlying logic has been in place for a very long time. The aura of respectability within the mainstream economics community depends both on technical foundations (with a high degree of mathematical sophistication, which makes the work largely inaccessible to the general public) and on the “reasonableness” of one’s political conclusions. It is worth recalling that the Nobel Prize in economics is not really a Nobel Prize at all, since Alfred Nobel never envisaged such an award for economics. That such a prize exists today is the result of the interests of the Swedish central bank, which in 1968, amid a bitter confrontation with the Swedish Social Democrats (then pursuing their reformist road to socialism), needed to burnish the reputation of those who defended its own (conservative) political positions. The great Swedish economist Gunnar Myrdal received the prize in 1974; but his own reading of the episode was that, because he was a “radical” (his word), they made him share it with a “reactionary,” Friedrich Hayek. In 1977, Myrdal wrote a short article acknowledging that he had been wrong to accept it, that economics is not a science on a par with physics or the “hard” sciences and — take note — that the Nobel Prize in economics ought not to exist. What he was ultimately suggesting is that the award is heavily burdened with political values and principles. What This Means for the Present Daron Acemoglu’s work has, to my mind, many limitations. Perhaps the most obvious is that, for all its focus on technology and technical change, it entirely omits any consideration of energy. To discuss the transformation of the nineteenth-century British economy without mentioning the role of coal is odd. Equally problematic is the scant attention paid to the imperial dimension of British industrialization — that is, to the fact that colonial trade, slavery, resource extraction, and Britain’s position in the world economy were part of the historical conditions under which the Industrial Revolution took place. This connects with another point for which Acemoglu has been widely criticized, this time from the heterodox side, and which is present in earlier books such as Why Nations Fail: The Origins of Power, Prosperity, and Poverty , cowritten with James Robinson. Acemoglu’s account of economic development is institutionalist, treating factors such as innovation, democracy, and types of government as the central variables determining which countries grow and which do not. But his interpretation of artificial intelligence, his emphasis on the task model — it is not jobs that are automated but tasks — and his insistence that the effects of automation depend on power and on institutions such as the law, are correct and crucial for our present moment. It is true that other traditions can offer sharper and more structural accounts of the role of technology, but Acemoglu’s originality and brilliance lie in having reached the same destination as heterodox authors, albeit by another route. Complementarities between the different traditions are well worth exploiting. His insistence across several papers that technology can be (mis)used to tighten control over workers (something that AI is already doing) could gain a great deal from the entire Marxist tradition that begins with Harry Braverman’s superb book , Labor and Monopoly Capital , and revolutionized labor market theory. The same goes for the role of income distribution, which is not only a moral question but also one of economic efficiency (particularly in models where demand plays an important part). Finally, many unresolved aspects of Acemoglu’s work will require development over the coming months and years, and they are decisive for public policy. His central proposition is that technology must be directed if it is to promote shared prosperity. His critics, including The Economist , point out how vague this is in practice. On this they are right: Acemoglu and his coauthors sketch a course that involves intervening in the outcomes of the self-regulating market in order to steer technology “somewhere else.” The historical comparison implies that the effect sought is the strengthening of trade unions, democracy, and counterpower against elites (read: the tech oligarchs), but the “how” of achieving all this is still missing. Acemoglu also insists that society has alternatives to deploying technology in ways that will seriously hurt the working class. He accepts that employers seek to raise their profits and increase average labor productivity, even where that deployment produces no social benefit (in his terms, where it does not raise the marginal productivity of labor). A structural analysis will object that an employer adopts a given profit-maximizing technology because competition compels him to, so it is unclear how things could be otherwise without recourse to classical instruments such as state regulation. At root, the ordinary grammar of any process of “directing technology” is that of planning, the state, and social protection; and that is probably where the next round of arguments in the field will be found. In sum, I hope to have shown why I believe, along with other economists, that The Economist ’s attack on a widely respected and brilliant economist can be explained. Acemoglu’s problem, from the mainstream’s point of view, is that he has used mainstream economics’ own conceptual tools to show that technology and the market do not automatically produce shared prosperity. To accept that argument is to call into question every promise made by the promoters of AI (and also its associated costs, in which energy once again plays a central part), and to argue for public policies utterly unlike those defended from the centers of power tied to the big technology firms. To my mind, that is a highly fruitful path, and it is clear that we need more shared prosperity, which is to say, more of the sort of interventions and policies that Acemoglu proposes.

For those in the know, cinematically, Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma is an event film, though a small, arty one defying the odds by getting a mainstream release. Writer-director Jane Schoenbrun had a cult-film phenomenon with her last horror film, I Saw the TV Glow (2024), and her next horror outing was keenly anticipated by those who seek out adventurous new auteurs. I could tell by the youthful audience attending the first wide-release screening with me — all a little unusual and, with the exception of one Goth couple, sitting strictly alone — that we’d all heard the jungle drums announcing an offbeat, must-see release. I was by far the oldest person there. Brilliantly cast, Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma stars Hannah Einbinder, stand-up comedian and star of Hacks , as Kris Williams, a nerdy, academically trained writer-director who’s been hired to reboot a moribund slasher film franchise that got its popular start back in the 1980s under the title Camp Miasma . Together at Billy’s wintry home in the mountains where that first low-budget Camp Miasma gore fest was set and shot, Kris and Billy begin a collaboration on the rebooted franchise film by exploring the meaning and impact of the Camp Miasma films through their own increasingly intimate and erotically charged relationship. While watching the first Camp Miasma film together, screened in Billy’s own unlikely movie house in the woods, Kris also has a knee-jerk tendency to announce “That’s the transphobic part” at the start of the scene when the camp counselor (Zach Cherry) describes to the teenage campers the backstory of the undead slasher-killer Little Death (Jack Haven), who was raised by two luridly disturbed hermaphrodite parents to be “a different gender every day.” Billy’s response to Kris’s interruption is to shush her. And she later explains in her extravagant Blanche DuBois Southern drawl that the first Camp Miasma movie — and the only one Billy starred in, withdrawing from the world immediately afterward — “isn’t about all that. . . . It’s about flesh and fluid.” So the stage is set for a sexually charged tutorial in which Billy teaches the repressed Kris to acknowledge the visceral thrills that are the basis for her obsession since preadolescence with the Camp Miasma films. In short, Kris finds them to be a huge turn-on, mediated through the processes of film spectatorship. She identifies in excitingly taboo ways with “the victim, the slut” who is invariably half-dressed and nubile. “Don’t forget nubile!” says Kris with sly glee once she gets into the spirit of things. Hilarious shots of Kris stripped down to panties and a tight T-shirt running in slo-mo through the woods in an exaggeratedly camp-erotic, breast-bobbing way, pursued by the spear-wielding Little Death, are part of the cocreated slasher film imagined by Kris and Billy. Kris also identifies with Little Death, as well as with the Final Girl, as played by sensitive, virginal young Billy Presley (Amanda Fix), whose first sexual experience in character is filmed in a deliriously complex set of shots alternating her own point of view with that of the approaching killer, Little Death. Little Death’s view of the sexually engaged about-to-be-victims is conflated with the camera POV as they approach the cot with phallic spear raised. Also intercut are increasingly probing close-up shots of “the look in her eye” that fascinates Kris, which includes the miniature reflection of the approaching killer in her pupil, as Final Girl/Billy and the teenage boy on top of her are about to be penetrated by the spear of Little Death. And I suppose I have to note here that “the little death,” that is “la petite mort,” is a French term for orgasm. Judging by agitated online commentary, I gather this comes as thrilling news to a lot of young viewers. A Scarred Veteran of Film Theory I’ve got to admit, a little of this kind of thing goes a long way with me, and I found the film rather wearing over the course of its running time. But then, I’m a scarred veteran of grad school myself, and the old wounds still hurt in rainy weather. My film studies dissertation advisor was Carol Clover, who literally wrote the book on the slasher film. She’s the author of Men, Women, and Chain Saws: Gender in the Modern Horror Film , the first academic analysis of the subgenre and the one who introduced the term “the Final Girl” that’s now in common usage. During the interlude following its publication, when Clover achieved remarkable fame for a professor, I was sitting in her office trying to get useful dissertation advice while she took calls from reporters around the world who wanted to interview her about The Texas Chain Saw Massacre, Friday the 13th, Halloween , A Nightmare on Elm Street , and all the well-known slasher film classics. Most viewers aren’t going to have this survivor-of-academia problem with the film — lucky them — and I hope most will have a more accurate idea of the tone of the film than the one I went in with. I was expecting a different movie entirely. Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma , which is both a study and a parody of slasher horror, is never scary. The geysers of blood are played for camp laughs. It’s a funnier film than I expected, with a satirical approach to Hollywood filmmaking that no doubt reflects Schoenbrun’s own insider experiences, including ludicrous Zoom calls with multiple producers. But the film is also considerably less wild than I anticipated. The promised “flesh and fluid” scenes, hyped in promo material that describes Kris and Billy descending into “a frenzy of psychosexual mania,” never really materialize. Sex scenes are mostly nurturing and, it must be said, quite tame, as the focus of the film remains on Kris’s remote, intellectualized, and heavily mediated route to sexual satisfaction. Jane Schoenbrun, who uses they/them pronouns, seems to have a great affection for vintage film theory of the 1980s–’90s. Though clearly, they also have a humorously critical take on it, given that theory-damaged Kris is its representative. Kris, after all, solemnly describes her first breakthrough film, which was well-received at the Sundance Film Festival, as a remake of Psycho “from the point of view of the shower curtain.” Schoenbrun has a much-discussed auteur’s investment in their films in terms of their own self-identification as trans and nonbinary and as a queer filmmaker. They’ve explored the experience of gender transition and dysphoria in their three films, We’re All Going to the World’s Fair (2021), I Saw the TV Glow , and Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma . In interviews , Schoenbrun says, “I’ve been treating my own gender transition as my muse” in films dealing allegorically with “getting to the place where you’re ready to transition and the coming-out process” and “speaking to the joy and horror of coming into your own body and self.” Their investment in media, the obsessive film and TV show viewing that helped get Schoenbrun and so many other alienated souls — myself included — through the torments of maturation in the United States, is manifest in Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma in innumerable ways through evocations of such multigenerational classics as Sunset Boulevard , Psycho, The Shining , and Videodrome . There are constant references to slasher films of yore, including the obscure Sleepaway Camp (1983), which is the clearest inspiration for the Camp Miasma film-within-a-film. The Enticement of Cinema Schoenbrun has an excellent cinematic eye, a real flair for striking and evocative imagery, including a love of lush color schemes, which does a lot to jolt Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma with energy whenever it threatens to bog down. For example, Kris and Billy walk casually from a realistically wintry forest into the alternate old-time cinematic world represented by a gorgeous matte painting of snowy trees behind them. There are sudden insertions of irradiated-looking negative imagery in vivid montages. The “split diopter” shot reverently identified by Kris during the Camp Miasma screening pays tribute to a popular technique of 1970s filmmaking that created a sense of depth in an image by holding two different areas on different planes in sharp focus simultaneously, with a slight blurring of the planes between them. Once Kris “goes into” the rebooted Camp Miasma film, she’s contained within the split diopter shots, and the penultimate shot of the overall film shows Kris and Billy together in split diopter, one near, one far, in equally sharp focus, exulting in the Final Girl’s quoted lines about Little Death from Camp Miasma : “He’s still down there, you know, at the bottom of the lake. He’s still hungry. Can you feel it? He’ll be back soon to feed again.” And Gillian Anderson’s Billy Presley character, with her delightfully exaggerated Southern drawl, her red lipstick and glamorous long blond tresses, and the rhinestone-bedazzled turban she dons to evoke the spirit of Norma Desmond in Sunset Boulevard , indicates the way she represents the enticement of cinema itself. In short, Schoenbrun keeps confirming in steadily higher-budgeted, wider-released, and more extravagant films that they’re an arresting new talent on the rise. For that reason alone, any cinephile should already be headed to the movies to see their latest.

Dolly Parton Used Her Voice to Tell Working-Class Stories

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Jarek Paul Ervin ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-26T13:55:18+00:00

Legendary singer, songwriter, and cultural icon Dolly Parton died on August 25 at age eighty. Her death immediately inspired a flood of praise from stars and public figures, as well as a flurry of chatter on social media. Few public figures right now would attract such universal accolades. The Empire State Building was lit up in pink in her honor, and President Donald Trump ordered American flags lowered to half-staff for a week. Indeed, Parton’s appeal transcends political divisions in a time when many cultural rifts seem beyond repair; Republican Speaker of the House Mike Johnson and his political opponent, Democrat Chuck Schumer , are only two of many politicians who expressed affection for the late star. Parton endures above all for her triumphs as a musician: she sold a hundred million records, landed forty-four albums in the country music top ten, and crossed over into the Hot 100. Of the more than three thousand songs she wrote, several are among pop music’s most beloved, including “ Jolene ,” “ 9 to 5 ,” and “ I Will Always Love You ” — the latter made instantly recognizable by Whitney Houston’s immortal cover . But Parton outgrew her humble origins as a Nashville songwriter, becoming a larger-than-life wisecracking icon in pop culture writ large. A March 2026 poll found that 70 percent of surveyed Americans had positive feelings about her, and many online commentators used her as a yardstick for likeability . During her career, Parton won acclaim for her philanthropy and support for marginalized social groups — especially the LGBTQ community, who has always had a thing for her. Even so, she explicitly refused to take a stand on current politics, saying , “I don’t do politics. I’m an entertainer.” Parton also had a series of gaffes later in life, including duetting with Kid Rock and rerecording “9 to 5” as a schlocky pro-business jingle for Squarespace. That doesn’t matter. Regardless of her views, Parton was one of the most consistent voices for working-class people. She used her songs to tell stories of hard work, poverty, and defiance, transforming the lives of ordinary people into subject matter for heroic ballads. Zohran Mamdani put it best when he characterized Parton as an “irreplaceable champion of the working class.” Today, we mourn that great champion of the working class. A Working-Class Voice Throughout her career, Parton defied polite norms and smashed the stereotypes that were used to dismiss rural women’s concerns. She was famous for self-deprecating quips like “it takes a lot of money to look this cheap,” embracing an aesthetic that was often dismissed because of its class origins. The singer often bragged that she modeled her look on her rural community’s “town tramp,” saying , “She was the prettiest thing I’d ever seen. When everybody would say, ‘Oh, she’s just trash,’ I’d say, ‘Well, trash is what I’m going to grow up to be.’” This sensibility permeated Parton’s music, which cast tramps, widows, hungry children, divorcees, and other working-class women as narrators. Her songs told tales of poor families who nevertheless held their heads high and carried on even when the deck was stacked against them. She also wasn’t afraid to tackle difficult themes like women’s sexuality and marital infidelity. These topics were central to her music during the 1960s and ’70s. For example, the title track from her 1969 In the Good Old Days ( When Times Were Bad ) captured the pain of farm workers who toil from sunup to sundown only to watch their crops get beaten down by hail. The song spoke of families too poor to go to the doctor as they watch their house fall into disrepair. The record also featured covers of Tammy Wynette’s “D-I-V-O-R-C-E,” which candidly addressed the pain of marital separation, and Jeannie C. Riley’s beloved crossover hit “Harper Valley PTA,” which told the story of a widow who exposed the hypocritical scolds who called her an unfit mother. These tracks directly linked Parton to an era of powerful women singers who tackled controversial issues. In addition to Wynette and Riley, that era saw some of the finest moments of singers like Bobbie Gentry, who boldly addressed women’s sexuality, suicide, and prostitution, as well as Loretta Lynn, who unapologetically spoke about gender inequality on tracks like “Don’t Come Home A-Drinkin’ (With Lovin’ on Your Mind)” (1966) and “The Pill” (1975) — the latter of which was explicitly about birth control. This era also hosted men like Johnny Cash, John Prine, and Kris Kristofferson, singers who documented the ugly and beautiful things that were often ignored or pushed out of view. Parton was only one of many artists who treated country music as a space for telling the hard truths about a society that was often cast in a rosy light by its apologists. She stands among the best of them for the power and passion that infused her treatment of her subject matter. In the 1970s, Parton continued to expand her themes and grow her reputation. Even while breaking into television as a cohost alongside the rhinestone-clad Porter Wagoner, she continued to put out albums that dove into the dark and difficult side of life. Her “Coat of Many Colors” (1971), for example, turns childhood poverty into quiet defiance. The track tells the story of a child who goes to school with patchwork clothes and worn-through shoes, ignoring the bullies because she knows her family is rich in spirit. My Tennessee Mountain Home (1973), a concept album about her childhood in East Tennessee, features “Daddy’s Working Boots,” one of the greatest odes to the toil and sacrifice of working people. And then there’s “Jolene,” from her 1974 album of the same name. It’s a potent story of a woman who fears she’ll be left behind for another woman. Working 9 to 5 Parton’s most recognizable and powerful depiction of class is “9 to 5,” a story of a woman who is “trapped in a rich man’s game.” The song was penned for the 1980 film of the same name, which saw Parton star alongside Jane Fonda and Lily Tomlin as three exhausted women who turn the tables on their sexist, exploitative boss. Parton wrote the track while working on the film. She drew inspiration from the clicking of her fingernails, imitating the clacking of a secretary’s typewriter. (Parton is credited on the track for performances on both lead vocals and “nails.”) The song landed at number one on three different Billboard charts — the Hot 100, Adult Contemporary, and Hot Country Songs. This track has sometimes been criticized as a resilience narrative, a paean to sucking it up and getting it done. That theme is central to Parton’s 2021 Super Bowl commercial for Squarespace, featuring a reworked version of the song where she tells the listener to start a business and “be your own boss.” This is unfortunate, given both the original track and the subject matter of the film. Opening with the clacking of a typewriter-like rhythm, Parton’s lyrics chronicle the experiences of an overworked and underpaid woman. She sings about giving your boss “all your time” while he gets “all the credit,” critiquing a system of “all taking and no giving” where workers are “barely getting by.” While the cheeriness of the track left it open to feel-good co-optation, the original is rife with anger about the inequities it describes. That’s further underscored by the film. Fed up with mistreatment, the three women contemplate murdering their boss and ultimately decide to kidnap him. While he’s hidden away, they take over the business, winning over their fellow employees by instituting reforms like fair pay, better hours, and on-site childcare. The Squarespace commercial has become the stuff of ridicule — the New Statesman called the song a “sell-out to toxic ‘side hustle’ culture,” while David Sirota described it as “the most dystopian ad I’ve ever seen.” But the original song is what people remember, a powerful testament to Parton’s working-class messaging at the high point of her career. Borrowing Her Voice In the end, Parton wasn’t the Left’s perfect hero. Her views were complex, often rooted in idealized images of resilience and entrepreneurship as much as in representations of a system that was never going to deliver success to everyone. In that sense, she embodies a tension that is at the heart of country music itself, a genre that has often captured working-class life better than many other genres, yet just as often resolves that pain into status-quo traditionalism rather than confronting its causes. But music doesn’t always have to double as a political sermon. Art isn’t just here to smuggle in orthodoxies like the textbook of a Marxist schoolmaster. Parton addressed what people were waiting to hear about: poverty, injustice, labor exploitation, and working-class pride. She did so in a way that was as funny as it was heart-wrenching, producing music that endures long after its moment. Those subjects are still too rare on the country and pop charts today, not to mention in the Left’s own conception of who its audience is. Parton gave a voice to those who are all too often forgotten, both in culture and politics. It wouldn’t hurt the Left to steal a page from her songbook.

FIFA’s Gianni Infantino Must Resign

Gianni Infantino must be shocked. He’s spent his near-decade-long tenure as FIFA president squeezing football for every cent possible. Now he’s weathering a global revolt against his leadership just for doing what he’s always done, after he quite literally tried to sell the World Cup to the highest bidder. Popular criticism toward Infantino has long been brewing, reaching fever pitch during the rip-off convention that was the 2026 World Cup. But secretly developing a scheme to open the world’s most popular (and already extremely profitable) sporting event to private equity has proven too much even for some of his closest cronies. Previously impressively indestructible, Infantino now genuinely seems to be in trouble, as one national football federation after another withdraws support for his reelection. The end of his reign would be cause for worldwide celebration. His ability to degrade an already corrupt and broken institution, and deeply harm football in the process, cannot be overstated. FIFA’s Folly Using FIFA to print money at fans’ expense has long been Infantino’s modus operandi. Still, his latest move crossed a line for even some of football’s most corrupt and moneygrubbing actors. JPMorgan Chase, which helped cook up the last big financial failure in football, the aborted 2021 breakaway Super League , spent a year fleshing out the plan in secret. Joshua Kushner, brother of Donald Trump’s son-in-law Jared Kushner, was primed to be to be a lead investor. When the deal fell apart, Kushner immediately moved on to trying to buy the Los Angeles Lakers , perhaps showing that his heart wasn’t really in football after all. Infantino’s FFE proposal only survived, publicly, at least, for a lamentable three days. It was scrapped shortly after it was leaked by The Times , soon prompting overwhelming criticism. Even typical Infantino sycophants, like regional confederations CONCACAF (North and Central America and the Caribbean) and AFC (Asia) were quick to reject the plan. Top Infantino adviser at FIFA and former investment banker Carlos Cordeiro quit in disgust . Whether football’s top brass are truly upset about preserving the sanctity of the game or merely frustrated about being cut out of the wheeling and dealing, they’re upset. Led by European federation UEFA, which has long been critical of Infantino, power brokers have seen enough blood in the water to try to force a coup. It argued that “When trust is broken through deception, when an individual places himself above the collective that entrusted him with authority,” the duty of service to the “football family” has “been abandoned.” UEFA has pledged to boycott FIFA tournaments and is pushing for Infantino’s ousting even after the failure of FFE. Even with the widespread criticism, many of the individual nations in these regional confederations — such as Mexico in CONCACAF or Qatar in AFC — have still voiced support for Infantino. CAF (Africa) has also rejected calls to push Infantino out. Nearly a decade of greasing wheels and courting partisans is paying off in a crisis. While some prominent players, like England star Lucy Bronze , and the Fédération Internationale des Associations de Footballeurs Professionnels ( FIFPRO ), the global players’ union, have also come out against FIFA’s beleaguered president, Infantino looks to have shrugged off immediate calls to step down. Morocco is already primed to host the tournament along with Spain and Portugal, but a location for the final match has yet to be determined. What could be more fair and transparent than the FIFA president unilaterally handing it out in a back room in Rabat? FIFA has internally largely rallied around Infantino and crushed any voices willing to dissent. Chief Operating Officer Kevin Lamour, who was publicly critical of the FFE plan, was just fired without FIFA providing a cause. As long as Infantino is still running things, he’ll do it with an iron fist. Can Things Actually Get Better? All of this is disturbingly extreme, even by Infantino’s standards. Still, it also continues in the same direction he’s long been dragging global football. The 2026 World Cup’s record ticket prices, commercial-focused “hydration breaks”, and god-awful halftime show were all dire indications of financialized football on steroids. FIFA also courted shady finance partnerships, like a sponsorship from an obscure Abu Dhabi–based prediction market prior to the World Cup. Though not directly under FIFA’s purview, the football world has become awash in private equity cash as Infantino has technically been the game’s global steward. The FFE scheme was cut from the same cloth, but Infantino was finally caught out for being too greedy. Still, it’s also worth recognizing that many of the same institutions that are now raking him over the coals were happy to watch the sport get watered down to make a quick buck, without ever worrying about footballing integrity. UEFA might be marginally less likely to rip off fans than FIFA — thanks solely to large-scale fan protests — but the organization that recently reformatted the Champions League and introduced the Nations League clearly has an eye on the bottom line too. If a serious push for a breakaway tournament were actually made, led by the richest countries in the world and the global footballing elite, it would also deserve real skepticism. Similarly, merely replacing the top of a corrupt organization is also unlikely to lead to much meaningful reform. Infantino himself came in on a wind of change, promising to clean up FIFA’s act in the wake of predecessor Sep Blatter’s corruption . If a reform-minded leader like Lise Klaveness, one of the more critical voices in global football, were to wrest control from Infantino, it would be a big win. Yet it’s an open question if reforming FIFA is possible, or if it needs to simply be blown up. In either case, global football desperately needs a real democracy to bring the people’s game back into the hands of the masses. The status quo of each FIFA member nation getting one vote creates a veneer of equitable democracy. Yet this has long since devolved into FIFA figures buying off administrators of smaller nations in order to secure votes. Whatever happens, institutions like FIFPRO, which represents the men and women playing at all levels, ought to be involved, rather than just grinding players into a pulp in the name of more games and more profit. Fan-based organizations like Football Supporters Europe also ought to have their say. Any organization that sees fans as merely customers to be fleeced and not the backbone of what makes football special — and events like the World Cup remotely possible — will only continue to falter under the weight of its own greed. Drastic changes that can give fans and players an integral role in shaping football seem unlikely, but there’s never been a better opportunity. Infantino’s shaming could be a once in a lifetime chance to give football back to those who make it beautiful. Either the most oppressive and corrupt elements of these institutions will be destroyed and the game democratized, or private equity can continue its inexorable takeover of the sport the world loves.

The Road to Power: A Socialist Guide to Winning in America

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Midwestern Socialist -- Chicago DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-25T17:18:54+00:00

The following is an excerpt from the upcoming book “The American Road to Socialism: The Unfinished Struggle for Democracy” Ask an American whether workers should own the results of their labor, and you will get a yes. Ask whether the economy is rigged in favor of corporations and the wealthy, and you will get a yes – 70 percent of respondents in a national poll last year, including 58 percent of Republicans, said the system is rigged and needs to be replaced. Ask whether your boss, your landlord, and your insurance company have too much control over your life, and you will get a yes. Then say the word “socialism,” and watch the yes turn into a maybe. That gap is the central strategic problem we in the American left face. This is a challenge we socialists have faced for decades, but the political foundations are changing. Sixty-two percent of Americans under thirty now hold a favorable view of socialism. Among Democrats , favorable views have climbed from 50 percent in 2010 to 66 percent in 2025. Meanwhile Gallup found the approval rate of capitalism slipping to 54 percent, its worst showing in over a decade of polling. Roughly 15 percent of 2024 Trump voters — younger, more racially diverse, and largely disengaged from politics — told pollsters they prefer democratic socialism to capitalism. These results have been matched with equal intensity in the waves of socialist primary victories across the country this cycle. DSA endorsed roughly 150 candidates in 2026. By the end of June, thirty-five had won their primaries or gone unopposed . These wins are only the beginning of building a durable socialist movement in this country. If we are to displace the political establishment rather than merely embarrass it, we have to build a movement that crosses the partisan spectrum. Closing the gap between what Americans already believe and what they think socialism is takes three things: understanding why the gap exists, learning to speak in a way that closes it, and building an organization disciplined enough to contest the communities where the gap will be closed. Why Has America Not (Yet) Embraced Socialism? We first have to address the elephant in the room: Why over the 250 years of this country’s existence has socialism failed to take root? There are four structural obstacles in this country that have historically prevented the United States of America from embracing socialism. First, the deliberate repression of the left by capital and the state cannot be ignored when answering this question. McCarthyism, COINTELPRO, the destruction of radical unions, elite-driven cultural backlash to eras of progressive political dominance, and the outright assassination of prominent leftist leaders in this country have all been tools used by the elite to prevent the destruction of capitalism. Second is elite-driven racial divisiveness. In a multiracial society like ours, racism has been the most reliable instrument the political and economic elite have for keeping the working class divided against itself, deployed by reactionary coalitions from the founding of this country, through the end of Reconstruction, the Dixiecrats and the Southern Strategy, and present day racial divides. When workers are pitted against each other, they cannot organize alongside one other against the capitalist minority. Third, unlike almost every other industrialized democracy, the United States has for most of its history lacked a durable labor or socialist party, leaving the working class without an independent political vehicle to organize. The Socialist Party, as well as the Democratic Party at certain points in its history, offered a space for working class political organization. But this wasn’t permanent. The structural explanation for this absence lies in the American electoral system itself. Winner-take-all elections, single-member congressional districts, and the Electoral College create nearly insurmountable barriers to third parties that simply do not exist in the proportional representation systems of Europe and Latin America. The fourth obstacle is closely related to the third—the cultural identification of socialism with foreign ideologies and anti-Americanism. For a century, the right has successfully branded socialism as something alien to the American tradition: a European import at best and Soviet totalitarianism at worst. This branding has been reinforced by a corporate-controlled media apparatus that has consistently marginalized socialist voices from mainstream political discourse while amplifying the association between socialism and authoritarianism. This is the obstacle that matters most, because all others can be overcome through organizing, institution-building, and political strategy. If Americans continue to believe socialism is fundamentally un-American, no amount of doorknocking will overcome that barrier. The cultural and rhetorical reframing of socialism that we present to the masses is the foundation everything else rests on. Speaking American: How We Talk About Socialism The reason why socialism in this country has failed to take off isn’t because Americans reject its core premises, but because they reject its branding. When we describe the actual tenets of democratic socialism: workers controlling their workplaces, affordable housing, universal healthcare, strong communities, freedom from corporate domination, etc., polls consistently show majority support among Americans. What Americans reject is the word socialism and the cultural associations it carries. The task is therefore to show them that what they already want is socialism. We can achieve this goal through five cultural and rhetorical strategies. The most effective brand of political communication is narrative rather than argumentative. People are not persuaded by policy papers, ideological frameworks, or abstract appeals to justice. What persuades voters are stories that connect political ideas to lived human experiences. When a worker describes watching the company they spent twenty years building get sold out from under them so that a private equity firm could extract its assets, that story makes the case for cooperative ownership more powerfully than any theoretical argument ever could. When a single mother describes choosing between filling a prescription and paying rent, that story makes the case for universal healthcare in terms no policy brief can match. Socialists must learn to lead with stories because stories create the emotional connection that opens people to ideas they might otherwise reject on ideological grounds. This is the basis of agitation as a political strategy—the simple act of using leading questions and “whose side are you on” to bring voters to your desired outcome—which then opens voters to political organizing. The American Dream with Socialist Characteristics Socialism isn’t a rejection of the American Dream, but is instead its ultimate fulfillment. The dream as originally conceived—that through hard work, anyone can build a good life in this country—has been hollowed out by capitalism. When wages stagnate, housing is unaffordable, healthcare bankrupts families, and student debt crushes an entire generation, the promise of a better life becomes a cruel joke for the working American. Socialism restores the American Dream by giving Americans the material security to actually turn their hard work into a higher standard of living. This is how we should communicate our vision to the American public—not as a radical departure from what they’ve been promised, but as a radical fulfillment of the promise they have known their whole lives. It is through socialism that the life of a happy family, a happy home, a good job, and a Fourth of July barbecue can be realized for the working majority of this country. You Do the Work, You Own the Results Producerism is the belief that those who produce wealth should get to control wealth. Producerism should be at the rhetorical and policy center of our movement. In a country where voters pride themselves on hard work, hard work should be rewarded. Socialism accomplishes this by giving workers control over their own labor. No longer is there surplus labor value for capitalists to extract. What you create is what you control. Producerism has deep roots in the American political tradition from Jefferson, Jackson, and the Populists all the way through the labor movement, the New Deal, and the Great Society. The twenty-first century version of this argument is simple: you produce the wealth, you should own it. Corporate executives, Wall Street traders, and trust fund inheritors produce nothing. Instead, they extract wealth from Americans who work for a living. Socialism—which is fundamentally the belief that workers should control the wealth they create—isn’t a foreign import. Cooperative ownership is the logical conclusion of the producerist principle that Americans have believed in for two centuries. When we talk to voters, we should frame our project as “we believe the people who do the work should own the results of their work.” That is a sentence that virtually every American will agree with, and it is the most basic definition of socialism. Your Neighbor is Not Your Enemy Americans have a deep attachment to community, whether religious, familial, neighborhood, or civic. Barn raisings, mutual aid societies, the labor union, and the tradition of neighbors helping neighbors were all core institutions of American culture hollowed out by capitalism. Capitalism has left the American worker small, isolated, and unable to defend themselves against either an abusive employer or an indifferent state. Cooperatives, tenant unions, and popular assemblies are the tools ordinary people use to rebuild that. Socialism is what happens when neighbors decide to look out for each other instead of leaving everyone to fend for themselves. Us Against the Oligarchs The defining feature of American politics right now is that voters across the spectrum are furious at elites. The right channels that fury at cultural figures, immigrants, and minorities. Our job is to channel it to the economic elites who actually control people’s lives. Democratic socialism is not “big government.” It is the opposite of oligarchy: a system where ordinary people hold power instead of billionaires. Every proposal we make should be framed as transferring power from elites to working people, not from the private sector to the state. Americans distrust both big business and big government, and our answer is therefore neither. Socialism is the democratic self-governance of working people, which is what the natural conclusion of the values most Americans ascribe to the founding of this country. What Freedom Really Means This is the framing we underuse most. The right insists socialism means the government running your life. In reality it is the capitalist class running it. Your boss controls your time, your wages, your conditions. Your landlord controls your housing. Your insurance company controls whether you get treatment. There is no meaningful freedom when every condition of your survival depends on the decisions of unaccountable private actors. Democratic socialism offers two freedoms at once: the freedom that comes from a baseline of material security, and the freedom that comes from actual control over your workplace, your building, and your neighborhood. What could be more American than that? “The Party Within the Party” That brings us to the question every American socialist has to answer: what do we do with the Democratic Party? Make a clean break and start a third party, or organize inside? The answer is both: the “party within the party.” This strategy is not new or revolutionary for the DSA, but it could use some sharpening and clarity. Building a third party under current conditions is not possible given the design of our electoral system and the entrenchment of the duopoly. When those conditions change, likely through our own efforts, so should our approach. But working within the Democratic Party carries its own risks. Without an independent organizational base we will be absorbed and neutralized, which is exactly what happened to progressive movements throughout the twentieth century. I can’t say when the DSA will reach a state where it can truly break from the Democrats and its bourgeois politics, but our goal is not to become Democrats. Instead, we must use Democrats’ existing infrastructure to build our own power and base of support. What we therefore need is an independent socialist organization with its own membership, funding, discipline, and platform. We can and should use the Democratic ballot line as a vehicle to win elections, gain national publicity, and push for reforms to the political system. As we do so, we will win over the sympathy of left-leaning voters while retaining the independence to hold our own officials accountable, mobilize outside election cycles, and build working-class institutions that outlast any party. In order to accomplish this, we must do three things: streamline our endorsement process, run for and win smaller local and state offices, and expand our fight to contest elections in more rural and conservative parts of the country. The Endorsement Process Ours should be an endorsement that candidates actively seek out, not one selectively and arbitrarily given. Right now the process is often slow, contentious, and dominated by factional dynamics that prioritize ideological alignment over strategic opportunity. Candidates substantively aligned with democratic socialist principles get denied because they fail to check a few boxes in our process, and races go uncontested by socialists because nobody clears the purity threshold. A strategically oriented process would test candidates against a short list of non-negotiables — economic democracy, racial justice, anti-militarism, labor rights — allow flexibility on secondary questions, and prioritize races we can actually win. The Local Governance Gap There are thousands of offices in this country decided by a few hundred or even a few dozen votes: city councils, county boards, township trustees, school boards, water reclamation districts, planning commissions. They are not glamorous. They generate no national press. They are also where the governance that shapes working people’s daily lives actually happens. A single organized chapter can win them with minimal resources. Winning them turns municipal and state governments into laboratories of socialist policy — and, just as importantly, makes voters comfortable with the idea of socialists governing, because they can see it working down the street. It is also how we build a bench. You do not produce candidates capable of competing at the highest level without officeholders who have already governed something. DSA’s habit of pouring electoral energy into high-profile congressional races while neglecting the offices where socialism can be practiced most tangibly is a strategic failure that caps our growth. Purple & Red Territory Fifty-eight percent of Republicans think the economy is rigged for the rich. Of the respondents who had never heard of DSA, 43 percent were more amenable to democratic socialism against 38 percent for capitalism — meaning our brand problem is, in a real sense, an introduction problem. Bernie Sanders and AOC draw enormous crowds in places where we currently have limited resources and support. These are communities where a candidate running the producerist message alongside concrete local issues — housing costs, healthcare access, job quality — can win, particularly in local and state races where national partisan dynamics matter least. The proof of concept of the socialist electoral model is no longer hypothetical, and it is no longer only applicable in progressive areas like New York. Zohran Mamdani started at 1 percent in the polls and beat the entire New York establishment twice, winning 30 percent of the districts Trump carried in 2024 — not because those voters had a conversion experience, but because Mamdani was the only candidate talking about their rent. Donavan McKinney just won a congressional primary in Detroit with a fraction of his opponent’s money, running on working-class representation in government, taxing the wealthy, and getting corporate cash out of politics. Neither man asked anyone to become a socialist. Our candidates have won by speaking to material conditions and letting the label take care of itself. They are using their success in office to bring people into socialism by example. That is a replicable model, and we have been rationing it. Imagine every chapter with a few hundred members identifying one winnable local race, recruiting a candidate committed to the platform, and running a real field operation. Expanding the Project But how do we accomplish all of these goals? There are several ways in which we can go about expanding the reach of the DSA organization which mostly entail building upon already existing programs. The Socialists in Office program (SIO) is a DSA-sponsored program that coordinates with and supports DSA officials in public office through a co-governance model. The organization works with SIOs on building coalitions with key communities, teaching our politicians the ropes of local governance, and on drafting and passing legislation. The SIO program has been incredibly effective at building a base of socialist politicians with strong constituent services and strong legislative skills, but the program could be significantly expanded. Imagine if the SIO program was involved in the electoral process before candidates won their races? The program should be the primary vehicle by which we elect socialists across the country. It should work with candidates during their campaigns, providing media training, fundraising support, and organizing training. It should work to build coalitions between DSA candidates and key groups like labor, students’ groups, and tenants’ organizations. And it should be involved in identifying candidates across the country to run for office under the DSA banner. But engaging in electoralism alone will not be enough to move the masses towards socialism. In essence, the DSA must break from the two-party tradition of only showing up in communities when it comes time for Americans to vote. DSA cadres must be continually present in communities, doing the work of building strong relations with local residents. As we expand our organizing capacity, we should engage in mutual-aid work, providing communities with key services that corporations and governments refuse to offer. From the public housing complex to the barn-house, we must be in the communities that will decide the future of this country, becoming a key resource for local residents without ever asking for a vote or donation in return. This is how we, and by extension socialism, can become the most trusted political project in this country. The road to socialism here will not be paved by one election, one candidate, or one project. It will be built by the sustained, unglamorous work of people who refuse to accept that the richest country in human history cannot provide a dignified life for everyone in it. The framings and arguments above are a toolkit to be adapted to the conditions of communities across the country. The question is no longer whether socialism can win in America. by Alex K. Many of the arguments I have heard against the Participatory Democracy proposal boil down to one simple phrase: “What if I can’t participate?”. Often a theoretical member who works the night shift, or is taking care of family members, or cannot attend these meetings for a myriad of reasons is given as to why Participatory Democracy is not only unfair, but akin to a poll tax or a literacy test. I would ask why the Cleveland DSA is required to replicate Liberal voting structures in an organization that looks to create a system outside of the current oligarchy built upon centuries of white supremacy, misogyny, and outdated Enlightenment ideals. Voting should be the end process of a long period of conversation, deliberation, and, most importantly, organizing. I dare those reading this to try and think outside of the individualist society that so many of us have grown up in to think of voting not as a question of “What happens if I cannot vote?”, but as a question of “Am I organizing effectively enough to trust my comrades who can?”. While the title of this article, “You Aren’t Special – And Neither Am I” is meant to be inflammatory, I challenge the reader to think of Cleveland DSA not as an organization made up of individuals, but as an organized mass that is anchored in the idea that the collective is always stronger than the sum of its parts–of which voting is only one minor part. As a Marxist, and a part of the Marxist Unity Group (MUG), I am interested in theories of Democracy and the ways that we can make our structures more Democratic, and I reject the idea that the ultimate form of Democracy is casting a ballot. Patricia Springborg notes that Marx sees the “positive case for democracy as bearing a peculiar and intimate relation to politics and its emergence as a historically specific cultural complex–a uniquely intimate relationship that is not shared by other regimes such as monarchies and aristocracies.”(1) Though this may seem complex at first, the crux of Marx’s argument is simple: Democracy is more than existing under a political system, it is about being able to have a true, meaningful relationship to the way that a person interacts with and shapes their own political system. While this point may seem to express, at the very least, sympathy towards One Member One Vote, Springborg expands on this basis to show that there is more to Democracy than the simple act of voting. There are two additional points that Springborg makes about Marx’s views on democracy: one is that “democracy does not require the participation of all members of society as individuals in the decision-making process and that, therefore, the debate over the relative merits of direct or representative democracy is misdirected.”(2) This goes hand-in-hand with the final argument Springborg describes–“political participation turns not on the function of deputies or representatives but rather on political suffrage,” and in quoting Marx, reiterates that “Voting, ‘considered philosophically … is the immediate, the direct, the existing and not simply imagined relation of civil society to the political state.’”(3) Together, these arguments paint a picture of Democracy based on a foundation of universalism, rather than individualism. Democracy does not come about through giving everyone a day off to cast a ballot. Rather, it is essential that everyone can cast a ballot, but that casting a ballot comes about through people having a deep connection to what they are voting on, understanding that part of democracy is that not everyone who is able to participate does participate, and that voting is a political act–not the political act. Marx clearly states that if members of the state–or perhaps Cleveland DSA if you are so inclined–“are an integral part of the state, then it is obvious that their social existence is already their actual participation in it. They are not only integral parts of the state, but the state is their integral part.”(4) Those who choose to be in Cleveland DSA are an integral part of it, and it is notable that being part of Cleveland DSA is a unique choice that exists outside of, though not independent from, the larger American political sphere. Springborg expands on Marx’s rejection of the separation between the self and the state in how focusing on individual political actions as “all” or “not all” “falsely presumes the political to be constituted by single political acts performed by individuals, focusing exclusively on the legislature as the locus of popular participation.”(5) Voting is the ultimate realization of a single political act performed by an individual. I challenge all persons in Cleveland DSA to think beyond themselves to a politic based on all of us, together, regardless of a single person’s circumstances. In writing this, I look to challenge ideas of what it means to vote as a member of Cleveland DSA, outside of the historical understanding of what Democracy can, and should, look like. What does it mean to be Democratic as the systems made to suppress and silence political engagement are potentially replicated in Cleveland DSA? In an organization whose program clearly states that “[The Democratic Socialists of America] are workers fighting to transform society: to win the battle for democracy, draft a new constitution, and create a democratic socialist republic,”(6) it is essential to look beyond American individualism and the idea that the ultimate form of Democracy is exemplified through a ballot. I do not believe that replicating the same systems that have not only failed us, but were designed to oppress and suppress, will create the future we all look towards. It is only through meaningful, powerful organizing that the systems we look to transform can truly change: not through the vote of a single individual. A lively debate has been taking place in Jacobin over what to do about artificial intelligence. AI is clearly a technology with potential for enormous benefit and harm — and perhaps both at the same time. Financially, it could put even more income in the hands of billionaires, or it could produce a bubble the implosion of which pushes the net worth of investors, innocent and guilty alike, off a cliff. Either way, it needs to be redirected away from narrow financial imperatives and toward social ends, and this requires a change in ownership. But what change, and how? The obvious go-to for socialists is to give government a controlling interest, and that’s what Bernie Sanders has proposed : use a onetime tax to transfer half the equity in US-based AI companies to a new sovereign wealth fund, which would then have veto power over future decisions affecting the technology while sharing in the profits it generates. But there is also unease with the idea, not least because Donald Trump has also floated federal ownership stakes — and even a remote similarity is grounds for suspicion. More precisely, given the state of American democracy and the grotesque imbalance of political power favoring the ultrarich, it is reasonable to doubt that a large public stake would yield the outcomes we want to see from it. To design social ownership so it truly reflects social purposes in a globalizing world, we need to rethink the issue from the ground up. Just why do we need to remove ownership and control from the hands of wealthy investors? Exactly what changes are we trying to bring about? The Why and How of Public Ownership The first question has a three-part answer. First, we are suffering from the overbearing power of an oligarchy that transforms vastly excessive wealth into suffocating political and social influence. If AI generates the economic benefits advertised for it, even greater riches will follow. AI companies convert vast stores of collectively produced knowledge and culture, along with publicly supported research, into private wealth. If AI does produce the economic benefits its exponents promise, those gains should be broadly shared rather than accrue to a small group of plutocrats. Second, the breakneck pursuit of market domination is generating immense costs while failing to deliver benefits that can’t be readily marketized. It is undertaken either nakedly for enrichment or in the name of a perverse, zero-sum notion of patriotism (any loss of market share to Chinese companies, some say, imperils America’s “national security”). Finally, AI is a complex, rapidly evolving technology, and, as such, it is almost impossible to regulate. Yes, broad regulations on the use of AI and the resources it vacuums up are feasible and should be enacted as soon as possible. Personally, I’d begin with a prohibition on any chatbot or similar program using the first person in its messaging: “I” is a lie. But regulation of how models are trained, how they respond to prompts, and how they may soon be integrated with reasoning engines — these matters are almost impossible to regulate from the outside. It’s a game of never-ending catch-up that regulators, even if aggressive and well-motived, are unlikely to win. So, to sum up the why , we need to share the wealth, dial back the profit motive, and incorporate public regulation within the enterprises developing and applying AI. Which brings us to the crucial problem of how . Public ownership would deal with the first problem, and no doubt that’s a major reason why Sanders and others want to take this step. It might also help with the third, since, if nothing else, the government as equity investor could demand that all information relevant to regulation be made public in real time. Those are both significant advantages that should not be disparaged. But public ownership also poses problems of its own. One, as a previous Jacobin writer correctly emphasized , is that government control and democratic control are typically quite far apart. Even in the best of circumstances, people get to vote only periodically for political representatives. By the time decision-making snakes its way down to lower levels in the bureaucracy the democratic mandate is weak. And today those circumstances are light years from democratic perfection. Public Pluralities Yet the problem with public ownership is even more fundamental. To see it, we need to think concretely about what it is we want AI to do — and not do — and ask how ownership models alone can resolve potential conflicts. Here are several issue areas that occur to me: Privacy and personal freedom . AI is being deployed to create systems of private and public surveillance without equal in human history. This ranges from surveillance pricing, where what we pay is a function of what an AI agent determines we are willing to pay, to state profiling of our political values for purposes of social control. A frightening prospect is the use of AI to target and annihilate places and people beyond human oversight, as exemplified by Israel’s Lavender program. Management and employment . Much of the putative economic value of AI stems from its claimed ability, now or soon, to assume direct management of businesses, government agencies, and other organizations, with the wholesale dismissal of the human beings who now do this work. That can lead to frustratingly unresponsive or unhelpful services — an aspect of Cory Doctorow’s enshittification — and of course it raises the specter of mass unemployment. Yet replacing drudgery with automation can also be an enormous social boon; just think of the millions of secretaries whose typing, filing, and other repetitive clerical chores were taken over by word processing apps. (I was in their ranks once.) Key choices about how organizations should be run, and who will be needed to run them, are at stake. Environment and resources . Unlike earlier iterations of digital technology, AI makes massive, almost mind-boggling demands on our water resources, energy systems, and rare minerals. This is due to its brute-force training methods, and it might be mitigated by more specialized training combined with greater use of logic. In any case, there are important issues to resolve about how to trade off computing capacity and resource use, along with whose resources should be tapped and how they should be compensated for them. Applications across society . As much as attention has focused on the issues above, this may be just as important in the long run. Already educators are struggling with the slash-and-burn effects of AI on classrooms. The impacts on writing, music, and visual culture could be enormously positive, negative, or (most likely) both. AI shows great promise in research, but a lot will depend on whether it augments our understanding of the fields to which we apply it, or simply pulls results out of a black box. This would matter even if agents were perfectly reliable, which they’re not. Who can we trust to design these applications in a way that expands our potential as learners and creators? There are important conclusions to draw from even this cursory survey. One is that many kinds of choices must be made. Some are distributive — who gains, who loses — but others involve fundamental values. Another is that, given the many dimensions of AI’s future development, it’s impossible to specify a single public interest that all of us would agree to sign onto. It’s difficult to overstate how important it is to understand this. The public whose interests should guide us is the actually existing one, with all its diversity of attitudes and priorities — not an imaginary public in which everyone thinks just the way you or I do. Even if everyone in the United States adopted progressive values, choices would still have to be made among competing priorities. The issue is not data centers everywhere versus nowhere, or AI-generated documents versus only human-written ones, or any other dichotomous choice. It is about priorities, emphases, adjustments, and reimagined designs. The current choices are mercenary and terrible, but there is no simple alternative upon which everyone will agree. Purposeful Ownership So we need not only democracy in the AI world but also true pluralism: different implementations existing side by side from which we can choose. This is also necessary for standard economic reasons, since we won’t know in advance what approaches to the development of AI will turn out to be best, either in technical or social terms. Again, pluralism. Fortunately, we already have the beginnings of the alternative we need: ownership by purpose trusts. Up to now, this has progressed one retirement at a time, as public-minded owners such as Patagonia’s Yvon Chouinard, who chose to place control of their companies in the hands of trustees legally bound to protect particular purposes. In the case of Patagonia, this was an environmental commitment . Under the stronger version of the model proposed here, profit would become a means, not an end. The trust deed would stipulate that earnings, after necessary reinvestments, be devoted to advancing the firm’s overriding purposes rather than distributed to private investors. If you scan a directory of such firms, you’ll find a variety of mandatory purposes, ranging from worker benefit to community development to enhancing local food systems, with many designating more than one of them. Which is how it should be: we are a diverse society, and our purposes are many and do not always lead to the same choices. However, to adapt this model to AI we need to make two very large changes. First, the public, acting through its elected representatives, would have to initiate the conversion of existing firms to purpose trusts. This would require a process for recruiting trustees and, above all, financing the purchase of firms’ equity. Some form of tax on profits would obviously be essential. Second, the purposes would need to be chosen not by enlightened, aging entrepreneurs but by society itself. Because multiple trusts could coexist, they could pursue a range of purposes that would not always agree with one another. Some roughly democratic method would be needed to ensure that the allocation of funding among the trusts corresponds to the proportion of the community that supports respective purposes. A side note: Anthropic is not itself a trust, but its governance structure includes a long-term benefit trust . It differs from the mode described here in several crucial ways: investors maintain their claims on profits; Anthropic’s mandate was focused on the long-term risks posed by AI, leaving most business questions unaddressed. It is relatively easy for equity owners to dissolve the trust whenever they wish. It is no secret that Anthropic is preparing for an initial public offering. The Purpose-Trust Advantage Another big advantage of the purpose-trust approach over public ownership is that it is far better suited to handle the pushes and pulls of the global economy. It can partner with foreign firms if it chooses. It can pursue international strategies consistent with its purposes separate from the passing predilections of politicians — a huge consideration since China is also a key player. And it can use these relationships to promote people-serving purposes beyond our borders, which can add a potentially progressive twist to globalization. This flexibility follows from the model: once capitalized, trust-owned firms are operationally autonomous and governed by their legally binding purposes. This last point is key. Consider a trust-owned AI firm whose legally binding objective is to help users be more creative and productive. This would obligate the firm to develop tools that enhance user’s capacities and to prohibit the development of applications that are routinizing or disempowering. But such a broad purpose could only be realized through sustained interaction with users. Some of this would take the form of standard market feedback, as when a gardener buys an AI helper that makes gardening more worthwhile, or someone learning a new language purchases an AI teaching tool. Our hypothetical trust-owned business would still have to earn enough revenue to cover its costs, just like everyone else. Now imagine that its tools were intended for use by people at work, making it possible for unions and professional or advocacy groups to have a voice in their design and deployment. Organizations of all sorts could partner with our AI developer, since trust ownership is a form of social ownership that preserves the firm’s independence. One trust-owned firm could also collaborate with another when their missions overlap, or if they operate in related parts of the economy — for example, an AI developer joining forces with a pharmaceutical company, with both owned by purpose trusts. And, as mentioned above, national borders do not set boundaries on these kinds of partnerships unless specific laws are passed to regulate them. Conversely, two trust-owned firms with opposed missions might compete if they’re in the same market — not to amass the greatest possible profit but to advance their respective missions. This would be social ownership without the myth of a homogenous, same-thinking society. Of course, unlike public ownership, social ownership via purpose trusts does not come ready-made. We have limited experience to draw on, and important questions remain unanswered. There will surely be a learning curve. But the need for a vibrant, diverse, and purpose-driven alternative economy is more urgent than ever, and the way to figure it out is to start creating it.

Fidel Castro centenary: the last Homeric hero

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Dayan Jayatilleka ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-26T22:18:44+00:00

The month he turned 50, my father, Mervyn de Silva, and my mother, Lakshmi, were in Havana watching Fidel Castro take over the chairmanship of the Non-Aligned Movement at its 6th Summit. After my father died, I discovered in his briefcase the embossed invitation on stiff parchment. He had treasured it for two decades. I still do. As I look to turn 70 this December, almost to the day that Fidel Castro, Che Guevara, Raul Castro and others landed on the shores of Cuba in the leaky yacht Granma in 1956, I am struck by the good fortune of having lived consciously in a period of History studded with giants: Mao Zedong, Zhou Enlai, Ho Chi Minh, General Vo Nguyen Giap, Josip Broz Tito, Fidel Castro, Che Guevara, Amilcar Cabral, Samora Machel, Agostinho Neto, Ayatollah Khomeini, and Nelson Mandela. From the Global South and East, these were great, heroic leaders of the Age of Liberation and Revolution. Fidel lived longer than the others and impacted us more because he spoke and wrote – articulated his views, addressing his people and the world – much more than did the others. Though Ronald Reagan was called “The Great Communicator,” Fidel Castro was the far greater global communicator. Humanity’s tribune Through speeches and interviews, international conferences and volunteer health workers, military training and action, Fidel also took more initiatives in support of causes and peoples all over the planet than any other world leader of our time. While patriotic, he was also the most internationalist. From a country with a smaller population than Sri Lanka, he gave voice to the peoples everywhere, and helped shape the world we lived in. He was a ceaseless “Tribune” of the people against injustice, and this eventually made him the most prophetic global statesman. At the 70th anniversary celebration of the October Revolution in Moscow in 1987, Fidel warned that “we may wake up one day and find that the Soviet Union has disappeared.” In the aftermath of 9/11, he condemned terrorism, expressed solidarity with New York (a city he loved), offered the victims medical help in Cuba, and signaled a willingness to cooperate against Al Qaeda, mainly in the field of intelligence But, as a former guerrilla leader, he attached a warning: given the terrain, failure would confront the US if it launched its planned all-out war, including ground troops, against Afghanistan. He proved prophetic when the US withdrew in failure two decades later. Israel’s Netanyahu worked incessantly to entangle successive US presidents in a war on Iran. In 2010, Fidel invited Jeff Goldberg of The Atlantic to Havana – because Goldberg had written on Israel and Iran, and Obama read him closely. In the interview Fidel argued compellingly that a US-Israeli war against Iran would be disastrous regionally, globally, and for US interests. Netanyahu was unable to convince Obama, and the US shifted seriously to the negotiation track that culminated in the JCPOA. Caught in a debt trap as Sri Lanka is, we should salute Fidel Castro as the world leader who campaigned earliest against foreign debt – even though his own country was not indebted. From the mid-1980s onwards he spoke exhaustively about the debt burden, its roots and consequences, hosting international conferences at which Professor Joseph Stiglitz was honored guest and prominent speaker. During the worst of the Vietnam War, Fidel took the enormous personal risk of visiting a liberated zone in Quang Tri in September 1973, and raising the Viet Cong banner. Having liberated his own country, he went on to help many others directly and personally, from Latin America through Africa to Asia. Years after his death a contingent of Cuban doctors flew to Covid-ravaged Italy to combat the disease at its frontlines. Fidel and Che The most luminous and universally embraced iconic hero of our time was and remains Che Guevara. Young Dr. Ernesto Guevara was an exceptionally brilliant, daringly adventurous, well-read Marxist, almost saintly in his work among the lepers. But Ernesto Guevara of The Motorcycle Diaries would never have become the immortal Che Guevara except for an encounter and conversation one night around a dining table in Mexico with one man who was out of jail after leading the attack on the Moncada barracks in Cuba in 1953, and planning to return by boat to begin a revolutionary war of liberation against the dictator Batista. That man was Fidel Castro. No Fidel, no Che. When Che Guevara was captured and executed in Bolivia, it could have been seen as a defeat, a failure and an end. But Fidel was present to dialectically invert such an interpretation, just as in “History Will Absolve Me” at his trial after Moncada he had transmuted a military defeat into a moral victory. In a long oration late at night in Havana to a gigantic crowd of sorrowing Cubans, he transformed Che into a towering saint and martyr of revolution; the archetypal hero of our time, projected in the consciousness of generations from then to now and tomorrow. That night, Fidel Castro resurrected Che and immortalized him in History. Soft power, hard power On Fidel’s watch, his country was so prestigious and influential in the world arena that it was nicknamed “the pocket superpower.” He demonstrated how a country of the Global South with a small population, located in close proximity to a hostile giant – the sole superpower – and with a military encampment of that superpower on its very territory (Guantanamo), could still maintain its dignity and earn respect and honor in the world without bartering its sovereignty. Fidel’s politics had at its heart the duality that Machiavelli captured in the image of the Centaur to which Antonio Gramsci returned centuries later, denoting the combination of coercion and consent. In contemporary terms this is the combination of hard power and soft power. The image of Fidel in uniform leaping off a battle tank, having rushed to the Bay of Pigs where he inflicted a defeat of the US and Cuban counterrevolutionaries in 1961, epitomizes his mastery of “hard power.” So does the historic battle of Cuito Cuanavale in 1987-1988 in Angola, the largest military battle in Africa since the end of WWII, where Fidel commanded from Havana the Cuban forces who defeated the invading military of industrial, NATO-equipped, nuclear-armed apartheid South Africa. His friendship with Ernest Hemingway; his interviews to Playboy (1967, 1985); his long interviews with famous television journalist Barbara Walters (1977, 2002), and his four-hour speech to the congregation at the Riverside Church in Harlem, New York (2000) are evidence of his mastery of soft power. Patriot and Internationalist Fidel’s politics made a fundamental contribution to the field through the unity of patriotism with internationalism. He explicitly said he was not a “nationalist” but a patriot, while insisting that internationalism was “an imperative.” Fidel’s patriotism encompassed his country and all the people of all races within it. A factor that motivated his decision to send troops to Angola to resist apartheid South Africa’s invasion was that Cuba’s sugar industry had thrived on the backs of slave labor from Africa. In the struggle for Cuba’s independence from Spain, a powerful revolt had been led by a Black woman, a slave. Angola, explained Fidel, was Cuba’s chance to repay the people from and of Africa. The Cuban internationalist military expedition was named after the Black slave woman leader of that anti-colonial rebellion: Operation Carlota. His patriotism was not a matter of crassly pragmatic “national interest.” There was a thaw in Cuba’s relations with the US under President Gerald Ford, and had Fidel believed in a narrow pragmatism, Cuba may have materially benefited. However, at that very moment the troops of apartheid South Africa had penetrated Angola (at Dr. Kissinger’s suggestion) and were within sight of its capital Luanda. Fidel decided that the principle of internationalism was more crucial than immediate material gain, and therefore answered the call by Agostinho Neto leader of the MPLA, the primary Angolan liberation movement legitimately wielding power in Luanda, for urgent support and assistance. Fidel’s was an existential choice, sourced in a higher way of being in the world. It swiftly changed history. Upon release from jail after 27 years, Nelson Mandela’s first visit outside Africa was to Cuba, to thank Fidel for defeating the apartheid South African military, humbling the apartheid state and system, and opening the road that led to his freedom. South Africa’s President de Klerk met Mandela the year after Cuito Cuanavale, in 1989, and his release (1990) came a mere two years after Cuba’s victory there. Castro and Christianity Fidel Castro was a contributor to a confluence in the history of ideas which will outlast him by centuries. He designated it the “most explosive” fusion—Christianity and Marxism. Liberation Theology within Catholicism in particular, and Christianity in general, was the crystallisation in Latin America of the consequences of the Second Vatican Council initiated by Pope John XXIII, intersecting with the impact of the Cuban Revolution led by Fidel Castro. Baptised and raised a Catholic, a stellar product of elite Jesuit private schools, Fidel wore around his neck the image of Our Lady of Charity of El Cobre during his years as guerrilla leader in the Sierra Maestra. The later Fidel was secular, not a believer in the divine, but he remained a follower of the teachings of Jesus Christ. In “Fidel and Religion” (1985) and “My Life” (2006/7), he revealed himself shaped by his Spanish Jesuit teachers and reading the Bible, primarily the Gospels. In his massive “spoken autobiography” My Life (2007) comprising long conversations with Ignacio Ramonet, he put it plainly: “With the teachings of Christ, you end up with a radical socialist program, whether you are a believer or not. ” (My Life, 2007, p156). Most striking is his revelatory self-definition at age 80: “Not long ago I was telling Chavez, the president of Venezuela – because Hugo Chavez is a Christian and talks about it a lot – ‘if people call me Christian, not from the standpoint of religion but from the standpoint of social vision, I declare that I am a Christian.’ On the basis of my convictions and the objectives I pursue. ” (My Life, 2007, p156). In the last decades of his life, Fidel met three Popes: John Paul II, Benedict XVI and Francis. Pope John Paul II made a historic visit to Cuba in 1998. Fidel expressed warm admiration for him, despite the obvious paradox of the latter’s role in unravelling socialism in Poland – not least because the Pope I was among the earliest and foremost critics of neoliberal globalization. Ethics and the hero My own book “Fidel’s Ethics of Violence: The Moral Dimension of the Political Thought of Fidel Castro” (2007) focused on Fidel’s contribution to the political, strategic and philosophical problem of the ethics of violence. His was not the Gandhian renunciation of violence, nor the purely tactical use of violence such as that of Mandela and the ANC. Yet he rejected the practice of terrorism and the doctrine that any kind of violence, including the targeting of unarmed civilians, was legitimate in the course of a struggle for a cause deemed just. Though paralleling and perhaps derivative of the Christian doctrine of Just War, Fidel’s ideas, strategy and practice differed from it in a major respect. Christian Just War theory was for states and Christian princes, mainly fighting each other – Just War “from above,” in which rulership was understood as ‘legitimate authority’. Fidel’s Just War theory pertained to struggles from below: wars for liberation and emancipation by the peoples against unjust, illegitimate authority. He insisted on and consistently practised the avoidance of violence against non-combatants and disarmed enemies. This extended from his own guerrilla war, through the counter-guerrilla war against CIA-backed Cuban émigrés, to large-scale war across the oceans – 12 years in Angola, fighting the armed forces of apartheid South Africa. In his practice of what Marx called “the critique by weapon,” Fidel bridged the gulf between the archetypes of “The Rebel” and “The Revolutionary” posited by Albert Camus, which fed into his debate with Jean-Paul Sartre. Camus commended the Rebel with his or her limited aims and limited violence, as distinct from the Revolutionary whose drive for wider transformation inevitably led to untrammeled violence. Fidel transcended the antinomy by being the exception . He was Rebel and Revolutionary both, maintaining scrupulous selectivity in targeting while waging and supporting wars for deep-going revolutionary objectives; practising an ethic of violence which enabled him at all times to occupy the moral high ground by universalist-humanist standards – not self-proclaimed, or nationally and culturally self-referential, norms. Television news is a constant if inadvertent reminder of what the world was when Fidel was alive and what it has become without him. Without him to play that planetary role of spokesperson, prophet and champion, humanity is lonelier and poorer. Mine disaster in Central African Republic sparks debate on how best to regulate the sector

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Zoe. ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-26T21:17:17+00:00

More than 100 artisanal miners have died following the collapse of a gold mine in Zamboye, Central African Republic (CAR), near the border with Cameroon. Several others were injured, while some remained missing as rescue and recovery operations continued. The disaster on August 18 has once again brought attention to the dangerous conditions under which thousands of artisanal miners across Africa work. The collapse occurred at a sprawling, low-tech artisanal gold-mining site where miners worked in and around underground tunnels and open pits. Authorities subsequently closed the Zamboye site, citing non-compliance with mining standards and administrative directives, while a judicial investigation was opened to establish the circumstances of the collapse and possible responsibility. The tragedy is just one of many, indicating the persistent safety risks in the country’s largely informal small-scale mining sector. CAR experienced several deadly mining accidents this year, including collapses in February, March, and June. Risks of artisanal mining Across the African continent, artisanal and small-scale mining provides an important source of income for communities where formal employment opportunities are limited. But miners frequently work with inadequate protective equipment, weak shaft-support systems, limited technical assistance and little access to emergency rescue services in case of a disaster. Benedict Aminer, a Kenyan researcher who has studied artisanal gold mining communities, told BreakThrough News the problem needs to be understood beyond the immediate causes of individual accidents. “Artisanal mining sites become vulnerable to deadly accidents when miners are working with limited technical knowledge, rudimentary equipment and inadequate safety systems,” Aminer said. He identified poorly supported shafts, flooding, inadequate ventilation, unsafe blasting, insufficient protective equipment, and limited emergency preparedness among the principal risks confronting artisanal miners. Research undertaken in Kenyan mining communities, he said, has encountered many of the same problems seen in CAR. “During research in Ramula, Siaya County, we encountered miners transporting the body of a colleague who had died following a pit collapse,” he said. “Later, five miners died following the collapse of a shaft at Kilima Pesa in Lolgorian.” Artisanal mining and the question of livelihood The Central African Republic disaster also points to a difficult question surrounding artisanal mining, how to protect miners without destroying the livelihoods on which communities depend. Artisanal mining is often described by authorities as illegal or informal, but for many workers it represents one of the few available sources of income. In areas facing high levels of unemployment and limited economic opportunities, people may enter mining despite the dangers because they have few alternatives, some corporates also exploit this for low wages. “The tragedy in the Central African Republic therefore needs to be looked at beyond the immediate question of the mine collapse,” Aminer said. “It raises a much bigger question about the conditions under which artisanal miners are forced to work.” This, he argued, should not be interpreted as an acceptance of unsafe mining practices. “Miners have a right to safe working conditions, and governments have a responsibility to protect life,” he said. The problem is that individual miners generally lack the resources required to undertake geological assessments, install sophisticated shaft-support systems, acquire emergency rescue equipment or obtain professional engineering advice. The result can be a dangerous cycle in which poverty, informality and inadequate technical capacity reinforce one another. Kenya as a case study Kenya has developed a legal and institutional framework governing artisanal and small-scale mining, including provisions dealing with licensing, environmental management and mine safety. But the experience of miners on the ground differs considerably from what the regulatory framework provides on paper. Aminer said research in Kenyan mining communities found that many miners and cooperatives were willing to formalize their operations but faced difficulties with licensing procedures, compliance costs, access to information, and technical requirements. Some miners have organized themselves into cooperatives in the hope that collective organization would make formalization easier. But obtaining a license does not necessarily resolve the practical challenges of mining safely. “Formalization can still remain difficult,” he said. The result, according to Aminer, is a cycle in which informality restricts miners’ access to government support, while the costs and complexity associated with formalization make it difficult for them to enter the formal system. From criminalization to support The Central African Republic disaster then raises questions about how African governments should approach artisanal mining. For Aminer, the starting point should be support-based formalization rather than criminalization. Formalization, he said, should provide miners with tangible benefits, including access to designated mining areas, technical assistance, safer equipment, appropriate financing, geological information, training, transparent markets and government extension services. “Miners generally want safer and more organized mining, but they need support to achieve it,” he said. Responsible mechanization should also form part of that approach. Mechanization does not necessarily have to displace artisanal miners, particularly where appropriate technologies can improve safety and productivity while remaining accessible to small-scale operators. Preventing disasters before they happen Mine inspection is another critical area. Rather than responding only after fatalities occur, governments need to develop preventive systems involving regular inspections, geological and geotechnical assessments. The CAR disaster demonstrated the consequences of weak emergency capacity. Rescue efforts were reportedly hampered by limited equipment and expertise, making it difficult for workers to reach people trapped beneath the rubble. Better access to finance and mineral markets is also a key issue. Many artisanal gold miners depend on brokers and have limited bargaining power over the price of their production. Transparent mineral-buying systems, accurate weighing and testing, collective marketing and stronger cooperatives could improve miners’ incomes while reducing their dependence on intermediaries to corporates. A continental challenge Simply declaring mines illegal does not eliminate the economic conditions that drive people into them. Nor does closing an unsafe site necessarily make mining communities safer if miners subsequently move to another unregulated operation. “Closing an unsafe mine may sometimes be necessary to protect lives,” Aminer said, “but closing it without providing safer alternatives or addressing the livelihood needs of the community can simply push miners into another informal and potentially more dangerous operation.” The challenge, therefore, is to move beyond a regulatory approach centered primarily on controlling miners. The objective should be to support miners to work safely, responsibly and legally.

How to Use DeepSeek Harness

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: [:en]THE TRICONTINENTAL[:es]TRICONTINENTAL[:] ／ 著者: 不明 ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-26T06:08:51+00:00

On 13 August 2026, DeepSeek released DeepSeek Harness , an open framework for building and customising AI agents. Its philosophy can be summed up in two words: freedom and openness . Instead of giving users a finished agent and asking them to work within its limits, Harness opens up the system itself, allowing users to inspect, modify, and extend it. What Makes DeepSeek Harness Different Customisable It is not a finished product, but a box of building blocks. Tools such as Claude Code and Codex are largely finished products: you can change their settings and add instructions, but the underlying system, including how the agent works with the model, uses tools, manages sessions, and presents its interface, is largely determined by the developer. DeepSeek Harness takes a different approach. Its basic principle is ‘everything is a plugin’. The model, tools, skills, agent loop, session system, sandbox, and even the user interface are all built as replaceable components. There is no single ‘official’ core that users are expected to leave untouched. This is made possible by Cordis , the plugin framework underneath DeepSeek Harness. In simple terms, Cordis is what allows these different pieces to be added, removed, connected, and replaced without rebuilding the whole system. It also keeps track of which pieces depend on which others, and cleans up the changes made by a plugin if it is removed. For users without a technical background, this has a simple meaning: you are not limited to the features the original developers decided to give you. You can add new capabilities, replace existing ones, or even change how the interface works. The growing plugin ecosystem already includes tools that add new functions and new interface components. Later in this article, we will see what this means in practice: using natural language to change the interface, and then turning a research skill into a visual dashboard with a timeline and information cards. Traceable The agent’s work leaves a detailed trail. Instead of seeing only the final answer, you can follow the whole execution record: what the agent was instructed to do, what tools it called, what those tools returned, and what information was passed into the next step. This distinction matters when something goes wrong. Imagine an AI spends several minutes searching files, reading documents, and making changes. If you only see a condensed account of what it did, you may know that the final result is wrong without knowing where the mistake happened. With a detailed execution record, you can go back through the sequence and ask: What did the agent see? What did it do? What did the tool return? At which step did the wrong turn happen? That means debugging does not have to begin by simply running the entire task again and hoping for a better result. You can inspect the path that produced the result and identify where it went wrong. This is also what makes the visual dashboard later in this article possible. The dashboard is, in effect, turning the agent’s execution record into something a human can read and understand at a glance. Make a Plugin for Your Skill In an era when AI is often used together with skills, many people simply type in the name of a skill and wait for the output. The execution process, however, remains a black box: if the output is not satisfactory, we have no choice but to run the skill again, which is a waste of time and money. By using DeepSeek Harness, we can upgrade our skills with visual interfaces that allow us to monitor and intervene in the operation of the AI. The section below uses POMASA as an example. POMASA is an open-source skill that creates a multi-agent research system to investigate a given topic; the code is available at https://github.com/eXtremeProgramming-cn/pomasa . Its defining feature is that it separates the research process into a variety of stages and executes them step by step based on the given topic. We can therefore build an interface for the various stages, see which stage is currently running, and see which files each stage generates, thereby keeping control of the implementation. For example, we can view the intermediate files while the AI is running, without having to search for them in the Finder, and spot errors in good time. To get started, we need to install POMASA in our project folder. Simply speaking, POMASA separates a complicated task into several stages, provides methodology, generates initial ideas, and builds a reusable research system according to your study. We can use the official prompt as an example. Help me create a multi-agent research system for analysing AI trends in healthcare. Plugins serve as a visual representation of the thought process behind skills, which means that users do not need to understand a skill deeply to intervene in its process. This makes the skill more complete and opens up possibilities for users without advanced technical skills to customise their experience. Conclusion DeepSeek Harness is more than a tool for running AI agents. Its significance lies in opening up the agent itself: users can add, replace, and customise the components that make it work, rather than being limited to a fixed system. Built around a plugin-based architecture, and supported by the Cordis framework, Harness gives users greater freedom to shape both the agent’s capabilities and its interface. Just as importantly, Harness makes the agent’s work more inspectable. By retaining a detailed record of the execution process — including the instructions, tool calls, intermediate results, and other information passed between steps — it becomes easier to understand what the agent actually did. The larger promise of DeepSeek Harness, then, is not simply that it can run more capable AI agents. It is that it gives users more freedom to shape the system and see how it works — making AI workflows more customisable, inspectable, and controllable.

The world’s worst hunger crisis unfolding in war-torn Sudan is set to worsen as El Niño parches the riverbanks of the Nile. A climate pattern caused by periodic warming of surface water temperatures in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific Ocean, El Niño disrupts atmospheric circulation, causing droughts, floods, and extreme temperatures worldwide. Its effects, made more acute by global warming, vary regionally.​ In western parts of East Africa, including Sudan, South Sudan, northern Uganda, western and central Ethiopia, Djibouti, and Eritrea, “El Niño is associated with below-average rainfall during the main rainy season (June to September),” FAO explained. Some regions of these countries “have received less than 60 percent of normal rainfall.”​ Its impact is especially acute on Sudan, where the war between its army and the paramilitary, now in its fourth year, has pushed nearly 20 million people, over 40% of its population, into “crisis levels of food insecurity” . Amid this protracted hunger crisis, exacerbated by the rising fuel and fertilizer costs due to the US-Israeli war on Iran, Sudan is now hit by the dry spell cast by the strongest El Niño in 75 years.​ It is expected to peak into a “super El Niño” between November and February, but its impact is already forbidding. “I’m 63, and I have never seen the Nile this low in August,” a farmer in Al Jazeera state, a breadbasket of Sudan, told the AFP . In its neighboring Gedaref state, also a major agricultural producer, fields that are usually green with crops in August are still brown, AFP reported .​ Further east, across the border in Ethiopia, where the Blue Nile flows into Sudan, the water level in the Grand Renaissance Dam in July was six meters lower than at the same time last year.
