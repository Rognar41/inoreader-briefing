Beyond Plantation Capitalism

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Dick Price, Sharon Kyle, Peter Laarman ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-19T21:43:41+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. An interview with Rev. Peter Laarman by Dick Price and Sharon Kyle What will it take to dismantle an economic system that concentrates extraordinary wealth in the hands of a tiny elite while millions of working people struggle to survive? And how do we build a movement capable of changing not only the policies that sustain inequality but also the values that make such inequality seem acceptable? These questions were at the center of a recent LA Progressive conversation with Rev. Peter Laarman, a retired minister who formerly led Progressive Christians Uniting in Los Angeles. A member of the National Council of Elders, Laarman also serves on its King and Breaking Silence team. Speaking with LA Progressive co-founders Dick Price and Sharon Kyle about his article, Curbing Cancerous Capitalism: An Organizing Challenge for the Ages , Laarman explored the enduring influence of racial hierarchy, the economic legacy of slavery, and the challenge of building a movement that confronts both. Plantation Capitalism (27:12) The Legacy of Plantation Capitalism Central to Laarman's argument is a phrase frequently invoked by the late Rev. James Lawson Jr., the legendary civil rights strategist and advocate of radical nonviolence: "plantation capitalism." Laarman knew Lawson through their overlapping work in Los Angeles, including labor organizing and economic justice initiatives. Lawson had been instrumental in the Justice for Janitors campaign and Clergy and Laity United for Economic Justice, helping connect struggles for workers' rights with a broader movement for social transformation. For Lawson, plantation capitalism was not simply a reference to the historical plantation economy. It described an enduring relationship between concentrated economic power and the people whose labor generates wealth. "The real point," Laarman explained, "is the default wage for most of the history of people on this continent was zero," referring to the uncompensated labor imposed through chattel slavery. He argued that the assumption among powerful interests that labor should cost as little as possible never disappeared with emancipation. Kyle connected that observation to contemporary wage labor, noting that although chattel slavery and wage employment are fundamentally different legal arrangements, both raise questions about who controls the wealth produced by workers. Laarman agreed that wage labor is inherently extractive under capitalism, pointing to the growing concentration of economic power. The conversation turned to the industrial transformation that accompanied the Civil War era. Laarman described how skilled workers in the 1840s and 1850s valued their independence and feared that emerging factory systems would strip them of economic autonomy. He also emphasized that opposition to slavery's expansion did not necessarily translate into support for Black equality. Related: Marlon James Uncovers Depths of Depravity in Evangelical Christianity The Two Walls That Defeated Reconstruction Laarman identified two major forces that undermined the promise of Reconstruction. The first was white racial resentment, particularly resistance to repairing the enormous harm inflicted through slavery. The second was Wall Street's determination to industrialize the nation and exploit its resources following the Civil War. Many northern industrialists who had supported abolition were nevertheless unwilling to support meaningful economic restitution for formerly enslaved people. Instead, Laarman argued, they accepted the continuing racial caste system because it served the interests of industrial capitalism. That history reveals a distinction too often obscured in American accounts of emancipation: ending the legal ownership of human beings did not end the struggle over who controlled labor, property, and the wealth labor produced. Kyle drew a connection between that history and today's extraordinary concentrations of private wealth, questioning why the economic contributions of millions of workers are so routinely attributed to a handful of billionaires. Electoral Victories Are Not Enough The discussion then moved from history to the political challenges confronting the United States today. Laarman warned against assuming that Democratic electoral victories, however welcome, would produce the fundamental changes required to confront inequality and racial domination. Even a sweeping Democratic victory, he suggested, could encourage complacency among comfortable constituencies. He worried that political leaders might leave intact extraordinary executive powers, expansive immigration enforcement structures, and the economic arrangements that direct wealth upward. The danger, he argued, is that temporary political relief could substitute for lasting structural change. "I firmly believe that a transformed politics has to follow a transformed culture," Laarman said, describing the work ahead as both a spiritual and psychological challenge. But he rejected the notion that people should wait for some sweeping moral awakening before acting. Immediate resistance remains essential, particularly in defending vulnerable communities, building mutual aid networks, and exposing the influence of wealthy political donors. Related: Chattel Slavery vs. Wage Labor: Who Is Really Free? The Search for a Different Kind of Movement Kyle pressed Laarman on a question that has become increasingly urgent for those who recognize the depth of America's problems: Where are the organizations that understand the structural nature of economic and racial domination and are actually developing ways to overcome it? Laarman acknowledged that the answer remains incomplete. He described emerging initiatives seeking to connect organizers, abolitionist movements, and communities committed to social transformation. Within the National Council of Elders, he said, discussions are underway about developing local "begin-again circles." These gatherings would offer activists something different from another meeting, campaign, or organizational obligation. They would provide spaces for reflection, renewal, trust, and meaningful connection. Laarman envisioned places where participants could be vulnerable, where everyone would be heard, and where spiritual nourishment would not depend on religious affiliation. Such spaces, he suggested, could help sustain people engaged in exhausting struggles against entrenched power. He also pointed to historical moments when profound cultural changes accompanied political transformation, including the struggles in Poland, Czechoslovakia, and South Africa. Although no example guarantees a lasting transformation, these experiences demonstrate that deeply established systems of domination are not necessarily permanent. Challenging the Wealthy to See Their Common Interests Price raised another formidable obstacle: America's affluent professional and business classes, many of whom benefit substantially from the existing system. Laarman acknowledged that this group represents an important political constituency whose members frequently identify with billionaires rather than working people. Yet he argued that smaller business owners and millionaires are increasingly vulnerable to the same concentrations of economic power, particularly through private equity and emerging technologies. The organizing challenge is to persuade them that their interests are not necessarily aligned with those of the ultrawealthy. That may prove difficult in a culture that encourages people to imagine themselves as future billionaires, even when the economic system makes such outcomes extraordinarily unlikely. Related: Recalling the Torments of a Radical Truthteller Reaching for a Higher Plane Throughout the conversation, Laarman returned to Lawson's conviction that economic justice cannot be separated from the moral and spiritual condition of society. His argument was not that political organizing should give way to religious reflection. Rather, the two must reinforce each other. Movements need practical strategies, durable institutions, and the power to resist exploitation. But they also need a shared understanding of human dignity strong enough to challenge a culture that treats unlimited accumulation as an unquestioned virtue. For Laarman, the task is neither to dismiss existing movements nor to wait for a perfect new organization to appear. It is to connect struggles already underway while inviting participants to imagine a more profound transformation. "We're gonna reach higher," he said. "We're not gonna piss on other people's efforts, but we're going to invite people to step up to a higher plane." The challenge raised by Lawson's concept of plantation capitalism remains: Can Americans build an economy in which the people who create society's wealth also share meaningfully in its benefits and exercise power over the conditions of their lives? Laarman does not pretend to have a complete blueprint. But his insistence that structural change must be accompanied by cultural transformation offers a direction for those determined to move beyond simply managing an unjust system. The task is not merely to curb capitalism's most destructive excesses. It is to organize a society in which human dignity matters more than the unchecked accumulation of wealth.

OPINION: No on Q3 – Jungle Primaries are Bad for Working People’s Interests

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Working Mass: The Massachusetts DSA Labor Outlet ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-19T21:34:50+00:00

Boston during a nor’easter approaching Election Day (Working Mass) By: Jonathan Cohn The most dangerous words in the English language are: “we’re from private equity, and we’re here to help.” When private equity swoops in to “help” a struggling business, that typically means worsening labor conditions and worsening product quality—but increasing the money of those private equity executives. That’s exactly what private equity executives are trying to do with democracy in Massachusetts with this year’s Question 3, making it a place where they can more easily buy candidates and forestall progressive victories at the state and federal level. These donors are seeking to curb the rise of more populist messaging and policy that centers working people rather than the rich and large corporations. The fact that jungle primaries are detrimental to workers’ interests is why labor unions and progressive groups across Massachusetts are united in urging you to vote NO on Question 3. Question 3 would change MA’s electoral system to a jungle primary system like California and Washington. Proponents have been saying that this is about democracy, accountability, choice, competition, but a quick look at where the money comes from says otherwise. According to campaign finance disclosures, 70% of the money raised for Question 3 comes from private equity, with most of that from Bain Capital or Berkshire Partners. Indeed, just six private equity executives account for half of all the money raised by the campaign. These are overwhelmingly donors to conservative Democrats (like MA’s Jake Auchincloss and Seth Moulton) and Republicans (like Susan Collins and Bill Cassidy) who are friendly to the corporate centrism that the financial industry loves. These donors have a long history of funding education privatization ballot questions, and not a lot of history caring about democracy. What is Q3? Question 3 makes several noteworthy changes to Massachusetts elections. It’s important to understand the risks of all of them. The marquis part of Question 3 is the all-party primary system, often called a jungle primary, in which all candidates run on the same ballot and the top two candidates of any party advance to the general election. More on that in a moment. Question 3 also changes how MA elects Lieutenant Governors. Currently, MA has a system with the perhaps inartful moniker of “shotgun marriage”: Governor and Lt. Governor candidates run separately in the primary, and then are paired together in the general election. Under Question 3, gubernatorial candidates would pick their running mates upon formally filing as a candidate with the Secretary of State. Vetting possible running mates is a major hurdle for grassroots and less institutionally connected candidates, and if people don’t think you can win (grassroots candidates often peak late), why would anyone want to bet a year of their time on your campaign? It’s a very clear way to limit the possibility of who can consider running. Question 3 also changes ballot design in Massachusetts. In a bastardized version of fusion voting, it allows multiple minor parties to endorse the same candidate on the ballot. Fusion voting in states like New York strengthens democracy by enabling voters to choose minor parties’ ballot lines and help them to build a base. They earn that ballot line by having actual voters. By contrast, in Massachusetts, you can get minor party status with only 50 people petitioning the secretary of state. With such a low threshold, any slumlord can create a minor party (say, “Massachusetts Housing for All”), and start endorsing candidates on the ballot with deceptive names. The SuperPACs that flood your mailboxes in election season have to disclose their donors, but imagine if they can just get their endorsements printed directly on your ballot. An empty store (Working Mass) A Warning From California California’s recent gubernatorial primary should be a warning to anyone thinking about voting YES on Question 3 this November. This year, given the diverse Democratic field and narrow Republican field in the gubernatorial race, polling consistently showed that two conservative Republicans would advance to the general election, denying voters real choice. Out of fear of such a lockout, the CA Democratic Party started pressuring candidates to drop out of the race, and the two dominant candidates became a self-funding billionaire and the candidate backed by every corporate PAC in the state. Actual policy debate was sidelined, and party elites and big money donors were empowered. Whenever there is a push for consolidation, those with power, money, and privilege will always claim a right to stay in a race, regardless of whether they have the best ideas (often, they don’t). One of the biggest advocates for California’s jungle primary system has been the California Chamber of Commerce . They have touted its ability to help them get more corporate-aligned Democrats elected on the basis of lots of outside special interest money, conservative crossover votes in Democrat vs. Democrat general elections, and the loss of cues through which people typically understand the differences between candidates. And they are right, with the emergence of a new bloc of corporate-backed Democrats more loyal to Uber, Chevron, Meta, and crypto than to any constituencies or values. That’s not a solution to any problem we have in Massachusetts; it will make the ability to pass policies to help working people even harder. Getting Diverse, Working-Class Candidates to Run I have worked for many years to try to recruit more candidates to run for office and support them when they do. When I talk with prospective candidates, the two biggest barriers for them when considering running for office are time and money. Running for office takes up a lot of time. It might require you to take an extended leave from work, or balance a full-time campaign and a full-time job. It also costs a lot of money to run for office, and most people don’t start out with a readymade list of high-net-worth donor prospects. If we want to see more working people run for office, then we need to find ways to rein in the time and financial burdens of running for office. Question 3 makes both of them worse, increasing both the time and money involved in running for office–benefiting incumbents and wealthy candidates for whom neither poses a true obstacle. Ranked Choice Voting vs. Jungle Primaries Although Massachusetts voters unfortunately rejected Ranked Choice Voting in 2020, there has been a robust and inspiring push on the local level to adopt Ranked Choice Voting on the local level. The push for a top-two primary system takes the wind out of those sails. New York City’s mayoral race last year showed us the wide-ranging benefits of ranked choice voting. Zohran Mamdani’s Democratic primary was due to an amazing field operation and a compelling message, for sure, but NYC’s Ranked Choice Voting system was essential to how the campaign played out. When voters can pick multiple candidates and register their preferences accordingly, progressive candidates aren’t in a fight to the death: they want each other’s voters to also rank them. We saw that dynamic in 2025’s greatest bromance: the cross-endorsement between Zohran Mamdani and former Comptroller and current Congressman-elect Brad Lander and the positivity and goodwill surrounding it. When progressive candidates lift each other up, the ideas and the movement benefit. In a ranked choice system, voters also don’t need to be afraid that voting for their top choice could lead to their least favorite choice getting elected. If you were a voter who did not want to see Andrew Cuomo become governor, you didn’t need to spend hours agonizing about how others might vote: you just vote for multiple progressive candidates and leave Cuomo off your ballot. A jungle primary, by contrast, is what Andrew Cuomo wanted to jerry-rig New York’s election system into. After losing the primary, he insisted on staying in the election, and he wanted to force out other candidates in order to form a cross-party conservative coalition, backed by a lot of money and a lot of racism. Cuomo was able to strong-arm Mayor Eric Adams out of the general election, but failed to convince Republican candidate Curtis Silwa out. Nonetheless, Cuomo still got 73% of Republican voters to vote for him, according to exit polls . With Mamdani pulling 50.78% of the vote in the general election, one can easily see how Cuomo’s theory of the election could have worked if he bullied his way into a two-party race. Democracy and the City of New York would have been worse for it. Just Say No Question 3 is shaping up to be a “Whose side are you on?” question. On the yes side: private equity, venture capitalists, and investment bankers. On the no side: every major labor union and progressive group in the state. The yes side, bankrolled by those who have been fighting for years to make working people’s lives worse, and the no side, backed by those who have been fighting to make working people’s lives better. The choice is clear: NO on Question 3. The Unhinged Racist Response to France’s School Protests

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Marlon Ettinger ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-19T18:13:58+00:00

After protests and riots swept France this weekend following the escalating social movement sparked by high schooler blockades, right-wingers internationally responded with a straightforwardly racist narrative. They’re portraying the protests as the work of Muslim invaders, in a treasonous alliance with astroturfed left-wingers, who are working to torch the country. “France is burning!” Donald Trump reacted . “The immigration of people from an entirely different world, people with hatred in their hearts, has shattered that once great country. Close your Borders and take control, before it is too late!” “Western liberals imported the third world into France and other countries in the name of diversity and compassion. They ignored fundamental questions of assimilation and compatibility. They were told it would lead to this and they didn’t listen,” Trump’s vice president, JD Vance, posted in reaction to a video of the bell tower of the Limoges Town Hall falling in flames. “I pray that France, a beautiful country of amazing people, will stop this madness before more of its landmarks burn. And I hope Americans are paying attention. This is the future congressional Democrats seek for your country.” The narrative ignores any of the popular demands, including France’s out-of-control cost of living, a degraded school system, and legitimate explosive anger over the murderous violence the police unleashed on high schoolers at the beginning of the month. Since the movement began, replacing such concerns with blind-to-reality, downright racist grievance-mongering has been the official line taken by international reaction. “What’s happening in France is nothing less than out of control mass migration,” Trump posted to an official White House government account early this month, as the movement started making global headlines. “This isn’t about schools, this is about Islam wanting to take over a once great Country!” “Europe opened its borders and failed to confront radical Islamism,” Texas Senator Ted Cruz commented then. “AOC, Mamdani, El-Sayed, and Talarico want to take us in this direction.” “We need to recognize that the pro-Palestinian protests are fundamentally protests against the West, and their ambitions are much greater than eradicating Israel from the river to the sea,” hedge fund billionaire Bill Ackman bloviated in response to a chairwoman of a Berlin-based think tank describing a video of protesters chanting “We’re all children of Gaza” as the beginning of an “Islamic Revolution in Europe.” “In Iran, it took roughly two years to go from this stage to complete chaos and the establishment of the Islamic Republic,” that chairwoman, Maral Salmassi, who helms the Zera Institute, wrote . “We saw the same symbolism, the same flags, and heard the exact same slogans. Brace yourselves.” Fox News echoed and boosted the theme early on, publishing an investigation on October 9 attempting to delegitimize the student movement by portraying it as a global conspiracy between communists and Islamists. “A transnational network of 50 groups mobilizes around student protests, only 1/3 are youth groups,” read the breathless subheading of the piece, which claimed to have uncovered a globe-spanning operation of “far-left communist and socialist organizations” who are using the “fiery student protests” to push a “political and ideological agenda.” France’s hysterical racists, boosted by far-right American media organizations devoted to pushing racist propaganda, also weighed in early on. “You have to be blind not to see what’s happening here,” Thaïs d’Escufon, the former spokesperson for the since dissolved French white nationalist group Génération identitaire , told One America News host Chanel Rion, describing the student protests as “ethnic riots.” “They only show footage of the students of the bourgeoisie, of the center of Paris, but actually most of the protests are from cities or areas with a lot of replacement,” d’Escufon explained, using “replaced” to mean areas with a lower percentage of whites. “It’s hard to see this footage. . . . You look at this and it doesn’t look like Paris,” Rion emoted after showing overhead footage of an anonymous crowd of protesters at the Place de la Nation in Paris after a march. “It looks like Mogadishu. . . . And yet you saw the photo that the BBC used. They make it look like it’s just native French students upset by a lack of blackboards.” Fox’s investigation has also endorsed the idea that the media has sanitized the movement by portraying it as “students protesting [against] deteriorating school conditions.” In reality, Fox claimed, the movement “is. . . . part of an extensive infrastructure of unions, communist activists, far-left political parties and international media organizations. . . . [who] are organizing demonstrations, calling strikes, mobilizing supporters and broadcasting the unrest around the world.” Fox’s investigation argues that only fifteen of the fifty organizations involved in the movement are actual student groups, and among those groups are communist youth movements and a Muslim student association. Singling out the communist and Muslim organizations, Fox concludes that there’s a “‘red-green’ alliance of leftists with Muslims.” “The communist Red and the Islamist Greens have nothing in common but a common enemy, the Judeo-Christian, capitalist, free West, led by the U.S.,” Mike Gonzalez, a senior fellow at the Heritage Foundation, told Fox. “Their goal is to dismantle us.” British fascist Tommy Robinson has attacked the movement on similar grounds. “The ‘French students’ are destroying Paris again right now,” he joked last week, above a video of French police running past a burning car. This past Wednesday, Robinson attacked Bally Bagayoko, the France Insoumise mayor of Saint-Denis, in a post linking to a video of Bagayoko announcing a restriction on selling alcohol around the city’s train station between 7 pm and 6 am. “Bally Bagayoko made history in March 2026 by becoming the first Black and Muslim mayor mayor of Saint-Denis,” Robinson wrote . “Joined the invader riots last week. Today he announced a ban on alcohol. Wake the fuck up France.” Al-Trojan House The messaging echoes a pernicious narrative in French media that has sought to portray any political organizing by French Muslims as a self-evident Trojan horse for Islamism. “Behind demands about the degraded situation of high schools, the Étudiants musulmans de France (EMF) organization and its allies push their battle against ‘state islamophobia,’” reads a subheading from a story in Le Point claiming that “Islamist networks” are trying to amplify the movement. The article points to a Muslim student organization it claims a government report last year identified as the youth branch of the Muslim Brotherhood. Accusing organizations like EMF of “recuperating” the protests, the article says posts from the organization “speak volumes” about their strategy. One offender from the beginning of the month is a statement in support of the high schoolers’ fight “for a decent and dignified learning environment.” EMF’s statement goes on to oppose government austerity in public services, including universities; a law that raises fees for foreign students; government measures canceling rent subsidies for foreign students; “university partnerships with genocidal and ecocidal businesses”; the selective and “racist” system of university admissions; and government repression of the protests. Le Point also points to AJ+, the Qatari-funded social media wing of Al Jazeera , for their viral social media output highlighting atrocious school conditions and police violence as a vector for spreading this narrative. “Most of them return to the topic of police violence, without a word about the protesters’ [violence], and accuse the ‘mainstream media’ of lying about the ‘violence’ of the marches,” the Le Point article reads. A Liberal Response Massive protests over the weekend of at least a half million people descended into violence, with the police in Paris reportedly losing control of the situation as black bloc militants and vandals took advantage of the chaos to loot some of the city’s toniest quarters. Videos like the clip of the Limoges bell tower falling in flames went viral as examples of destruction being wrought by Third World “barbarians” on Eternal France. “I hope the world is paying attention to the riots in France,” said Tim Kennedy , a celebrity soldier and MMA fighter. “There are no French flags being flown at these protests. This is the consequence of mass immigration.” The idea that there were no French flags being flown at the protests is a flat-out lie. I saw many over multiple days . But the post is representative of the hardening of the narrative laid down earlier in the month. Pro-European liberals also joined in on the hand-wringing and condemnation. “Poland will not repeat the mistakes of other European countries, nor will it pay for them,” Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk announced without making a direct reference to the protests in France. “We are firmly defending the border against illegal migration, we have our own asylum policy (we do not accept applications), we deport those who break Polish law.” “Shocking videos and images are coming out of France,” Hungarian President Péter Magyar posted. “In the face of this senseless destruction and unjustified violence, perhaps it is now clear to everyone why Hungary advocates the strictest possible approach to migration, and why we will not weaken our border protection or immigration policy under any pressure.” Feckless American liberals, while condemning the straightforwardly racist framing and acknowledging that the student protesters are “an extremely diverse coalition,” have flirted with the narrative. Thomas Chatterton Williams, in a piece in The Atlantic titled “The Violence Sweeping France Was Never Really About Schools,” dismisses any real content in the protests after a review of five viral social media clips. “Much of the protesting by students has become an exercise in nihilistic resentment. . . . beneath the pretext of school reform,” Williams writes, pointing to clips showing acts of violence, property damage, or students rallying and shouting, often from social media aggregator accounts that push an anti-immigrant narrative, frequently portraying Arab and African immigration in Europe as a Trojan horse for Islamism. “The violence hasn’t been limited to one side,” Williams admits charitably, drawing parallels between students making anti-police declarations in crowds or singing along to rap songs with distasteful references to terrorism and life-changing, disfiguring injuries inflicted on students by the police. “A police officer fired a tear gas grenade at a teenager’s head, knocking out most of his teeth. More recently, a 15-year-old lost his hand after an officer fired a stun grenade.” At the end of the day France is “at the mercy of protesters,” Williams argues. “The French protest too much,” and “in recent years, France’s protest culture has been indulged and glorified to the point of insanity.” “Some of these demands may be valid,” Williams allows, “but the violence fanning out across France has little to do with pedagogical concerns.” Protesting Right French Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu made a similar argument on Thursday when he claimed that the social movement was “no longer a high schoolers’ movement” but a “movement of urban violence, the intensity of which has increased further in recent days.” In a speech on Sunday evening, President Emmanuel Macron blamed the violence from the night before on “troublemakers from far-left splinter groups, joined by numerous delinquents,” while Lecornu denounced the supposedly “important role” of France Insoumise presidential candidate Jean-Luc Mélenchon and his party in fomenting it. While the French government hasn’t engaged in the same grotesquely racist theories of the American, British, and French far right, they are united with them in their narrative that this social movement is the product of outside, illegitimate forces that are trying to instrumentalize the demands of the high schoolers. That point was driven home particularly forcefully in a report from the Le Canard enchaîné on Wednesday, which revealed that Lecornu had personally directed the interior minister to explore the idea of prosecuting Mélenchon for “inciting rebellion.” Lecornu only backed off after getting cold feet about what type of effect jailing Mélenchon might have on the movement. The paper reported nearly seven thousand arrestees over the past three weeks, 86 percent of whom were between the ages of fourteen and eighteen. Authorities made around two thousand additional arrests over the weekend. “The goal of the tactic is to frighten the students and their parents into stopping the uprising as quickly as possible,” one chief of police told Le Canard . The government remains scared of this social movement because of the fact that it isn’t a foreign plot, the work of immigrant invaders, or an ” Islamo-leftist ” conspiracy. Powerful racists will continue to push the idea that it is because it’s a convenient way to wipe away the demands of the masses, but it won’t change the reality on the ground. Macron’s government has brought violence to France’s streets. A report on Monday morning that Macron rejected the Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez’s offer to resign sends a clear message that he supports what the police are doing despite last night’s pro forma calls for dialogue and rote condemnation of violence. French students have legitimate demands, and many of them are determined to continue protesting until the government answers them. Joint labor/socialist effort made Tax the Rich Day on October 10 a statewide success

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: California DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-19T16:19:50+00:00

California is the fourth largest economy in the world, with a growing number of billionaires, and an expanding crisis of poverty due to concentration of wealth and divestment from public goods like education and healthcare. This has been exacerbated by the Trump administration’s HR1 (“the big beautiful bill”), which cut $1 trillion from Medicaid nationally, resulting in a $100 billion cut to Medi-Cal, and a $20 billion cut to Calfresh, public programs that support our most vulnerable neighbors. After the California Federation of Labor Unions, led by Lorena Gonzalez, voted at its August 4 convention to endorse two ballot measures to tax the rich – Propositions 3 and 40 – California DSA took the opportunity to reach out with a proposal: a California “Tax the Rich Day” on October 10. Gonzalez liked the idea, and the Labor Fed entered into a partnership with California DSA and SEIU-UHW (the proponent of Prop 40). In 2012, the California Federation of Teachers (CFT) and a progressive tax coalition organized across the state in an effort that culminated in the passage of a measure that put a tax on the top 2% of income earners in California in order to fund public education and healthcare. This year, voters will decide on whether to make that tax permanent via Prop 3 . It currently generates between $5 to $15 billion annually. If it does not pass, the tax will sunset in 2030, and 1 in 6 public school teachers will be facing layoffs. In response to HR1’s cuts to Medi-Cal, Service Employees International Union - United Healthcare Workers West (SEIU-UHW), a labor union of over 120,000 healthcare workers across California, advanced a ballot measure, Prop 40 , to create a one time 5% tax on the wealth of billionaires, payable either all at once, or via 1% increments over 5 years. How Revolutionary, Really, Was Christianity?

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Molly Judd ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-19T16:07:28+00:00

In the Acts of the Apostles, the followers of Jesus Christ are repeatedly described as belonging to “the Way.” In the modern Christian lexicon, this phrase is often understood as a spiritual path rather than a physical one. One travels the Way from a life without Christ to one with him. Returning to the original ancient Greek texts, the classicist Tim Whitmarsh points out that the word that underpins the idea of the Way, hodos , can mean a spiritual path, but it can also, and more often, means a physical road — or a journey along one. Paul and other early Christian missionaries were on a spiritual journey, but they were also on what Whitmarsh calls a literal road trip, traveling across modern-day Turkey, Syria, Greece, and elsewhere to convert anyone who would listen. They made these journeys through a Roman world whose network of roads had been dramatically reorganized and extended under Augustus, facilitating movement across the empire. Whitmarsh’s new book, Rome’s Age of Revolution: Augustus, Empire and the Making of Christianity , asks how revolutionary the religion really was, and does so by situating its original texts in the context from which they emerged and which aided their success: the Roman Empire, and the myriad cultures that it encompassed. The question of Christianity’s influence is usually fought out at the extremes. On one side are the New Atheists of the 2000s, among them the late Christopher Hitchens ( God Is Not Great ) and Richard Dawkins ( The God Delusion ), who saw religion as a poison to be reasoned out of existence. On the other are the likes of Tom Holland, whose Dominion argues that Christianity broke with the ancient world and shaped the West’s ideas of morality, compassion and human dignity — arguments he has carried to a vast audience through The Rest Is History podcast. Whitmarsh does something neither camp can. He does not argue against Christian belief; he argues against Christian uniqueness, and his tool is philology rather than polemic. Reading the texts in their original languages, he shows that much of what seems distinctive about the Christian narrative stems from cultural tropes already circulating in the Roman Empire and that it spread along the infrastructure the empire provided. Because he finds the revolution in Rome rather than in the church, he comes close to dismantling Holland’s thesis that Christianity was revolutionary, without having to debunk the faith: if roads, languages and the constant movement of people made Christianity possible, then it was the empire, not the new religion, that remade the world. He is not anti-Christian so much as anti-exceptionalist. He develops this through two concepts. The first is what he calls “swirl,” the unprecedented movement of people, goods, and ideas across the empire, which allowed a religion drawing on Jewish, Greek, and Roman culture to flourish. “Paradox” is the idea that the Roman order, stretched across such vast territories, tended to provoke its opposite, as it did in the birth of Christianity. Swirl is where Whitmarsh is at his best, his grip on ancient languages taking him back to original sources. A Boy Is Born Roughly two thousand years ago, a biographer told of a boy whose birth was preceded by a portent announcing that nature was pregnant with a king, after which the city’s leaders, fearful of losing power, ordered that all new male offspring be killed. You’d be forgiven for thinking this is the story of the birth of Jesus, so close to the slaughter of the innocents ordered by Herod. In fact, this account comes not from the Gospels but from Suetonius — and the boy in question was Augustus. We find similar accounts in the poetry of the time. Virgil’s Fourth Eclogue , composed around 40 BCE, prophesies a golden age ushered in by the birth of a boy, arriving when virgo (the “virgin,” evoking Astraea, goddess of justice) returns. Whitmarsh doesn’t propose a direct line from Virgil to the Gospels; rather, he suggests the poem may be one strand in a wider swirl of savior-birth imagery circulating in the Roman world — imagery that Christian writers would later draw on independently. More speculatively, he traces the poem’s own birth narrative back further still to the birth stories of Alexander the Great and to Jewish scripture. In Jewish scripture, Isaiah predicts that a time of prospering will be preempted by a sign, when a woman will conceive and bear a son named Emmanuel (7:14). Returning to the original Hebrew, Whitmarsh points out that the word used to describe the woman is almah , which generally means a young woman of childbearing age rather than specifically a virgin. In the Greek Old Testament, this was translated as parthenos , a word whose range of meanings includes “virgin” — and which Matthew later uses when quoting Isaiah in his account of Jesus’s birth. Whitmarsh takes this a step further, asking whether Virgil (who could read Greek) may have encountered versions of this Jewish tradition as well as narratives of Alexander the Great’s birth. Drawing these threads together, Whitmarsh suggests that the Christian story of the virgin birth emerged partly through this movement between languages: Jewish scripture translated into Greek, reinterpreted by Christian writers, and read against the same swirling Greek-speaking culture that Virgil himself inhabited. On this reading, Virgil and Matthew are less cause and effect than two writers independently reaching into the same pool of Greek-Jewish material — Virgil’s virgo , already carrying the multiple meanings of Astraea and justice, potentially absorbing yet another resonance in the process. It is an arresting example of his swirl in action. Imaginative Spaces Whitmarsh is on firm ground when dealing with language and translations, but his arguments feel somewhat shakier when he turns to larger hypotheses. He proposes that Augustan dominance of both the physical and political spheres provoked a counterreaction in the form of a seeking out of alternative imaginative spaces. Noting the tendency of Greek literature of the second century to look up or down to venture into new spaces — whether in the form of narratives concerned with deep-sea diving or trips to the moon — Whitmarsh argues that this fascination with finding new spaces beyond governmental control extended to the first Christians too. Early Christian literature conveys a powerful sense of space and travel — whether Paul’s journeying in Acts or the Gospels’ descriptions of distant and unfamiliar places. In time, it would be the desert that would dominate the setting of the Christian imagination in the Life of Anthony . It is here, though, that Whitmarsh’s textual precision gives way to something looser. For instance, the claim that a whole imaginative geography, from lunar voyages to the desert fathers, can be read as a counterreaction to Augustan order is suggested rather than demonstrated; Rome’s Age of Revolution asks the reader to accept a fair amount of inference, in a book otherwise so careful to show its working. Paradox, unlike swirl, is a harder idea to ground in the sources. A Novel Approach In the final sections of the book, Whitmarsh moves away from a focus on Augustus and his revolutions and toward a dissection of the Greek-speaking culture of the eastern empire and its role as an incubator of early Christianity. It is here that he lands his biggest argumentative punches, showing both swirl and paradox at work. He takes aim at the “Hollywood image” of persistent, structural opposition between Christians and Romans. Executions and persecution certainly happened, he explains, but martyrdom stories exaggerated and dramatized them, and they did so using narrative forms borrowed from the newly emerging genre of the Greek novel. Take the martyrdom tale The Passion of Perpetua and Felicity , which tells of the arrest and public killing of the young noblewoman Perpetua, still nursing her infant, and her fellow Christian Felicity, who gave birth shortly before their execution, in 203 CE. Crucially, the text was produced in a world in which Christianity was not subject to a straightforward empire-wide ban. A stable Roman order, paradoxically, gave Christians the space to write up stories of persecution and for these tales to flourish. Whitmarsh argues, against those who read the story as eyewitness testimony, that it relies on strategies borrowed from the Greek novel: pity, tension, and, most of all, eroticism. In the arena, he notes, the women are stripped and put in nets, and the crowd recoils at the sight of Perpetua’s lactating breasts. They are then thrown to a “ferocious cow,” an animal chosen, the text tells us, to match their sex. Perpetua is tossed by the cow, and she is forced to rearrange her clothing to cover her thighs. Later the crowd demands that the martyrs be brought into the center of the arena, so that “they might join their eyes with the sword that penetrated their bodies.” The text is laced with innuendo and sexual imagery, drawing the reader in and allowing them to enjoy it under the alibi of a dedication to Christian martyrdom. Notes on the Visual The Greek novel is Whitmarsh’s final argumentative device, and though successful, as an art historian I am left wondering if he has neglected the visual culture in which early Christianity was operating. Rome’s Age of Revolution is primarily concerned with textual analysis, though if it does have a visual signature, it’s the cover: a detail from a Roman mosaic of Dionysus, found in a Roman villa excavated at Corinth. It’s a striking choice — the kaleidoscopic mosaic tiles presenting a literal manifestation of the swirl of which Whitmarsh writes. The content of the mosaic speaks directly to Whitmarsh’s argument: Corinth was a Greek city remade under the Roman Empire, and Dionysus was a Greek god displayed here within a Roman urban setting. Whitmarsh shows (without referencing the cover) that several pagan texts, even those written as late as the second century CE, align Dionysus with Jesus: he had a divine father and mortal mother, could turn water into wine, performed supernatural wonders, and died before coming back to life. But beyond the cover and a few brief mentions of material culture in the book, Whitmarsh excludes this visual line of enquiry. This is understandable, given he is not an art historian, but there are many times when a brief interlude may have added to his argument. On a recent trip to Rome, a label in the Vatican Museums taught me that the Good Shepherd motif so central to early Christian iconography was modeled on the kriophoros , or ram-bearer, a figure already centuries old in Greek art and closely associated with the god Hermes. The label went further, suggesting Christians adopted this borrowed imagery partly because its familiarity let them worship somewhat covertly under Roman eyes — the same shepherd could read as Hermes to a suspicious neighbor and as Christ to a fellow believer. It’s a tidy story but I found myself doubting it, and Whitmarsh’s dismantling of the Hollywood image of relentless persecution gave me reason to look deeper. Many scholars of early Christian art are now cautious about treating such imagery primarily as a form of disguise: Christians may instead have been adapting a visual language their neighbors already knew how to read, recasting an image associated with care and safe passage within a new theology, rather than smuggling one past the authorities. If that’s right, the Good Shepherd isn’t evidence of a persecuted religion in hiding — it’s another instance of exactly the kind of confident, opportunistic repurposing that Whitmarsh traces through Christian texts. Whitmarsh gives us a masterclass in tracing swirl through language, translation, and narrative and a more tentative case for paradox. But the book’s real achievement is to ask how revolutionary Christianity was without taking either of the usual sides. Christianity, he shows, can be taken seriously as a historical force without being treated as an exception to history. The mosaic on the cover makes the point better than any argument: like the empire itself, it is assembled from borrowed pieces and is no less striking for it. It repays close looking, and so does this book. The most significant demographic shift happening in the U.S. is not just the rise of a Hispanic minority, but the creation of a lasting, multicultural Hispanic-American community spanning various states and metro areas. This community is supported by millions of people, trillions in economic activity, a growing voter base, established Spanish-language institutions, and ongoing cultural ties to Latin America and Spain. While English remains the dominant language nationwide and language assimilation persists over generations, the large scale of Hispanic America shows that integration no longer means cultural loss. Instead, Hispanics are both becoming more American and enriching the U.S. with Hispanic culture. The figures are remarkable. In 2024, the Hispanic population reached around 68 million, making up approximately 20 percent of the U.S. population. Hispanics contributed nearly 71 percent to the total population growth between 2022 and 2023, mainly due to births and immigration. By 2024, 79 percent of U.S. Latinos were American citizens, with approximately two-thirds being citizens by birth. This shift shows that Hispanic influence in the United States is no longer just about newcomers arriving; it now involves millions of American citizens actively transforming American culture in both appearance and sound. Hispanic America Is Older Than the United States The traditional view of immigration often overlooks an important point: Hispanic civilization in North America predates 20th-century immigration. Spain founded St. Augustine, Florida, in 1565, over forty years before Jamestown. Spanish exploration and settlements stretched across Florida, Texas, New Mexico, Arizona, and California. According to the Library of Congress, Spain governed much of the present-day American Southwest from 1598 to 1821, and Spanish missions, presidios, towns, roads, and ranching communities created cultural landscapes that persisted beyond the periods of Mexican and American control. Modern Hispanic growth can be seen as both a result of immigration and a re-emergence of an established Hispanic North American culture. Cities like Los Angeles, San Antonio, Santa Fe, San Diego, and El Paso, among many others, show that Hispanic influence is not an external addition to North American history but part of it. What has changed is the extent of this presence. Related: Puerto Rico Doesn’t Need to Beg Congress for Citizenship. It Needs Sovereignty. The Emergence of a Hispanic Cultural Region Hispanic America is not a single contiguous region and is not culturally uniform. It includes diverse communities such as Mexicans, Puerto Ricans, Cubans, Dominicans, Salvadorans, Colombians, Venezuelans, Spaniards, among others, each with unique national histories and identities. The federal definition of Hispanic includes individuals with Mexican, Puerto Rican, Cuban, Central or South American, or Spanish cultural backgrounds. However, these populations are gradually creating what can be described as a Hispanic Cultural Region : a connected geographical and institutional area where factors such as population density, Spanish-language use, commerce, education, media, political engagement, and transnational networks enable Hispanic culture to sustain and reproduce itself. It looks more like an archipelago than a single territory. One hub includes California, Arizona, New Mexico, Nevada, Colorado, and Texas, all former Mexican territories and previously part of New Spain. Another focuses on Florida and its large Caribbean and Latin American communities. A northeastern corridor runs from metropolitan Washington through New Jersey, New York, Connecticut, and Massachusetts. Chicago serves as a key center of Mexican and Puerto Rican cultures and peoples, while Hispanic populations are growing in Georgia, North Carolina, and other Southern states. Within this large cultural area, Miami comfortably operates in both Spanish and English. Spanish is prevalent in daily commerce in Los Angeles, San Antonio, and El Paso. Throughout New York, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania, neighborhoods are influenced by the Puerto Rican, Cuban, Mexican, and Dominican communities. Connecticut and Massachusetts both have large Hispanic populations, especially Puerto Ricans. Although these regions are definitively part of the United States, they also culturally engage with a broader transnational Hispanic community, participating in both American and Hispanic worlds. Spanish and the Limits of Assimilation Language serves as perhaps the clearest indicator of this resilience. Spanish remains the dominant non-English language nationwide. Census data indicates that approximately 62 percent of Americans who speak a language other than English at home speak Spanish. Within Hispanic communities, 68 percent still speak Spanish at home as of 2024. Pew Research shows that around 75 percent of Latino adults can hold a conversation in Spanish effectively. Assimilation definitely occurs, but not in the manner many Americans want or agree with. As generations pass, English skills improve, and Spanish usage decreases among some people, but not all. However, this does not automatically end the culture. Spanish has unique strengths that most immigrant languages once lacked: millions of speakers within the country, regional concentration, ongoing connections with Latin America, widespread Spanish TV and digital content, international cultural scenes, bilingual businesses, universities, churches, schools, and ongoing migration. The outcome might not be full linguistic separation or total assimilation, but the slow development of a continental bilingual Hispanic cultural system in which English remains the national lingua franca, while Spanish becomes more institutionalized, important, and ubiquitous in key regions and major urban centers. Education plays a crucial role in this process. These connections situate Hispanic Americans within Hispanidad, a transatlantic community that shares language and culture and includes Latin America, Spain, and other Hispanic groups worldwide. As a result, America’s future might lie beyond mere assimilation or cultural division. The Political Economy of Cognitive Dissonance

Silkwood , a 1983 dramatization of the life of worker-activist Karen Silkwood , starring Cher, Kurt Russell and Meryl Streep, is the best and probably the only Hollywood movie about occupational safety and health. It tells the story of the transformation of Silkwood from a carefree worker in a Kerr-McGee nuclear processing plant into a soberly effective opponent of corporate malfeasance. The focus is on her, but the changing attitudes of her coworkers play an important supporting role. A wonderful detail occurs early on, when Silkwood, showing her disdain for even the minimal safety precautions in the plant, sashays up to a radiation monitor designed to flag excessive exposure and briefly flutters her hand in front of it. Later the same device will trigger intense, terrifying experiences for her. Spurred by officials of her union, the Oil, Chemical and Atomic Workers (OCAW), Silkwood begins to recognize that the dangers in a production system she first viewed as routine are criminally excessive. The other workers’ attitudes are mixed. Most of the workers at a union meeting with its safety experts appear to follow Silkwood’s lead, but several others, if anything, become even more accepting of the risk. OCAW wins an election to avoid decertification, but the 80–60 vote shows a substantial minority do not want to rock the boat over radiation hazards. At the beginning, worker acquiescence to risk on the job is shown to be the result of one-sided information coming from the employer in the form of bland reassurances that exposures are well below “permissible” levels. Yet when the union steps in to provide the true story, some workers, including two highlighted in the movie, refuse to believe it. They actually deny the expert evidence that their jobs may be killing them, and they take the boss’s side in the developing conflict. Anyone who has worked in struggles over unsafe working conditions will recognize this dynamic: all too often the workers most at risk resist recognizing it. They may disbelieve expert evaluation or trivialize it, or even close their eyes and ears to it altogether, rejecting the messenger rather than heeding the message. It is easy to blame them for their failure to heed and take action — easy but wrong. To understand why, we need to turn to one of the best-established concepts in psychology, cognitive dissonance. This theory, first developed in the 1950s, has many versions and extensions , but a rather general approach will be all we need. The core idea is that holding two conflicting ideas in one’s mind at the same time causes discomfort — the “dissonance” the theory is named for. This can influence behavior, although its effect will vary from one person and situation to another. Courage to Change the Things One Can A common way to avoid this dissonance is denial. This entails ignoring or disbelieving information that, if accepted, would generate mental conflict. If one is strongly committed to a particular point of view, whether political, religious or something else, it takes an effort of will to acknowledge information that contradicts it. It also shows up in the familiar reaction to disturbing news, “My child [or spouse, dog, or favored political candidate] couldn’t have done that.” There have been hundreds of studies documenting this tendency and weighing the factors that make it more or less prevalent, but little attention has been given to the one that matters most for situations like the one depicted in Silkwood : whether people believe they can act on difficult information to improve outcomes they care about. A useful way to think about the issue is to distinguish between two effects of uncomfortable information. First, it is, well, uncomfortable; one feels worse experiencing the tension it engenders in one’s mind. That’s the dissonance avoidance effect. But taking in challenging information can also help one better understand the problems one faces so to deal with them. You may not be happy to find out that your house is at greater risk of flood or fire than you thought, but armed with that knowledge you can perhaps take better precautions or explore options for moving to a safer location. You may be disturbed to find out your country is responsible for offenses against people in other parts of the world, but this can also be a basis for pooling your political influence with others who feel the same way, so together you can change these policies. The second effect, then, is the usefulness value of information: its contribution to effective action. Put this way, it becomes obvious that power, or at least the perception of it, plays a central role. Cognitive dissonance, uncomfortable as it may be, can be a step toward achieving higher goals, but only if those on the receiving end of new information and ideas feel they have the power to put them to use. To be powerless, then, is to experience the pain but not the gain, which means denial becomes a more likely response. We can see this dynamic in Silkwood. In fact, the central character’s transformation is a vivid example of the effect that growing awareness of power can create on openness to a new and uncomfortable perspective. At the beginning of the film, Karen Silkwood is portrayed as moderately knowledgeable about radiation risk, comforting an older colleague who panics after she sets off an alarm for external exposure and counseling her to get a test for internal contamination. Her approach to her own safety, however, can best be described as cavalier. But as she begins to identify more closely with her union and realizes how the hidden information she has access to can be used as leverage against the company, she takes safety much more seriously. Her sense of potential power overcomes the force of cognitive dissonance. The Cost of Denial But not so for all her coworkers. For dramatic purposes, the hostility some feel toward Silkwood is distilled down to two characters, at least one of whom is explicitly in denial over the dangers of radiation. The fear such workers express is that, by protesting, they will succeed in nothing more than getting the plant shut down, costing everyone their only hope of work in an economically depressed region of Oklahoma. From this perspective, Kerr-McGee’s workers are hostages, and if uncomfortable information about the plant is brought to light, it’s better to look the other way. In fact, that’s what happened in real life, after the events depicted in the movie: Karen Silkwood’s inside information about the cover-up of faulty fuel rod welds was revealed, and the plant was shuttered the following year. This doesn’t justify denial, of course, but it puts it in context. Economic vulnerability raises the cost of solidarity without decreasing its value, since OCAW’s efforts and that of its allies benefitted workers across the country. In a situation like this, susceptibility to denial may depend on the extent workers think in terms of “I” rather than “we.” Denial is often stark in occupational safety contexts, where the economic and health stakes for workers can be large, but it shows up in other contexts too. A strong case can be made, for instance, that it plays a role in public acquiescence to the ever-worsening climate crisis. On purely scientific grounds, 2026 should be a turning point in climate politics. Unprecedented heat waves in Europe, increasingly extreme weather in the United States and elsewhere, and a further upsurge in wildfires are making headlines, while evidence of intensified carbon cycle feedbacks is disturbing scientists . Meanwhile, a September report by the United Nations Environment Programme, Limiting Overshoot , announced that holding global warming to the Paris Agreement target of 1.5 degrees Celsius is already impossible and that we should prepare for even hotter scenarios, which we will somehow have to reverse in the coming decades. Taken together, or even separately, these should evoke an urgent activist response, but instead climate politics is moribund. Climate change is almost invisible in the US midterm elections and political battles elsewhere. Opinion polls in this country show no upsurge of alarm , as the worsening climate trails nearly all other issues as a voter concern. It would be a mistake to attribute this weird disjunction between objective danger and the lack of political response to any one factor, but cognitive dissonance is a likely contributor. There are many points of potential denial along the chain from science to politics: denial that the climate is changing at all; that its changes are due to human action (especially the burning of fossil fuels); that its effects are already harmful and are on track to become catastrophic; and that we have the tools to take action and prevent the worst. And there are also many competing economic, political and social entanglements that might make people resistant to processing the deteriorating news. One factor is worth stressing, however: the feeling of abject powerlessness . After decades of agitation and debate, the kind of climate action that might have forestalled the current crisis has remained far out of reach, leaving many with little hope that the future will be any different. If this is a common feeling, one may decide to avoid the anxiety and just skip the latest distressing news on the climate front. Facing Reality Can Fortify Mass Politics To the extent that such a dynamic is in play, we’re caught in a debilitating loop: discouragement from past political failure leads many people to pay less attention to a given problem. By paying less attention to those problems, people make continued political failure more likely. Arguably, this explains significant part of the mess we’re in. To fight discouragement, some climate activists have been making the case that, while we could be doing better politically, in economic terms things are going forward: the costs of wind and solar energy are dropping dramatically, and investment in them continues to rise. Fair enough. The intention of such sunny observations is to interrupt the denial-and-defeat loop. But it needs qualification. New green energy cuts emissions when it displaces fossil fuels, but adding capacity alone does not guarantee an overall reduction in emissions. Alas, carbon emissions have continued to rise year after year , and the infusion of wind and solar hasn’t yet bent the long-term curve of emission increase. Even worse, warming depends on cumulative emissions, so even a modest decline, much less a leveling off, won’t be enough. Annual emissions must fall and ultimately reach net zero to halt CO-driven warming. This is truly a difficult situation to find ourselves in. There are no easy answers to offer those settling into discouragement, since only a much more ambitious and resolute climate activism than we have seen has the potential to restore a sense of popular power — the conviction that we who reject the nightmare climate scenario really can force action to thwart it. Doing this will mean wedding the climate movement to a broader struggle against the suffocating power of capital, although what that entails is a story for another article. The bottom line: Power in the hands of ordinary people matters not only for what it can achieve directly but also because it encourages us to face challenges we would otherwise find it easier to ignore or deny. We are all Karen Silkwoods on the greenhouse planet we call Earth. Chattel Slavery vs. Wage Labor: Who Is Really Free?

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. In 1860, nearly four million Black people were enslaved in the United States. Their labor produced enormous wealth, but they had virtually no control over what they made or who benefited from it. They planted and harvested cotton, cultivated tobacco and sugar, built homes, maintained households, and practiced skilled trades. Today, American workers produce goods and services on a scale unimaginable in 1860. Yet millions struggle to afford housing, healthcare, education, childcare, food, and retirement. The question connecting these eras is not whether the systems are the same. They are profoundly different. It is this: Who controls the wealth people create, and who is really free? Two Systems, Different Forms of Power Under chattel slavery, people were legally treated as property. They could be bought, sold, separated from their families, and subjected to violence. They could not freely leave or negotiate the terms of their labor. Wage workers, by contrast, are legally free. In theory, they can change employers, negotiate pay, organize unions, own property, and exercise political rights. Those freedoms matter. But legal freedom does not guarantee economic independence. People with little access to land, capital, or other sources of income generally have to sell their labor to survive. When necessities depend on a paycheck, refusing unfavorable work can be difficult. Critics have called this condition wage slavery . The phrase should not erase the violence and total denial of personhood at the heart of chattel slavery. It points instead to a question about economic power: How much control can people exercise over their lives when they depend on owners and employers for access to work and income? The 1860 Debate—and the Civil War By 1860, slavery and wage labor were expanding in different parts of the country. Southern enslavers defended an economy built on forced Black labor. In the North, factories, railroads, commercial agriculture, and wage employment were growing. The fight to expand slavery—and the political power of enslavers—helped drive the nation toward civil war. The conflict was not a balanced contest between two equivalent labor systems; slavery was central to secession and the war that followed. www.nps.gov Still, people at the time debated the insecurity of wage labor. Proslavery writer George Fitzhugh argued that Northern employers treated workers more cruelly than enslavers treated people they held in bondage. He claimed enslavers had a paternalistic duty to provide for them, while employers could dismiss workers when they were no longer profitable. Fitzhugh was not calling for workers’ liberation. He used the risks of wage dependence to defend human bondage, while ignoring slavery’s violence, family separation, sexual exploitation, and denial of personhood. His argument nevertheless shows that the unequal power between employers and workers was already a subject of public debate. Abraham Lincoln imagined a different possibility. In an 1859 address, he described wage work as a stage from which a person might earn, save, acquire tools or land, and eventually work independently. His vision assumed workers could move beyond dependence on wages. abrahamlincolnonline.org That path was never open to everyone, and it became harder to follow as industrial capitalism grew. But the shift toward wage dependence was not simply inevitable. Worker-owned cooperatives and other forms of shared ownership could have let people produce at scale while retaining collective control over their lives, their work and its rewards. Political choices could have supported those alternatives. Emancipation Without Resources The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery, except as punishment for a crime following conviction. But legal freedom did not return to formerly enslaved people the land, wealth, or authority they had been denied. In Life and Times of Frederick Douglass , Douglass describes formerly enslaved people being turned out of their quarters and left to find food and shelter. He writes that they were “without food, without shelter, without land, without money, and without friends,” and describes them as “naked to the open sky.” en.wikisource.org The episode makes the former enslavers’ sense of ownership starkly visible. Even after emancipation, some acted as though the land and shelter where people had lived—and the resources needed to survive—still belonged to them. Douglass’s account shows how little emancipation alone changed the material power of those who had controlled people’s labor and lives. That history matters to this article because freedom is not only a legal status. It also depends on whether people have the resources and power to make meaningful choices. Wage Labor Won—but It Wasn’t the Only Option Today, paid employment is the predominant way people earn a living. For much of American history, however, people worked on farms, in family enterprises, or in independent trades. As wage employment expanded, more people rented their time and labor to employers, surrendering some control over how their lives and work was organized and who received its proceeds. That transition helped build extraordinary productive capacity in the United States. It also concentrated decision-making into the hands of the elite. In many workplaces, owners and executives determine what gets made, how work is organized, and whether workers keep their jobs. The wealth that is extracted by those businesses is generated by labor. It is a collective effort, but authority over that wealth is often concentrated in relatively few hands. The Collective Makes the Fortune We are often told that hard work guarantees success: work hard, make the right choices, and you will get ahead. But effort alone does not determine who becomes wealthy. Ownership does. Workers make goods and provide services. Consumers create markets by buying them. Families provide care that sustains workers and raises the next generation. Taxpayers support infrastructure, education, research, and public institutions businesses rely on. Great fortunes depend on this broad, collective contribution, even when the returns flow disproportionately to owners and investors. By fall 2026, public estimates had placed Elon Musk’s net worth above $1 trillion, largely reflecting the value of his ownership stakes. Such estimates can change with markets, but the scale points to a larger concern: concentrated wealth can bring concentrated power. businessinsider.com Billionaires—and a person valued at over a trillion dollars—can make decisions that reshape workplaces and livelihoods, including firing large numbers of workers. The criticism is not that owners have no role in building companies. It is that legal ownership can give a few people sweeping authority over enterprises whose success depends on the work of many. Workers can be treated as disposable while the people who control the assets act as though the wealth belongs to them alone. Ordinary people are not to blame for participating. People work, shop, and pay bills within a system where opting out can mean losing access to necessities. The danger is that our essential participation helps build private power over which we have little say—and that power can then shape the conditions under which we live and work. Who Is Really Free? Under chattel slavery, the law assigned control of labor and its proceeds to enslavers. Under contemporary capitalism, much of the power to direct production rests with owners, investors, and executives. Workers can organize; government can regulate and redistribute; cooperatives and public institutions can provide alternatives. Those possibilities depend on political choices. The United States abolished the legal ownership of human beings without ending the concentration of economic power. It developed extraordinary productive capacity without ensuring that everyone shares in its benefits. America can produce enormous wealth. The question is whether the people who produce it will gain enough power to decide what it is for—and who benefits. Emancipation ended the legal ownership of human beings. The struggle for economic democracy asks what freedom requires beyond that.

California’s comparative fault rules allow people injured in car accidents to seek compensation even when they share some responsibility for the crash, although their compensation may be reduced by their percentage of fault. Ventura, California, is a coastal city where drivers travel along busy roads, local streets, and major routes such as Highway 101. Traffic congestion, changing road conditions, and driver mistakes can contribute to collisions throughout the area. After a crash, consulting a Ventura, CA, car accident lawyer can help an injured person understand how fault may affect an insurance claim or personal injury lawsuit. California follows a pure comparative negligence system. Under this rule, an injured person can recover damages even if they are found to be mostly responsible for an accident. However, the amount awarded is reduced according to their share of fault. Understanding California’s Comparative Fault Rule Comparative fault is used when more than one person may have contributed to an accident. For example, one driver might have been speeding while another made an unsafe turn. Investigators, insurance companies, or a court may consider the actions of both drivers when determining responsibility. California’s system differs from rules in states that prevent people from recovering damages if their share of fault reaches a particular threshold. In California, a person may still recover compensation even if they are found 90% responsible, although the recovery would be reduced substantially. The legal foundation for this principle includes the California Supreme Court’s decision in Li v. Yellow Cab Co. (1975), which adopted comparative negligence in California. How Shared Fault Can Reduce Compensation The percentage of fault assigned to an injured person directly affects the damages they may recover. Consider a situation in which a driver suffers $100,000 in total damages but is found 20% responsible for the accident. If the remaining 80% of responsibility belongs to another party, the injured driver’s recoverable damages would generally be reduced to $80,000. The calculation is straightforward, but determining the correct percentage of fault can be more complicated. Each party may present different accounts of the collision, and the available evidence may support more than one interpretation. Insurance companies may argue that an injured driver was speeding, distracted, or failed to take reasonable steps to avoid a crash. The injured person may dispute that assessment based on witness accounts, photographs, vehicle damage, or other evidence . Preliminaries General collections & surveys: Scott, The Peasantries of Europe from the Fourteenth to the Eighteenth Centuries and the second, expanded edition of Shanin’s Peasants and Peasant Societies (Shanin 1987) are both excellent collections; the massive recent Handbook of Critical Agrarian Studies deals with a very wide range of topics; then there are contemporary regional surveys like Ayeb and Bush (2019) and Rigg (2020); and at least one concise introduction to various debates in agrarian studies, Bernstein (2010). Probably the only country in Europe for which a continuous history of the peasantry can be written from the thirteenth century to the eighteenth (when it finally disappeared, forced out of existence by a last round of enclosures) is England – see Titow (1969), Smith (1998), Campbell (2005), Allen (1992), Whittle (2000), Neeson (1993) (in that order), and, by way of a postscript, Howkins (2003). Colonial impacts have been described in detail by (mostly) historians: Poncet 1962 (Tunisia), Gibson 1964 (Mexico), Bourdieu and Sayad 1964 [2020] (Algeria), Taylor 1972 (Mexico), Jennings 1976 (New England), Ngo Vinh Long 1991 (Indochina), Baker 1993 (Central Provinces, India), Hardiman 1996 (Western India), Mousa 2006 (Palestine), Nadan 2006 (Palestine), Fields 2017 (Palestine), Ali (2018) (Bengal), and Shokr (2025) (Egypt), nearly all of which can very largely be read as histories of dispossession . Moneylenders flourished throughout colonial South Asia. Baker’s monograph on the northern Central Provinces is our best account of how the British revenue system worked to allow for the amassing of land by moneylenders and the widespread impoverishment that ensued in the ‘terrifying decade’ of the 1890s. The classic question that was posed in the last decades of the nineteenth century was “What would happen to the peasantry with the growing impact of capitalism?” By the last decades of the twentieth century, this focus had largely been displaced by a rather different question – “What happens to agriculture with the growing penetration of industrial capitals?” In the decades after 1945, it was the late 1960s and 1970s that saw a resurgence of interest in peasant studies, related, no doubt, to the (in Europe largely academic) attraction of Maoism in the 1960s and the success of the Vietnamese in expelling the French and then defeating the world’s most formidable power. Wolf’s Peasant Wars , which appeared in 1969, was emblematic of this moment. Henry Bernstein’s interview (in Capps and Campling 2016) conveys a vivid sense of the intellectual atmosphere in those decades. Capitalism, peasants, family farms . This complex of themes can be seen through at least four lenses or in four different ways: (i) the competitive decline of family farms, (ii) differentiation of the peasantry, (iii) the subsumption of household production by capital, and (iv) the much wider process of the capitalist transformation of agriculture itself. In his writings on France, Marx saw the peasantry as a class , one defined by its fragmentation and localism as well as its hostility to all other classes, and referred to its enslavement by capital . The defining image is that of smallholders saddled with mortgages, ‘ruined by debts, at odds with society and authority’. All posterity seems to have retained from those passages of the Eighteenth Brumaire is his description of the mass of France’s peasantry resembling a ‘sack of potatoes’. Yet Marx veered round to a less condescending view later in life; for the various drafts of his reply to Zasulich, see Shanin (1983). Rejecting any false notions of inevitability, in 1881 he saw the Russian rural commune ( mir ) as containing the potential for a direct transition to ‘large-scale co-operative labour organised on a large scale’. The assumption was that Russia would see a socialist revolution in the coming years. II. Classic Texts and Writers Engels’s Bauernfrage , his tract on the Peasant Question in France and Germany , was written in 1894 against the background of German agrarian lobbies that sought to draw the small peasants to their side. ‘By small peasant we mean here the owners or tenant – particularly the former – of a patch of land no bigger, as a rule, than he and his family can till, and no smaller than can sustain the family ’ (Engels 1990; MEW 22 , p. 488). These were farm sizes that required no recourse to the employment of labour and were sufficient to sustain farm families in an average year. It’s worth clarifying, therefore, that this is not how later Marxists would define the poor peasantry and what Engels is talking about is what later writers usually see as a middle peasantry. (The ‘middle’ peasants who figure in the tract are a more substantial stratum and almost always coupled with big peasants.) If this is correct, it is extraordinary that, in the section on Germany, Engels should go on to say, ‘we consider the small peasant living by his own labor as virtually belonging to us’, meaning a constituency socialists would have to address the way they addressed the mass of workers. Small peasants were ‘irretrievably doomed’ under capitalism. On the other hand, ‘No lasting revolutionary transformation is possible in France against the will of the small peasant’. What emerged more broadly from the classical discussion that began soon after Engels were three strands of theory represented by Kautsky, Lenin and Chayanov respectively. Kautsky (1988; orig. 1899) was at pains to reconcile the plain fact of the survival of the peasantry with the general outline of Marx’s theory. The strategy he chose in doing this was his emphasis on capitalism in agriculture following its own laws (cf. Watts 2023), not simply replicating those of industry, and it is this that has made his analysis so attractive to more recent scholars working on contemporary agriculture, especially those in the US. If Kautsky was an agrarian fundamentalist, profoundly impressed by large holdings and giant estates and their use of economies of scale and management, he was as concerned to demonstrate why the survival of large numbers of small owners (over 5 million of them in Germany by 1895) was rooted not in any economic or technical superiority but in the ability of these households to drive themselves (their children included) and tighten their belts (what Kautsky calls ‘overwork’ and ‘underconsumption’) when confronting the competition of bigger, more efficient producers. (Both Allen 1992 and Bray 1986 can be used to qualify this picture in different ways.) Kautsky was convinced that Europe’s small peasants were worse off even than most wage-labourers, but willing to acknowledge that such farms were not in the process of disappearing. He was prescient in other ways, too, for example, suggesting that ‘[w]here this industrialisation of agriculture does not entirely eliminate the smallholder, it binds him to the monopsonist power of the factory and converts him into a serf of industrial capital, working to its requirements’–a remarkable anticipation of trends that became widespread almost a century later. Chayanov’s systematic elaboration of the theoretical categories appropriate to this sort of small to middling peasantry opened a wholly new perspective on the calculations that were being made in family-labour farms, where the crucial consideration was the trade-off between the degree of household demand satisfaction and the degree of drudgery involved in working the family any harder (Chayanov 1966). As economists discovered later, the ‘backward-sloping supply curve’ characteristic of peasant household behaviour when the latter is not constrained by contracts is strong evidence in favour of this view. Nor did Chayanov deny that ‘social differentiation’ could occur among peasant households but he argued that the way to establish that (and establish how important it was) was not statistics on sown acreage but, as he wrote, by ‘direct analysis of capitalist factors in the organization of production, i.e. hired labour on farms’, a perfectly orthodox criterion, even if the forms of exploitation ranged more widely, as the Agrarian Marxists began to realise. Chayanov’s approach is more meaningful than the fiction (Marx’s, Theories of Surplus Value 1 , p. 408) of seeing the small peasant as a capitalist, wage-earner and (‘perhaps’) landowner all rolled into one (e.g. Gibbon and Neocosmos (1985), who propound a theory of ‘capitalist petty commodity production’ that generates the wholly implausible picture of middle peasants as both capitalists and wage-labourers and sees even poor peasants as ‘petty capitalists’; not so Luxemburg (1963): ‘it is an empty abstraction … to conceive of the peasant as entrepreneur, wage labourer and landlord all in one person … (what is peculiar to the peasant in an economic sense is) the very fact that… he is not a representative of capitalism at all but of simple commodity production.’). In any case, it makes no sense to keep contraposing Chayanov to Marx (cf. White 2018 for the same view), not least because he fully accepted Marx’s categories and analysis and used them, for example, when analysing the role of trading firms in the Russian countryside; cf. the following passage in his book on peasant cooperatives: ‘Capitalism, owing to the technical conditions which we have described, was unable to organize agriculture according to the principles of horizontal concentration and invariably looked for other ways of gaining control over the anarchy of agriculture and of organizing it according to capitalist principles . Instead of the not very well-suited forms of horizontal concentration, the gaining of control took the form of vertical concentration ’ (Chayanov 1991 [1926], pp. 5–6). It is worth clarifying that, in the 1890s, Lenin did not argue that ‘differentiation’ leads to distinct classes (in The Development of Capitalism in Russia , he scoffs at the term ‘differentiation’) but that the dissolution of the peasantry does so. The key marker of that was the disintegration of the middle peasantry , something that clearly did not happen in the years that followed (Shanin 1972, mapping the more likely patterns of mobility among Russian peasant households). Lenin’s last statement of how he saw classes in the countryside was written in 1920 as ‘draft theses on the agrarian question’ for presentation at the Second Congress of the Comintern (Lenin 1966). These were meant to cover the whole of Europe and contained a broad outline of six agrarian classes (of which the first three were said to be ‘the majority of the rural population in all capitalist countries’) – 1. agricultural wage-labourers of various kinds, 2. semi-proletarians, 3. small peasants, 4. middle peasants, 5. big peasants, and 6. estate-owners). Leaving the agricultural wage-labourers aside, within the mass of the peasantry proper there were thus four classes or strata – semi-proletarians, small peasants, middle peasants and big peasants – with very clear demarcations between them, and some version of this would become canonical in later Marxist writings, starting, of course, with Mao Zedong and the Hunan report in early 1927 (‘There are three kinds of peasants, the rich, the middle and the poor peasants’, a step back from his own more nuanced analysis of 1926, which remained untranslated till Schram 1969 [at pp. 241–6]). Much more interesting is a later work of Mao’s that was never published in his lifetime. The Report from Xunwu , scheduled to appear in 1937, was eventually published only at the end of 1982. It is, as its translator says, an ‘extraordinary document’, Mao claiming that, as late as 1930, ‘I still did not completely understand the problem of China’s rich peasants. At the same time, in the area of commerce, I was a complete outsider’. It confirms, if confirmation were needed, that the vast mass of China’s rural population consisted of poor peasants (seventy per cent of the county’s rural households in his enquiries) and that the purely landless (those without access to tenancies) were of no importance. Among landlords, the ‘progressive’ element were described as those ‘who have been influenced the most by capitalism … Most of them have merchant backgrounds’. Among the poor peasants, the Report distinguished no fewer than four layers, of whom the great majority (60 per cent) were landless households whose only asset was an ox. Towards the end, Mao notes that men and women had very different attitudes to divorce, and that the Xunwu County Soviet in one of its district meetings had endorsed the slogan ‘Absolute freedom in divorce and marriage’, despite the fact that, among the poor peasants, married men were strongly opposed to the right to divorce, since the work done by women was crucial to the survival of such households. Poor peasant women, Mao had claimed in the essay on Hunan, had ‘more power to make decisions in family affairs’, and ‘enjoy[ed] considerable sexual freedom’ (Schram 1969, p. 258, translating the original version of the report. Hu 1974 is a rare discussion of this topic.) Truong Chinh and Vo Nguyen Giap (1937) used both expressions ‘class’ and ‘strata’ when describing the peasantry of Indochina, arguing that ‘In class terms, peasants are members of the rural petty bourgeoisie, that is to say, a class of people who own some means of production used to support themselves’, but that this class was ‘divided into several strata’ – landless peasants, poor peasants, middle peasants, and rich peasants. The fluidity of these distinctions is clear from their statement that the poorer middle peasants ( trung nong ngheo ) were scarcely any better off than the two categories below them. The point to note in this analysis is that the country’s innumerable tenants ( ta dien ) were included under landless peasants and therefore seen by them as part of what they called ‘the proletariat in the countryside’. It was the presence of this massive landless element that explains the widespread support for the Việt Minh soon after it was formed in 1941. (Much of the field research that Giap did for The Peasant Question was probably done under the academic cover of collecting data for Pierre Gourou’s monumental thesis Les paysans du delta Tonkinois (1936), see Kleinen (2005) for this suggestion. On the rice economies of Asia, by far the most coherent account is Bray 1986, who shows why ‘[e]ven today, wet-rice economies still prove remarkably resistant to the transition to capitalist farming’.) The stratification of the peasantry and of agrarian classes more widely takes a wide variety of forms depending on where and when it occurs. In Russia in the 1920s, it was discussed not in terms of the emergence of capitalists and proletarians as fully formed classes but of ‘class groups’, that is, groups ‘sharing certain class tendencies’ or groups ‘defined by their class tendencies’, following Kritsman’s ideas. The essential reference here, to the work of Kritsman and his school, is Cox and Littlejohn (1984). The greater mass of rural households, some sixty per cent, were thought to belong to a ‘middle peasantry’, known in Russian as serednyak . Lewin (1966; 1968) is lucid on the bafflement caused by the identification of classes in the Soviet countryside, not least of who counted as a capitalist and how widespread that layer was. Crucially, Lewin notes that there were no hard and fast boundaries between different ‘strata’ of the peasantry in the 1920s . In Russia, after the revolution, agrarian structure was largely coterminous with the peasantry and peasant stratification, since the large estates had been seized by the Bolsheviks or the peasantry directly (seventy-five per cent of all estates had been confiscated by February 1918) and the aristocracy had fled or ceased to exist. Conze’s famous essay on the liberal agrarian reforms of the mid-nineteenth century (Conze 1969) maps more typical situations. Central Europe emerges as a vast patchwork of agrarian structures defined by very different degrees of class-division within the peasantry in each of his broad regions. In Bavaria and the Alps the medium and substantial peasantry was described as ‘a class clearly distinct from smallholders and landless people’; in Lower Saxony and Westphalia, ‘a wide social gulf existed between the substantial and medium peasants on one side and the agrarian proletariat’; in Prussia, ‘the peasantry emerged weakened and changed from the decades after 1816’, succumbing to the expansion of large estates; in Estonia and Latvia a substantial peasant class had holdings that ranged from 20 to 60 hectares, while allotment holders and the landless predominated numerically; in Congress Poland, by 1846, forty per cent of the rural population was landless, many forced to migrate overseas or work as seasonal labourers on the estates in central and eastern Germany; and so on. Staying with agrarian structures in this sense, in pre-famine Ireland, Clark (1978) identifies at least five ‘classes’, after explaining how he constructs these, that is, by triangulating between ‘(1) whether or not they controlled land and if so how much of a working day they spent on land that they controlled… (2) how much land they controlled, which is the critical difference between small and large landholders; and (3) the nature of the control they exercised over land, which underlies the difference between tenants and owners’. On this view, Ireland’s small farmers (those with less than 20 acres) had more in common with labourers of one kind or another than with large farmers, and some ‘three-quarters of the adult male agricultural labour force belonged to the rural poor’. In Vietnam in the 1930s, the agrarian structure comprised chiefly landowners ( dien chu ) and tenants ( ta dien ), and the vast majority of peasant families were effectively landless (see Ngô Viñh Long 1991). And, in Iran, nothing like a peasantry was even discernible between the extremes of large landowners ( ῾umdeh malek ) who owned entire villages, smaller town-based landlords ( khurdeh malek ) who owned portions of different villages, and the great mass of sharecroppers and landless labourers who kept those other classes alive (Lambton 1969 is the classic study), at least until the land reforms of the 1960s and 1980s restored a semblance of peasant farming to the heart of Iran’s agriculture, with large numbers of small and middle peasants emerging beneath a handful of ‘large peasant owners’ with 25 acres or more. Mostafa Azkia has argued that the way the Islamic land reform of 1980 was implemented reflected a compromise that ‘favored the Council of Guardians’ view of minimizing the amount of land to be distributed’, so that ‘a potentially radical land reform was transformed into a conservative one’, with less than 6 percent of all rural households counting as beneficiaries of the postrevolution land-distribution programme (Azkia 2002). (The basic study of the first and seemingly more sweeping reform, with the overwhelming majority of peasants acquiring ownership rights of what were essentially mere subsistence holdings and the mass of agricultural labourers driven into even greater destitution, is Hooglund 1982). In India, the post-Independence land reforms made a huge difference to revitalising a middle peasantry which was also drawn largely from the middle castes such as Jats and Yadavs. See Brass (1981) for their political strength in the north Indian states of UP and Bihar and for how caste over determines class relations between the peasantry and the landless, e.g. ‘ All the landed castes share an interest in thwarting the economic and political demands of the low castes, but the votes of the latter are numerous and can be decisive in many constituencies in U.P.’ III. Capitalism & the Countryside There are three distinct strands in this literature (each indicated by Roman numerals in brackets), the first discussing classic forms of capitalist agriculture (which is what ‘agrarian capitalism’ should strictly refer to), the other two more modern variants of the same. To start with, nothing is more confusing than lumping very different sorts of enterprises under the general label ‘family farm’. As Watts had to point out in the Handbook , there is ‘considerable heterogeneity in what constitutes a family farm’. The same expression has been used to cover Chayanov’s family-labour farm, the quintessential peasant farm; commercial agricultural households such as the American family farms of the northern plains whose wheat poured into world markets from the last quarter of the nineteenth century (Friedmann 1978; 1980); modern British family-farm businesses that outsource effective management to industrial and finance capitals (Marsden 1984; Marsden et al . 1986) (here, one is dealing with capitalist farming which is based on the family); American family farmers who are ‘essentially workers’ (Vogeler 1981), and so on. (i) Family farms & agrarian capitalism Allen (1992), Shaw-Taylor (2005, 2012), Marsden (1984, 1986). The most direct investigations of this are those by Allen and Shaw-Taylor. Allen (1992) dated the destruction of the English peasantry to the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Richard Smith concludes his own chapter in Tom Scott’s collection (Smith 1998) by saying, ‘the present discussion … sees no case for not regarding England unambiguously as a peasant society in which “owner-occupancy” reached its apogee early in the seventeenth century’. All of which is a substantially different account of England’s agrarian history and the rise of capitalism to the one argued or implied in Brenner’s work. The fact that agrarian capitalisms only develop where conditions are most favourable (in terms of soil quality, reclamation, markets, etc.) means that their distributions are always geographically uneven. England, with its north/south contrast is an obvious case of this. ‘By the middle of the nineteenth century agrarian capitalism and family farming had very distinctive regional geographies ’ (Shaw-Taylor 2012). South-eastern England was where the large capitalist farms were found, south and east of a line from Norfolk to Dorset. In contrast, ‘if family farms survived in large numbers anywhere in mid-nineteenth century England’, they did so across large parts of the north and north-west (Shaw-Taylor 2005). Capitalist farms are defined here as farms ‘sufficiently large that the majority of labour would have had to be supplied by wage labour’. The key variable is the ratio of labourers to farmers rather than farm size, although the latter obviously influences the former. Any ratio above two to one is treated by Shaw-Taylor as capitalist. And the dominance of farms dependent on wage-labour is seen as a distinctively English characteristic compared to the Continent where family farming was more strongly entrenched. It is worth adding that Shaw-Taylor (2012) adds important correctives to Allen’s book, lowering his acreage thresholds for what would or would not count as a peasant farm. ‘Allen has almost certainly substantially overestimated the size of farms that could be farmed without recourse to hired labour in the south midlands in the early modern period.’ Elsewhere, estates structured their capitalist relations differently. For various accounts of this, see Schissler (1978), Owen (1981), and Robles-Ortiz (2009), who deal with Prussia, Lower Egypt and Central Chile respectively, discussing mainly estates that employed some form of service tenancy ( Instleute , tamaliyya and inquilinos were all labour tenants). Robles-Ortiz shows that the mechanisation of the haciendas from the close of the nineteenth century had a crucial impact on the agricultural sector, contributing to the ‘proletarianisation’ of inquilinos and the extension of wage-labour. Owen notes that the estate owners could ‘use the labor of the tenants’ wives and children’ at discounted rates of pay. The subtitle of his paper, ‘Capitalism of What Type?’ implied a conscious effort to break with binary ways of thinking (yes/no answers) when faced with the question “Was it capitalism?” (The subtitle was a covert reference to an earlier paper by the Egyptian Marxist Anouar Abdel-Malek, where the latter had argued that with Egypt’s integration into the world market in the late nineteenth century, ‘the whole socio-economic structure had shifted to a new level, i.e., capitalism’, concluding, ‘Not “feudalism”; but then, if capitalism, of what type?’) Frank Snowden’s Apulia with its razor-sharp division between the latifondisti and the giornatari (day labourers) gives us a third version of what agrarian capitalism could look like. Here, the mass of casual workers ‘lived not on the land but in large agro-cities’ that were populated ‘almost exclusively by farm workers’ (Snowden 1986). A broadly similar picture was true of southern Spain, where wage-employment on the estates ( cortijos ) consisted of casual labour working in gangs of 20 to 25 men or women for short spells of a few weeks. Martinez-Alier (1971) described Andalusian latifundismo as ‘wage-labour latifundism’. In short, on the wider canvas of a global history, agrarian capitalism took very different forms and the English version, however we construe that, is scarcely a paradigm of its evolution. As the dissolution of the peasantry in the form Lenin had once envisaged became less and less obvious across much of the twentieth century, two alternative models were developed from the late 1970s and in the 1980s. Both were grounded in Capital and suggested new characterisations of ‘simple commodity production’ (Mooney 1988 being an excellent example of the latter). (ii) The subsumption of simple commodity producers by capital This can also be described as peasant commodity production being ‘reproduced in subordination to capital’, the extent of subordination varying from, say, trader-moneylenders subjecting indebted households to the regular exaction of their crops (this is capital subsuming the labour process ‘as it finds it’) to the much tighter forms of control that affect growers ‘contracted as household units of production’ (Watts 1994). That Marx saw the former, looser end of this spectrum of control as a form of capitalist domination is clear from Theories of Surplus Value 3 , p. 188: ‘In this case [meaning the Irish small tenant] profit is called rent, just as it is called interest when, for example, as in India, the worker (although nominally independent) works with advances he receives from the capitalist and has to hand over all the surplus produce to the capitalist ’. In Marx’s day, cotton, opium and indigo were all grown on the advance system, with Marx even willing to allow that the opium advances embodied a circulation of capital. Chowdhury (1964) discusses the way advances were used in the Bengal indigo trade to bind peasants to the planter’s factory. Planters were convinced that having cultivation done by households on their own lands under contract was much cheaper than hiring wage labourers – as one planter told the Indigo Commission (1860), ‘the natives will not value the labour of themselves and families at anything, while working for themselves, whilst they charge very highly and do very little if hired for money by Europeans’. Unpaid family labour was the decisive consideration for capital . Bernstein (1977), Banaji (1977), Roseberry (1978), Cowen (1979, 1981), Williams (1981) are the main contributions here. Goodman and Redclift (1981) have an early discussion of some of this literature, extending it to Latin America. Chayanov had described this form of subsumption of household producers as ‘vertical capitalist concentration’ and seen it as characteristic of the way commercial firms dominate the peasantry. Cowen (1981) was an early description of the system and by far the most sophisticated theoretically . Against the orthodoxy about the economic dissolution of the peasantry, he showed that in parts of postwar Kenya a regeneration of the peasantry was made possible as the advanced, international layers of capital promoted contract farming with firms like Brooke Bond. Cowen argued that ‘household production has been expanded as an integral part of the development of capitalism ’, that, in sectors like dairy cattle and tea, ‘middle peasant production was reproduced in subordination to capital’, but also that ‘The expansion of commodity production provided a space in which households were able to resist direct proletarianisation’. This was seminal work and it was done in Nairobi in the 1970s. In his study of the differential capitalist organisation of the tea industry in China and India, Liu (2020) has two chapters discussing the way rural household labour was integrated into supply chains dominated by powerful Shanghai firms known as ‘tea warehouses’, the livelihoods of several millions of tea growers depending on the circulation of their loan drafts to inland merchants. (iii) Capital & modern agriculture Goodman et al . (1987), Mann (1990), Little & Watts (1994), and Lewontin (1998). In line with the argument that Goodman and his colleagues would lay out in their book, Goodman and Redclift (1986) redefined real subsumption to mean the ‘transformation of agriculture into an industrial process’. Goodman, Sorj and Wilkinson (1987) is groundbreaking. It remains by far the most compelling account of what modern agriculture has come to mean (and leads naturally to the various contributions in Kimbrell 2002, which is subtitled The Tragedy of Industrial Agriculture ). They argue that the long-run tendency of capital is ‘to eliminate the labour process as a “rural” or land-based activity’, suspending the purely natural, biological processes that govern agriculture. Barriers to accumulation are thus only really removed when industrial capitals are able to ‘reduce or weaken the importance of nature in rural production’ (this strategy is ‘appropriationism’) or eliminate the natural base of production altogether either ‘by using nonagricultural raw materials or by creating industrial substitutes for food or fibers’ (‘substitutionism’). In short, ‘for capital, the central constraint is not the (limited) autonomy of the farmer or owner-operator but the inability to eliminate the risks, uncertainties and discontinuities intrinsic to a natural or biological production process’. Studies of contract farming can flow from either of the above models (ii) – (iii), the first more narrowly focussed on labour processes and the control of labour. The crux is that ‘contract farming disguises a wage relationship between buyer and grower , so that peasants are relegated to being hired hands on their own land’ (Little and Watts, Living Under Contract ). Taking North America specifically, one of the earliest analyses of US agriculture can be found in Lenin’s study of 1915, which argued strongly against studying capitalist farms in terms of acreage rather than output values (Lenin 1964). Recent studies include Vogeler (1981), Davis (1980), Buttel (1983), and Wright (1988), of whom the last presents a much less sanguine picture of family farming than does Friedmann. So too does Adelman (1994), writing ‘the family farm, faced with increasing capital costs, required extensive credit to keep it in operation’ (p. 213). To summarise some of this work briefly: Davis (1980) sees contract farming in the US extracting surplus-value from family farms, with farming based on a capitalist labour-process, where work intensity and the length of the production period are controlled through the use of piece-wages. He describes the farm family as ‘a propertied labor force that is non-unionized, self-directed, and willing to work without the guarantees of minimum wage, job security, insurance, and other benefits’. Buttel (1983) captures the decline of commercial family farming, working from the bimodalism that began to characterise US agriculture in the 1970s, in other words, the dominance of a large-scale (capitalist) sector, a ‘subfamily’ sector supporting itself on off-farm income, and a declining middle segment of American farming comprising the traditional ‘independent, full-time, medium-sized family farm’. Buttel makes what he calls the ‘crucial point’ that, ‘while family farmers are by no means inherently politically reactionary, neither are they inherently progressive’. Against the classical picture of the spread of capitalism in agriculture (namely, agrarian capitalism or what Chayanov called ‘horizontal concentration’), Lewontin (1998) argues that, in the US, it was the commercial use of biotechnology that played the decisive role in extending the control of capital over agricultural production by securing the subordination of growers to seed companies. Moreover, this became part of a much wider process of vertical integration of farm production, not least of the kind embodied in contract farming. Lewontin, a Marxist and well-known biologist, defined proletarianisation to mean the farmer’s comprehensive loss of control over the labour-process, with the control of inputs and farming practices entirely in the hands of industrial capital. By far the best survey of contract farming is the long essay by Watts (1994), which shows that contracts can cover growers of very different economic statuses , from large capitalist growers and ‘prosperous’ middle peasants to impoverished peasant households. And, while it may help to think broadly in terms of different models of contract farming (led, say, by states, agribusinesses, and merchant capitals respectively), the same international firms can employ a ‘vast array of contracts’ not restricted to any given social form. Oya (2012) reinforces the point about the irreducible heterogeneity of contract farming arrangements, but adds that, from the standpoint of private businesses, in much of Africa such farming is thought to be unsustainable unless firms are able to enjoy a degree of monopsony power over growers. The fact that this was because of what Oya calls the ‘risk they face in losing the advances given to thousands of hard-to-monitor outgrowers’ only goes to underscore what I myself once described as the ‘partial and unstable character of the capitalist’s control’ in the way subsumption is structured outside tightly defined industrial contexts of the kind discussed by Watts (2004) in connection with large U.S. integrators and the agro-food chains they dominate (Banaji 1977, cf. Theory as History , p. 97). The sheer complexity of the business models now encapsulated in modern agribusinesses is nicely illustrated by Bernhold and Palmisano (2022), analysing the investment strategies of a giant Argentinian business with roots in a family of big landowners. And finally, adding to the convoluted nature of the transformations that are currently underway, there’s the kind of recent penetration of agriculture and farming by contemporary forms of finance capital (investment banks, institutional investors, private equity, etc.) discussed in Clapp and Isakson (2018). IV. Classes of Labour This was mooted initially (Bernstein 2006) to cover those sections of the peasantry that can be described as ‘marginal’, in the sense that their holdings no longer yield a sufficient income for the family, forcing them to seek employment elsewhere to cover the bulk of their subsistence. In India, marginal farmers and landless labourers today form the bulk of the rural labour-force, households with holdings below 2 acres having expanded rapidly in the 1980s and 1990s. However, I have suggested that Bernstein’s concept can usefully be extended to cover a much wider range of rural classes and strata , from middle peasants who work the land entirely or largely with family labour (Engels’ small peasantry) and poor peasants as conventionally understood to a mass of rural workers comprising small tenants, sharecroppers, workers permanently resident on estates ( colonos , peones and inquilinos in the Latin American literature), and so on. Of course, also included among these ‘classes of labour’ would be the landless agricultural workers who, unlike tenants and others, have no access to land and look for work wherever they can find it. It is these completely landless workers, those with no access to land, who tend to embody pure models of class struggle, e.g. Cardoza (1982) on the braccianti , largely because, unlike tenants, labourers have no direct stake in the land they farm (Bray 1986 makes this point). In Italy, it was the countryside that secured the first major gains for Fascism as small farmers, sharecroppers and other ‘intermediate agricultural classes’, strongly wooed by the fasci (Corner 1975), deserted the Socialist unions and landless workers from the end of 1920 in northern provinces like Ferrara, where the divisions between the landless and the others ran deeper. It is worth noting that many of those just described as ‘rural workers’ self-identified as peasants rather than workers, they retained a peasant consciousness, if one can call it that, since they worked the estates along with their families and fought hard to retain their status as employees with access to estate resources such as subsistence plots and grazing rights. (See Martinez-Alier (1973; 1977), Bertram (1974), Maltby (1980), and Jacobsen (1993) on the huacchilleros or shepherds on the livestock haciendas of the Peruvian sierra and their resistance to wage-labour; and Keegan (1987) and Bradford (1987) on South-African labour-tenants, e.g., Keegan: ‘Most black workers on white-owned farms would have regarded themselves as essentially members of peasant households who were providing tenant labour as rent to the white holder of land on which they lived’, or Bradford: ‘Although blacks living on farms were being proletarianized, their struggles often still centred around their status as tenants rather than labourers ’). In these cases, and the case of rural workers more generally, ‘proletarianisation’ takes on a more specific meaning, not the usual one of independent producers being driven off the land (losing their own farms) to become wage-labourers, but the progressive loss of rights that ensued as agrarian employers began to rationalise production in the wake of growing international competition from the end of the 1870s. Rural workers faced worsening conditions as farm sizes were drastically cut, work controls tightened, and eviction formally conceded to employers as their ‘sole means of restoring discipline’. Snowden (1979) refers to the ‘emergence in sizeable numbers from the end of the century of a variety of new sub-categories of semi-proletarianised mezzadri ’, who no longer lived on their plots but commuted to the land from neighbouring villages or from labourers’ barracks and had, crucially, ceased to work in family units. Bradford documents a similar offensive against black labour-tenant families in South Africa in the 1920s, with the ICU waging struggles that were ‘permeated with a spirit of peasant insurgency’. And, in Alabama in the 1930s, black share-tenants faced mass evictions as cotton acreage was cut in half and the owners of large plantations switched to the use of hired labour. The Share Croppers’ Union was an organisation of black tenant farmers and agricultural workers, with a strong presence of women, that was driven into decline by those massive transformations as well as the ‘white chauvinism’ of the Popular Front (Kelley 1990). V. Farm Servants, Day Labourers, Migrant Workers Some of the best studies include Martinez-Alier (1971), Newby (1977), Carter (1984), Snowden (1986), Anthony (1997), Mitchell (1996), Street (2004), Breman (2019), and Linhart (2023; orig.1980). That landlessness is a purely modern phenomenon is an Arcadian myth. Brent Shaw calculated that something like half a million seasonal workers were needed to handle the massive grain harvests in Roman North Africa (Shaw 2013). And the numerous large estates that dominated the Mediterranean in the late empire were worked by labourers ( coloni ) who were essentially landless. Their modern counterparts, the tens of thousands of migrant workers who harvested California’s wheat crop in the ‘flat, hot plains of the Central Valley’ during the wheat boom of the late nineteenth century, are the subject of Richard Street’s vivid and beautifully written history. This was a ‘large-scale, mechanized industrial farming system’ where men ‘routinely worked twelve to fourteen hours a day’ in lethal conditions (‘Throughout the 1870s, hundreds of men died annually in thresher engine explosions, and hundreds more were maimed and crippled’), and against a background of widespread unemployment. Street does not shy away from the racism against Chinese and Japanese farmworkers that ‘permeated the unemployed masses’. Today, indeed for the greater part of the postwar period, the biggest single issue confronting agricultural workers has been unemployment. In Martinez-Alier’s Spanish field study, demands for the expropriation of estates ( reparto ) were ‘always made on the grounds that unemployment would diminish’. There was a strong feeling of solidarity among labourers, which involved ways of behaving that were seen as conducive to more employment. When asked whether a redistribution of land would be better ‘in comunidad or in parcelas ’, most workers answered that dry land estates were best run by cooperatives of labourers, while the irrigated land could always be parcelled out. Colectividad was their expression for ‘a system under which an estate would be taken over by a group of labourers, all earning a daily wage and, at the end of the year, what would be left over and above would be distributed among the members’ (Martinez-Alier 1971). Nothing illustrates the heterogeneity that has always defined agricultural workers as a class than comparing those Andalusian labourers with the Suffolk farm-workers behind Newby’s more famous study The Deferential Worker (Newby 1977). With the rural working population shrinking dramatically in post-war Britain, there were fewer workers on each farm, while rapid mechanisation (combine harvesters especially) brought greater isolation in the labour-process. However, the feature that struck Newby most is that with the proportion of farm-workers living in tied housing actually increasing by the 1970s, the surviving farm-workers were more dependent than ever on their employers, especially in remote rural areas: ‘the threat of eviction hangs over all workers’. And although ‘[l]iving on a farm … engenders a much greater identity between farmer and worker than is typical in large-scale industry’ (Newby’s italics), the final conclusion of the study is interesting: ‘the deference of the agricultural worker is more apparent than real and the image of the agricultural worker as the deferential worker par excellence is due more to the powerlessness of his situation which hinders any overt expression of dissatisfaction than to a genuine endorsement of his own inferior position in society’ (p. 406). VI. Agrarian and Political Struggles Wolf (1969), Malefakis (1970), Davidson (1974), Loveman (1976), Scott (1985), Carr (1987), Davidson (1982), Bradford (1987), Kelley (1990), Zappi (1991), Jodhka (2021), MacMaster (2022) are all relevant here. Davidson (1982) summarises the debate between Bordiga and Gramsci, underscoring the latter’s position that ‘simple anti-capitalist slogans would not work among the peasantry’. Gramsci described the poor peasantry of the South as ‘the most revolutionary class after the proletariat’ and advocated the slogan for a workers’ and peasants’ government. To combat the hold of Fascism, it was essential for the PCI to build alliances in the South. ‘The peasants had to be won, and in Italy won from the Fascists, if a revolution was to be made’. And the line reaffirmed at the party’s Basle Congress in 1928 included the demand for land to the peasants . Carr (1987) is a brilliant survey of agrarian radicalism in postrevolutionary Mexico. On the Laguna cotton estates, many of the tens of thousands of seasonal workers ( bonanceros ) who would gravitate there from July to November are described as supporting the peons or resident labourers during the great strikes of 1936, an exceptional case of solidarity among very different categories of workers. Among Latin-American Communist parties, the Mexican party was ‘the first to develop strong roots within the peasantry’, building these initially in the densely populated western and eastern areas of central Mexico where indigenous villages survived in greater numbers, then extending its base to a Laguna ravaged by the depression, where, in the mid-thirties, it established mixed unions ( sindicatos de obreros y campesinos ) on the haciendas . In the general strike of 1936, more than 150 estates were involved. What eventually emerged from those estates were ejidos where some section of the landless were ‘repeasantised’. MacMaster (2022), a remarkable work, reconstructs the rural background of the Algerian Revolution through the prism of two massifs north and south of the Chelif valley, mountains where, by 1961, after five years of counterinsurgency, some 75 per cent of the peasantry was forcibly removed to camps in a movement the French called regroupements . Bourdieu described those mass displacements as ‘among the most brutal known to history’. Over two million peasants were uprooted and another one million fled to urban shanty towns, while their hamlets in the mountains were declared ‘forbidden zones’. Yet MacMaster insists that ‘peasants were not simply passive objects of French power’ and shows in detail in what sense this was true. He shows how a major part of the Algerian Communist Party (PCA) was forced to abandon its anti-peasant purism and extend the war into the mountains, which explains ‘why the rank and file in rural Algeria were able to merge so rapidly into the FLN and ALN in July 1956’. About Bourdieu, MacMaster writes: ‘Bourdieu, like many “Marxisant” historians and sociologists of the 1960s, followed the orthodox socialist perspective that illiterate, subsistence peasants were incapable of developing a political consciousness … Bourdieu, like the Communist Party, denied agency to the peasantry and thus reinforced the widespread view that they were not meaningful actors in the anti-colonial struggle’. In short, War in the Mountains destroys the ‘orthodox interpretation of the Algerian peasantry … as an inert mass …devoid of political consciousness and unable to determine a destiny that was always decided by others’. When Fanon (1967) famously wrote that the peasantry of Algeria constitutes ‘ the only spontaneously revolutionary force of the country ’ (p. 98), not only was his context Algeria and the revolutionary struggle against French rule, but the key term in that fateful sentence was surely the word ‘spontaneously’. The peasants’ hatred of the system was not the product of long years of patient education and revolutionary work by middle-class intellectuals, but an attitude endemic to the culture and lived experience of a class that had been massively dispossessed in the nineteenth century and then subjugated time and again in what Mostefa Lacheraf described as a ‘conquest without end’. (Fanon’s line on the peasantry and his views about the ‘redemptive force of armed struggle’ are themes that run through Shatz’s new biography, Shatz 2024.) For Bourdieu, what Algeria inherited by the start of the 1960s was a ‘broken and marginalized peasantry’ that had lost its bearings. Déracinement exactly captured the state of existential homelessness that some two million Algerian peasants had been condemned to since 1957 as part of France’s flailing counterinsurgency (Bourdieu and Sayad 1964; 2020). Bourdieu was appalled by Fanon’s positions and would attack them repeatedly over the decades that followed. In one of these later attacks (a 1994 interview) he even suggested that ‘It was the Chinese myth that was forced on Algerian reality’, implying (quite wrongly, of course) that Fanon’s position was a Maoist one. A former student of Bourdieu, Fanny Colonna, would later write that Bourdieu’s image of Algeria’s peasants was a ‘model of radical deprivation of the peasantry’ that ‘left the peasants’ own cultural resources entirely out of the picture’. Its fatal presumption was that ‘the peasants were not in a position to reflect critically on their own lives’ (Colonna 2009). Clegg (1971) read Algeria’s autogestion in strictly class terms; for a different, more plausible view, see Stone (2024). And his assessment of Fanon, which is consistently hostile, shows just how confused the Left’s ‘objectively/subjectively revolutionary’ trope really is, since he simultaneously affirmed and denied the ‘objectively’ revolutionary role of the Algerian peasantry, with starkly opposing statements at pp. 100, 180. (See Knauss 1977 on how Algerian peasants viewed their lot after the Revolution.) James Scott’s ideas about ‘the silent and anonymous forms of class struggle that typify the peasantry’ (Scott 1985) were built around an ethnography of class conflict in a small village in Malaysia’s Muda irrigation district that had seen the introduction of large combine harvesters and a dramatic fall in the employment and earnings of small tenants and landless labourers. And Wolf, Peasant Wars is certainly still worth reading, even if its central thesis about middle-peasant protagonism in modern peasant movements has often been rebutted, e.g. by Charlesworth (1980) and Pouchepadass (1981). Closer to Wolf’s general model than to Scott’s, the peasant uprisings in 1960s Portuguese Africa prompted Basil Davidson’s interesting essay characterising those struggles as ‘modernising peasant movements’ whose success necessarily required a transition from mass support to active participation (‘cultural and political far more than military’) by the peasants themselves. In Brazil, the Movement of Landless Rural Workers (Movimento dos Trabalhadores Rurais or MST for short) emerged in the 1980s to build a substantial base among the rural poor, chiefly through land occupations and the communities that came out of them via expropriations and settlements ( assentamentos ). Wright and Wolford (2003) and Carter (2010) discuss the MST as much more than a purely agrarian struggle, viewing it, as much, as a model of public activism and grassroots democracy in a society where degrees of class repression and inequality are broadly in the same league as India’s. At a different level from all of the above, Martinez-Alier (2002) deals with environmental struggles led by peasants. Rural exodus began in a big way in the last decades of the nineteenth century. To take a particularly striking case of this, between 1876 and 1905, Calabria lost over a third of its entire population to emigration to the Americas, with ‘peasants’ ( contadini ) accounting for sixty percent of those flows and landless labourers for another fifteen (Cinanni 1975). The theme of exodus is mapped in different ways by Kazemi (1980) for Iran, Seddon (1981) for Morroco, Lora-Wainwright (2012) for China, and Gaggio (2016) for Tuscany. The massive waves of cross-border migration that sustained much of the postwar boom in Europe drew on what John Berger would describe as ‘millions of peasants migrating to countries with which they have had no previous connection’ (Berger and Mohr 1975). By the 1960s, sociologists had coined the term ‘sub-proletarians’ ( sous-prolétaires ) for these unintegrated sectors of the working class who, for Bourdieu, were simply urban counterparts of a deracinated peasantry, permanently haunted by unemployment and immune to revolutionary politics (Bourdieu 1962; 2013). In Europe, the decline and abandonment of rural jobs was widespread by the 1950s. Gaggio deals with the rapid demise of sharecropping in post-war Italy. The exodus would subsequently become widespread in most parts of the world, with small and ‘marginal’ farmers having to sustain their involvement in farming through off-farm employment. On how diversification of the farm economy affects the peasantry, see Elson (1997), Zhang (2015), and Rigg (2020). VII. ‘Marx in the Field’ The title of Mezzadri (2021), a wide-ranging collection of essays that starts from a vision of fieldwork as a kind of research that works through the abstractions of theory to enrich and develop them further, as the fieldworker learns something new about the world by engaging with it. This is closer to what Marx in the Grundrisse calls ‘capital in its reality’. In Mezzadri’s collection, where what matters is the movement from the abstract to the concrete, class, capital, and circulation are all brought into sharper focus, Susan Newman’s chapter on the coffee trading companies being a brilliant example of this. Mencher (1974), Gough (1981), Hill (1982), Hart (1986), Stolcke (1988), Jeffrey (2001), Lerche (2003), Harriss-White (2008), and Tsing (2015) were all based on exemplary field-studies. Harriss-White analysed the circulation of capital within rice markets in what was then West Bengal as a chain in which Marwari commercial firms in control of the rice mills borrowed from banks and the money market to lend to paddy purchasing agents who advanced cash loans to farmers for repayment at post-harvest market-prices. As in so much of Indian agrarian history, ‘the main objective of moneylending was to secure supplies in crowded markets’. Mencher (who died in August this year) pointed out that, in much of South India, even poor peasants ‘do not see a commonality of interest with the landless’, explaining this in part by the degree to which agrarian relations were permeated by caste. Kathleen Gough’s fieldwork in Tamil Nadu was done in 1951 and 1952. Reflecting on those years, she wrote: ‘My greatest difficulty was in persuading the landlords to let me visit the Harijan streets, an act strictly forbidden to Brahmans and avoided by Non-Brahmans’. She goes on to say, while the ‘flogging of agricultural laborers continued into the 1950s’, one of her two fieldwork villages (the one called Kirippūr) recognised that ‘the days of tying men to coconut trees and flogging them were ended’, attributing this to the absence of a discernibly dominant caste of landlords in that village and to the presence of the Communists there. Since the 1950s the most serious challenge to upper-caste dominance in India has come from the dalits of UP, who were mostly agricultural labourers in transition to non-agricultural work. Jens Lerche’s paper is a minute description of the dramatic changes this brought in one east UP village where ‘abuse, violence and rape appear[ed] to belong to the past’, when he studied it in the 1990s. Jeffrey showed how entrenched the power of the Jat rich peasantry remains in the districts further to the west in western Uttar Pradesh. Ramakumar (2017) has a fine analysis of how a major explosion of communal violence in one west UP district in 2013 (the victims were largely landless Pasmanda , viz. lower caste, Muslims) reflected the combined impact of the self-organising of the Jat peasantry through the BKU and the khaps and the BJP’s successful penetration of both khaps and BKU in the 2000s. Van Onselen’s biography of Kas Maine, a black South African sharecropper, shows how tightly patriarchy was woven into the lives of sharecroppers and labour tenants (the group I called ‘rural workers’), since women were bound by contracts entered into by their husbands, as were sons and daughters by their fathers, and, of course, none of this transpired seamlessly. Van Onselen had described himself writing the biography ‘within a broadly defined Marxian approach’, and what emerges is the social history of a dying peasantry seen through the microscope of one family in the western Transvaal. Calls for justice and for the reopening of Nolan Wells’ case are mounting after a grand jury declined to bring criminal charges, despite the question of Wells’ cause of death remaining unanswered. Wells, a young Black student at Southwest Mississippi Community College, went on a boating trip on July 4 to Horn Island, just off the coast of Mississippi, with several white friends. He never returned. His friends, who returned to the mainland with Wells’ cell phone but without him, claimed he chose to stay behind. Wells’ body was found in the water near Horn Island two days later. A state medical examiner declared the 18-year-old’s cause of death “undetermined.” The grand jury, however, claimed that the conditions of his death were “consistent with drowning.” When the autopsy reports were publicly released after the grand jury decision, they revealed that there were two contusions on the back of Wells’ head. The family’s attorney and others have described these injuries as blunt-force trauma, insisting that they do not believe Nolan drowned. The autopsy also reported a lack of fluid or debris in his airways, and that his stomach was empty. Newly released messages between Wells’ friends fuel suspicions and calls for justice A 292-page report of cell phone messages between Nolan Wells’ friends was released on October 15. In one group chat, the friends that returned to the mainland without Wells appear to be discussing the investigation by authorities. “Don’t speculate about s— yall.” “Because they can try to get your a–.” “If you talk more,” said one user. As information about the case became public, sparking calls for answers about Wells’ death, the messages appear to express frustration at the increasing suspicion toward this friend group. One user said, “small minded individuals who are so full of hate towards themselves that they can’t help but try and push it onto other people.” Another said, “lay low” “and pray I love yall but we don’t needa be texting ab this.” In a different groupchat, presumably of several Black friends of Wells, users expressed suspicion toward the group of white friends that came back from Horn Island without Nolan. “Ts weird af,” said one friend. “Dem white n—- mane.” “They did sum wit bra,” said another. “i lowkey feel like they drugged his ahh assum icl,” said one friend. “All them white —— was lying ——- was crying otw last night while we was just asking questions,” said another user. “wtf,” responded another. Returning to the majority-white group chat of friends that were with Wells on July 4, one user said: “We need to go to Nolan’s funeral and get with all our other black friends to prove a statement for the media.” “It would shut everyone down.” The conversation then turned toward the Wells’ family attorney, Ben Crump, and the Wells’ funeral. “Whoever paid for the funeral wants it to be some huge thing,” said one friend. “it’s almost like their brainwashing Nolan’s parents and taking advantage of them while their overwhelmed with grief.” “it’s so evil.” The group continued to exchange messages expressing frustration at what they described as “racism” being directed towards them by the public as questions around Wells’ death mounted. A spontaneous movement is now demanding Wells’ case be reopened and investigated thoroughly until justice is served. Movement for Nolan Wells surges across HBCUs Following the grand jury decision, spontaneous student walkouts were seen across dozens of historically Black colleges and universities across the South. “It was clear to us living here seeing the rage around the Nolan Wells case start to come to a boil,” said North Carolina-based organizer with the Party for Socialism and Liberation Prince Williams, speaking to BreakThrough News . There was a walkout at Atlanta University Center on October 1, another at North Carolina Central October 6, and Prairie View A&M University on October 7. After these spontaneous walkouts, students at Jackson State University called a Southern Day of Action on October 15, demanding justice for Nolan Wells. Specifically, they called for the investigation into the death of Nolan Wells to be reopened immediately. “The call spread like wildfire, especially in North Carolina,” said Williams. “Six schools called walkouts and demonstrations.” In total, about a dozen schools across five states in the South answered the call from students in Jackson, he said. From an institutional perspective, the case is a settled matter after the grand jury decision. But organizers on the ground say the institutional legacy of racism, white supremacy, and Jim Crow, have not been forgotten. And neither has the historical tradition of Black resistance. Generations of resistance in the US South “There has never been a moment in the history of the US where Black Americans could rely on the government to get justice for us,” said Williams. Instead, he pointed to the countless ancestors who, through relentless struggle, freed themselves from slavery and broke the back of Jim Crow. “That reality has created a generational responsibility for us to see our own power when we come together on one accord to find justice for ourselves.” As suspicious deaths of young Black Americans are on the rise, Williams says there is little trust in law enforcement . “About 7 in 10 Black adults say prisons, policing, and the court system were designed to hold Black people back.” The recognition of a highly racist system enforced by these very institutions is widespread, he added. The suspicious deaths we are seeing today are a continuation of a long history, especially in Mississippi, where kidnappings of Black people are “written off as disappearances” and lynchings are ruled “accidental drownings.” “We must stay vigilant and continue to raise this case in our daily lives with everyone we know.” National solidarity in the call for justice for Nolan Wells Organizers urge all students and communities across the United States to support the campaign for justice and accountability. “Put pressure on local, state, and federal officials to reopen Wells’ case,” said Williams. However, he emphasized that this can’t be done effectively in isolation. “We need to join organizations of like-minded people that are sustaining these campaigns who can take this movement far beyond the Southern day of Action on October 15.”
