Donald Trump’s Coalition Is Coming Apart

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Jared Abbott ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-17T18:54:27+00:00

Republicans heralded Donald Trump’s 2024 victory as the birth of a new majority: a multiracial, working-class coalition powered by newfound support among black and Latino voters, particularly men, who had finally abandoned the Democrats over inflation, the cost of living, and immigration. While that conclusion may have been warranted in November 2024, it is not anymore. Indeed, over the course of 2026, Trump’s new coalition has been coming apart at the seams. Over the first half of this year, we surveyed Trump’s 2024 voters three times: 1,940 in February, with extra interviews among working-class black and Latino voters, nearly eight thousand in April and May, and 2,360 in a national survey in late June and early July. We found that the share of 2024 Trump voters who are not committed to voting Republican in 2028, what we call “Trump waverers,” has risen appreciably over the course of the year. In February, one in five Trump 2024 voters had become Trump waverers. By April, it was 23 percent, and by July more than a quarter reported that they were not committed to voting Republican in 2028. Wavering Spreads to White and College-Educated Trump Voters The 2024 Trump voters least likely to say they currently plan to vote Republican again in 2028 are the ones at the bottom of the income scale, and that has remained steady over time. In February, 28 percent of Trump voters earning under $50,000 were wavering, against 15 percent of those earning over $100,000. By July, the gap was nearly identical, though wavering grew slightly among both groups, to 32 percent and 18 percent. By contrast, we see a sharp increase in wavering between February and July among 2024 Trump voters with a college degree. In February, we saw a modest diploma divide: noncollege Trump voters wavered at 22 percent and college graduates at 18. But by July, the gap had disappeared, as wavering among college graduates rose more than six points while noncollege wavering remained unchanged. The biggest news is that Trump is finally losing white working-class voters. 2024 Trump voters of color, especially black but also Latino, were much more likely to be waverers in February and April than white Trump voters. By July, however, while black Trump voters were wavering at a rate of over 40 percent, the share of white Trump voters who are wavering rose substantially, from 18 percent in February to 24 percent in July. Wavering by both white and Latino working-class voters now hovers around 25 percent. Class divides are far smaller among whites than among African Americans and Latinos. In the spring survey (the only one large enough to break race down by income), 46 percent of Latino Trump voters earning under $50,000 were wavering, against 19 percent of those earning over $100,000, a spread of 27 points. Among black Trump voters, the difference was 18 points, 45 against 27. Among white Trump voters, it was two points. In short, there is no class divide among whites, but dramatic gaps among black and Latino voters. White Trump voters still have the lowest wavering rate of any racial group, but over the past six months, they have moved faster away from the Republicans than either black or Latino voters. This is true of both college-educated white voters as well as those without a four-year degree: among white Trump voters, wavering rose from 19 percent in February to 25 percent in July for those without a college degree, and from 16 percent to 23 percent for college graduates. Wavering rates are particularly high among Biden-to-Trump swing voters and have risen over time. In February, 57 percent of these switchers said they would not back the Republican nominee in 2028, while by spring, 63 percent did. Voters who stuck with Trump in both elections, by contrast, waver at only 16 to 17 percent. Why Trump Waverers Mostly Aren’t Shifting to the Democrats Leaving the Republicans, however, is not the same thing as flipping to the Democrats. Among the 25 percent of 2024 Trump voters who are waverers today, we found that fewer than one in five say they would vote for a Democrat in 2028. Twenty-two percent name an independent or third-party candidate, 5 percent say they would not vote at all, and 56 percent say they’re unsure. In other words, Democrats have picked up under 5 percent of all 2024 Trump voters. To better understand what’s stopping Trump waverers from supporting Democrats, we asked them what comes to mind when they think of the Democratic Party. Among the 1,055 waverers who answered in our spring survey, 49 percent named a specific reason they did not support Democrats. Twenty-nine percent had no strong view either way, and 18 percent said something favorable about Democrats. The biggest obstacle for the Democratic Party is a question of trust and competence: whether the party can be trusted to act for people like them, and whether it is capable of acting at all. Just under half of Trump waverers, 49 percent, said something negative when we asked what comes to mind when they think of the party, and 59 percent of those answers were some version of that doubt, with nothing to say about where the party stands on any issue. The largest single complaint, 37 percent of everything negative waverers said, was corruption and self-dealing, the charge that Democrats are in it for themselves. Some quotes we heard from interviewees: “When thinking of the Democratic party, I think of people who want power so they can become wealthy while pretending to care for the less fortunate”; “Money hungry, looking out for themselves”; “Only cares about holding power and enriching themselves.” Another 12 percent doubted that Democrats could deliver on anything because the party has no leadership and no fight in it: “A bunch of people who can’t get along and agree on anything”; “Scattered, no genuine leadership”; “Dishonest and lacking credibility”; “Not strong enough to take a stand. A lot of talking no action.” Still others, around 10 percent, related that they believe the Democratic Party does not know or care about people like them: “They have no idea what the average American life is like and they do not care”; “A party that used to be about the working class but now has focused too much on the wealthy elites”; “I been a Democrat all my life and I always thought they were there for the poor people. Boy was I wrong.” Self-interest, weakness, and neglect of ordinary people together accounted for around 60 percent of negative attitudes Trump waverers had toward the Democratic Party. By contrast, while complaints about the party’s ideology and its “wokeness” were clearly present, they were nowhere near as prevalent. Fifteen percent of negative attitudes toward the Democrats focused on divisive social issues, and a further 12 percent charged that the party has drifted to the far left. What’s more, many of the Trump waverers’ negative responses that focused on controversial social issues were complaints about the Democrats’ priorities rather than disagreements with the party on specific policies: “Worried more about LGBT than crime”; “They only care about woke politics. They’re in league with big corporations, neglected the working class.” The main problem, in other words, is not that Trump waverers think the Democrats are too radical. Instead, it’s that they don’t believe Democrats have their best interests at heart, they doubt the party would fight for them if it did, and they feel the people running the party are in it for themselves. Democrats Have an Opening Six months of our data paint a consistent picture: Trump is losing the working-class voters Republicans hoped would help turn his 2024 win into a lasting realignment and are losing more of them as we draw closer to the midterms. Disproportionately, the voters Trump has lost are poor, nonwhite, and young. But his support is increasingly eroding among white Trump voters and college graduates, who are now wavering six or more points below where they were in February. The Democrats’ strategic problem is that while many Trump waverers are likely populist on economics, they are also distrustful of both parties and, for now, far more likely to drop out than to defect to the Democrats. Effective outreach from Democrats will have to come with clear signals that they are fighting for the working class. According to a recent study we conducted, ten out of twelve of working-class voters’ highest priorities are economic, and they care about wages and economic opportunity as well as prices and inflation. Working-class voters also care about corruption: it should be dead easy for Democrats to provide examples of how Trump and his charmed circle of billionaires are making money at the people’s expense. Whether Trump’s cratering coalition becomes an opening for the progressives or just a growing pool of the disaffected depends on whether Democrats can convince them that the party offers voters something worth turning out for, before they begrudgingly settle on the Republicans as the lesser of two evils or give up on politics for good.

The Trump Administration Has Been Spying on Labor Unions

Last week, news broke of a sweeping undercover investigation by the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) in Minnesota early this year to infiltrate and surveil meetings and obtain the financial records of nonprofit groups and two of the country’s largest unions, the Communications Workers of America (CWA) and the Service Employees International Union (SEIU). This investigation was behind the indictments of fifteen people in June. The news should unite the labor movement in defense of constitutional rights and democracy as its members face what one defendant’s attorney calls “ political prosecution .” “CWA 7250 has learned that we have been subject to an illegal and aggressive federal investigation that used undercover agents to enter our office, infiltrate meetings we co-sponsored, subpoena our finances, and surveil one of our officers,” said CWA Local 7250 President Kieran Knutson: We know that this is because our members, from across the workplaces we represent, were active in the fight against the federal occupation and racist campaign of terror by ICE. CWA 7250 members joined neighborhood rapid-response groups, distributed warning whistles, protested at the Whipple Federal Building, marched in the streets and the snow, and helped build the January 23rd Day of Truth and Freedom. We are proud of our work and will never apologize or back down because of these dirty tricks and intimidation. “The most notable revelation to me was their infiltration of the February 15th Workers Assembly to Defeat ICE, a great event that brought over 300 unionists and community members together to democratically debate on next steps for the movement,” said UFCW Local 663 Executive Board member Paul Kirk-Davidoff, a meat cutter at Cub Foods supermarket: I helped put this together, and multiple unions sponsored the event. Four undercovers attended, and made sure to take detailed notes of all the speakers and political positions. This, along with the surveillance of unions, leads me to believe that the feds were frightened by the January 23rd mass strike and the possibility of more labor action. Bogus Conspiracy Federal Deputy Attorney General Brian Lynch participated in the January 6 insurrection at the Capitol. Now he is leading a task force dedicated to prosecuting left-wing activism, which the administration calls political violence. The task force is using Trump’s National Security Policy Memorandum (NSPM-7) to investigate the funding sources of the president’s opponents. Lynch is casting a wide net to go after Trump’s perceived enemies. The investigation targets, among others, the AFL-CIO, the Minnesota Association of Professional Employees, and the Minneapolis Federation of Educators. A slide that prosecutors showed to a grand jury portrays the unions, along with some nonprofits, left groups, and a bike shop, as part of an antifa conspiracy. Minnesotans are responding with solidarity. “This winter, Minnesotans stood together in rejecting the Trump regime’s occupation of Minnesota,” said a statement from a broad coalition of unions and nonprofit organizations, including many who had not been targeted in the investigation so far: Trump’s ICE [Immigration and Customs Enforcement] and Border Patrol agents broke countless laws while kidnapping Minnesotans based on racial profiling, violating our constitutional rights, and murdering our neighbors. . . . Across the state, Minnesotans joined with community, faith, labor, and neighborhood organizations to build networks that protected our neighbors and defended our constitutional rights. “Minneapolis educators worked in union to exercise our constitutional rights to peacefully protest Operation Metro Surge and other discriminatory policies of the president — and we will continue to do so,” said Minneapolis Federation of Educators Teacher Chapter President Marcia Howard in a statement. “For now, we have no further comment about this so-called investigation into whatever they’re calling ‘Antifa’ today.” Followed to Churches, Parks, and Union Halls In January, after federal agents killed Renée Good and union nurse Alex Pretti, investigators with the Department of Homeland Security launched the undercover mass surveillance and infiltration campaign, called “Operation Puppet Master,” targeting the widespread community and labor organizing against ICE and Border Patrol agents. “These agents surreptitiously recorded dozens of conversations with ordinary people who were simply exercising their First Amendment rights to protest ICE’s lawless rampage in our state,” wrote attorney Kevin Riach in a brief as part of a discovery filing on behalf of Isaac Sant, one of the “ Minnesota 15 ” who were charged in June with conspiracy to impede federal officers. “These wild conspiracy allegations are born from the fantasies of political retribution that animate the Trump Administration,” wrote Riach. Undercover agents followed protestors to public meetings at churches, parks, libraries, schools, and union halls. At one meeting organized by Sunrise Movement at Minneapolis Public Library, where participants learned about the history of peaceful sit-ins and de-escalation tactics, an undercover agent posing as construction worker tried to entrap a speaker, saying he “could build items that might assist with more ‘direct action,’” according to newly disclosed documents. The agent told the person “going to jail did not bother him.” Federal agents also used facial recognition software called Clearview AI to compile photo dossiers of activists and protestors. In one diagram shared with Labor Notes , the federal government mapped faces of union leaders, members, and staff to their affiliations into an organizational chart as evidence of a conspiracy. Homeland Security agents used administrative subpoenas to obtain the financial records of SEIU, CWA, and the International Federation of Professional and Technical Engineers, as well as the climate group Sunrise Movement and the nonprofit Voices for Racial Justice. When they pored over SEIU’s wire transfers looking for alleged “domestic terrorist financing,” a release of information filing showed, all that Homeland Security investigators could find were “various types of debits and credits to include annuity payments, strike payments, strike supplies, investigations, union member dues, retiree dues, medical expenses, and pension fund expenses.” A Proud Tradition Minnesota AFL-CIO President Bernie Burnham called the targeting of the labor movement an “unhinged conspiracy theory.” “Instead of holding federal agents accountable for shooting and killing Renée Good and our union brother Alex Pretti, or addressing the dangerous and deadly conditions within immigration detention facilities,” Burnham said in a statement, the Trump administration continues to weaponize our federal government to intimidate our unions, our communities, and anyone who peacefully opposes their radical authoritarian agenda. . . . They are now bringing back McCarthyism to paint unions and organizations that advocate for workers and families as threats to the nation. It didn’t work in the 1950s when Joseph McCarthy accused anyone he didn’t like of being unamerican and it won’t work for Donald Trump. “We are part of the proud tradition of ordinary Americans who join together in union halls and church basements to make this country better,” CWA President Claude Cummings Jr said in a statement . “But our values of unity, of community, are under attack by a president and his Department of Homeland Security that sends government spies to surveil community meetings in church basements . . . . “CWA is a union with a long history of standing up to bullies and billionaire bosses. We will not be intimidated, and we will not be silenced in our solidarity with our members, with our communities, and with our neighbors.”

Capitalism Is Killing Summer

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Liza Featherstone ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-17T16:50:10+00:00

It’s a “Hot Socialist Summer,” proclaims the New York City Democratic Socialists of America Instagram. Indeed, it has been a summer of socialist victories, with more socialists winning electoral office in June than ever, including Claire Valdez and Darializa Avila Chevalier, who are headed to Congress; and the socialist New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani is visible everywhere, from announcements about an impending municipally owned grocery store with cheap food staples to the Knicks victory parade to announcing huge gains in back pay for the city’s deliveristas, those who deliver food for DoorDash and Uber Eats.All this good news feels somehow related to the celebratory atmosphere of summer, a time of cookouts in the park and block parties and dips in bodies of water. But our ability to enjoy summer is deeply tied to the success of the socialist project. That’s because capitalism is ruining summer, and dear reader, only socialism can save it.Let’s start with the worst part: the weather, brought to you by fossil fuel capitalism. When we think of summer, most of us nostalgically recall childhoods playing on the block, going to the beach, running around barefoot, families grilling at the park — all made possible by the warm, sunny weather. People are still doing all those fun things, but there have been fewer days on which to do them as of late, because the climate crisis has brought some truly horrible weather.This summer has been marred by deadly heat waves all over the world, including in places like Spain, which used to be a summer vacation destination but at times reached 109 degrees Fahrenheit this summer. Not to mention the awful air quality, which produced many days we couldn’t go outside, caused by wildfires from Canada — a regular feature of the ongoing climate crisis. And on days that were not too hot or polluted to enjoy the outdoors, we were often pelted with flooding rainstorms. All of these forms of extreme weather, and many others, are products of or wildly exacerbated by the climate crisis.While Democrats, amply funded by fossil fuel capitalists, run away from climate as an issue, claiming it doesn’t poll well, a rising ecosocialist movement fighting for publicly funded renewable energy and against the vampiric, energy-sucking data centers, and electing politicians who will stand up to the fossil fuel and tech polluters, is our only hope.Of course, summer 2026 has not been an uninterrupted apocalypse. On some days, the weather has been perfect. But for many, capitalist wealth hoarding means that the places they can go to enjoy these days are limited. Since most people don’t have their own private island, the mass enjoyment of summer requires public space. In New York City — mostly because we had social democratic government and powerful unions in the middle of the twentieth century — we do have great pools, beaches, baseball fields, and shady parks. Ninety years ago this summer, as Ted Hamm recently chronicled for the Indypendent, 50,000 New Yorkers attended the grand opening of a new pool in Sunset Park.“This is the finest swimming pool in the country,” said then Mayor Fiorello LaGuardia, who socialist Mayor Zohran Mamdani has cited as a role model, “and it’s all yours.”That same summer, LaGuardia, who was never anti-capitalist but was closely allied with socialists and revered for his pro-worker policies, opened eleven new pools across the five boroughs, all of which are still open today.The Astoria Park pool in Queens, New York, one of eleven pools built in the 1930s with funding from the Works Progress Administration.New York has continued in this tradition, and the current socialist administration has done so with enthusiasm and intention. This summer, after seventeen years of disuse, Mayor Mamdani reopened the beautiful art-deco-style Orchard Beach Pavilion, built in 1936 with funding from the Works Progress Administration, now renovated with the historic architecture preserved, with upgraded concessions and communal spaces. Mamdani also expanded the lifeguard program to improve access to its wonderful pools and beaches; the lifeguard union pushed for better pay and bonuses, and the mayor widely publicized those improvements to the job, increasing the number by 150, expanding our access to safe swimming at the city’s fourteen miles of public beaches and seventy-nine outdoor pools. This administration has prioritized summer fun for all, allowing huge free street watch parties during the Knicks playoffs and the World Cup, as well as free outdoor film screenings.But in much of the United States, people are not so lucky. Good public swimming pools have become harder to find, thanks to austerity and privatization (as well as, in some places, the legacy of segregation). Less than half of Americans live within three miles of a forest. And tragically, 70 percent of our shoreline in the United States is privately owned, with only 10 percent protected by robust public access laws. The rich don’t want to pay taxes to make beautiful public spaces, and they’ve been allowed to not only hoard that wealth but also our most stunning natural resources.Wrecking the weather and robbing us of beautiful spaces aren’t the only ways that capitalists deprive us of the full fruits of summer. American-style capitalism and its insane work culture also robs us of the time to enjoy summer’s pleasures. As Claire Valdez, a trade unionist and socialist assemblywoman from Queens who was elected to Congress in June, pointed out on the popular Instagram show Subway Takes, New York City has the best beaches, but most people are so busy working that they don’t have enough leisure time to go. The United States is the only rich country that does not obligate bosses to give workers paid vacations; without a union to negotiate such benefits, or fair wages, and with so little else provided — health care, housing, college — many can’t afford to stop working even for a couple weeks.Socialism is the antidote to the wage slavery and work addiction that the boss class prefers. In fact, socialists invented summer vacation. Ninety years ago, in response to trade union demands and a “holiday movement,” Léon Blum, socialist prime minister of France, mandated paid vacation for all the country’s workers. Blum’s Popular Front government was so committed to summer vacation that it not only required the bosses to allow it but also invested in making summer getaways possible for workers, negotiating with railways to offer vacation packages that workers could afford. Communist governments were also ahead of other countries on summer vacation, requiring employers to offer time off and investing in resorts, campgrounds, and spas where workers could not only relax and return ready to work but also have adventures and experiences outside of work, whether exploring unknown places, engaging in activities like biking, or indulging in the apparently socially sanctioned “resort affair.”New York City summer benefits enormously from our own socialist history and a current administration eager to govern in that tradition. But as Hamm pointed out in his Indypendent piece on the swimming pools, 1930s working-class New York had a freely flowing spigot of federal money to rely on through the New Deal. Mamdani is trying to do a lot with a very different federal government, one seeking to not to improve summer vacation for working-class people but actually worsen its conditions, even cutting staff and access to cherished public vacation spots like National Parks.The vibes this summer are strong and the commitment to socialist summer fun meaningful. But it will take a much more fundamental rearrangement of our economy to address climate change, redistribute property and wealth for broadly shared public space, and rewrite labor relations to allow us the real time off that we deserve.

Kate Bronfenbrenner, the Union Organizer’s Scholar

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Alex N. Press ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-17T15:15:59+00:00

Kate Bronfenbrenner started by asking union organizers to walk her through their campaigns, from beginning to end. Bronfenbrenner, who retired this year from Cornell University’s School of Industrial and Labor Relations (ILR) after more than twenty-five years teaching and researching there, has spent four decades studying why some union campaigns succeed while others fail. What happened after workers first began talking to one another? When did management realize there was an organizing drive? Who served on the organizing committee? Why did one campaign succeed while another failed? Those questions first emerged in her dissertation research in the late 1980s. “There was no silver bullet, no formula,” she said. “It was more that unions were running very weak campaigns. The building blocks of organizing weren’t even being done, the basic things — knowing who you’re organizing, knowing who the employer was, having a committee that was representative of the unit and played an active role in the campaign. We’re not talking about fancy tactics. We’re talking about the basics.” Over the next four decades, Bronfenbrenner’s research on union campaigns, employer opposition, and global corporate restructuring became standard reading in organizing departments. The Strategic Corporate Research Summer School at Cornell trained generations of union researchers and organizers and will continue after Bronfenbrenner’s involvement: This summer, the program was held for the first time at the School of Labor and Urban Studies in the City University of New York (CUNY). Bronfenbrenner’s work reached well beyond academia. The AFL-CIO distributed her research to organizers around the country, and unions brought her in to train their staff. “Probably the most effective thing I did was the Blueprint for Change ,” a major assessment of successful union organizing strategies around the country, she told me. The AFL-CIO “gave it free to every organizer in the country, every union,” and “I think there are organizers who changed how they’re organizing.” Few labor scholars can claim as big of an impact on the labor movement as Bronfenbrenner. An Organizer Before an Academic Bronfenbrenner had not planned to spend her career studying union organizing. She grew up in Ithaca, New York, the daughter of immigrants from Germany and Russia. Her father, a child-development scholar who worked on the creation of Head Start, spent time in Mississippi during the civil rights movement. Her mother organized locally for Eugene McCarthy and George McGovern. As a teenager, she skipped school to attend demonstrations at Cornell, leaving notes explaining that she was participating in acts of civil disobedience before returning to serve detention. She spent two years at Kenyon College before transferring back to Cornell, where she studied prisons, welfare rights, and indigenous history. After graduating, she moved to Seattle, where she helped run a food bank and organized around welfare rights alongside legal-services attorneys, part of a network of labor, welfare-rights, anti-racist, antinuclear, and community organizations. “We actually mapped out and discovered that, for all of us, the people we were fighting against all lived in one gated community in Seattle,” she recalled. From there, she moved to Mississippi, expecting to organize pulpwood cutters with the United Woodcutters Association. “The night I got there,” she recalled, “I hear, ‘Thank God we got another woman. She can help with the bookkeeping and the housekeeping.’” Bronfenbrenner ended up building a credit union and tool cooperative with predominantly black pulpwood cutters, who were trapped in debt to the dealers who bought their timber and financed their equipment. She taught workers how credit unions functioned using drawings, because many had no more than an elementary school education. After the Mississippi campaign collapsed when employers classified the woodcutters as independent contractors, Bronfenbrenner moved to Massachusetts and went to work for Service Employees International Union (SEIU) Local 285, where staff organized new workers, bargained contracts, handled grievances and arbitrations, lobbied legislators, and represented existing members. There were no separate organizing or servicing departments. “You did everything,” Bronfenbrenner recalled. Her organizing career was interrupted by a random violent crime. In 1977, a stranger gained entry into Bronfenbrenner’s Seattle apartment and attacked her. She survived, but the attack damaged her spine, leaving her permanently physically disabled. She returned to organizing after nearly a year away from work, but over time bargaining, picketing, and the other physical demands of the job became harder. Friends in the labor movement suggested labor education. Kate Bronfenbrenner with United Steelworkers leader George Becker. (Courtesy of Kate Bronfenbrenner) Bronfenbrenner had sworn she would never get a PhD. “I wanted to be an organizer,” she told me. “I didn’t want to go back to college.” She returned to Cornell for graduate school and worked as a labor educator at ILR, teaching union members and staff while beginning research on problems unions were confronting in their workplaces. One of her first projects examined the growing use of part-time, temporary, and leased workers. Instead of running campaigns herself, she was now teaching, researching, and speaking with union leaders about the changing workforce. Bronfenbrenner would spend more than twenty-five years at ILR without receiving tenure. Academia has not always placed the same value on the kind of engaged research she did, much of it intended to be used by organizers. Learning to Ask That work soon brought Bronfenbrenner to the attention of Virginia Diamond, then–assistant director of organizing at the AFL-CIO. Diamond approached Bronfenbrenner after a presentation she gave in 1988 to the federation’s executive council on organizing part-time, temporary, and leased workers. Diamond asked whether she would be interested in conducting the first real national study of union organizing. Bronfenbrenner, on the hunt for a dissertation topic, was available. “A week later, eight boxes come to my office,” she recalled. Inside were surveys the federation had attempted to collect from organizers around the country, asking about union and employer tactics and the workers being organized. The project was far larger than the dissertation she had expected to undertake. “And I did not realize that if I did it once, I’d be asked to do it again, and then again, then again.” Most industrial relations research focused on the legal and economic environment surrounding elections rather than on organizing itself. Bronfenbrenner instead tracked down the lead organizer for each campaign and reconstructed the organizing drive. She later replicated and expanded the work in the public sector with sociologist Tom Juravich. In their 1995 paper , “Union Tactics Matter: The Impact of Union Tactics on Certification Elections, First Contracts and Membership Rates,” they wrote that there had been “surprisingly little micro-level research that looks intensively at the organizing process itself.” “There was a general belief that employer tactics were determinative,” Bronfenbrenner told me. “But by interviewing organizers and researching the companies, you could actually see what tactics mattered, what demographics mattered, what made campaigns succeed.” Bronfenbrenner found that “union tactics — what unions actually do during a campaign — as a group matter more than employer behavior or any other set of factors.” They found that unions could improve their odds by beginning “to act like a union and build strong rank-and-file organizations from the very beginning of the campaign.” The strategies associated with higher win rates included representative committees, personal contact, escalating pressure tactics, rank-and-file volunteers, a focus on dignity and fairness, and preparing for the first contract during the organizing campaign. Before submitting the dissertation, Bronfenbrenner sent a summary to every organizer she had interviewed, a practice she continued with later studies. “For a lot of organizers, it was like, ‘Thank you. Somebody’s paying attention to us, listening to us,’” she recalled. “It mattered that they got the results.” Soon the New York State AFL-CIO and unions including the United Auto Workers (UAW), SEIU, and UNITE HERE were bringing her in to train organizers. Union leaders readily embraced her findings on employer coercion and the weakness of labor law. Her findings about unions themselves were a harder sell. Many campaigns were underfunded, lacked representative committees, or relied on organizing staff that bore little resemblance to the workers they hoped to organize. “They loved the fact that I was tracking employer opposition and how terrible it was,” Bronfenbrenner said. “But they didn’t like the fact that I said, ah, but union tactics matter. You know the odds of changing what employers do, but the one thing you can change is what unions do.” The surveys also asked organizers systematically about race and gender. Allies in the AFL-CIO warned her that some affiliates would be furious. Women and workers of color made up much of the workforce being organized, while organizing staffs remained overwhelmingly white and male. Campaigns led by women and organizers of color had substantially higher win rates than those led by white men. Bronfenbrenner moved the demographic questions to the end of the survey so hostile respondents would finish it before abandoning it. “No one had had these kinds of conversations in depth before,” she said. Most organizers appreciated being asked. What Organizers Do By the time Bronfenbrenner began surveying organizers in the late 1980s, employers were using remarkably similar anti-union campaigns. “The majority of unions were still organizing by standing at the gate and handing out cards,” she told me. “Most didn’t have organizing departments. They didn’t invest significant resources in organizing. They didn’t even know who owned the companies.” “But they found a system that works,” Bronfenbrenner said of the employers. “Their management tactics really haven’t changed that much, except by adding technology. They know that threats and interrogation and promises and surveillance — all of that works.” Bronfenbrenner’s 2009 report , No Holds Barred: The Intensification of Employer Opposition to Organizing , drew on a random sample of 1,004 National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) elections held between 1999 and 2003 and detailed surveys of 562 campaigns. The report described an NLRB election process in which it was “standard practice for workers to be subjected to threats, interrogation, harassment, surveillance, and retaliation for union activity.” Employers threatened to close facilities in 57 percent of campaigns, threatened wage or benefit cuts in 47 percent, and discharged union supporters in more than a third. In two-thirds of elections, workers were required to attend one-on-one anti-union sessions with supervisors at least weekly. “Since the rise of the union-avoidance industry in the 1970s,” she wrote, “we have witnessed a significant increase in the intensity and aggressiveness with which private-sector employers have opposed organizing efforts.” As corporations restructured and globalized, “corporate anti-union strategies have become more sophisticated.” “The unions had not really figured out how to combat that,” she told me. “They were sending out lots and lots of leaflets and flyers, but not actually developing the committees to counteract the opposition within the workplace.” Campaigns that were well-organized still ran into a problem she hadn’t fully appreciated in the early surveys. Managers might discipline workers, hire them, or bargain with them. But decisions about ownership, investment, or shutting down a plant increasingly originated somewhere else. The Research They Wanted Some employers responded to Bronfenbrenner’s research in court. After she testified in 1997 at a congressional town hall meeting in Pittsburgh on employers’ anti-union conduct, Beverly Enterprises sued her for defamation. The nursing home company objected to her description of it as “one of the nation’s most notorious labor law violators” and sought $225,000 in damages. The company also sought Bronfenbrenner’s confidential surveys through discovery. Bronfenbrenner regarded access to the data as the real danger. Organizers had answered her surveys on the understanding that their identities and campaigns would remain confidential. “These SLAPP suits were designed to try to make all scholars afraid to do corporate research,” she told me, referring to “strategic lawsuits against public participation.” She feared that if she were ordered to surrender the confidential material, she might ultimately have to refuse. “I thought I was going to have to go to prison,” she said. A federal judge dismissed Beverly’s suit in May 1998, finding that Bronfenbrenner’s remarks were protected because they had been made in a legislative proceeding. But the case made her work a target of employer groups and conservative critics. “Cornell constantly got called with complaints,” she said. “People wanted them to fire me.” Years later, Bronfenbrenner flew to London to speak at a meeting of the International Bar Association. She learned while she was boarding that the panel had been changed: management consultants and employer representatives would be appearing alongside her. Bronfenbrenner came to believe it had been reorganized to expose her to a libel suit in England, where, she had been warned, defamation law was considerably more favorable to plaintiffs than in the United States. A British barrister met her at the airport. For the next hour, he prepared her for the session. His instructions were simple: answer every question by reading from her published work, and say nothing more. “They kept trying to provoke me,” she recalled. “But no matter what they said, I read.” No libel suit was filed against her. Strategic Research Public sector unions wanted the same kind of analysis Bronfenbrenner had done for private sector organizing. Bronfenbrenner had by then taken a job at Pennsylvania State University, where she and Juravich taught in programs for the United Steelworkers. “We got asked to do the Ravenswood project,” Bronfenbrenner recalled, “which became an ethnographic project that took six years.” The United Steelworkers’ fight against Ravenswood in the early 1990s became a key example of strategic corporate campaigning in the US labor movement. (Cornell University Press) The campaign had begun in 1990, when members of United Steelworkers Local 5668 were locked out of Ravenswood Aluminum in West Virginia after contract talks broke down amid company demands for concessions and disputes over safety. By the time Bronfenbrenner and Juravich began studying it, the dispute had expanded far beyond the mill. The union had traced the company’s ownership through Marc Rich’s commodities empire, identified its customers and business partners, and built pressure in the workplace, the community, and internationally. Workers had to hold together through a lockout that lasted nearly twenty months. In Ravenswood: The Steelworkers’ Victory and the Revival of American Labor , Bronfenbrenner and Juravich follow the strategic campaign. Decisions affecting the lockout originated with owners, lenders, customers, and investors who never set foot in West Virginia. In 1992, the company settled. The locked-out workers returned to the plant with a contract, and the episode became a key example of strategic corporate campaigning in the US labor movement. Ravenswood also changed the questions Bronfenbrenner was asking. Increasingly, the important decisions lay beyond the employer that workers saw every day. Across Borders After Ravenswood, Bronfenbrenner increasingly focused on companies whose operations, suppliers, customers, and workers crossed national borders. Her 2000 report , Uneasy Terrain: The Impact of Capital Mobility on Workers, Wages, and Union Organizing , examined how the threat of moving production affected organizing and bargaining even when companies never ultimately relocated. She began tracing ownership, subcontracting, and production across multinational firms rather than one workplace at a time. In February 2006, that work culminated in a three-day conference in New York titled “Global Companies–Global Unions–Global Research–Global Campaigns.” Bronfenbrenner and labor leaders including Rich Trumka, Bruce Raynor, and Ron Blackwell had spent nearly four years preparing it, and more than 560 unionists and researchers attended. Much of the conference was organized around multinational corporations rather than countries or individual unions, bringing together workers employed by different parts of the same company. Participants from the Global South were there not simply to describe conditions in their countries but to help plan campaigns alongside workers elsewhere. The conference organized research around ten multinationals, including Walmart, Alcoa, Sanofi-Aventis, Starwood, Kraft, and Exxon Mobil. Together they reconstructed each company — its ownership, customers, suppliers, finances, and operations — before asking where workers might actually exercise leverage across borders. One of the researchers was Aaron Brenner, then a financial analyst whose clients included mutual funds and hedge funds who also wrote occasionally for Labor Notes . “It wasn’t enough to get the unions in a room,” Brenner, who is now a senior capital markets analyst at the United Food and Commercial Workers, recalled. “You needed to give them the research and analysis that allowed them to design campaigns.” Brenner presented the Walmart research to unionists who had studied or organized the company in Mexico, Britain, and Canada. Brenner later said the conference was followed by a “mini-boom” in strategic-research jobs in unions in the United States and abroad. The proceedings became Global Unions: Challenging Transnational Capital Through Cross-Border Campaigns , edited by Bronfenbrenner. In the late 1990s, David Chu, then head of strategic research at the AFL-CIO, approached Bronfenbrenner about creating a program to expand unions’ research capacity. The first Strategic Corporate Research Summer School was held at Cornell in 2001. Bronfenbrenner developed the corporate-research model used in the program with Keith Mestrich and Tom Juravich. The course required forgetting that you were organizing workers. Participants were asked to think like the company instead. How did it make money? Who owned it? Where was it borrowing? Who sat on the board? Which customers, suppliers, lenders, investors, or government agencies mattered most? Which decisions were made locally, and which somewhere else entirely? Only then did they begin thinking about organizing strategy. Each team worked on a real company, usually one drawn from a participant’s own campaign. Over the course of the week, they reconstructed its ownership, finances, customers, suppliers, logistics, and governance before asking where workers might actually exercise leverage. This year, after Bronfenbrenner’s retirement, the program moved to CUNY’s School of Labor and Urban Studies under the direction of labor scholar Stephanie Luce. Nearly 150 people applied for roughly sixty spots this year, including union researchers, organizers, rank-and-file members, and recent graduates. Only after taking over did Luce fully see how much work Bronfenbrenner had put into each session. Bronfenbrenner read every application and built the student groups herself, matching participants whose skills and experience might complement one another. “She’s extremely conscientious about the quality of her research,” Luce said about Bronfenbrenner, “but also the ethics of what the research is for, never losing sight that at the end of the day this is about rank-and-file worker power.” Kate Bronfenbrenner, second from left, at a retirement party organized by former students. (Courtesy of Kate Bronfenbrenner) The Big Companies Amazon is the kind of company Bronfenbrenner thinks unions have to organize. “I think Amazon has multiple points,” she said. “Certainly, the distribution centers matter, but also the drivers matter, the ships matter, the airlines matter.” Bronfenbrenner doesn’t think unions can avoid companies such as Amazon and Walmart simply because they’re difficult to organize. “The answer is not to organize small,” she said. “You’ve got to organize the big companies, even though that’s harder.” She pointed to national campaigns by the Steelworkers, UAW, UNITE HERE, Communications Workers of America, SEIU, and the Union of Southern Service Workers as examples of unions experimenting with forms of organizing that do not fit neatly into the traditional NLRB election model. “If labor isn’t taking on the very same corporations that are telling Trump what to do. . . . ” she said. “You just have to take on those companies, and that means private equity, and that means Walmart and Amazon.” The question comes up again after an election. “How you define victory is problematic,” she said. “If you define victory as winning an election, that’s a big mistake. That’s just a step. But if you define victory as: you got the employer to make a change, and then you start fighting these fights all along, then you’re going to be able to get a majority of workers slowly but surely.” The Starbucks union drive is a prime example. Workers at a Buffalo Starbucks won the first successful union election at a company-operated Starbucks in the United States in 2021, but organizers continued taking the campaign to other stores rather than waiting for a first contract. “If the union had just said, ‘Oh, we’re not going to move ahead until we get a first contract at Buffalo,’ there would have been no campaign with Starbucks.” The same went for labor law reform: it mattered, Bronfenbrenner said, but whatever legal rights workers had, “you had to organize.” The Next Campaign At the final Cornell session last summer, Brenner recalled what Bronfenbrenner had told him years ago during that first week of the program that he attended: “She explained that I could get a job with a union, that unions needed my skills, that the labor movement needed my skills.” “As it turned out,” he recalled, “I didn’t learn that much more about researching companies. But I learned a ton about analyzing them for the purposes of organizing and bargaining.” Brenner eventually returned to the Summer School as an instructor, teaching alongside Bronfenbrenner for roughly two decades. For Chris Brooks, a former Labor Notes staffer who served as UAW President Shawn Fain’s chief of staff (and is currently a Jacobin columnist), “Bronfenbrenner is not just one of the smartest and most rigorous labor academics in the country, she is also one of our movement’s best strategists.” “It’s the tragedy of our movement,” Brooks said, “that so few have listened to her or taken these lessons to heart.” Today strategic corporate research is taught at the AFL-CIO, Rutgers, the University of California, Los Angeles, CUNY, the University of Massachusetts, and by unions and labor organizations in the United States and abroad. Through her decades of research, Bronfenbrenner changed the way organized labor uses research in assessing where companies’ pressure points are and how to fight back against anti-union tactics. Very few people were doing such work when she started. Now, as she retires, many aspects of her approach have become common sense across much of the labor movement. “Getting members to go through the hoops of fire that it takes to organize, it’s a big ask,” Bronfenbrenner told me. “It is always amazing to me that workers do organize rather than that they don’t, because it is hard to organize, particularly in this very divisive society, coming together and building solidarity.”

German Christian Democrats Are Paralyzed by a Far-Right Surge

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Alexander Görlach ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-17T13:42:48+00:00

Next month, three state elections will test whether Germany’s postwar consensus against governing with the far right will survive. On September 6, roughly two million voters in Saxony-Anhalt go to the polls; two weeks later, on September 20, they’ll be followed by voters in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania and the capital, Berlin. In these first two contests, the Alternative for Germany (AfD) is leading polls; in Berlin it is running second. In Saxony-Anhalt, it is far ahead, polling around 40 to 42 percent. It is within striking distance of an outright majority that would let it govern alone, something no far-right party has done in any German state since 1945. The AfD is a party whose branches in several Eastern states have been formally classified by domestic intelligence agencies as “confirmed right-wing extremist.” Yet today, it tops nationwide polls and has opened up its widest-ever lead over Chancellor Friedrich Merz’s Christian Democrats (CDU). Even in Berlin, a city that has never before been remotely within the AfD’s reach, it is now polling in the high teens, making it competitive for first place for the first time in the capital’s history. This is the backdrop against which Merz now governs. While he took office in May 2025 promising to cut AfD support in half, national polls this summer have shown his center-right camp trailing the AfD by around 7 to 9 points. Merz’s own disapproval ratings top 80 percent in some surveys. The “Brandmauer” — the “firewall” by which Germany’s main conservative force has refused any coalition or cooperation with the AfD since its founding — is visibly under strain. While at the federal level this remains a theoretical prospect, in the East a growing number of local and state-level CDU representatives are open to working with the AfD. Uncomfortable Allies Behind the bad poll numbers is a deeper, more structural problem: On the conventional political spectrum, the CDU and the AfD occupy the same broad camp: the center-to-far right. However, for much of the last two decades, including under Merz’s current coalition, the CDU’s habitual governing partner has been its historic opponent, the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD). Of the last six federal governments, four have been “grand coalitions” of this kind: arrangements born of necessity rather than conviction. Naturally, this has led to uncomfortable compromises, as the CDU and the SPD have different views on the economy and working life. Faced with the challenge posed by the far-right AfD, Merz set out to do the impossible: to position the CDU further to the right while simultaneously forming a coalition with the Social Democrats. That pattern is not unique to Germany. In the United States, Britain, and France, middle-of-the-road parties have increasingly been forced into uncomfortable alliances or minority governments to keep far-right movements out of power. In each case, the effect has been to hollow out the center rather than reinforce it. Germany is following the same trajectory: as the CDU and SPD have leaned on each other to govern, both have bled support — and not only to the AfD. Die Linke, the democratic socialist force in part descended from the heirs of East Germany’s ruling party, has also been gaining ground, particularly among younger and urban voters, and is now running competitively for first place in Berlin. A hollowed-out center produces strength at both ends of the political spectrum. What is unfolding looks a bit like a Greek tragedy: an inevitable outcome arising inexorably from choices already made. In state after state in the East, the CDU is running out of viable coalition partners other than the AfD. And the SPD, the Greens (both center-left), and the libertarian Free Democrats (FDP, center-right) together would not have enough votes to scramble together a coalition. The FDP seems not to even clear the 5 percent threshold required for parliamentary representation in any of these states. In this situation, the CDU could, at best, lead a minority government but would be dependent on ever-shifting majorities. One option for Merz’s party is to abandon the “firewall” and form a coalition with the AfD, on either the federal or the state level. Yet should such alliances ever be struck, this would almost certainly fracture the CDU. The fault line is already visible at the grassroots. While an increasing number of ordinary CDU members and local officials in the East have said publicly that they see cooperation with the AfD as inevitable, or even desirable, such a union is still anathema to most — yet not all — of the national leadership around Merz. This situation is also creating a challenge to Merz’s own leadership, barely eighteen months after his party came first in the 2025 federal election. The chancellor seems to lack any knack for success, either in governing or in dealing with people. He is perceived as aloof and lacking in empathy. Some commentators say a “palace revolt” is at hand. We may be set for a change of chancellor without new elections, a mechanism used only three times in German postwar history, as a way to stop the party’s decline and to hinder further damage. The name that keeps recurring in these discussions is Hendrik Wüst, the state premier of economic powerhouse North Rhine-Westphalia and, by most polls, the most popular figure in the CDU. Wüst represents the party’s more centrist wing, aligned with former Chancellor Angela Merkel. His supporters argue that only a course correction in that direction, away from Merz’s attempt to blunt the AfD’s appeal by moving right on migration and other issues, offers the CDU a way out. Wüst has not directly commented on the speculation, yet party insiders claim that he could be persuaded. The case for such a correction rests on a specific empirical claim: that Merz’s strategy of competing with the AfD on the AfD’s own terrain, for example matching its rhetorical intensity on immigration, has not won back voters but has continued to push them further right. Normalized Still, the strategic dilemma is not just about Merz’s own fate. In a state like Saxony-Anhalt, the AfD is far in the lead, and even its national advantage over the CDU is significant. Should Christian Democrats entertain a coalition with the AfD in such circumstances, this would likely make the party of Konrad Adenauer and Helmut Kohl the junior partner in a far-right-led government. It’s an unimaginable prospect. Analysts say that such a gain for the AfD in Saxony-Anhalt after the September 6 vote would be more than a symbolic win. Aside from the historical milestone of a far-right government, its consequences would ripple into the Bundesrat — the federal chamber representing the different German states — and further normalize the AfD ahead of six more state-level contests due before the next federal election scheduled for 2029. Berlin’s vote on September 20 — complicated by this January’s power-grid blackout, an unresolved housing crisis, and the withdrawal of the CDU’s own incumbent mayor from the race — adds a second, very different kind of test. It will tell us whether the AfD’s appeal, long concentrated in the former East, can also take hold in a diverse, historically left-leaning capital. For now, the AfD’s plans for Saxony-Anhalt reveal what Germany would look like once the party comes to power. It promises the abolition of public television and radio, an end to mandatory visits to concentration camps for school kids, the exclusion of children with disabilities and foreign children from public schools, and the abolition of Christian holidays in favor of pagan-Germanic ones. The CDU could never endorse any part of this platform. However, the party is considered highly adaptable when it serves its interest to remain in power, as demonstrated by its pivot from Merkel to Merz. Whatever happens in September’s elections, the underlying tension will remain. The CDU built its postwar identity on being the moderate anchor of the German center-right. Chancellor Merz could not keep his promise to halve the AfD’s results; in fact, the trend has moved the other way, and quickly. The result will either be a CDU that governs with the AfD or a CDU that finds salvation in picking up where Merkel left off. In any case, it will be a CDU without Friedrich Merz. He will go down in history as the chancellor who enabled the far right to triumph over the CDU.

DSA and the Blueprint for a New Party

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Reform & Revolution ／ 著者: 不明 ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-16T20:41:13+00:00

R&R member Diego Pajuelo revisits Seth Ackerman’s classic “Blueprint for a New Party” in the light of recent victories for socialists at and beyond the ballot box. DSA’s electoral project has scored incredible back-to-back victories this year. They have happened everywhere from city councils to congressional seats, from Oregon to New York, and we haven’t even seen the last of the congressional primary wins! This has generated tons of excitement and mass recruitment. DSA has grown immensely to 120,000 members and continues to grow with every passing day. Since so much of our growth has emerged from electoral victories (in part due to considerable media attention), it is important that we evaluate our current strategy. What’s the reason for it? What has been done, and what needs to be improved? The rationale for DSA’s current electoral strategy largely originates in the article published in Jacobin in the Fall of 2016, “A Blueprint for a New Party,” written by Seth Ackerman . It is important to evaluate the article, its strengths and weaknesses, as well as where DSA is currently in relation to party-building. Ackerman’s article has dominated discussions of DSA’s electoral strategy for the past decade for good reason. Prior to this, there were really only two roads to go down, there were the proponents of Michael Harrington’s “realignment,” who emphasized the need to take over Democratic Party structures such as internal leadership and meetings, and then there were the sects who proclaimed the need for a new workers party in the abstract, without much elaboration on the actual implications of establishing a new party or the method by which it could be built. As it currently stands, the strategy pursued by DSA is fairly different from the original strategy proposed by Harrington. There is no longer a major push for DSA members to be actively implanted in the Democratic Party’s internal structures, or to make the Democratic Party as a whole adopt socialist policies. The attempts to do this in Nevada proved this a meaningless endeavor. Instead, DSA has shifted its focus to building our own structure, membership lists, electoral infrastructure, and distinct political program. It is a substantial break from Harrington’s strategy, but it is not quite a break from the Democratic Party. At the time, the article was a breath of fresh air. It brought forward an approach to party-building without relying on stale truisms. To this day, it remains one of the best analyses of how political parties work in the United States and why the American electoral system is different even from other two-party dominated systems like the United Kingdom and Canada. The article stresses the importance of program, political education, and deliberative membership democracy. Crucially, these are expressed not as “good ideas” but as the mutually-binding forces which can form, in effect, a political party. It also points out how the Democratic Party itself has very little influence on how Democratic primaries function, and so wherever the Democratic Party has neglected its base, socialists can fill in the gaps and run in Democratic primaries. …a national political organization that would have chapters at the state and local levels, a binding program, a leadership accountable to its members, and electoral candidates nominated at all levels throughout the country. As a nationwide organization, it would have a national educational apparatus, recognized leaders and spokespeople at the national level, and its candidates and other activities would come under a single, nationally recognized label. And, of course, all candidates would be required to adhere to the national platform. This is a piece of serious, worthwhile political analysis. However, over the years certain deficiencies in the article have been revealed, but they often do not come up in conversations about the article within DSA. For instance, while Ackerman writes at length about how the Democratic Party doesn’t actually have any input on how Democratic primaries are run, he gives little attention to the Democratic caucuses within city councils, state legislatures, or more importantly in Congress and the Senate. These are some of the biggest ways in which the Democratic Party can exert a considerable amount of direct control over elected officials, and undermine the accountability Socialists In Office are supposed to have to our organization. Probably the most high-profile instance of this happening was in Indianapolis, where the city council’s Democratic caucus purged an at-the-time DSA city councilor, Jesse Brown. While the Democratic caucus did not explain his expulsion, councilor Brown would clarify in his first interview after his expulsion that he believed it was related to his opposition to the Democratic Party’s plan to privatize public schools. On the congressional level, Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (AOC) has been notable for being one of the few congresspersons to pay dues to the Democratic Party . This is not at all a requirement: many congresspersons and even other DSA members have never paid dues. However, AOC is able to get better committee assignments with the payment of dues. This relationship between her and the Democratic Party has resulted in many seeing AOC as more aligned with the Democratic Party than she is with DSA. Crucially, the article leaves out important details when it describes the downfall of a major attempt at a third party prior to DSA, the Labor Party during the 1990’s . The article makes the argument that the Labor Party (LP) failed due to the implications of building a third-party ballot-line. This narrative of alternative ballot-lines always leading to the same outcome for many becomes a sort of secret law, where all third-party ballot-lines are doomed to reproduce the marginality of the Green Party. Conveniently, this means that the question of political strategy never comes up in these conversations. In a 2002 article in Socialist Alternative’s newspaper, Justice , “ Labor Party’s 3rd National Convention ”, comrade Ramy Khalil published his experience as a participant in the Labor Party. In “Blueprint for a New Party,” Ackerman mentions that the Labor Party was scared to set up challenges to Democratic politicians who might be in favor of labor, but does little to critique this, and this observation is eclipsed by the arguments on the ballot-line question. The assumption gave rise to an intractable dilemma: if the party took a separate line and ran candidates against incumbent Democrats, it would destroy relationships with Democratic officeholders who might otherwise be sympathetic to unions, and thus lose the support of the unions that depended on those officeholders. Khalil offers a more comprehensive critique of the LP leadership’s strategy, citing key events such as the forceful disbandment of the Labor Party’s New York Metro chapter: A key turning point was the shutting down of the New York Metropolitan chapter — the largest, most vibrant chapter in the country with over 1,000 members. The LP Interim National Council turned a blind eye when the NY State LP body disbanded the local chapter because Socialist Alternative members had been elected into the leadership of the chapter and were preparing to run local LP candidates. Khalil also places emphasis on the Labor Party’s weakness when confronted with the war on Afghanistan, as well as a political inability to initiate a broad, open discussion when confronted with the need for the LP to take a different approach from the AFL-CIO tops: The LP should have taken a principled stand by condemning the horrific terrorist attacks of 9/11 but also explaining how Bush’s war in no way represents the interests of workers and will only exacerbate terrorism. A fighting workers’ party would win support by standing in elections and opposing the two parties’ identical agenda of budget cuts, attacks on democratic rights, racism, and war. If the Labor Party is unable to maintain an independent working-class position in times of war, then it wouldn’t be able to withstand the enormous pressures to compromise with big business if it were to get candidates elected to office. On the AFL-CIO leaders and the LP’s dependence on them, Many left-wing union officials endorsed or affiliated to the LP on paper. Yet they refused to allow the LP to run candidates because if it did, they knew AFL-CIO President John Sweeney would have declared war on the LP and the union officials who supported it. A Labor Party would have to seize this opportunity to open up a debate in the labor movement, from the rank-and-file on up, on why the AFL-CIO continues to waste members’ dues on the same Democratic Party that gave us NAFTA, the WTO, and other attacks on labor. As LP polls have indicated, there is more support for a labor party than the Democrats or Republicans. Instead, LP leader Tony Mazzocchi’s strategy was to avoid this inevitable clash with the AFL-CIO leaders by getting a significant number of labor leaders to endorse the LP before running candidates. With this account in mind, it becomes clear that the narrative around the ballot-line question does not cover the entire story of the Labor Party. It also shines a light on how DSA itself has been able to avoid certain pitfalls that the Labor Party failed to navigate. DSA’s democratic character and relative independence from the existing trade union leaders enabled its transformation through the mass influx of new political layers during and after the Sanders campaign of 2016. This shift was signified by the 2017 National Convention’s break with the Socialist International and declared support for the Boycott, Divestment, and Sanctions campaign against Israeli oppression of the Palestinian people. The LP, on the other hand, did not take the political initiative to run candidates (on any ballot-line) that could capture the energy of the anti-globalization or anti-war movements of its time, and was instead eclipsed by Ralph Nader’s presidential campaign in 2000. DSA’s organizational strengths, independent message, and prolonged electoral efforts have enabled it to put a crack in the influence of the long-dominant nonprofit and labor-liberal left, as evidenced by DSA member Claire Valdez’s victory over Working Families Party candidate Antonio Reynoso in the New York City’s congressional primary elections. Overall, while Ackerman’s article has many strengths in emphasizing the party-like structures of program and education, deliberative membership democracy, and elected accountability as crucial to a political party even without the official legal recognition of a political party, its evasion of wider strategic debates and lack of emphasis on direct means ofDemocratic Party control are important weaknesses. The fight for working-class political independence today requires socialists to take steps beyond the party-surrogate model. Today, advocates of the party surrogate within DSA such as the Groundwork (GW) and Socialist Majority (SMC) caucuses claim to carry on the strategy advocated by Ackerman. In spite of this, their work in practice has entailed important departures from the more radically independent vision laid out in “Blueprint for a New Party.” Within SMC for instance, two articles were published with competing views on the so-called “dirty stay,” the notion that DSA can or should utilize the party surrogate tactic indefinitely. Both articles ultimately come to the conclusion that DSA as a political force must put its weight towards an alliance with the “progressive” wing of the Democratic Party, manifested in the nonprofit left and labor liberalism. SMC member David Duhalde wrote explicitly in his post-election article “Stay Dirty”: For DSA, I especially want us to stop pretending we are building a third-party when a better and more honest orientation already exists, one in which we are fostering a left-wing faction within the Democratic Party, the party which most members will gravitate towards anyways. In another article , Genevieve R writes that it is not possible to be politically independent and states outright: Speaking as someone with years of experience working with New York’s Socialists in Office, that description could not possibly be more wrong. SIOs—virtually always a small minority of a given legislature—spend nearly all their time either coordinating to get enough Democrats to vote with them to win legislative battles, or responding to corporate Democrats with opposition in news cycles and elections. While indeed, Socialists in Office would communicate in some manner with others in their legislatures, attempting to win support for our legislative priorities does not imply that DSA does not exercise political independence. NYC-DSA has made use of movement pressure in numerous campaigns, and Zohran and others have spoken to the Democratic Party leaders’ hostility to the NY State bloc of SIOs. However, the logic of the left-labor-liberal alliance presents major problems for NYC-DSA’s strategy. Orienting towards such a cross-class or “popular front” style bloc has entailed major limits to deliberative democracy within the chapter and an unwillingness to allow membership critique of electeds. The chapter also often struggles to articulate a wider political vision beyond the demands in its immediate campaigns. NYC-DSA has no local platform or program, and a major disconnect exists between the chapter and the national organization. This is the consequence of a pragmatist and reformist strategy that holds winning the aforementioned immediate demands as the primary aim of the socialist movement. In common DSA parlance, this is called “taking state power”… … “Taking state power” on this basis requires the governing body to manage the interests of the state and the ruling class. “Compromise,” within a reformist approach, begins to mean something different than taking the best settlement when there’s no other option. Failing to navigate these contradictions effectively can create crises of political credibility … The obstacles to member democracy in NYC-DSA are the necessary outgrowth of this strategic orientation. This approach has also meant that working-class people are seen as a constituency to be mobilized rather than a social force capable of acting in their own name. It results in a politics of preemptive compromise, where the socialist movement makes concessions not on our terms but on those of the ruling class. A Contemporary of the Patriot Act and the War on Terror ICE is formally called the Bureau of Immigration and Customs Enforcement. With their current budget at $8 billion, their stated purpose is to enforce federal laws concerning immigration and customs, including illegal imports such as narcotics, laundered money, and sex exploitation materials. At the time of this writing, ICE has more than 20,000 agents in more than 400 offices in the United States and elsewhere. Although agencies and legislation predating ICE performed many of its current functions, the period succeeding the September 11 attacks on the World Trade Center and Pentagon marked the beginning of a more centralized approach to immigration control. Its authority comes principally from the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA ) and later amendments enacted from the 1980s onward. The history of ICE is therefore best understood as the history of U.S. immigration enforcement gradually moving from a relatively centralized immigration bureaucracy into a large national-security and law-enforcement system involving federal, state, and local governments. President George Walker (George W.) Bush signed the Homeland Security Act on November 25, 2002. The Department of Homeland Security (DHS), of which ICE is a constituent part, began operations on March 1, 2003. The United States federal government immigration machinery is divided into three parts: the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS), which handles immigration benefits, citizenship, green cards, asylum applications and petitions; Customs and Border Patrol (CBP), which handles borders, ports of entry, border patrol and customs inspection; and ICE, which handles interior immigration enforcement, detention/removal and major criminal investigations. ICE is therefore not the only federal government agency which handles immigration-related matters, but is rather an aliquot part of immigration and customs law enforcement infrastructure whose operations are internal relative to the services focused on enforcing international boundaries, and coercive relative to documentation and application related services. Under the George W. Bush administration, ICE would perform massive workplace raids and detainment procedures often including hundreds of people at a time. ICE’s early activity involved arresting individuals who already had final removal orders, seeking out persons who had likely probable cause for arrest and detainment, and issuing deportation flights. The Barack Obama administration would de-emphasize mass workplace arrests in favor of prioritizing individuals perceived to be criminal and national-security hazards. The first Donald Trump administration would follow the Bush model, the Joseph Biden administration would follow the Obama model, and the second Trump administration would follow the Bush model, creating a firm line of demarcation between Republican and Democrat regarding this matter. As an organization also involved in customs, ICE would also engage in investigations concerning money laundering, export violations, commercial fraud, and international criminal organizations. Given the corrupt state of the US federal government, it is easy to imagine the potential for fraudulent behavior on ICE’s part with the combined immigration and customs law enforcement powers. What ICE Actually Does Although the corporate media mainly focuses on the enforcement and removal aspect of ICE to sensationalize violence for the purpose of profiting from domestic bloodshed, that is not the only purpose allocated to ICE by the federal government. As a core constituent of the DHS, ICE has a deep and highly involved organizational infrastructure that provides it with wide immigration and customs related powers. The coercive law enforcement powers of ICE depend on a comprehensive intelligence apparatus which allows them to collect migrant-related data and direct violent action against individuals they determine to be in violation of federal law as it relates to immigration and customs. There are officially four departments within the ICE organizational structure: Homeland Security Investigations (HSI), which is the intelligence apparatus involved in criminal investigations related to immigration and customs; Enforcement and Removal Operations (ERO), which is the coercive apparatus which is involved in the physical arrest, detainment, and deportation procedures; Office of the Principal Legal Advisor (OPLA), which ensures legal reconciliation and compliance within the overall ICE structure; and Management and Administration (M&A), the supervisory directorate which orchestrates the operation of the other three departments to ensure their mutual cooperation. Only the ERO and HSI will be discussed. The ICE that you actually witness on corporate media committing unwarranted acts of violence against innocent people within United States borders are under the directive control of the ERO. This section of ICE agents concerns itself with immigration arrests, detention, removal proceedings, deportation logistics, fugitive operations and coordination with jails. It includes the armed federal police that physically enforce federal government authority within its specifically defined role. They’re involved in the 287(g) related federal-state cooperation procedures that allow state governments to perform immigration and customs related law enforcement operations on their behalf. What separates ICE from a simple police force, however, is its intelligence apparatus. The HIS deals with criminal investigations involving trafficking, smuggling, narcotics, money laundering, customs violations, child exploitation, export controls, cybercrime and other transnational offenses. They’re the ones who decide which migrants in the US are involved in affairs sufficiently sordid to justify coercive measures seeking their detainment and/or removal from the country. They also investigate private employers and property managers to verify the presence of undocumented or otherwise illegal individuals so that probable cause for arrest can be procured. Although agents associated with the ELO are the ones who define the typical mental image of ICE, the agency would be much less effective at violating human rights in America were it not for HIS. How ICE Impacts Humanity Although many of its proponents’ claims are exaggerated, ICE has incidentally performed some social functions. Very few people would fault ICE for capturing foreigners involved in narcotics, human trafficking, or child predation. There is, however, fault in attempting to justify the entire operation of ICE on account of this alone. As is common practice in America, these actual, real-life problems have been used as a justification for far more broadly encompassing control measures on people living in the country while certain non-foreign individuals outside ICE’s authority are allowed to continue those exact same involvements. The American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) has identified numerous violations committed by ICE in the name of law enforcement. Human rights abuses including but not limited to racial profiling, breaches of due process, excessive use of force, and operation in sensitive locations such as schools, courthouses, and places of religious worship have been committed by ICE. Many in ICE custody are denied medical care for chronic conditions. Its facilities provide squalid, crowded living conditions where detainees are often subjected to physical and sexual abuse by protective staff. Prolonged and improper use of restraints and solitary confinement are leveled against vulnerable detainees, often those who suffer from mental illness. Numerous deaths have been reported in ICE detainment camps which have been investigated by separate government entities. Let’s not mince words here. ICE has been directly responsible for innocent bloodshed across the country. It’s been heavily documented, and news of it has been widely broadcast by the corporate news media. Perfectly decent hard-working people have been forcibly removed from their communities and places of employment for the sake of domestic peacekeeping. Families have been torn apart, lives have been destroyed, and the American taxpayer has been the one to cover the bill. This is nothing but a heinous affront to the working class of the world. The lie that ICE is needed to keep local communities safe from violent crime and illegal narcotics has been costly to the entire human race. This relic of post-9/11 hysteria and government duplicity needs to be abolished. The call to action is this: within the confines of the law, demoralize ICE agents wherever you find them, protest any legislation which involves state or local cooperation with ICE of any description, and provide any vulnerable members of the community with the necessary support to avoid violent ICE reprisal. This is the work that any decent member of society should do to combat ICE and fetter the constant oppression of the common laborer by the capitalist establishment. The Empire Has No Borders

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 / 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Socialist Forum ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-16T16:22:19+00:00

For well over a century, Americans have fiercely debated U.S. immigration policy: its merits, flaws, limits, and implications. In recent decades, it has become a central question in electoral and identity politics, largely responsible for the current state of political discourse. Conservatives have long argued that the nation has “lost control” of its borders and frequently decry what they deem as “open borders.” On the other hand, liberals lament what they perceive as a dysfunctional bureaucracy incapable of reconciling humanitarian obligations with the realities of a globalized economic order. Even critics of mass deportation and anti-immigrant dehumanization campaigns begin from the premise that the system has failed to accomplish what it was designed to do . In reality, the opposite is true. Unauthorized migration is not evidence of a broken immigration system; it is one of its defining features. Undocumented migration has persisted despite massive and sustained investment in walls, surveillance, detention, deportation, and legislation designed to stem the flow of “illegal” immigration and strip migrants of access to even the few basic social safety nets that remain after decades of austerity. These policies tell us less about the state’s inability to enforce immigration laws than about their real purpose. For socialists to effectively respond to this system, we must first engage in a systemic re-examination of what immigration policy does, its origins, and its implications. The dominant narrative assumes that the state’s role is primarily to stop the flow of “illegal” immigration. Conservatives and liberals largely disagree only about the degree and the mechanisms that should be employed: Should enforcement be harsher or more permissive? Should legal immigration be expanded or reduced? Should asylum be more accessible or further restricted? These are important questions, but they obscure a more fundamental reality: migration has never been something the United States has tried to eliminate. It is instead something that the U.S. and global economies necessarily produce, demand, and manage. The roots of mass (unauthorized) migration lie in the nation’s history of capitalist development, globalization, and imperialism dating back to the nineteenth century—with implications that remain woefully unexamined, let alone resolved. Undocumented workers occupy a unique position within American capitalism. They harvest crops, process meat, clean hotels, build infrastructure, staff restaurants, care for children and the elderly, and perform essential labor that keeps the economy functioning, consumer prices low, and capital flows stable and predictable. When labor shortages arise, these workers are welcomed. But when economic downturns and crises arise, they are blamed for conditions they neither created nor control. They are, as one journalist put it, “essential but unwanted.” From this perspective, immigration enforcement is less a failed attempt at exclusion and more a remarkably successful regime for governing and disciplining labor. We need not imagine some vast, secret conspiracy among employers, politicians, and border agents. This entire process plays out—and has always played out—in broad daylight. Nor does it require ignoring the competing interests involved: employers have always sought unfettered access to cheap, disposable labor; enforcement agencies have demanded (and received) ever-ballooning budgets and terrifying levels of plenary power; politicians have exploited economic and cultural anxieties by treating migrants as scapegoats in order to win elections without addressing structural inequalities and the material conditions of their constituents. Socialists and labor unions have oscillated between exclusion and solidarity. The system that emerges from these tensions nevertheless coalesces and capitulates to capitalist logic, even if immigration policy was never fully planned or perfectly coherent. At first glance, this logic may appear contradictory. If American employers depend so heavily on immigrant labor, why does the government deploy such a robust immigration enforcement regime and spend billions trying to stop it? The question is reasonable, but embedded in it is the assumption that immigration enforcement exists to shut down unsanctioned migration, to eliminate it altogether. In reality, enforcement serves to regulate flows and to guarantee the presence of a useful but precarious workforce—always available to employers but invariably vulnerable to detention, deportation, and exclusion. This vulnerability suppresses workers’ collective bargaining power while allowing politicians to claim that they are securing the border and preventing some imagined “invasion.” The result is not that migration stops, but that it is carefully managed: providing enough workers to satisfy labor demand and at the same time, enough enforcement to preserve the illusion of border control and the distinction between deserving citizens and “illegal” workers whose status and presence always remains conditional. The case of Mexico The U.S.-Mexico border itself is a product of conquest, settler colonialism, and imperial expansion. The “line” separating the United States and Mexico did not emerge from some natural geographic feature, or for that matter, from differing cultures or economies. It was drawn and imposed through war. After its independence, the United States grew rapidly and ferociously at the expense of Black, indigenous, and immigrant populations. By the end of the 19 th century, the nation had expanded across the continent, eventually stretching from the Atlantic to the Pacific. The budding American empire then looked south to Mexico. After Mexico’s independence, Texas in particular posed a dilemma for the new nation: promising but underdeveloped land and few Mexicans to govern it or protect it from constant Indigenous raids, which were common at the time. The solution was to recruit Americans to settle, improve, and invest in the region and its land. Mexican authorities quickly became resentful of its American migrants: hundreds, then thousands of Americans heeded the call but rejected Mexican culture, refused to learn Spanish or embrace the predominant religion, or even migrate through legal channels. The irony, in hindsight, is breathtaking. In time, these arrivals, along with many of the Mexican citizens living there, developed their own sense of identity and in 1836, fought for independence from Mexico. That came to fruition when James K. Polk signed a joint resolution drafted and passed by Congress, officially admitting Texas to the union as its 28 th state in 1845. Tensions remained, as Mexico continued to claim Texas as part of its legitimate territory. Even when it reluctantly relinquished claims to the state, disagreements regarding the precise boundary of the new border lingered—with the U.S. claiming that Texas extended south to the Rio Grande and Mexico holding claims to the Nueces River further north. On April 25, 1846, Mexican soldiers engaged an American military patrol, killing eleven soldiers, wounding six, and capturing some fifty others. This was the justification Polk needed for an all-out assault, declaring that Mexico had “invaded our territory and shed American blood upon the American soil.” Congress declared war on May 13, 1846. Using its riches and overwhelming military might, the United States made quick work of the fragile Mexican army. In just two years, the assault left 25,000 Mexicans dead (some 13,000 Americans died during the war) and the Mexican military and political establishment humiliated. In exchange for peace and $15 million, Mexico ceded not only claims to Texas but all of what is now California, Nevada, and Utah, as well as large portions of current-day Arizona, New Mexico, and Colorado, as well as smaller portions of Wyoming, Kansas, and Oklahoma. Notably and critically, it also absorbed some 50,000-100,000 people who lived in those territories. For the United States, it was a windfall: it acquired over half of Mexican territory, which included rich land, natural resources, and economically enticing geography, all while limiting the number of brown people it had to accept into its newly drawn borders since the region remained sparsely populated. The U.S. conquest of northern Mexico redrew the political map of North America, even as it left intact longstanding economic and social relationships. Communities that had long existed as coherent social spaces suddenly found themselves divided by an international boundary. The border moved over people long before people moved across the border. Promised U.S. citizenship, these new Americans remained second-class citizens: their land claims denied, their culture attacked, their language suppressed, and their rights violated. While human mobility was restricted, capital crossed the newly created border with minimal friction. Railroads linked Mexican agricultural regions to American markets, U.S. investors acquired vast holdings in Mexican mining, agriculture, and transportation. The border allowed capital unrestricted mobility while creating artificial divisions of previously Mexican territories and communities. During the regime of Porfirio Diaz (1876-1911), foreign investment in Mexico reached $1.6 billion, a sum overwhelmingly controlled by U.S. interests (much of the rest belonging to Britain and France). The result was that Mexican workers increasingly found their livelihoods shaped by economic forces far removed from their local communities. Industrialization, commercial agriculture, and foreign investment transformed rural life, creating some new opportunities but displacing millions. By the end of the first decade of the twentieth century, Mexico had become a political pressure cooker. Vast wealth lined the pockets of the Diaz regime and foreign investors, even as peasants lost communal land and workers in factories, railroads, and mines faced increasingly dangerous working conditions, the loss of legal protections, and stagnating wages, leading to peasant revolts and ultimately a full-scale civil war. The Mexican Revolution left one million dead and led hundreds of thousands of survivors to flee the country and seek refuge in the United States. Migrants included ordinary workers, intellectuals, and leftist radicals, like the Magón Brothers—Ricardo, Enrique, and Jesús—who lived in exile in the United States, where they organized an international political movement, published the radical newspaper Regeneración , and worked with labor movements on both sides of the border, aiding the revolutionary effort from places like San Antonio, St. Louis, and Los Angeles, even as they faced severe political repression there themselves. After a decade of civil war, Mexican society was fundamentally transformed, replacing the Porfirian regime with a revolutionary state that claimed legitimacy through social reform, nationalism, and inclusion of workers and peasants in political life. Nevertheless, proximity and the entangled political economies of both countries would continue to complicate Mexico’s revolutionary promise for decades, and outmigration remained a central piece of the puzzle, primarily by serving as an “escape valve” for economic refugees, and an opportunity for capitalist exploitation, corruption, and systemic violence. Creating the Border By the 1920s, the U.S. had begun to institutionalize the border apparatus, one recognizable to us today. Nativism was far from new, of course. Earlier waves of nativism had targeted Irish and Chinese immigrants, but by this point, a coalition of eugenicists and anti-communists pushed for broad legal restrictions against foreigners, and after World War I, the U.S. passed a major piece of racist immigration legislation (“Johnson-Reed”) that drastically reduced the flow migrants from Asia as well as southern and eastern Europe. With the exception of the most radical industrial unions, like the IWW, most labor groups sided squarely with restrictionist factions, operating under the assumption that foreign workers diluted wages, weakened labor power, and prevented assimilation of immigrant workers—or worse, imported radical ideologies that would make the movement a target of Red Scare terror. Mexican migration was not, however, initially a major source of concern. Like the rest of the Hemisphere, Mexico was exempted from the 1924 quotas, not out of benevolence, but because agricultural interests relied on their cheap labor, which was largely circular and seasonal and mostly invisible to most Americans. However, attitudes toward Mexican migrants changed rapidly during the Great Depression as jobs became scarce and relief programs strained, making these workers prime political scapegoats. Governments—at both the federal and state/local levels responded by waging a mass “repatriation” campaign that resulted in the expulsion of some one million ethnic Mexicans, a majority of them U.S. citizens. The dynamics of Mexican migration evolved again during and after World War II. As the U.S. entered the fight against the Axis Powers, with over sixteen million men eventually fighting overseas, the home front faced a massive labor shortage, especially in agriculture. The solution was a bilateral agreement known as the Bracero Program, which imported hundreds of thousands, eventually millions of men (the program was not available to women) to work in that sector, along with some others (especially railroads). Alongside the Braceros came larger numbers of undocumented, unsanctioned workers representing roughly twice the number of contracted workers. The program, which was initially slated to end at the conclusion of the war, did not in fact end then. Instead, it was formalized when the Korean War began. In time, those who “saved the crops” became the targets of nativist ire. By the early 1950s, some one million Mexican workers, most of them unsanctioned, were thought to be working in the United States. The nativist panic that ensued, driven by the recession of 1953 and by racist fantasies about the perceived dangers of Mexican men, prompted the government to respond, but it did so in ways that revealed in stark terms the true nature and function of immigration policy. With the backing of restrictionists, white supremacists, and even the leading Mexican American civic groups who viewed Mexican migration as a threat to assimilation and labor organizing, the U.S. launched a mass deportation campaign, dubbed “Operation Wetback.” Officially, the U.S. deported some one million people. But this was starkly different from repatriation in the 1930s: first of all, the entire undertaking was little more than a publicity stunt. As historians Kelly Lyttle Hernández, Ramon Garciá, and others have noted, the vast majority of those expelled were voluntary departures or deportations to border areas where migrants were given a proverbial wink and access to a quick return. Again, illustrating the ways in which migration control served the interests of capital, the 1952 Immigration and Nationality Act had made it a felony to smuggle, transport or harbor unauthorized workers, but thanks to pressure and influence from Texas growers, the “Texas Proviso” loophole inserted into the law shielded employers (nationwide, not just in Texas) from prosecution. It allowed authorities and employers to collaborate in remarkably brazen, corrupt ways: many “illegals” were summarily “paroled” and given Bracero permits, sometimes on the spot. Others, officially deported, were recruited again as Braceros almost immediately. The entire operation, in other words, was designed to give employers near-total control of labor flows while selling a vision of law and order to the American public. Family Reconciliation: Myth and Reality The Bracero program finally ended in 1964, after pressure from civil rights leaders, Mexican American intellectuals, and labor organizers who rightly concluded that the use of “semi-slaves” as the leading Mexican American labor organizer Ernesto Galraza called them due to their status as effectively rightless “guest” laborers, made labor organizing impossible. That same year, Congress passed the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1964 (“Hart-Celler” named after its main sponsors, Michigan Senator Philip A. Hart, a Democrat and New York Congressman Emanuel Celler, a Republican). It eliminated the blatantly racist underpinnings of the national origins act of 1924 so as not to embarrass the United States in its alleged crusade for freedom in the Cold War against the Soviet Union. One of its stated goals was family reunification. However, the new immigration regime created massive obstacles for Mexican (and more broadly, Latin American) immigrants, as they now faced limits on legal migration and a tiered “preference” system. Skilled workers and those with immediate relatives found a relatively easy path to legal status, but millions of ordinary workers without family ties to the United States seeking work in the country had to do so illegally. Backlogs grew consistently throughout the decades , even as the number of undocumented people did too. By the 1970s, some one million unsanctioned laborers were thought to be in the country again, and a new nativist wave dominated the political response, in the process further exposing the flaws and contradictions of capitalist logic. The Resistance to capitalist illogic While nativists and restrictionists sought a hardline approach against migration, employers insisted that they were entitled to hire foreign labor, claiming that Americans refused to do the work that immigrants did. Although still taken as gospel among pundits and observers, the cliché that “immigrants do the work Americans won’t do,” was patently false. The reality was that Americans refused to do the work as cheaply and under the same precarious conditions as immigrants. At the same time, left-wing organizers, including many leading Mexican Americans—once fiercely opposed to immigrant labor—were rethinking established assumptions and strategies. Labor activists like Miguel Pendas and Humberto “Bert” Corona, both socialists based in California, argued that playing by capital’s rules could never yield gains for workers. What if, they asked, instead of seeking restriction and criminalization, undocumented workers could be organized? What if the entire logic of border politics was fundamentally flawed and anti-worker? If capital could ignore national boundaries in pursuit of profits, they reasoned, why shouldn’t workers across borders also see themselves as “one people without borders”? Only trans- and international solidarity could alleviate the problems facing the working class. The most radical immigrant rights activists, like the Chicano restauranter and convicted gun runner Mario Cantú suggested that the system as it existed rewarded capital and punished workers on both sides of the border: Mexican authorities could simply push poverty out of sight and out of mind by encouraging its most impoverished populations to migrate north, even as those workers were used to suppress labor gains in the U.S. For Cantú and other revolutionary thinkers, nothing short of armed rebellion in Mexico could solve the problem. Other activists remained more focused on internal and more gradual political solutions, even as they looked abroad for models of transnational solidarity. In Europe, where “irregular” migration had also become a hot-button issue, some of the more militant unions had taken a different approach to migration. Switzerland’s Clothing and Leather Workers Union, France’s General Conference of Labor, and the French Democratic Federation of Labor adopted the then-novel position that migration and its ill effects on workers was the result of “super-exploitation” and argued that immigrants should be approached as “an integral part of the working class” and protected and integrated into the class struggle. The logic of the immigration debate, they contended, had to be flipped on its head: by deliberately taking advantage of the most vulnerable workers—often immigrants—employers found it easier to undermine organizing efforts and to suppress wages and efforts at improved working conditions. Conversely, a united front of workers, foreign and domestic, could produce a meaningful opposition to exploitation and abuse. In the U.S., most labor unions remained unconvinced that embracing the undocumented was prudent. The United Farm Workers Union, led by the now-disgraced Cesar Chavez, continued through the early 1970s to oppose immigrant rights. Under Chavez’s leadership, the union regularly called for raids, deportations, and restrictions—at one point even creating its own (poorly trained, ineffective) border patrol (the so-called “wet line”) to detain and turn over migrants to immigration authorities. Only after intense criticism and pressure from Chicano leaders and radical organizers, did the union shift its position in 1975, with Chavez passively admitting that “mistakes” had been made “in the heat of the struggle.” Its reversal was less a result of altruism and moral reflection than a recognition of the changing realities in the fields. By that time, undocumented workers made up a huge proportion—in some regions a majority—of the workforce. If the union was to represent anyone, it would have to accept that those workers were unsanctioned. Other unions, however, remained recalcitrant. The AFL-CIO had begun to soften its anti-immigrant rhetoric, and signs appeared that change was in the air, as when the federation voted, for the first time in 1980, on whether to adopt a more inclusive posture. Immigrant rights activists, however, lost the vote. George Meany and his associates remained unwilling to reverse course for some time. The AFL-CIO only officially took a more explicitly pro-immigrant stance in the year 2000. The Politics of immigration Through the 1980s, policymakers responded to mass migration by seeking new reforms that attempted to ease the contradictions of the immigration regime without fundamentally restructuring its foundations. It was under Jimmy Carter that the concept of “compressive immigration reform” first took shape. Carter established the Select Commission on Immigration and Refugee Policy in 1978, tapping Ted Hesburgh (then president of the University of Notre Dame and a universally respected figure) to lead it. The commission studied the issue extensively and presented its recommendations in 1981 , suggesting a three-pronged approach to immigration reform: increased enforcement to satisfy restrictionists, the creation of an agricultural guest worker program akin to the Bracero system to appease employers, and amnesty for undocumented people already in the country designed to gain favor with the growing and increasingly pro-immigrant Hispanic lobby. With the bipartisan sponsorship of Alan Simpson, the Republican senator from Wyoming and Romano Mazzoli, the Democratic Congressman from Kentucky, Ronald Reagan endorsed the legislative package. Congress, after several failed attempts, finally passed the law in 1986, with Reagan promising that it would “improve the lives of a class of individuals who now must hide in the shadows, without access to many of the benefits of a free and open society” while also “[increasing] enforcement of the immigration laws.” However much Reagan depicted the law as a grand solution, it did little to resolve the contradictions embedded in liberal capitalist logic. Migration continued, and so did nativist politics. With huge numbers of undocumented workers arriving in California in particular, restrictionists ferociously attacked immigrant workers and families there. A group of restrictionists, led by state assemblyman Dick Mountjoy, former INS commissioner Alan Nelson, former regional director of the INS Harold Ezell, and Orange County accountant Ron Prince, crafted a harsh and punitive ballot initiative, Proposition 187, that sought to deny undocumented people and their families access to social services, including education and healthcare. Few imagined that the law would prevent migration or do anything about the number of immigrants already in the state. Instead, it embraced the logic of the libertarian economist Milton Friendman, who some decades prior had outlined perhaps the most honest assessment of how free market fundamentalists viewed the function of immigration policies and priorities. Asked in 1978 whether he, as a libertarian, supported more open borders, Friedman had replied that “Mexican immigration . . . is a good thing. It’s a good thing for the illegal immigrants. It’s a good thing for the United States. It’s a good thing for the citizens of the country. But it’s only good as long as it’s illegal.” In other words, the economic contributions of immigrants were invaluable, but only if they did not expect anything in return from the society that extracted their labor. Proposition 187 was popular and its proponents wildly successful at the ballot box, with voters supporting it by a two-to-one margin. Organizers quickly mobilized to wage legal battles, and within a few years, the law’s provisions were struck down as unconstitutional due to its brazenly discriminatory, racist cornerstones. But anti-immigrant attacks and the paradoxes of immigration control did not fade away. Neither was it purely a right-wing cause; it was broadly bipartisan. If Prop 187 failed to achieve its draconian goals, immigration control continued by other means, with the full and enthusiastic support of the liberal-Democratic political establishment. Under Bill Clinton, Operation Gatekeeper increased the number of border patrol agents from 4,200 in 1994 to almost 10,000 in 2000 and doubled the border budget from $400 million to $800 between 1993 and 1997 (by 2010, it had reached $7.6 billion). Tighter security in busy (urban) crossing areas funneled migrants toward more dangerous crossing paths, resulting in the deaths of several thousand border crossers over the next several years. At the same time, the United States continued to foster illegal migration through its disastrous imperial foreign policy. “Free trade” initiatives, most notably the North American Free Trade Agreement flooded agricultural Mexico’s markets and displaced Mexican farmers. The lingering effects of military interventions and support for anti-democratic regimes in Central America (among them Guatemala, El Salvador, Nicaragua, and Panama) over the previous decades, and the parallel development of what historian Adam Goodman calls a “deportation machine,” led to political instability, mass poverty, and the growth of gang violence in those countries. Naturally, that motivated ordinary Central Americans to seek an escape from these conditions—ironically but revealingly, into the very country that had caused those conditions. The more migrants the United States produced, the more it fed anti-immigrant sentiment at home, and the more the U.S. government responded with harsh restrictions, thus creating and recreating a vicious and inhumane but entirely predictable cycle. And if Prop 187 had failed the constitutional test, its architects played the long game. In fact, there is ample evidence that their goals were far more ideological than legal. By positioning aggrieved Americans as the victims of not only migrant populations but also an unresponsive state, they built a robust nativist machine and movement that demonized immigrants and blamed them for economic and social woes. Ron Prince, who financially ruined himself funding Prop 187, suggested in 1996, while the case was still being heard in federal courts, that the entire episode proved “we were right” and promised to be “the ACLU of the 21 st century.” In this reactionary environment, further exacerbated by the post-9/11 “war on terror” and by liberalism’s failure to offer a compelling economic response to globalization’s disastrous effects on workers, the rise of Donald Trump and his reactionary “Make America Great Again” movement in the twenty-first century was as predictable as it was tragic. In response, immigrant rights activists have continued to wage a courageous fight over the years: in the streets, in courtrooms, and notably, in workplaces. Most labor unions have reversed their historical aversion to organizing undocumented workers: the United Farm Workers Union, though a shell of its former self, often downplays its anti-immigrant history. The now weakened AFL-CIO fully embraces “immigration reform.” And unions representing economic sectors with a high concentration of immigrant workers, like the Service Employees International Union, have taken a decidedly pro-immigrant stance. Still, too often, these groups fall into the trap of framing the immigration system as broken, as a national problem, and as something that can be understood and addressed apart from its bloody imperial history. Mere pro-immigrant rhetoric that insists the system must be “reformed” offers little chance of addressing, let alone countering, the true function of immigration control, which is worker discipline and the dismantling of transnational, multi-ethnic worker solidarity. It’s a moment for both hope and hesitation: recent victories in Michigan, Colorado, Minnesota, and New York suggest gathering political momentum on the left. Yet a defeat in Wisconsin and a judge’s temporary stay of New York City’s pied-à-terre tax offer reminders that every movement for social progress breeds its own countervailing reaction. But how does that reaction itself build power? The scaffolding for backlash, as the New York City decision suggests, is often found in the legal system.Steep barriers to court access as well as the formidably technical nature of many legal disputes mean that insiders commonly have the ability not merely to secure the outcomes they want, but also to shape the rules under which notionally fair competition unfolds. This “playing for rules,” rather than for specific results, means that law often offers the inside track for incumbents’ ground-rigging projects.Across seemingly unrelated legal developments, lawyers and judges have been pursuing such projects of late. Each change is leading to a concentration of power in electoral politics and economic power, in ways that insulate insiders. And the effects accumulate: they feed each other, leading to a vicious circle of increasingly obdurate concentrations of power.Until Francesca Hong’s defeat this week, it was possible to write off the central organs of the Democratic Party as wholly incapacitated by debt and internal dysfunction under the leadership of Ken Martin’s hapless Democratic National Committee (DNC). Yet the institutional party has lately gained two tools that give it greater leverage, if not to win general elections then at least to police ideological purity at the primary stage.The first is a newfound power for partisan gerrymandering. This is a gift from our antidemocratic Supreme Court, which has anointed partisan advantage as a justification in mapmaking while dismantling the Voting Rights Act’s protections for minorities. To see the consequences, consider what unfolded earlier this year in California. Responding to Republican gerrymanders in Texas and Florida, Governor Gavin Newsom pushed through a referendum and then a new congressional map. Its lines were drawn by a “veteran Democratic redistricting expert in Sacramento” in “consultation” with the state’s Democratic congressional delegation. In court, the state’s lawyers successfully defended the map on the ground that it protected Democratic insiders’ seats.However justified California’s response to the Texas and Florida gerrymanders may be as a matter of national politics, it illustrates an unexpected effect of the new partisan gerrymandering: It is inevitably incumbent insiders who draw the maps. Just as they can be expected to draw out their opponents across the aisle, so too they can be expected to hobble challengers from the margin. Gerrymandering does not just make general elections less competitive; it also allows the locking out of challengers at the primary stage.The institutional party has gained further thanks to a July Supreme Court decision striking down the federal campaign finance prohibition on coordinated spending by parties for candidates. The anti-coordination measure prevented parties from becoming conduits for dark money. The decision likely helps the Republicans in the short term, but in the medium term bolsters both parties’ insiders at the expense of outsiders. To be sure, there is no assurance that Martin’s DNC will be able to capitalize on this new leverage this cycle: power matters only if you are competent enough to use it. But Democratic incompetency seems unlikely to endure forever.At the same time that the law is making the political landscape less competitive, a series of corporate law developments are concentrating power in the commanding heights of the data economy. According to the official story of American capitalism, a corporation with publicly traded equity is strongly responsive to its shareholders. Its original technicians took pride in how their design choices ensured that minority shareholders couldn’t be exploited by managers or controlling shareholders. At least in its own telling, corporate law diffuses concentrated power, and so enables collective choice via market discipline.Yet over the last two decades, venture capitalists in Silicon Valley have perfected corporate governance tools to circumvent even the residue of shareholder primacy. Their main instrument has been something called the dual-class share. Elon Musk’s SpaceX, the world’s largest ever initial public offering (IPO), provides an example.SpaceX’s public offering sold one kind of equity, called Class A shares, to the public. Each share gets one vote. But there are also super-voting Class B shares, with ten votes per share. As a result of his concentrated ownership of Class B shares, Musk currently owns about 40 percent of the company’s equity capital while controlling about 80 percent of the voting rights. And the company has bound itself to selling Class B shares only to Musk and Musk-related entities in the future. In effect, the dual-class structure locks in Musk’s control — and eviscerates ordinary shareholders’ power — forever.Dual-class shares aren’t the only legal trick for shoring up corporate insiders’ power. OpenAI and Anthropic alike use complex governance devices — a nonprofit holding company and a long-term trust — to much the same effect. As they follow SpaceX into the public equity markets, expect the founders of those firms to preserve near-total control of those companies. The result will be that the most important and wealthiest public companies in the United States will be under the almost total control of a small coterie of hyperwealthy founders, one that can comfortably fit in a conference room. The concentration of power over the economy will be immense.Dual-class shares aren’t new. As David Kampmann has brilliantly documented, the rise of dual-class shares started in 2004 with Google’s IPO; Facebook then followed a similar route in 2012. Their success led to a wider acceptance of a “founder control” model of corporate governance. What’s different today is the fact that dual-class firms now occupy not a niche, but the most profitable and consequential sector of the US economy.These two trends are closely connected. Contrary to some public narratives, campaign financing over the past two decades has not been the province of mainly large corporations. Rather, it has been a handful of “megadonors” — including, of course, Musk — who have increasingly dominated political spending. The durable concentrations of equity created by dual-class shares can serve as collateral for such donors, enabling them to borrow profligately for their pet political projects. This is a strategy known as “buy, borrow, die,” commonly used by the hyperrich to create liquid wealth even as they hold on to equity, then passing it on to heirs under a relatively sparing tax regime.Founders thus become political rainmakers, while their heirs retain both a firm hold on the corporate heights and the ability to translate that corporate control into political hegemony. The potential for dynastic control, over and above the forces that ensure familial transmission of wealth and privilege in the ordinary course of things, is obvious.The legal architectures of concentrated economic and political power are joined at the hip. This functional link between esoteric details of campaign finance law and corporate governance is easy to miss. Even legal scholars tend to look at these fields in isolation, missing their connections. Yet to grasp the scale and vector of contemporary reaction, it’s necessary to understand how the law matters to the Left’s political projects. Only an exacting accounting of the subtle filaments by which it joists economic and political power illuminates the stakes for what they truly are.

The Dangerous Rise of Victimhood Nationalism

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Kap Seol ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-16T13:56:43+00:00

South Korea has strong traditions of class struggle and left-wing political organization. Unfortunately, this has generally not been reflected in the development of Korean radical theory, which has tended to be heavily derivative of work published in other countries and languages. Lim Jie-hyun’s book Victimhood Nationalism is a welcome exception to this rule. Lim is a historian at Sogang University in Seoul, and his book is a genuinely original contribution from a country that has long relied on intellectual imports. It draws on case studies from Poland, Germany, Israel, Korea, and Japan to illustrate a phenomenon that is becoming increasingly familiar as its champions lay claim to a status of “hereditary victimhood.” Between Perpetrator and Victim He defines the concept of “victimhood nationalism” (not the nationalism of victims) around which the book is structured as “a narrative template to grant moral superiority and political legitimacy to a present nation.” For Lim, victimhood nationalism involves a dual process of de-historicization and over-historicization. Historical facts are cleansed of their complexity and refashioned to serve the nationalist needs of a country. Along the way, victimhood nationalism objectifies and dehumanizes the victims of past injustices. Collective memory is no longer about collective reflection: it has become a historical asset to be used in the pursuit of contemporary legitimacy and power. While Lim does not explicitly make this point, victimhood nationalism appears to be underpinned by a form of popular demand from below to forget, sanitize, or reinterpret a nation’s darker past in ways that serve present-day needs. Germany’s collective memory of the atrocities of World War II, for example, frequently lingers behind broader narratives about the universal causes of war and human violence. In Japan, meanwhile, wartime guilt is often subsumed within a discourse of absolute pacifism, blurring the distinction between perpetrator and victim. The longest film series in Japanese cinema is The Human Condition (1959), adapted from the eponymous 1958 novel and centered on the pacifist protagonist Kaji. Kaji’s insistence on moral innocence follows him through every experience from wartime prostitution to Soviet gulags. When I read the novel as a teenager in Seoul, I was struck by the dissonance between Kaji’s professed humanism and his racist condescension toward a Korean “ comfort woman ” who is infected with a sexually transmitted disease from Japanese soldiers. This contrasts with his devotion to his pale-skinned wife Michiko, which becomes the thread that sustains him all the way from the corruption of Japanese militarism to the bloodstained Manchurian front and ultimately into Soviet captivity. Drawing on the idea of victimhood nationalism, I now find it easy to see why the book and film served a need for the postwar Japanese, liberal and conservative alike. It functioned as a vehicle to reframe wartime experience in ways that emphasize Japanese innocence and pacifism. It is not that Kaji’s story does not reflect the broader experience of the Japanese masses during the war. However, it elides what is ultimately at stake, turning such sentiments into a unilateral apologia and obscuring the structural forces driving the violence. Borrowing From the Holocaust In Lim’s account, this postwar memory regime, often tinged at its margins with residual feelings of collective guilt and moral unease, underwent a significant transformation as it became enmeshed with the memory of the Holocaust. By 1963, Japanese peace activists and Holocaust survivors were already chanting “No more Hiroshima” and “No more Auschwitz” together at the annual ceremony in Poland marking the liberation of the concentration camp. One strong point of Lim’s case for victimhood nationalism lies in his observation of how the memory of the Holocaust, often understood primarily as a singular historical catastrophe inflicted upon a single ethnic group, has become a template through which nations cast their own history of suffering. This means recasting sufferings as diverse as nuclear bombardment, colonialism, and Stalinism in ways that invite comparison with the Holocaust, transforming national memories into collective narratives of victimhood. However, this project has often come at the expense of downplaying — or entirely overlooking — the agonies of real victims of these nations, from Japan’s military sex slaves to the political prisoners and detainees from other ethnic minorities who were persecuted under Adolf Hitler’s rule. Lim discusses the dialectical interplay between the globalization and nationalization of this transnational memory regime. The incorporation of a particular nation’s victimhood narrative into the global memory formation of the Holocaust helps it gain moral capital and dignity, which in turn reinforces the global narrative, as seen in the adaptation of “Never again” by Japanese peace activists. For Lim, the Holocaust has become a foundational frame of reference through which victims of historical tragedies are reinterpreted as martyrs, drawing on the way in which Israel commemorates the Holocaust. In the dominant Israeli national victimhood memory, the Israeli state was founded on the ashes of the Holocaust. The underlying logic is that if a Jewish state had predated the rise of Hitler, the Holocaust would not have occurred. Consequently, those who perished in Hitler’s gas chambers were not merely victims but martyrs. Germany and Japan In Germany, there was an effort to forge a postwar self-image that cleansed the sins of humanity by replacing the concrete realities of German wartime atrocities with vague, abstract questions such as “Why must humans wage war?” In collective memory, Lim argues, the Holocaust and other Nazi atrocities were seen as crimes committed by Hitler and his henchmen, for whom justice had been served through the Nuremberg Trials. Ordinary Germans, meanwhile, tended to view themselves as the first and last victims of Hitler and, ultimately, as victims of an unjust memory. For many Germans, the most horrifying memories stemmed from the experience of Germans who were interned in detention camps before their expulsion from Poland and Czechoslovakia. As early as 1949, West German political leaders already began to decontextualize German war crimes, with Hans-Christoph Seebohm, transport minister in the government of Konrad Adenauer, equating the suffering of German expellees with that of Holocaust victims. According to Lim, victimhood took center stage in Germany’s nationalist narrative after the end of the Cold War. He cites a 1995 survey commissioned by Der Spiegel , in which about 40 percent of respondents age sixty-four or older said the postwar expulsion of Germans from Central and Eastern Europe was a crime against humanity on a par with the Holocaust. This form of victimhood nationalism, based on the dichotomy between “a few Nazi criminals” and “most innocent Germans,” was central to reinforcing the national identity of the unified Germany under Helmut Kohl. Japan’s postwar memory culture, Lim suggests, was little different. He presents its cultural memory as a form of national victimology that defined the fifteen years of military rule between 1931 to 1945 as a historical aberration departing from an otherwise successful modernization process. This framework presented Japanese militarism as a deviation from, rather than a product of, Japan’s broader imperial development. Much of the Japanese public imagination attributed responsibility for wartime atrocities solely to amorphous entities such as “militarism” or “the system” while depicting ordinary Japanese as innocent victims of the war. This narrative recast the peoples of Japan and the Asian nations it conquered as common victims of Japanese militarism. For many Japanese, the memory of the atomic bombings supplies the dominant lens through which the war is remembered. Japan’s global identity as the only nation to have fallen victim to nuclear bombings has functioned, in Lim’s words, as a “screen memory that conceals Japan’s war crimes against Asian neighbors.” The dead and maimed of Hiroshima and Nagasaki are cast as victims of an “absolute evil.” In turn, this narrative of absolute evil gave rise to a form of absolute pacifism that condemns all wars as evil, detached from its historical context. The issue of accountability is largely absent from Japanese pacifism because it identifies no clear subject of responsibility. De-historicized and decontextualized, Japan’s dominant version of pacifism is used to condemn war and nuclear bombs while rarely holding Japan accountable for the imperialist wars that devastated its neighbors. In 1994, Kurihara Sadako, a poet who was critical of her country’s victimhood narrative, wrote the following words in a poem: If the atomic bombing was mercy so was the imperial army’s massacre of 20 thousand in Nanjing so was the Nazis’ gas chamber that massacred 6 million mercy. Victimhood and Violence Victimhood nationalism sanctifies victimhood into a collective form of martyrdom. Historical experiences are emptied of context, as certain elements are emphasized while others are marginalized. Lim refers to this as a combination of “historicization” and “de-historicization.” One of Lim’s insightful observations is that de-historicization and globalization can serve as instruments of national cohesion and obstacles to transnational solidarity. By lifting national experiences of suffering up to a globally recognized moral framework associated with Holocaust remembrance, they strengthen collective identity at home. At the same time, they obscure the suffering of colonial subjects and other victims of historic persecution, evidently limiting the possibility of a genuinely universal memory politics. The timeline of Lim’s book stops in the early twenty-first century. We are already living in a period when victimhood nationalism appears to be undergoing another transformation following an earlier phase marked by apologetic and self-pitying narratives of victimhood. Recently, we have witnessed cases in which nationalist narratives of victimhood took a violent turn in Russia, Israel, and elsewhere, with claims of historical suffering invoked to justify the infliction of violence on others in the name of “Never again.” Benjamin Netanyahu’s explicit linking of his atrocities in Palestine with the fearful memory of the Holocaust has been widely documented. Vladimir Putin’s rhetoric portraying Mother Russia as a historical victim of the West is also well known. Sooner rather than later, we may observe Japan and Germany using their own victimhood narratives to rearm themselves against China and Russia. Ahistorical narratives of nationalism are particularly corrosive. Trap of Universality In principle, the Left is internationalist, ultimately opposing all forms of nationalism even while critically defending the right of the oppressed to self-determination. For the Left, victimhood nationalism constitutes a particular challenge. While it is often articulated in universal moral languages — human rights, peace, and Holocaust remembrance — it blurs, or even glosses over, the memories of the devastation wrought by imperialism, colonialism, and authoritarian rule. Lim’s case is a warning against the political use of remembrance, and that scope can likely reach beyond his proposition of victimhood nationalism. In order for “Never again” to retain its universal meaning, the Left must remain vigilant against all forms of narratives cloaked in the language of universal victimhood.

A prominent human rights lawyer in the United Kingdom has been charged with criminal contempt for his closing speech in the trial of his client, Charlotte “Lottie” Head, one of four Palestine Action activists who were sentenced as terrorists over their involvement in a 2024 protest and raid on an Israeli weapons factory. Their sentencing marked the first time a British judge issued terrorism sentencing enhancements on people who were not actually convicted of terrorism. An additional 21 currently jailed Palestine Action protesters could face similar sentences, which carry multiyear prison terms. The charges against Rajiv Menon are similarly unprecedented in British legal history. He is accused of encouraging a jury to acquit Head according to their conscience. Menon denies this accusation and says he was simply informing the jury of a prior legal case that is memorialized in the courthouse at which the trial took place. He adds that, along with the U.K. government’s ban on Palestine Action under anti-terrorism laws, the case against him is intended to have a “chilling effect” against pro-Palestine protest, and carries “serious constitutional implications.”

South Africa’s unemployment rate rises to 33.6%

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Nicholas Mwangi ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-17T14:01:51+00:00

South Africa’s unemployment crisis has deepened, with the latest labor-market figures showing a rise in the official unemployment rate to 33.6% in the second quarter of 2026, up from 32.7% in the first quarter. The figures underline the persistent difficulty South Africa faces in generating sufficient decent and sustainable employment, particularly for young people and women. According to Statistics South Africa (Stats SA ), the number of unemployed people increased by 345,000 to 8.5 million in the second quarter, while employment declined by 16,000 to 16.7 million. The labor force consequently increased by 329,000, or 1.3%, over the quarter. The official unemployment rate, also referred to by Stats SA as the LU1 measure of labor underutilization, therefore increased by 0.9 percentage points, from 32.7% in the first quarter to 33.6% in the second. Formal employment under pressure as informal work grows The employment figures also point to continuing changes in the structure of the labor market. Employment in the formal sector fell by 41,000, while employment in the household sector declined by 9,000. Informal-sector employment, by contrast, increased by 34,000. The latest figures point to deeper problems in the country’s economic structure, including its capacity to generate productive employment on a scale sufficient to absorb a growing working-age population. Stats SA’s labor-force data also shows the particular vulnerability of young South Africans. In the first quarter of 2026, the official unemployment rate among people aged 15–34 stood at 45.8%. A crisis rooted in South Africa’s economic structure Speaking to BreakThrough News in a personal capacity, Dr. Phillip Dexter, former African National Congress (ANC) member of Parliament and South African Communist Party (SACP) member, says that South Africa’s employment crisis has deep historical roots. “The unemployment crisis must be understood as the result of two key factors. First is the structural crisis caused by colonialism, apartheid and imperialism.” According to Dexter, South Africa’s economy was historically structured around the extraction of resources and the utilization of low-waged and relatively low-skilled labor. In his assessment, the persistence of unemployment therefore reflects structural features of the country’s political economy rather than simply inadequate short-term economic growth. He argues that these historical problems were compounded by the economic changes of the 1980s and 1990s, including deindustrialization and the adoption of neoliberal economic policies. Read more: South Africa’s anti-immigrant protests expose a deeper national crisis “The system was thrown into a crisis in the 1980–1990 period when deindustrialization started,” Dexter said, arguing that the influence of the IMF and World Bank and subsequent neoliberal policies contributed to the difficulty of transforming the inherited economic structure. He also pointed to state capture, corruption and maladministration as factors that have further complicated attempts to transform the economy. The unfinished transformation More than three decades after the formal end of apartheid, South Africa continues to grapple with highly unequal patterns of ownership, wealth, and economic opportunity. Dexter says, one of the central failures of the post-1994 period has been the inability to fundamentally alter these structures. “The failure to adopt radical, progressive policies and implement strategies to reform land ownership, create economic opportunities, to consistently fail to plan to build infrastructure beneficiated minerals,” he said, has contributed to the continued reproduction of what he describes as the political economy of apartheid. While successive ANC-led governments introduced social programs and measures aimed at reducing poverty and expanding social protection, Dexter argues that these interventions did not fundamentally transform the ownership and accumulation patterns underlying the economy. “The political economy of apartheid is alive and well and reproducing,” he said. This assessment comes against a labor market in which formal employment remains under pressure while informal employment has continued to absorb some of those unable to find work in the formal economy. The latest Stats SA figures show a decline in formal-sector employment alongside an increase in informal-sector employment. For Dexter, this is part of a longer-term trend rather than an entirely new development. “This trend started in the 1980s and continues,” he said. He adds that South Africa’s industrial policy has failed to deliver the scale of transformation required to rebuild productive capacity and create mass employment. Read more: “A declaration of war against the poor”: AbM leads resistance against South Africa’s anti-occupation bill “The ANC-led governments have paid lip-service to industrial policy measures that should have transformed the economy,” he said. The consequence, in his view, is an economy that continues to reproduce substantial inequalities in wealth and employment, despite the political transition to democracy. Young people carry the heaviest burden The unemployment crisis is particularly acute among young South Africans. With the youth unemployment rate already at 45.8% in the first quarter of 2026, the transition from education into employment remains one of the country’s most serious socioeconomic challenges. Dexter is critical of approaches that place excessive emphasis on entrepreneurship, skills development, and individual employability without addressing the limited capacity of the economy to create jobs. “While any measures, such as promoting entrepreneurial opportunities, learnerships, apprenticeships etc. are welcome, it is the failure of capital to invest and of the state to discipline capital to invest” that remains a fundamental problem, he said. South Africa’s mineral wealth and the employment question South Africa possesses substantial mineral resources; an established industrial base and sophisticated financial institutions compared to other African countries. But these advantages have not translated into employment on the scale required to substantially reduce joblessness. For Dexter, a different development strategy would require greater emphasis on industrialization, infrastructure, public investment, and the ability of the state to direct economic resources towards productive purposes. He points to a range of international examples of both progressive and reactionary development models that have overcome similar economic crises. He points to countries such as Japan and South Korea, which benefited from massive Western investment as part of efforts to develop them as bulwarks against socialist countries, as well as Malaysia, Indonesia, and Taiwan, which followed different paths of state-supported development. He contrasts these experiences with China and Vietnam, which he argues have pursued models designed to ensure that society as a whole benefits from the wealth it creates. “This requires vision and planning,” Dexter said. He also points to Scandinavian countries, the Netherlands, Germany and, later, Ireland, arguing that even these social-democratic economies have developed more progressive economic models than South Africa. For South Africa, he argues that the essential priorities should include investment in people, infrastructure and the social wage, alongside stronger efforts to prevent the continued extraction of wealth from developing economies. A structural crisis requiring political choices The latest unemployment figures therefore raise questions that extend beyond the immediate movement of the unemployment rate. The increase from 32.7% to 33.6% in a single quarter, accompanied by a fall in employment and an increase in the number of unemployed people to 8.5 million, demonstrates the scale of the challenge confronting South Africa. Dexter believes that addressing the crisis ultimately requires political choices about who controls investment, how natural resources are used, the role of the state and how economic growth is distributed. “Ultimately it’s about the political will of leaders to act against exploitation and to lead the drive for people-centered development,” he said. The persistent unemployment and broader economic difficulties have also contributed to the recent scapegoating of migrants in South Africa, as frustration over limited employment opportunities is increasingly directed towards foreign nationals. The question for South Africa is therefore not simply whether the economy can grow, but what kind of growth is being produced, who benefits from it, and whether it can create secure and dignified livelihoods for the millions of people excluded from meaningful employment. The International Monetary Fund’s Africa policy is underdevelopment

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Grieve Chelwa ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-16T17:11:12+00:00

Across Africa, the International Monetary Fund (IMF) presents itself as the custodian of macroeconomic stability. Its latest Article IV staff reports on Ethiopia . Malawi , Nigeria , South Africa , and Zambia are written for economies with very different histories and institutions. Yet they converge on a familiar prescription: fiscal consolidation, tighter monetary policy, market-determined exchange rates, subsidy reform, deregulation and “private-sector-led growth.” No serious economist can dismiss macroeconomic stability. High inflation destroys wages, foreign-exchange shortages choke production, and out-of-control debt eventually forces adjustment. But stability is a means, not a development strategy. Read together, these five reports expose the central weakness of the Fund’s approach in Africa: it is much clearer about how the state should retreat than about how African economies will acquire the productive capabilities needed to grow, diversify, and create decent jobs. Article IV consultations are formally surveillance rather than loan agreements, and not every recommendation is a binding condition. But the distinction is less reassuring than it sounds. Zambia and Ethiopia are already under Fund programs; Malawi’s program expired without a review; and in Nigeria and South Africa the Fund’s assessment influences credit ratings, investors, and the terms on which governments can borrow. These reports therefore define the boundaries of what international finance considers “sound” policy, even when the Fund supplies no money directly. The country details are revealing. In Malawi , the Fund calls for urgent stabilization to move from aid dependence to a private-sector-led economy. It stresses fiscal and monetary tightening, exchange-rate adjustment and the removal of administrative obstacles. Yet Malawi’s private economy consists overwhelmingly of small farmers and informal firms facing expensive credit, unreliable infrastructure, and scarce foreign exchange. Calling for the private sector to lead does not conjure into existence firms capable of industrial investment. In Zambia , fiscal consolidation, fuel-subsidy reform, and debt restructuring sit alongside hopes for more mining investment, public-private partnerships and competition. The report itself notes that bank lending rates averaged more than 26% in early 2025 and that private credit was below half the sub-Saharan African average. Under those conditions, which Zambian manufacturer can compete with a multinational mining company that raises finance abroad? The Ethiopia report is more explicit about transitioning from public- to private-sector-led growth. It endorses exchange-rate liberalization, reduced financial repression, greater foreign investment, and the possible privatization of commercially oriented state enterprises. But Ethiopia’s previous development model, for all its faults, used public investment and directed credit to build infrastructure and productive capacity. Dismantling those instruments before strong domestic firms and development-finance alternatives exist risks replacing state leadership not with competitive Ethiopian capitalism, but with foreign ownership of the most profitable assets. For South Africa , the Fund declares that the state-led model has reached its limits and describes private-sector-driven growth as the “only viable option.” It recommends greater private participation in electricity and logistics, product-market liberalization, and more flexible labor markets. South Africa has a more developed domestic capitalist class than most African economies. Even there, however, ownership and finance remain highly concentrated, while mass unemployment weakens domestic demand. The issue is not simply state versus market, but which institutions direct investment, towards what sectors, and under whose ownership. In Nigeria , the Fund praises fuel-subsidy removal, an end to monetary financing and foreign-exchange reform for restoring investor confidence. In the same breath, it acknowledges that poverty and food insecurity have risen. Credit is concentrated in oil and gas, with virtually no new lending to small firms and households. This is the contradiction in miniature: improved indicators for international investors are treated as progress even when productive domestic investment and household welfare remain depressed. Throughout these reports, “the private sector” is presented as a single actor. It is not. Africa has millions of traders, farmers and small firms, but in many countries only a thin domestic class of firms with the capital, technology, and organizational capacity to invest at scale. The investor positioned to benefit from privatization, liberalization, mining concessions, and public-private partnerships is therefore often the multinational corporation, not an emergent national industrialist. This matters because multinational investment can generate exports while remaining an enclave: importing machinery, expatriating profits, minimizing taxes and establishing few links with domestic suppliers. Foreign investment can contribute to development, but only when disciplined by local-content rules, technology-transfer requirements, strategic procurement and institutions that nurture domestic firms. The IMF’s framework treats these industrial-policy tools principally as distortions rather than as mechanisms through which today’s rich and industrializing countries built productive capacity. Even the Fund’s own research identifies an additional constraint. A 2025 IMF working paper estimates that sub-Saharan African sovereigns pay an extra 46 basis points on Eurobond issuance after controlling for credit rating, maturity, currency and global conditions. During global shocks, this “African premium” exceeds 120 basis points. African states are thus urged to withdraw precisely because borrowing is unusually expensive, while weak public investment makes their economies less productive and keeps risk perceptions high. This is a vicious circle, not a route to transformation. Kevin Gallagher and Njūgūna Ndūng’ū recently argued that the Fund remains caught in an austerity trap : programs regularly underperform their growth forecasts, underestimate fiscal multipliers, and fail to transform the export structures that cause balance-of-payments crises. Their proposed alternative, country-specific, countercyclical programs centered on growth and redistribution, should inform the Fund’s current review of program design and conditionality. Africa does not need indifference to inflation or debt. It needs a different sequencing of adjustment. Protect public investment and universal services; restructure debt earlier and more deeply; tax mineral rents, wealth, and illicit financial flows; expand concessional finance; rebuild development banks; and use industrial policy to create domestic firms capable of learning, exporting, and employing people. Social-spending floors should be binding, not decorative, and adjustment should be measured against employment, poverty and productive investment as well as fiscal balances. The relevant choice is not between an inefficient state and an idealized market. It is between an economic settlement that reproduces commodity dependence and foreign corporate dominance, and one that builds African productive power through an industrial policy, a monetary policy, and continental thinking. Until the IMF confronts that choice, its programs may stabilize balance sheets on behalf of the external creditors while leaving the structures that generate crises for the people firmly intact. The IMF policy for Africa is to develop underdevelopment. This would remain the case irrespective of the fate of its ongoing talks with Oman to devise a common mechanism to manage the traffic through it, Iran has said.
