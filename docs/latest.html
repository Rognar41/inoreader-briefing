Debating Victor Serge

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Mitchell Abidor ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-18T20:14:25+00:00

When I began writing Victor Serge: Unruly Revolutionary , I expected to write a biography in keeping with the image common on the left of Serge as an uncompromising enemy of Stalinism who never fell into anti-communism and fought all his life for revolutionary socialism. This is what we find in the many writings of Richard Greeman, who single-handedly rescued Serge from oblivion by translating his novels and tirelessly writing about him. In fact, my biography began as a collaboration between me and Richard, one we abandoned due to differences in interpretation. Suzi Weissman presents that image as well in her biography of Serge, The Course Is Set on Hope . A fuller account of this Serge was what I thought I was going to write. Things did not turn out that way. I worked mainly from primary sources from archives around the world as well as long-forgotten newspapers and magazines for which Serge wrote. What I found was a man far different from the one we find in Greeman’s and Weissman’s books (and all those who draw from their work) but also the one Serge presented in his justly famous Memoirs of a Revolutionary, 1901–1941 . I found a man who ceaselessly evolved politically, which I found admirable. But the evolution I found went in directions even I was shocked to find: that Serge was a man who came to believe in his final years that socialist revolution was no longer in the cards, and that those who believed it were frozen in the past. I was also shocked to discover in his correspondence, articles, and notebooks that Serge became an anti-communist and in certain regards an apologist for the West in the early days of the Cold War. There was no denying the evidence, and everything I wrote is documented in my book. I knew I’d upset people, and I offered to debate some of those I told of my discoveries. I was never offered the chance to do so, and the recently published critique of me and my findings first appeared in several European publications and now in English in Jacobin . To fully respond to the attack by Suzi Weissman, Carlo Albertani, and Christian Dubucq on Victor Serge: Unruly Revolutionary , I would have to quote the entire book. I respond here, however briefly and incompletely. An example of their hasty and incorrect reading is their claim that I imply Serge was a police spy at the time of the Liabeuf Affair. The trio writes that “Abidor notes that the socialist journalist Gustave Hervé was prosecuted for public statements, whereas Serge was not. Although he acknowledges that no documentary evidence explains why Serge escaped prosecution, he nevertheless invites the reader to infer that his immunity reflected dubious dealings with the police.” I wrote or implied nothing of the sort, and what I said is unambiguous. In fact, I very specifically said that in writing about the Liabeuf case, “Gustave Hervé took a similar tack [to that of Serge], and in fact used many of the same words that Serge would a couple of weeks later. It is indicative of the relative threat posed by Hervé and Serge that, though they wrote the same things, Hervé went to jail for his opinions while Serge went undisturbed by the police.” Yes, I note Serge went undisturbed by the police, but I clearly say that this was because he was not a threat, while the well-known Hervé was. How this can be read as an accusation that Serge was a police informant is a mystery. I’m not even certain they read the book. My critics write, “As an anarchist, one might reasonably have expected Abidor’s criticism to focus on Serge’s decision to join the Bolshevik Party in 1919, marking his break with the anarchism of his youth. But this is not the case.” Had they actually read the book, they would have seen that the lengthiest and densest chapter is on Serge’s dishonesty about the Soviet Union in his writings on the new state and anarchism. I make the case that Serge lied when writing propaganda pieces for the international Communist press. In these articles, he claimed that the USSR was the fulfillment of the anarchist dream. As I show, using contemporary accounts in the anarchist press by anarchists from several countries who met Serge in Moscow in the early 1920s, Serge privately told his former comrades that the Soviet state was awful, while writing articles that claimed the opposite and encouraged anarchists to join him there. This is part of a chapter in which I also detail the Bolsheviks’ brutal and underhanded war on anarchists and anarchism. I will plead guilty to one thing that this group didn’t bring up. I attempt to explain why Serge might have knowingly written falsehoods. Is this an incidence of the “psychologizing” I’m attacked for? I look at this matter from many angles, all sympathetic to Serge, who was truly in a bind. Anarchists have told me that Serge’s conduct was inexcusable, and that my search for extenuating circumstances was unacceptable: lies are lies, and leading anarchists to move to the USSR was leading them to their deaths. Sometimes a biographer just can’t win. Weissman and her friends write, “Abidor treats the anarcho-individualist movement, in which Serge was an active participant for nearly a decade, with scant sympathy.” This is absurd. I would have hoped that people who admire Serge would be aware that I translated and edited Anarchists Never Surrender , a collection of Serge’s anarchist writings in 2015, the first and only one of its kind in English. That book is an admiring one, and in both that volume, the biography, and two other books I’ve dedicated to French individualist anarchism, I defend this tradition that Serge once so ably represented. I do subject the school to lengthy analysis and draw some critical conclusions, but that’s not a sign of “scant sympathy”; it’s proof I take it seriously. Had my critics actually read every page of Unruly Revolutionary , they’d have seen that I also note and criticize Serge’s harsh treatment of anarchist individualism in his novel The Birth of Our Power . I even located and approvingly quoted the review of that book by his former coeditor of the anarchist paper L’Anarchie and first wife, Rirette Maîtrejean, who condemned her former companion for the way he had “evolved’ and for his “disdain” for his former individualist anarchist comrades. In any case, criticizing my treatment of anarchism is odd coming from Weissman, who dedicated three pages to Serge’s anarchism in her book on him, none of it showing any familiarity with Serge’s writings in French. Credit where credit is due, Carlo Albertani is not guilty of this error in the first volume of his biography of Serge, published in France last year. Serge’s ideas evolved greatly over the course of his life, and his attachment to revolutionary socialism took a blow during and especially after World War II. He grew increasingly exasperated with his fellow left-wing anti-Stalinist exiles in Mexico, who thought World War II would end in revolutions, just as World War I had. He turned his back on Marxist orthodoxy and denied that possibility. I write about this approvingly and at length in the chapter “The Revolution Revised.” The working class, in his eyes, had suffered an enormous defeat and was not in a state to carry out revolution. In this book and in my other writings on Serge, I have praised him for this change in point of view. He wrote extensively of his shift in his Notebooks , and I highly recommend that anyone who hasn’t already read that essential volume do so. In it, we find this diagnosis by Serge of the politics of the revolutionary socialists among his fellow exiles: “Their extremely optimistic and schematic conviction that the Russian Revolution will soon be repeated in Europe. ‘The workers will occupy the factories (Pivert), they’ll take power (Gironella), etc.’ So deluded are they that the POUMists think that they’ll be back in a free, socialist Spain in six months, and Pivert speaks proudly of the potential for the formation of a Red Army in France!” All of this revolutionary sloganeering was detached from reality, and Serge told his comrades just that and did so bluntly. The final years of Serge’s Notebooks are full of evidence supporting the case for Serge’s abandonment of the “revolutionary” in revolutionary socialism. So bad had things become that Serge was informed in a letter that “the comrades of POUM . . . and Marceau [Pivert], Stein, and Jacob unanimously consider that, for ideological and political reasons, collaboration with you is impossible.” It’s certainly not by chance that Weissman and her friends refer only to the French edition of the book, which is an essential and impressive piece of work. To speak of it as Serge’s Notebooks , published by New York Review Books, would require that they acknowledge that the translator, who would necessarily know precisely what is contained in a book that details Serge’s evolution was . . . me. The immediate postwar period proved Serge right about revolution and permanently soured his relationship with the revolutionary left. His ignoble physical fight with fellow exile Jean Malaquais in 1944 was over the latter’s accusation that Serge called for an alliance with the Tories. This was an exaggeration of Serge’s actual position, since he hoped for a “socialist-leaning” future, but Serge was in no way a revolutionary socialist at the end of the war and in his final years. As I show, in his final interview, coincidentally published the day he died, he nowhere even mentioned socialism. His primary concern, one that recurs in his public and private writings, which I discovered in his archives, was the protection of the human person. Personalism had become his guiding philosophy. Victor Serge, the Anti-Communist My most controversial claim is that Serge was an anti-communist, for which I provide ample evidence, too ample to lay out here. I don’t speculate about what his future direction would have been. I wrote, “To say Serge would have evolved in the same directions as his comrade in anti-Stalinism Boris Souvarine, who eventually supported the US in Vietnam, might be going too far. But it’s hard to deny, given the positions he took and the language he employed in expressing his opinions . . . that Serge agreed with the point of view of ex-communist anti-communists like Arthur Koestler.” I spoke only of where he was, not where he might have gone. And where he was was obsessive anti-communism. A large chunk of the book is dedicated to this period and this phase in Serge’s political evolution. To spell everything out would extend this response to dozens of pages. My claim is not based solely on his writing for the anti-communist press, like the liberal anti-communist New Leader . His private correspondence is replete with references to “reds” and “commies.” He considered communism “the worst danger, a mortal danger” to humanity, more of a danger than the West, which he considered a benign, even positive force. Weissman and co. argue he wrote for the anti-communist New Leader because the communists blocked him from publishing elsewhere. Were the communists really that powerful everywhere that they could sway publishers at will? As I ask in the book, how is it that the French Communists were unable to block Koestler from publishing Darkness at Noon , which appeared with no difficulty and to great success? No less a figure than Dwight MacDonald , as I show in my book, warned Serge against the path he was taking. His correspondence with Emmanuel Mounier, the brilliant philosopher and founder of the variant of personalism to which Serge adhered, is filled with anti-communist screeds and attacks on Mounier for being soft on communism. When Serge died, Mounier wrote, “Since the war his letters were polarized around a single concern: the Soviet dictatorship and the camps in the USSR. . . . Far from France, he spoke inaccurately of realities he didn’t know. But we forgive him this, for men of his mettle and humanity are few.” Serge also suffered from the paranoia of a Cold Warrior, seeing the communist hand everywhere, particularly within the United States and its government. In a 1940 letter, he wrote, “I know that the Stalinist work is being done in the United States on a large scale by a goodly number of journalists, and Stalinist intellectuals who have arrived there. They even have a certain amount of support in the Administration. . . . Their action is and will be camouflaged. . . . They would do anything to get all their personnel installed in the United States.” Elucubrations like these are clearly the work of an anti-communist or, to phrase it better, a premature Cold Warrior. See this quote from a 1946 essay in a collection of Serge’s articles called The New Russian Imperialism . I wrote in the biography: He downplayed the threat and even importance of Western imperialism. In that publication he insisted that the British Empire, led by the Labour government, “is evacuating from Egypt and offers India its independence. The old methods of colonial exploitation are exhausted.” As for the exemplar of the new form of imperialism, the United States, Serge claimed that he “knew Americans sufficiently well to know that they don’t contemplate imposing tyrannies on the five continents and on the contrary desire a sustainable reorganization of international relations.” Leaving aside how poor this all was as a prediction, especially as regards America, this is hardly revolutionary socialism, which I think we can all agree would be anti-imperialist. The “exhaustion of the old imperialisms” Serge spoke of didn’t prohibit France from fighting for years to hold on to Indochina and Algeria. The Mau Maus of Kenya would have been surprised to learn that England was gladly setting their colonies free. Weissman and co. omit any mention of Serge’s 1947 letter to André Malraux, in which Serge says that if he were in France, he would have voted for de Gaulle. Though his son claimed the letter was a fake, there’s no reason to think it was, and the sentiments it expresses are in keeping with his beliefs at the time. He so hated communists and communism that he would have been willing to support France’s main barrier to Communist victory in France’s elections. This shocked the revolutionary left of the time, none of whom cast any doubt on the reality of this letter or Serge’s authorship. Two final things: As I did with Weissman and Richard Greeman, whose lifelong work on Serge I benefited from, I informed Birchall while I was writing the book of the conclusions I was reaching. He expressed his disagreement, and before reading the book told me that my thesis was incorrect. Ian and I disagree about many things, but he is an honorable man. He read the book with an open mind and was convinced by what I had to say about Serge’s anti-communism. Ian is a lifetime Trotskyist and a respected scholar and activist. The baseness of Weissman and her friends is revealed by their attack on him for being convinced by my documentation. Suzi Weissman has held fast over the years to a mythical version of Victor Serge, revolutionary socialist sans peur et sans reproche, one that was also propagated by my friend and mentor, Richard Greeman. I did not start writing Victor Serge: Unruly Revolutionary thirteen years ago as the critical volume it turned out to be. It was my reading of his anarchist writings, which contradicted his claims in his memoirs about not supporting anarchist illegalism; the transcript of the Bonnot Gang trial; in which his conduct was less than stellar; and what I found in archival sources that led the book to go in an unexpected direction. It was not my “anarchism” that led me to write the book that so displeases those who cling to the myth. It was the facts and, as I say in the introduction, my lack of the phrenological bump for veneration that forced me to write the book I wrote. Serge was at one time a revolutionary socialist, and at one time he wasn’t an anti-communist. He ceased being the former and became the latter in his final years. There’s no shame or surprise in the fact that Serge’s ideas changed with circumstances: that was a constant throughout his life. Let me end by quoting some apposite words of Serge’s concerning his fellow exiles who remained attached to out-of-date schemas. He said they were “idealists hemmed in by the sclerosis of doctrines and circumstances, and dominated by their convictions and their emotional attachments; in short, by fanaticism. Under such conditions the person who disturbs the inner security of the others is a hateful heretic.” Weissman, Albertani, and Dubucq cling to an imaginary Serge, one who fits their political needs and desires. They’re free to believe they’re right, but hagiography is not biography, and facts are stubborn things.

Flock Off: How Westland DSA Members Pushed Flock Cameras Out of Their City

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Detroit DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-18T19:21:10+00:00

By filling city council meetings, educating the public on mass surveillance, and cooperating with like-minded allies, DSA members in Westland shot down a Flock contract renewal. By Dave N. and Kathy E. In July 2024 the city of Westland signed up for a trial run with Flock Safety that would last until August 2026. Flock’s cameras are Automated License Plate Readers (ALPR) that mark the date, time, and location where a vehicle passes. Flock claims that its technology does not scan human faces, but its cameras are able to fingerprint cars based on minute details like rust or bumper stickers. The technology is similar to that used in facial recognition software. These services are sold to law enforcement and advertised to cities as a tool for safety (e.g. tracking stolen vehicles or resolving car accidents). In reality, police officers have used this technology to stalk ex-spouses, target women seeking abortions, and aid ICE raids. Given privacy concerns, civil rights, and the actual safety of citizens, it was in the interest of any resident of Westland to shoot Flock down. We learned, however, through hearing from citizens at Westland city council meetings and by talking with council members, that Westland is a very pro-police city. Even with a DSA-endorsed socialist, Mike McDermott, as council president, the majority of the council were convinced by the police department’s arguments for Flock. Upon approval in 2024, ten new Flock poles were placed in the city. In 2025 Metro Detroit DSA began organizing in western Wayne County by forming a geographic working group that became known as Socialists Organizing Western Wayne ( SOWW ). In the beginning, we were learning about one another, how to organize, and even what socialism meant. After six months, we had grown to over 20 members with 15–20 consistently attending meetings. Organizing around the Flock contract turned into our first real project. The group’s main goal was to find a way to guarantee four council votes (a simple majority) against renewing the Flock contract. Elijah Nemr led the anti-Flock campaign. He was a relatively new DSA member who leveraged his technical background to facilitate meetings, organize members, research, and speak at Westland city council meetings and other civic events. Most of the city council members are openly pro-police. Mike was able to guarantee one vote besides his own, from the only Republican, Emily Bauman. Mike’s ability to gather two more votes was hampered by a law prohibiting four or more council members to meet in private. SOWW’s Flock sub-group saw that public pressure was the main way to gather two more votes. Mike proposed two approaches to achieve this pressure, and we executed both: the inside and the outside. The inside approach was to have SOWW members attend city council meetings and speak publicly to council members. We also spoke to the other residents attending the meetings to create public anger and gain more support. The outside approach was for SOWW members to canvass in neighborhoods and distribute petitions to pressure Westland not to renew the contract. While canvassing we learned that most people didn’t understand the difference between Flock cameras and CCTV cameras. CCTV cameras require a warrant or a subpoena to access the footage, while Flock cameras upload information to an unaccountable database that law enforcement can access at any moment. Once we showed examples of police who misused the technology and explained how Flock cameras didn’t require warrants, residents immediately became disturbed and supported our efforts. One of the strongest tactics we used was to leverage a wide array of talking points when addressing city council. We got help on our petitions from our sibling group in Ferndale, the Ferndale Area Organizing Committee (FAOC) , which had successfully pressured Ferndale not to renew its Flock contract. FAOC gave us many resources, literature, and debate strategies that had been effective there. We began forming speeches personal to each Westland resident about why they opposed Flock. For example, we have several mothers in our group and one of them, Liz, decided to highlight the predatory threats Flock cameras posed to children. At a council meeting, she stated: As a parent, I’m deeply concerned about the ever-increasing levels of surveillance our children are being raised to believe is normal. There are many stories emerging of Flock’s inability to keep the sensitive information they collect safe, further raising concerns about this kind of surveillance technology and how it impacts our youth. Liz’s story, along with framing that focused on the safety concern that Flock cameras pose, immediately garnered public support. This video of one public comment session shows Liz and others who spoke about why they oppose surveillance. We allied with the president of the Westland Democratic Club to put more pressure and speak out against Flock to the council. We consulted with independent journalists and collaborated with other organizations such as The People’s Assembly . Peoples’ Assembly and DeFlock (a national open-source project that maps Flock cameras) sent speakers to our council meetings. The first meeting of July showed the fruits of these efforts — instead of SOWW members being the main public commenters on Flock, multiple constituents who had not spoken before on the topic rose to voice their concerns about the cameras. That meeting was the boiling point — within a few days, councilwoman Melissa Sampey posted to Facebook the council’s decision not to renew the contract and to let it expire. Sampey’s post revealed the force behind this decision: “Community Engagement Matters.” SOWW was able to demonstrate that a DSA geographic group can multiply the efforts of our SIOs. Mike McDermott is our only SIO on city council, and SOWW demonstrated that a DSA geographic group can multiply the efforts of our SIOs. We built an issue campaign to create public pressure on elected officials, through a unified group that devoted time to forming relationships with constituents. Attending Westland Democratic Club meetings and Westland cultural events allowed us to create allies, and those allies came out and spoke against Flock. SOWW also cultivated organizing skills, and new members gained memorable organizing experience that they can take beyond the Flock campaign. SOWW trained new organizers to speak publicly, compile literature, and canvas. Growing as an organizer also requires personal development. One of our new members, Kate, said she was “inherently a socially anxious person, but working with such a supportive and motivated group really helped me feel comfortable speaking up.” People are motivated to try things they struggle with when it’s for a greater cause and for others; that’s a core tenet of organizing. The support and camaraderie in SOWW made a space to develop future organizers. And there’s a need for those organizers: our work on ending mass surveillance is far from finished. Inkster, Livonia, Garden City, and Romulus all have Flock contracts. Our group needs to carry this momentum to one of these cities, but we need to decide soon. SOWW was created to organize locally alongside our Socialists in Office (SIOs) — Congresswoman Rashida Tlaib, State Representative Dylan Wegela, and Westland City Council President Mike McDermott — where their districts overlap in Westland, Romulus, Inkster, and Garden City. If you are interested in the work we do, please contact Jill Rossiter, Elijah Nemr (Elijah S), or Kathy Elrick on Slack or fill out our interest form . David N. has been an active member of DSA since July 2025. He is not a part of any caucus. In SOWW he has contributed to power mapping Westland, town hall organizing, and mutual aid. Kathy E. has been an active member of the chapter since 2023 and is co-chair of SOWW. She started hosting DSA coffee socials in western Wayne County in 2024. She is uncaucused and active in starting a local DSA, community garden collaboration in Norwayne, and Mutual Aid 101 and 102 events. Stiffing Workers, Rationing Toilet Paper: Who Really Makes WPI Run?

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Working Mass: The Massachusetts DSA Labor Outlet ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-18T19:21:10+00:00

Resident Advisors on strike in 2024 take their picket line to Earle Bridge. Worcester DSA members join with a banner that reads: “Workers on strike, unite and fight!” By: Julia Z & Jake S WORCESTER – Students have returned to Worcester Polytechnic Institute (WPI). Freshmen are being shuttled through new student orientation, professors are dusting off their syllabi, and returning students have long soaked in their remaining days of freedom. Many new students were no doubt won over by WPI’s aggressive promotion of its cutting-edge Robotics Engineering, Computer Science, and AI undergraduate programs along with its highly-celebrated R1 Research University status. Many are surely excited for the “project-based learning” described by eager campus tour guides, ready to sell them a vision of high-tech projects on a charming New England campus. But a university campus is much more than its students, teachers, and overpaid administrators. WPI may claim non-profit status on its tax filings, but in order to compete in a cutthroat higher education industry, it still carries all the fixtures of a corporate enterprise — in many ways, becoming more a landlord/buffet/hedge fund/private police department conglomerate that offers college degrees on the side — including a massive staff force to keep the lights on, the students learning, and the tuition dollars rolling in. So who are the faces that keep those lights on? Who does everything from preparing curriculum, to the research that won WPI its R1 status, to taking out the trash and setting the tables for big donor wine-and-dine sessions? And, more importantly, why are they increasingly becoming self-organized into trade unions to represent them against the interests of WPI’s endowment managers? Working Mass sat down with representatives of three organized labor unions at WPI, plus the campus chapter of the American Association of University Professors (AAUP), to talk about the importance of their work, their organizations, and their conflicts with the bosses on campus. Trades, Groundskeeping, and Custodial Staff Service Employees International Union (SEIU) 32BJ SEIU 32BJ is the international union representing facilities workers at WPI. Without their labor, repairs and maintenance on buildings would go unaddressed, trash would pile up in every classroom and office, and the grass on the Quad wouldn’t get cut — in other words, the campus itself would probably become an overgrown ruin. Every few years, by being organized in their union, facilities staff negotiate a contract with WPI to lay out fair wages, decent benefits, and dignified working conditions. Between every contract cycle, the contract needs to be enforced; the staff, through their union, sometimes find themselves continuing to battle with WPI’s administration to follow the terms of the agreement that both parties have already signed. Mark, a union steward elected by fellow custodians to address such problems on the job, told us about a recent fight, and how the union united his coworkers to push WPI toward a fair resolution that protected their jobs and made their lives easier. About a year ago, the administration brought in consultants from Core America to evaluate the campus practices, supposedly as a checkup to make sure everything was properly staffed and maintained. Core America went to work to cut good union jobs and clamp down on upkeep costs. Part of what they developed was a proposal for communal trash bins in office buildings: rather than entering individual offices to pick up trash and recycling, office staff themselves would take on the work of custodians and deposit their trash in a larger, central bin. Less time spent gathering trash meant WPI could try to get away with paying fewer custodians to do it. The plan would cut five jobs and open the door to more “reorganizations” (i.e., more cuts). As Mark said, nobody thought to talk to custodians themselves about the change. Not only was WPI trying to skip past a contractual obligation to negotiate over these changes with the people who do the work, it wouldn’t even have worked out the way they wanted. “They don’t know anything about our jobs,” Mark said, of the administration and their outside consultants. “If you had office staff doing this, I’d be spending more time getting everything into the right bin than I would have spent just walking through their offices anyway. President Wang used to work at a school that tried this idea out, and that’s exactly what happened.” Union representatives called a meeting with management and were quick to demand the plan be reversed. Union members took collective action by delivering a petition to back up what their reps were saying, and WPI backed down. Core America’s recommendation wouldn’t go through. Mark reflected on the outcome: We got them to open those five positions back up. Did you know they don’t approve purchases in May until the fiscal year ends? At certain points in the year, we have to be really careful with the supplies we have left. Students pay tens of thousands a year to go here, and management has tens of thousands to spend on these consultants, but they want to cut our jobs and make us ration toilet paper? WPI estimates the annual cost of attendance while living on campus — which is required of first- and second-years — to be just shy of $85,000. The total evaluation of its endowment sits at well over half a billion dollars. According to Propublica, in the fiscal year ending June 2025, WPI’s Chief Finance Officer — who reportedly interfaced with Core America consultants — was paid roughly $600,000, while the school’s President took home just over $1 million total. Resident Advisors United Auto Workers (UAW) Local 2322 Resident Advisors (RAs), Residential Mentors (RMs), Community Support Assistants (CSAs), and Community Development Mentors (CDMs) come together to form a union affiliated with United Auto Workers Local 2322. Student workers in any of these four positions perform key roles in keeping students safe, submitting requests for maintenance and repairs, and building community amongst undergraduate students. Without their labor, the dorms would be less safe, less maintained, and much more lonely places to live. We met with Zoey and Dom, two union stewards for this group of workers, which voted to unionize in 2024 and won its first contract with WPI the following year. One core reason the Res Advisor Union (RAU) formed was to challenge constant changes to job descriptions imposed by WPI. In fact, WPI only had a single Res Advisor role, but very soon after RAs won their union election, the administration proposed breaking the job into its current roles, removing most RAs from on-campus housing. Admin claimed this would lighten the workloads of RAs, but Zoey and Dom know it was another cost-cutting measure. Most of the new roles would receive no meal plans, no housing, and less pay. Moreover, RMs would be charged with developing a meaningful relationship with 75 or even 85 students at a time, where in their old role, RAs were only expected to handle around 35. “This seemed to be the personal project of the Dean of Undergraduate Students,” Zoey told us, “but nobody wanted to actually engage with us to figure out a solution that would actually work.” During intense contract negotiations, RAs went on strike from October 31 to December 15, 2025, after which the RAU won a decent contract with WPI that includes just-cause firing, increased pay, housing vouchers, meal plans, and workload caps. And while many of the job description changes were ultimately accepted, they’ll be implemented gradually over a 2 to 3 year phase-in period with a joint Union-Management Committee to oversee the transition. If the new roles aren’t working for Res Advisors themselves, the union will return to the negotiating table in 2028 with an opening to fight again for a better system. Graduate Student Workers United Auto Workers (UAW) Local 2322 Several hundred graduate student workers are organized under the WPI Graduate Workers Union, WPI-GWU, a UAW Local 2322 affiliate. Most doctoral students, and many master’s students, often work as Research Assistants (RAs) or Teaching Assistants (TAs), with others working as administrative staff while pursuing their graduate degree. As a teaching and research institution, the value of this work is obvious. Without TAs, grades wouldn’t be submitted; without RAs, WPI would attract no grant funding; without administrative staffers, very little work could get done at all. Although faculty generally direct teaching and research, graduate workers perform an enormous share of the day-to-day work: office hours; proctoring exams; grading student work; running experiments; and applying for research funds. Stephen is a doctoral student in Biomedical Engineering, a co-chair of WPI-GWU, and an officer in Local 2322, which not only represents grads and res advisors at WPI, but thousands of academic and non-academic workers across New England, including similar workers at UMass Amherst and the University of Vermont. The GWU won its first contract in 2023 after several months of tough negotiations. Prior to the contract, grad workers could barely afford to live in Worcester, and a litany of safety problems plagued their research labs. Stephen told us that some of his colleagues were forced to live on food stamps and simply couldn’t make rent. The ambiguity of their student-worker status even led to delays and complications in receiving worker’s compensation for lab injuries. Their first contract changed everything. Texas Wasn’t Always a Right-Wing Evangelical Powerhouse

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: David Griscom ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-18T16:44:06+00:00

There’s a common view of religious history that holds that the further back you go, the stronger the smell of brimstone and the more conservative the beliefs. It isn’t true, and it obscures how much the human hand makes the world, including our ideas of the sacred. Joseph L. Locke’s new book One State Under God: A History of Religion in Texas is an important reminder that the conservative evangelical Christianity that dominates Texas, and through it has consolidated its influence over the country, was not a natural outgrowth of Christianity itself, but a product of historical developments. Texas today is at the forefront of the campaign to tear down the wall between church and state. The Bible has been reintroduced in Texas classrooms, along with a new law requiring classrooms to display the protestant version of the Ten Commandments. Texas gave the country the prosperity gospel preached by televangelists, including the megachurch pastor and multimillionaire Joel Osteen. The state has been the proving ground for a fiery marriage between evangelicalism and conservatism. Religion has been woven through Texas history, and in turn Texas has woven a particular form of religion into American life. But in One State Under God , Locke shows that the marriage of Texas conservatism and evangelicalism was never preordained. Indeed, Texas was once considered a “godless” land. Christianity did not come easily to Texas. The first imperial attempt to impose it failed. Spain saw Texas as a bulwark against French expansion but never conquered it, physically or religiously. The missions of San Antonio, ancient by American standards, were sparsely populated. When Mexico declared independence from Spain, only two priests remained in Texas — and neither, observed the Tejano revolutionary Juan Seguín, was particularly religious. A Spanish official lamented the failure to make Texas a Catholic land, writing that it remained “under the domination of the barbarian.” Locke takes us through Mexican and revolutionary Texas, where religious expansion ran into similar obstacles. For most of this period, the governance of Texas was an afterthought — the territory was administered as part of Coahuila y Tejas, literally tacked onto a neighboring Mexican state. And religion was an afterthought, too. There was scant religious infrastructure in early Texas. Most of the Anglo migrants were protestants of the frontier variety, whose faith ran on circuit riders and camp meetings rather than settled clergy and church buildings, and Mexico had little interest in supplying Texas with priests. Much of life was secular: children went unbaptized, marriages went unrecognized, and burials were conducted in secular graveyards. Texans today may be taken for a religious bunch, but in those years they were seen as living at the edge of civilization, godless and rough. As a Presbyterian minister wrote at the time, these Texans “generally appear in their personal depravity to have forgot if ever they did know or pretend to believe, that there was a God, or a devil,” and behaved as though “as soon as they had crossed the Sabine line, they considered also that they had passed over the line of spiritual accountability.” When conservative politicians call on Texas to embrace its Christian roots, then, it’s not entirely clear what roots they’re talking about. The Bible Belt Gets Its Buckle Anyone who has spent time in the South today can see the sway that Christianity holds over our politics. It wasn’t always so. According to Locke, a term like “Bible Belt” would have drawn puzzled looks in the early twentieth century. Nationally, “the South was not yet seen as an especially religious region, and religion did not seem particularly active in public life there.” Most religious Texans considered this a good thing. Separation of church and state was taken seriously by Texas politicians and Texas Baptists alike, who believed that it kept religion “pure and left the rest of the world to its hellish ways.” What, then, brought Texas evangelicals into politics? One State Under God supplies the answer: prohibition, the jingoism of World War I, and the Ku Klux Klan. The fight over alcohol was the opening salvo. Governor Oscar Colquitt defeated his prohibitionist challengers in 1910 by stoking the state’s long-standing opposition to religious rule, saying, “The place of a preacher is in the pulpit and not in politics.” But his victory was short-lived, as the forces that had amassed to outlaw drinking on religious grounds found they rather enjoyed politics as a means of social purification. By the early 1920s, the prohibitionists had won, the war had made loyalty a religious test, and the Klan had put a hundred thousand Texans in white robes. The preacher who best understood what all of that made possible was J. Frank Norris, who can be thought of as the nation’s first megachurch leader. Norris preached a fiery, reactionary Christianity, condemning communists, liberals, modernists, Catholics, and Jews. He turned on Baylor University, his own alma mater and the world’s largest Baptist college, hounding a professor out of his job for teaching evolution. He made common cause with the Klan. And he had a violent streak: Norris was tried for arson in 1912, and in 1926 he shot an unarmed man dead in his church office. He was acquitted both times. Starting with a congregation of little more than a dozen, Norris built a church of thousands, and his radio show and newspaper reached many more. He founded a second church in Detroit and spent the 1930s flying back and forth between the two, in the early days of aviation. Norris had influence, and he used it. When the Democrats nominated Al Smith for president in 1928, Norris raged against the Catholic from New York, prophesying that if a Catholic won power, “they would behead every Protestant preacher and disembowel every Protestant mother. They would burn to ashes every Protestant Church and dynamite every Protestant school.” Conspiratorial and paranoid religiosity had been let loose in Texas politics, and it would stay. God and Landlords The rise of conservative evangelical Christianity happened against the backdrop of two major mobilizations of working-class Texans. The first was the populist uprising, which united laborers and farmers, white and black, in a People’s Party that vied for power with the ruling Democrats. That movement was put down violently , and the defeat was followed by severe restrictions on the vote — not only for black Texans but for poor whites as well. Then the Socialist Party of Texas competed with the Democrats for the support of tenant farmers across the state. When James Edward “Pa” Ferguson won the governor’s mansion, he did it largely on a platform of tenant-farmer reforms lifted from the Socialist Party. Both movements leaned heavily on religion. Locke notes that Christianity shaped the populists, and that the movement borrowed organizational methods from the Methodists, like sending out traveling lecturers. As I observed in my own book on Texas’s populist past, Thomas Lewis Nugent, one of the movement’s major leaders and its candidate for governor, called the life of Jesus Christ the “beginning of Christian socialism.” Black populist leaders like John B. Rayner preached to rural Texans as well. But Locke argues that despite these more radical strains, “Texas’s new wealth infected the Baptists and the Methodists and the Presbyterians,” especially once the oil boom erupted. His account of the two Christianities that divided Texas through slavery and Reconstruction is the book at its strongest: one belonging to the slaveholders, which preached a paternalistic apologetics for chattel slavery, and one belonging to black Texans, which held out liberation for the enslaved and judgment for the wicked in the next life. It is a distinction he largely stops drawing once emancipation arrives — a shame, because the decades that follow are full of it. Take the example of John B. Rayner. After the defeat of the populist uprising, he began taking money from the same landlords and bosses he had once opposed and wound up in the employ of the lumber baron John Henry Kirby, no longer speaking of liberation or the cooperative commonwealth but of personal responsibility and the self-help programs of Booker T. Washington. Kirby at the time was trying to break the Brotherhood of Timber Workers, one of the first interracial unions in the South. What Rayner was hired to do, preachers were hired to do across the cotton fields of Texas and the South: attack the “sinful ways” of the poor and keep their attention off the men robbing them. This was a Christianity bankrolled by the wealthy to keep working people in line. Locke does not ignore the alliance between the evangelical movement and the rich; he shows in detail how religion in Texas was cultivated for political ends. What he leaves out is this particular use of it, as a weapon against the agrarian revolts of the populists and then the socialists, who had made religion a common language for uniting tenant farmers across the state. Texas is a big place with a long history, and any book about it has to make cuts. In the decades that followed, the familiar alliance between evangelical Christianity and free-market conservatism came into the open. As Locke notes, the change was captured in Ronald Reagan’s line to a gathering of evangelical leaders in Dallas in 1980: “I know that you can’t endorse me, but I want you to know that I endorse you.” That rally was organized by the Texas televangelist James Robison, and men like him had by then steamrolled what was left of the prohibitions on religion in politics. The long campaign to remake religion in the image of the Right was complete. Today Texans live in the evangelical right’s world: abortion is banned, the Ten Commandments hang in classrooms, and the Texas GOP has its eyes on ending no-fault divorce. But the great service of One State Under God is to show that this world was deliberately made — and that what was made can be unmade.

With her landmark 1999 debut, Who Paid the Piper? The CIA and the Cultural Cold War , Frances Stonor Saunders “single-handedly established a new branch of sub-history,” as novelist Ian McEwan observed. The book won many accolades, though sympathizers of Western anti-communism clamored to downplay its revelations: that between 1950 and 1967, intelligence agencies partnered in a covert front, launched by the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA), to manipulate Cold War intellectuals in a program whose basis was propaganda and censorship. With abstract art exhibits and classical music tours, the CIA’s Congress for Cultural Freedom also founded dozens of intellectual magazines (most famously Encounter in London) while its associated fronts funded press agencies, anti-communist student activists and labor unions, ethnic studies, and the pro-war Vietnam Lobby; sent moles into Hollywood back offices to censor scripts; and used its growing apparatus to enforce Western cultural domination. Though Saunders was born the week the Congress for Cultural Freedom was exposed in the New York Times as a CIA front, The Cultural Cold War , as her debut was called in the United States, was an invaluable guide to many writers publishing in her footsteps — including me, in writing my first book Finks: How the CIA Tricked the World’s Best Writers . Saunders died of a brain tumor on August 31 at age sixty. She published three other works of nonfiction, a feature documentary, and a miniseries for British Channel 4. After learning that her own manuscript was dropped by her original publisher, I came to view the censorship we face today — on college campuses, in nongovernmental organizations, in the media, and in the broader culture wars — as seeded by this official covert anti-communism revealed in The Cultural Cold War . If her editor had gotten his way, Frances Stonor Saunders’s history of literary spooks may have remained a casualty of censorship. Her book The Cultural Cold War: The CIA and the World of Arts and Letters examined the CIA’s meddling in literature, magazines, and media. But in a late draft, her editor insisted she praise the CIA as “with the angels.” Her refusal almost tanked the US edition. It’s worth celebrating Saunders’s investigative triumph — one that has spurred ongoing debate, helping launch an academic cottage industry that has been dubbed “cultural Cold War studies.” Scholar Edward Said called The Cultural Cold War “a major work of investigative history,” adding that “the dispiriting truth it reveals, or confirms, is that few of ‘our’ major intellectual and cultural figures resisted the blandishments of the CIA.” But today, many misrepresent what the cultural Cold War actually was, suggesting that the program was an unmitigated success, helping “win” the Cold War, or that vast swaths of the rebel left are “synthetic” still — that is, counterfeit, as if the Left were created out of whole, fake cloth by the state. When I met her for lunch on a rainy September day on Grove Street, she is still livid, twenty-five years later. I place a transcript of my 2016 interview with her on the table, and she orders smoked salmon and eggs with crème fraîche and capers. She makes small notes in the margins, amplifying or augmenting her struggle to tell the story of the Congress for Cultural Freedom (CCF) properly. “If I’m going to do this,” she vowed, “I’m going to be absolutely clear with anyone I’m talking to; I’m not doing anything off the record,” lest she continue “repeating some of the errors of the whole program.” After interviewing a key player in the Congress for Cultural Freedom, she grew demoralized, an obstacle course of sorts having spread before her. And yet, after wringing the truth from those who were expecting more praise and whitewash, she was most taken aback when her own editor nearly tanked the book before it could be published. Old News Starting out, one of the Congress’s sentinels, Irving Kristol, browbeat her with a disingenuous warning. “It’s an old story,” he said. “Everybody knows this. . . . Why are you doing this now?” Growing nervous, she “almost fell for it.” “Oh, God,” she recalls thinking, “there’s a whole library out here on this stuff,” and it was already mossy. To protect the secret program’s crown jewels, the old saw that the CIA’s involvement in American cultural institutions was an “old story” was honed. Overshadowed by Vietnam, the CCF scandal erupted in the New York Times eleven days after Saunders was born. Born in London, Saunders studied English at St Anne’s College, Oxford. On her mother’s side, she hails from a blue-­blood pedigree that occasionally embarrassed itself in correcting for extremism. (In a much-­discussed memoir, her mother exposed her grandmother’s Nazi sympathies). On her father’s side were Jews exiled to England during the war. The mash-up spawns a twin persona — ­ the dispassionate proceduralism of a well-­heeled Oxbridge barrister colliding with the radical cussing of a Vivienne Westwood Aries. Dressed in preppy but understated layers, she dons glasses with red frames and simple pearl earrings. The phrase she has done most to popularize was used in 1968 by the historian Christopher Lasch, who described the same New York Intellectuals like Kristol — who formed the American CCF and later neoconservatism — as a clique of “high-level McCarthyites” who sometimes supported Sen. Joseph McCarthy explicitly. Critics like Lasch went ignored in the debate that followed revelations of the CIA’s subsidies to intellectuals. It is only for this reason that those who further his critique — like Saunders — can be called “revisionists.” In this light, Saunders felt it to be “a story that hadn’t been told fully. I just felt like” Kristol’s “was a strategy and he was trying to put me off. He was calling all his friends, saying, ‘Don’t talk to this woman.’” So when the cohort insisted, “You’re fifty years too late,” she started to realize, “They don’t want me to do this.” One claim was, “Yes, it was a necessary evil” to use CIA funds, “but none of us did or said or wrote anything that we wouldn’t have otherwise, and freedom of expression was in no way curtailed. It was, instead, advanced by this [program].’ That was the Gloria Steinem line. We still hear it all the time.” (Steinem served as the director of the Independent Research Service, which sent American students to communist-sponsored international youth festivals to promote American-style politics and counter Soviet influences in those festivals, in the late 1950s and early 1960s. She long defended her engagement with the organization.) Saunders found it contradictory. “What a peculiar defense of the organization built covertly for the theft of culture — saying it didn’t matter because they were going to do it anyway.” On the other hand, some of those she interviewed were bluntly honest. “The ones I have more respect for said, ‘You know, it was a question of exigency, and we did it and we’re glad we did it. . . . But the number of people trying to keep every level of their involvement secret, it just made me think that these two things don’t sit together.” Digging into the archive, she read the former operatives’ letters. But the old secrecy kicked back in. A few of the guardians of free speech blocked her with threats over copyright. “You can’t on one hand say, ‘It was necessary and worth it, I’m proud I was associated with it,’ and at the same time say, ‘I don’t want any of the details of this to come out, and I’m not going to give you copyright to print my letters.” Saunders adopted a savvy counteroffensive, showing up to interviews with her notebook hidden, hoping to establish trust. In one case, she met a former CIA officer in a bar, as he drank himself silly on cheap wine. Her patience yielded if not a “smoking gun,” then an opening. After Thomas Braden worked directly for CIA Director Allen Dulles (under the pseudonym Homer D. Hoskins), in his post-­CIA life he was co­host of Crossfire and creator of My Three Sons . But sitting with Saunders, he let slip “that the CIA had a power of veto” over its editors and writers — ­ a euphemism for censorship. She convinced him to admit this by guessing, asserting it with conviction, watching him calculate, then agree. Initially, it stunned her. “Okay, that’s interesting,” she recalls thinking. But again the narrative defaulted to stonewalling; and her doubts returned. “For a long time, I believed pretty much what I’d been told, that it was really theoretical,” that the veto “was never used, and it didn’t need to be, because everybody was seeing it from the same” script. As the cover stories knocked her off the scent, she came to view the uniformity as “interesting in itself. Because people were agreeing” on what to publish. By uncovering the agency’s veto, she wielded new leverage in interviews. As she read Encounter ’s editorial letters, she knew what to look for, and found “at least twenty or thirty instances of direct intervention stroke censorship by the CIA acting as executive editors.” (“Stroke” in the Queen’s English is like “slash” in the colonies.) After our first interview, she emails me a list of fifty entries. Referenced by date and subject, each letter records CIA officers invoking the need to censor its authors — ­ exercising this infamous “veto” over its editors’ judgements. In one letter, CCF executive Nicolas Nabokov reminds his editors of a rule from the magazine’s founding: that anything politically controversial must be okayed at Paris headquarters. The letter proves that agency censorship was explicit and structural. When enforced, it warned editors away from controversy or criticism of US policy — ­ especially foreign policy. For three decades, CCF veterans had maintained three holy tenets: 1.) they never censored; 2.) the CIA never meddled; and 3.) they published great writers. With the Nabokov letter and the larger batch, Saunders killed the first two tenets. (Gabriel García Márquez caveated the third, when he wrote his editor, “I will never write for you again.”) But the CCF would not relinquish its story readily, and she considered how to frame it against an anticipated chorus of denial. For the examples of censorship she found, she imagined “many, many more. But I would still say that it’s a small percentage of the total output, in all media, of what the Congress for Cultural Freedom and these other CIA fronts were doing. It would be wrong to say that they had a department in the CIA that was frantically trying to keep this riotous lot in order.” But if the CIA embarrassed its editors by making them undo an acceptance, ­ as happened in the 1950s,­ the editors’ embarrassment was itself instructive. Is Trump Crashing the Dollar Empire?

With more than half of global trade conducted in dollars, the United States is still the world’s largest economy as well as its dominant military and political power. But Donald Trump, in his second presidential term, has put both these facts at unprecedented risk, using America’s global position for political gain and personal enrichment all while undermining the pillars of the nation’s financial dominance, such as the rule of law and the security of global trade flows. Yet according to economist Mona Ali, little of this behavior is truly new. The global financial system is, at its core, a political system, though Trump and his advisers are admittedly wielding this system more carelessly than any administration has in a generation. In a wide-ranging discussion, Ali explains who benefits from the dollar’s dominance and whether the world’s reserve currency has any plausible challengers. Jacobin It is often said that the dollar is the world’s reserve currency. What does this mean, and how does it relate to the currency’s dominance? Mona Ali The dollar’s dominance is often attributed to its status as the key international reserve asset. This shorthand lends the impression that money is a commodity — a thing — when in fact, for the most part, money is credit — a social relation. While it is true that trillions of dollars are held as safe assets by investors and governments around the world, the bulk of these dollars in countries’ foreign reserves are credit contracts, predominantly US Treasuries. While dollar dominance is often attributed to its reserve currency role, the dollar’s entrenchment in the financial system arises from its dominance in international credit creation. The dollar is the unit of account undergirding the world’s deepest and most dispersed credit system. This includes, but is hardly limited to, Treasuries and bank loans. The power to create dollar-denominated credit isn’t restricted to the United States’ monetary authorities; foreign banks issue more dollar loans than US banks. Crises in the dollar system — a globe-spanning credit regime — have global consequences. When excess credit creation results in financial crisis, the United States’ central bank, the Federal Reserve, steps in to stabilize dollar markets. While analysts emphasize the ex post, ad hoc nature of Fed bailouts, the Fed’s interventions reveal the stratification embedded in the international monetary hierarchy. Rich countries with standing access to dollar loans through Fed swap lines enjoy ease of access to emergency dollar liquidity. Low- and middle-income countries, which do not have easy access to the Fed’s credit facilities, must face discipline and punishment by international bond markets. Facing acute dollar shortages, many countries have little choice but to seek costly and conditionality-laden credit from the International Monetary Fund (IMF). Almost a hundred countries were in IMF loan programs in 2022–23. The dollar’s public backstop — Fed lines for the few, IMF loans for the rest — is imperial in structure. Jacobin How is this position used to advance the United States’ interests? Mona Ali Perhaps paradoxically, the dollar is also the preeminent source of US hegemony. The global demand for dollars is robust. Because the US monetary authorities issue the world’s most powerful currency, the United States enjoys a softer budget constraint. It allows the United States to devote more than a trillion dollars toward military expenditures every year. But as Darializa Avila Chevalier has pointed out in her “Babies, Not Bombs” platform, it is the hallmark of a decaying polity that it neglects working-class communities at home while fighting pointless wars abroad. The fact is that the dollar empire is largely in private hands. Like dark matter in the physical universe, dollar balance sheets are invisible to the public eye. Dollar dominance runs the gamut from the centrality of dollar-denominated debt instruments in financial markets to the sensitivity of the global economy to movements in the dollar’s exchange rate, which systemically impacts global trade and financial conditions. While Treasuries and the bulk of US bank loans have been backstopped by the Federal Reserve, large parts of the system aren’t governed by US monetary authorities and policymakers. Most credit contracts in the global dollar system aren’t protected by the Fed. These shadowy parts of the dollar system exist offshore and off–balance sheet, in short-term funding instruments such as foreign exchange (FX) swaps. Derivative contracts in which one currency is exchanged against another, FX swaps are a predominant source of dollar borrowing even if, technically speaking, they aren’t credit instruments. With trades averaging $4 trillion per day , the FX swaps market, in which one currency is exchanged for another by means of a derivative contract, is by far the largest market in dollars globally. Lightly regulated, large in transactional amounts, and informal in governance, which occurs by way of a voluntary global FX code, the market in FX swaps is at times prone to a liquidity mirage. In other words, true liquidity may be overstated. These instruments are the “known unknowns” of the dollar system. The potential vulnerabilities in this fast-growing $130 trillion market remain obfuscated. It should be clear that the markets that comprise the dollar system aren’t just prone to volatility; they are dysfunctional. Rather than raising capital for factories or infrastructure, dollar funding markets are largely in the business of refinancing debt contracts. Three out of every four transactions in financial markets involve refinancing of some sort. Jacobin Some economists have described the United States’ ability to use massive global demand for its currency as an exorbitant privilege, because it allows America to run large deficits and live beyond its means. Is this a privilege that benefits all Americans, or even all sections of American capital, equally? Mona Ali For several decades, the United States has run trade deficits, the largest component of its current account balance, by importing more goods than it exports. The US current account balance (-$1.18 trillion in 2025) gets much more attention, but its financial account balance, which represents net US borrowing from foreigners, is larger still (+$1.3 trillion in 2025). The United States has principally shaped global imbalances, the large financial and trade imbalances that have been a defining characteristic of the world economy over the last quarter of a century. As the issuer of world money, the United States can finance its trade deficits more easily than other countries can. At present, the United States’ ability to borrow via the Treasury market — the deepest pool of government debt in the global liquidity system, one-third of which is held overseas — depends less on sovereigns such as Japan or China and more on the calculus of private investors such as hedge funds. From 2022 to 2023, higher interest rates and a stronger dollar drew 41 percent of global financial inflows to the United States. This uphill flow of capital into the United States was even greater in 2025. Trade imbalances have been explained in binary terms: as either benign or downright bad. The twentieth-century American economist Charles P. Kindleberger held a benign view of the US balance-of-payments deficits. The United States runs a current account deficit, he argued, so that it can throw dollars into the world economy. For Kindleberger, the role of the United States as the world’s banker was akin to peacekeeping. His more subtle point was that US deficits should be understood as deficits only in accounting terms. The fact is that US trade deficits and even larger financial inflows derive from the centrality of the United States in global trade and financial networks. US consumers have benefited from cutting-edge and cheaper products made in the rest of the world. Their spending in turn galvanizes growth in poorer places. Financial flows are much larger than trade flows. Global finance has profited from dollar trades and higher US interest rates. American exceptionalism is usually understood in purely financial terms, rooted in the power of the dollar, yet it also derives from the fact that US corporations capture the lion’s share of profits across a whole host of far-flung supply chains. Reduced costs from economies of scale and cheaper labor involved in overseas production redound to US firms and consumers. The ensuing US trade deficit is correlated with rising corporate profits. The dollar is not fully to blame for deindustrialization in the United States. Offshoring was a choice made by US corporations. However, the fact that prosperity in the Global South depends on accumulating dollars by selling to the US market makes for a very asymmetric global economy. The world economy runs on dollars, but from the perspective of the rest of the world, dollar dependence isn’t always benign; it can be explosive, as in 2022–23, when dollar shortages made for sovereign debt crises. Even for the US government, whose borrowing shows little sign of receding as interest rates on Treasuries climb (most recently because of the United States’ war on Iran), it is clear that borrowing from credit markets isn’t limitless. It involves costs — net interest payments are more than a trillion dollars this year — even for the monetary superpower. And there are real costs, such as the time to make weapon systems; concerns about exhausting matériel such as interceptors have driven the current pause in US air strikes on Iran. Jacobin Trump said tariffs would rebalance trade and strengthen America’s economic position. What has actually happened to the dollar, the Treasury market, and the trade deficit since Liberation Day? Mona Ali Having declared a trade war on allies and adversaries alike, Trump momentarily tarnished the safe-haven appeal of the dollar and the United States. A 10 percent decline in the previously expensive dollar has been the one silver lining of the Liberation Day storm. However, capital flows into the United States were unprecedented in 2025. Trump’s decisions are whiplash-inducing. While he has expressed preference for a lower dollar for, among other things, “rebalancing” trade, what the next four years of on-and-off presidential decrees will do to the dollar’s status will ultimately be decided by how financial markets, whose size vastly outweighs global trade, digest forthcoming shocks. While market volatility hurts households and Main Street, trading volatility has proven hugely beneficial for the big global banks such as JPMorgan Chase and Goldman Sachs, whose trading revenues have been at a decade high . The tremors in the Treasury market, adjacent Treasury repo market, and far bigger interest-rate swap derivatives market in April 2025, evidenced by widening interest-rate swap spreads, did not threaten US credit markets. However, Trump’s blustering that the United States should annex Canada and Greenland prompted Canadian and Danish pension funds to announce they will invest less in US private equity. As inflationary concerns rise again with the Iran war in 2026, Treasury yields have been going up once more. For the first time since 2007, thirty-year Treasury bonds were auctioned at 5 percent. With rising interest rates and the doubling of hedge-fund holdings of US Treasuries, the likelihood of an untoward credit event has increased. Trump’s much-watered-down tariff regime has diminished the US current account deficit only slightly, while the US financial account continues to record new peak inflows. These inflows militate against the correction of trade deficits. Rebalancing these annual trillion-dollar imbalances through tariff revenues is a fantasy. Jacobin What would happen in the event of an economic crash? Mona Ali Crashing the global economy is a surefire way to reduce US external imbalances. The last time the US trade deficit sharply declined was during the Great Recession. As the global financial crisis deepened, by October 2009, unemployed workers in the United States topped 15.7 million . Despite the turmoil, the dollar remained a safe-haven asset, in part because of the institutional support of the Federal Reserve, which pumped liquidity into offshore dollar markets by way of dollar swap lines . Also at play, albeit to a lesser extent, was adept financial diplomacy. Hank Paulson, the US Treasury secretary at the time, convinced China not to sell its holdings of US debt despite significant losses on China’s portfolio, which was heavy on agency mortgage-backed securities from the US housing market crash. Since then, portfolio losses on its US debt holdings as well as domestic political pressures have led China to reduce the share of its official FX reserves denominated in dollars. Illustration by Ben Denzer Jacobin What are the geopolitical threats to dollar dominance? Mona Ali The US-led immobilization of Russia’s reserves in 2022 highlighted the dangers of dollar dependence. There was much talk of de-dollarization among the BRICS nations. In 2024, China formally announced its plans to internationalize the renminbi. Early in the Iran war, the use of China’s Cross-Border Interbank Payment System (CIPS) ticked up as a mechanism for payments of internationally restricted oil. Alternatives to the dollar and its infrastructures, such as central bank digital currency and the China-dominated mBridge platform, have demonstrated increased activity after the breakout of the war. The United States’ and Israel’s unprovoked bombardment of Iran and Lebanon and Iran’s retaliatory strikes on US military bases and energy infrastructures in the Gulf are perhaps the most serious challenge to US power in decades. The blockade of the Hormuz Strait has led to an unprecedented supply shock to the global oil market. By reducing its oil imports, China has helped stabilize that market. Its actions stand in sharp contrast to the disorder inflicted by the United States. Trump’s 2025 tariffs had been particularly harsh toward China. From February 2025 onward, his administration ratcheted up tariffs on China from 10 percent to 145 percent . Although it ultimately lowered them, imports from China faced an additional “fentanyl” tariff of 10 percent on top of the baseline 10 percent general tariff. As Trump’s use of emergency powers to inflict tariffs on the rest of the world was struck down by the US Supreme Court, the executive office adopted a new tariff regime of 10 or 12.5 percent plus sector-specific tariffs using different legal provisioning, in this case sections 122 and 232 of the Trade Act of 1974. Trump has recently imposed 25 percent tariffs on Brazil and 50 percent tariffs on Canada using varying legal codes. Despite the aggression unleashed by Trump on adversaries and allies alike — five months into the Iran war, he raised tariffs on sixty countries — the penchant for dollar assets hasn’t diminished. While financial inflows into the United States have contracted thus far in 2026, the share of dollar holdings in sovereign foreign reserves has slightly increased. We are in a period of flux. Jacobin Are there any genuine potential alternatives to the dollar as the world’s preeminent currency? Mona Ali The development of alternatives to dollar-dominated financial infrastructures in Europe and Asia is still in its early stages. A small but significant appetite for gold on the part of certain well-stocked central banks appears to be an inflation or geopolitical hedge rather than a threat to the dollar’s dominance. How much should we read into declining foreign holdings of US securities in New York? Could large Treasury holders such as Saudi Arabia or China, at least in theory, leverage their holdings in geopolitical gamesmanship? Saudi Arabia closely hews to US interests, but along with Iran, Indonesia, Egypt, and the United Arab Emirates, it has joined the growing BRICS coalition. China has expressed a lack of interest in shaping global geopolitics, which is why the symbolism of China’s Ministry of Finance issuing $2 billion in dollar-denominated bonds in Riyadh turned a lot of heads in November 2024. The yields on these two different bond issuances were just one and three basis points above US Treasuries for the three- and five-year maturities. While the bond issuance was small — symbolic more than anything else — such an extraordinarily low cost of sovereign borrowing was unprecedented in the offshore dollar bond market. In 2025, China bested this record, issuing $4 billion in dollar bonds in Hong Kong at rates equivalent to US Treasuries. China, which has a stellar credit rating, is an active player in the global dollar system both as creditor and, more recently, as borrower. It has increasingly shown that it too can play hardball. China retaliated against Trump’s assaults by imposing tariffs on the United States, temporarily pausing its imports of US liquefied natural gas, and suspending exports of critical minerals and rare earth magnets to the United States — materials critical to American auto, semiconductor, and aerospace manufacturing. After the Iran war began, China issued blocking orders against US sanctions on Iranian oil, permitting its processing in its small “teapot” refineries. More recently, China has temporarily limited the reexport of helium, necessary for semiconductor chip manufacturing, which it imports from Russia. China faces its own troubles, however, not least of which is slowing growth. So-called virtuous creditors (trade-surplus-generating countries) face certain problems associated with low yields on government debt in the global dollar system. Low interest rates on China’s government bonds have dampened foreign investor demand. For China, internationalizing the renminbi through more debt issuance will involve reducing its deflationary trade surpluses. It appears that China’s path toward hegemony in the Global South is evolving from Belt and Road Initiative credit arrangements to green and AI-related investment. Larger renminbi and euro debt markets are easy wins for China and the European Union, ones they should double down on, especially as dominance without hegemony gets even uglier.

by Travis Covitz This month, I re-read To Kill A Mockingbird by Harper Lee for the first time since my freshman year of high school (which overlapped with the first wave of Black Lives Matter uprisings). Mockingbird was written by Lee (a Southern white woman) in the early 1960s; it follows the girlhood of Jean Louise “Scout” Finch, who is white and the daughter of a lawyer, in Depression-era Alabama. A major plot point of the book is Scout’s father, Atticus Finch, defending a local Black man who has been charged with rape of a white woman. Atticus is posed as a noble white hero in contrast with the ‘backwards’ racists in the countryside. Reading in 2026, the juxtaposition of the police brutality in Ferguson and the lawfare in Mockingbird seems like it was just begging for commentary. But at my school in the Phoenix, Arizona suburbs, where we had a very small Black population, I have no memory of such a conversation. What I do remember is, at some point during the year, one of the few Black girls in my grade commented that the murder of Michael Brown echoed the murder of Trayvon Martin. I know that she saw the parallels between Scout’s fictional world and the world we found ourselves growing up in. My mom has always been a huge fan of Mockingbird and the Finch family in particular. As the youngest in her family, my mom related heavily to Scout’s perspective as a young child surrounded by adults. Perhaps because of her connection with Scout, or perhaps just because of her own liberal white sympathies, my mom glorified Atticus as a paragon of virtue. I remember seeing a lot of discourse in 2020 about how white people preferred quoting this fictional white lawyer, instead of living Black people, to demonstrate their values of racial justice. My re-reading of Mockingbird was, in part, a desire to revisit the character of Atticus Finch and see just how valiant he really was. Was he a principled anti-racist or a white savior? I found myself thoroughly underwhelmed, but not surprised, with how Atticus actually behaves in the book. And then I read the sequel. Go Set A Watchman takes place in that same small Alabama town, centered on that same Finch girl (who is now a woman), about two decades later. Scout is in her mid-twenties (actually very close to my current age) and visiting her hometown on a trip away from her life in New York City. She is forced to confront the imperfections in the moral characters of her father (Atticus) and lover (Hank), who have joined the local white Citizens Council in protest of the 1954 Brown v. Board of Education decision. Watchman was only published in 2015, and takes place after the events of Mockingbird , but it is actually more interesting than a mere ‘sequel’. Lee wrote Watchman in the 1950s, as the first draft of this story, only for it to later be edited into the narrative we know as Mockingbird . Reading Watchman greatly enriched my appreciation of Mockingbird – but I think that Watchman was only able to be so profound because of that decade of relationship I already had with the Mockingbird story. I felt that the loss of Atticus’ heroism, rather than a betrayal, was a very plausible continuation of the character. In fact, I think that character journey is an important one to read for white readers who, like the young Scout, saw Atticus as a perfect gentleman. In my experience, Mockingbird is used by liberal Americans to assuage their guilt over racism, by allowing them to reassure themselves that they’re ‘one of the good ones’. It paints a picture of racism as a personal problem rather than a political one. The subtextual rebukes to this narrative which I found in Watchman actually allowed me to more deeply appreciate the world that Lee built. By showing how a kind, honorable white man – who treats Black people as equals in daily life – can still feel so threatened by the idea of Black people in power that he organizes to prevent it, Watchman illustrates how racism is a political issue, not just a personal moral failing. In Watchman , Scout is positioned as the moral hero in contrast to Atticus, but not necessarily enlightened, which I feel is the correct choice for the character. She is still learning the ways of the world, but we can see that she has deep moral convictions that ground her. If Scout had been written as perfect, it would have ruined the whole story, which is about coming-of-age and realizing that your heroes are only human. My revisiting of Lee’s work has been its own coming-of-age story, after all. Even though I generally appreciated the direction of Watchmen , I disagree with some of the political conclusions Lee offers. But this way in which I disagree with Scout paradoxically makes me relate to her even more. Just as her long-time deification of Atticus was confronted by a painful reality when Scout realizes she has her own moral positions, I had to realize my own values are based on the people I live and organize with – not just the books I’ve read. If you remember reading Mockingbird , and especially if you remember quoting Atticus Finch, I would highly recommend Watchman . It might be painful, but I think you will be all the better for realizing that even the Finches have flaws – and wondering what actual anti-racism would look like. Social Democracy Won the Century and Lost the Future

On Sunday, Sweden’s Social Democratic Party (SAP) emerged as the largest party in an election that will be remembered for all the wrong reasons. Magdalena Andersson is poised to return as prime minister, with her four-party bloc holding a one-seat majority in a 349-seat Riksdag. The SAP won just 28 percent of the vote, its worst showing in more than 110 years. This is what victory looks like today for the most electorally successful left-of-center party in history. The SAP went from pioneering social democratic governance, ruling almost uninterruptedly for nearly half a century, and even debating the transfer of large firms to worker ownership to serving as a fading bulwark against the far right, with little more than a quarter of the electorate behind it. The Swedish “model” can only be described in the past tense. How that happened is not a story of betrayal. It is a story about what a strategy for reforming capitalism can achieve and about the point where it runs into the structure of capitalism itself. In 1976, Swedish Prime Minister Olof Palme remarked that there were two paths for socialists: “Either to return to Stalin and Lenin, or take the road that joins the tradition of social democracy.” As the leader of the state that embodied the latter, his choice was obvious. The Swedish model had such prestige that even a Gaullist like French President Georges Pompidou said his ideal society was “Sweden — with a bit more sun.” But though the divergent paths of socialism could not have seemed starker in Palme’s day, it wasn’t always so. In the immediate aftermath of the Russian Revolution, significant social democratic minorities joined communist splits, but most social democrats rejected insurrection and accommodated themselves to the democratic republic as the political form for their ambitions. At the same time, almost all these social democrats were still doctrinally Marxist and shared a horizon of socialism. Their parties had been built on the model of the German Social Democratic Party’s 1891 Erfurt Program, which married a maximal goal — the socialization of all private production — to a minimum program of immediate reforms, and which assumed that capitalism’s own development would eventually deliver the first by way of the second. For most, that meant a nationalized economy in which the tyranny of the market gave way to rational planning. They wanted to bring about a successor system to capitalism and doubted how much could be changed within capitalism. Social democracy never achieved its desired ends, but the reforms it brought about proved successful far beyond expectation. Sweden in the 1970s was not simply the most livable society in history; it was also the European country where, after World War II, socialists got the furthest along in undermining capital’s power. While capitalists worried about Nikita Khrushchev’s shoe-banging promises to bury the West, another threat to capital was emerging in Scandinavia, where the combination of a universal welfare state, full employment, and centralized unions gave labor enormous power. Swedish trade unions even came out with a proposal for wage-earner funds in 1976 that would have slowly socialized private firms. How Sweden’s social democrats got to that point, and why their experiment eventually fell apart, is an unlikely, instructive story. Socialists in Office Yet to understand the success of postwar Sweden, we first need to understand the failures of interwar social democracy and the important lesson they offer about the pitfalls socialists encounter when governing without a plan to achieve economic and political change. European socialists were presented with the chance to move from opposition to power more quickly than many imagined. They came out of World War I with a great deal of legitimacy — in some cases, as in Germany, because elites were discredited; in others, partly because of their own wartime support for the national cause. As Karl Kautsky put it in 1924, “We had learned how to be an opposition” before the war. Now “we had to take over Government, and that in the fullest sense; in industry, in the localities, in the State.” But his party, like other socialist parties during the interwar years, governed only through minority or coalition governments. Left parties had varying success pushing through democratic reforms. They tried to remove any existing barriers to universal suffrage and democratize upper houses of parliament. Surviving monarchies across Europe were stripped of their political power. Like his predecessor Jean Jaurès, French socialist Léon Blum saw the republican state as a tool to “define, protect, and guarantee the condition of the working class.” The radical dream — replacing capitalism with a socialist economy operating for the common good — was still alive. In the immediate postwar years, strike waves created fertile terrain for new demands, and with the onset of the Great Depression, capitalist collapse became a reality. Nationalizing big firms and introducing economic planning would be the first steps. But social democrats only had a vague idea of what they wanted to do. Indeed, outside of France, no firms were nationalized by interwar social democracy at the federal level (despite socialists participating in eight other West European governments). Instead, socialists formed commissions to study the subject, grappling for the first time with the technical difficulties of constructing a new political economy. Not much resulted from these commissions, and even Kautsky was forced to admit within a few years that “the creation of a socialistic organization is therefore not so simple a process as we used to think.” The 1929–31 British Labour government under Ramsay MacDonald was the most extreme example of interwar futility. Labour had long been more moderate than many of its European counterparts; the party eschewed Marxism and from the beginning operated within a liberal-constitutional framework. It was a party driven by trade unions’ interests, and it never had the same radical ideological influences as the German Social Democratic Party (SPD). Labour was refused admittance into the Second International for years for its emphasis on class collaboration, but it took a turn to the left after the Great War. Clause IV of its constitution, adopted in 1918, called for “the common ownership of the means of production, distribution and exchange.” The party’s first stint in power came after the 1923 general election, in which Labour won over a million fewer votes than the Conservative Party but was able to form a minority government with Liberal support. The experiment only lasted ten months, and with fewer than a third of the seats in Parliament, MacDonald was unable to pass anything other than minor education, housing, and employment reforms. A minority government always runs on borrowed time, but Labour also had to contend with a red-baiting Conservative campaign challenging its mild diplomatic overtures to the young Soviet Union. In 1929, Labour ran on a platform calling for public works construction and a reduction in the workweek to combat unemployment. It was rewarded with a 136-seat gain, making it the leading party in Parliament, though it was short of a majority. Again Labour was dependent on Liberal Party backing. MacDonald’s second ministry was formed in June 1929, just a few months before the start of the Great Depression. The timing couldn’t have been worse for Labour’s reform agenda. As unemployment rose, the party leadership clung to a rigid economic orthodoxy rather than expanding a public works program. The leaders wanted to reassure markets, and they faced a run on the pound and a growing deficit. Advocating austerity, MacDonald reasoned that defending the currency was a more urgent task than fighting unemployment, and that maintaining free trade and “the strictest regard” to the prevailing economic wisdom would in time allow the unemployed to be reabsorbed into industry. Their urgent task was to avoid shipwrecking democracy “on the hard rock of finance.” MacDonald himself came from exceptionally humble origins, but he clashed with the trade unions and saw himself as a responsible steward of an entire society, not just a class. Some Labour parliamentarians closer to the unions opposed cuts to unemployment benefits and welfare, advocating increased state planning and spending instead. Though they had far different political aims from MacDonald’s, most of the extraparliamentary left shared the party leadership’s belief that not much could be done through government. “No matter how able, how sincere, and how sympathetic the Labour men and women may be who undertake to administer capitalism, capitalism will bring their undertaking to disaster,” an article in the Socialist Party of Great Britain’s Socialist Standard would say. The MacDonald government ruled without attempting to bring about a socialist alternative and without believing that it could reform the existing system. At best, it reassured workers that they alone would not be asked to sacrifice in an era of scarcity, but that fatalism helped bring Labour to disaster. In August 1931, MacDonald broke with his party and formed a National Government with Conservatives and Liberals. In October, that coalition won a landslide, while Labour suffered a crushing defeat. It was the economist John Maynard Keynes, a liberal who believed socialists were well-intentioned idiots, who presented the best approach of the time to taming capitalism. The methods laid out in his 1936 work, The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money , once implemented, would help spur employment, ensure productive investment, and mitigate crises. Before the Keynesian Revolution, the reigning classical theory claimed that cyclical swings in output and employment would be self-adjusting — as aggregate demand fell, production and employment would decline, along with prices and wages. Lower prices and wages would then encourage capitalists to make employment-generating capital investments, restoring growth. Any interference in that cycle would only prolong agony for workers. The Great Depression, however, was not going away. Wages were low, but unemployment remained high. Keynes advocated a countercyclical fiscal response: deficit spending, tax cuts, and other measures to stimulate aggregate demand during a recession, and tax increases and spending cuts when times were good. During MacDonald’s second government, however, Keynes had not yet published his General Theory , though he already advocated public works to combat unemployment. Just as the workers inspired by Ferdinand Lassalle in the nineteenth century clung to a belief in an “iron law of wages” that limited the gains of trade unionism, it would be a struggle to disabuse the twentieth-century workers’ movement of orthodox economics. Two years after its sympathetic remark on the “sincere” effort of MacDonald’s Labour Party, the Socialist Standard would look back on the experience of government as definitive proof that “it is not possible for the Labour Party or any other party to administer capitalism in such a way that the workers’ problems can be solved within the framework of the existing system.” The Popular Front’s Limits The Popular Front government of French socialist leader Léon Blum (1936–37) was more determined than MacDonald’s to deliver change. French socialists had lost much of their industrial base to the new communist movement but were still very much Marxists. Rebuilding the party’s infrastructure throughout the 1920s, Blum grappled with the question of why and under what conditions a socialist would enter government. He distinguished between the “exercise of power” (taking office to prepare the groundwork for socialism) and the “conquest of power” (the actual dismantling of capitalism). In the end, Blum settled for “the occupation of power,” to keep it out of the grasp of fascists. When the Jewish radical Blum rose to power in 1936, the antisemitic politician Xavier Vallat complained, “For the first time this ancient Gallo-Roman land will be governed by a Jew.” Just before becoming prime minister, Blum was dragged from a car and beaten, nearly to death, by a right-wing mob. A picture of him, heavily bandaged with swollen features, appeared on the March 9, 1936, cover of Time magazine. Reactionaries hated Blum both for being a Jew and for being a socialist. He could have complained that his immediate ambitions were not so shocking. The French Communist Party supported the Blum government, but, against his wishes and those of its own leadership, it was pressured by Moscow to stay out of the cabinet. Under the sway of the Comintern’s Third Period strategy, from the summer of 1928 until a year or two after the rise of Nazism in 1933, the communist parties had seen reformist social democrats as their primary enemy. They even went as far as to call them “social fascists.” But by 1934, relations between socialists and communists in France had become more fraternal. In a dramatic about-face, the Comintern began seeking alliances — “popular fronts” — with other left movements where possible. The justification for staying out of Blum’s cabinet in 1936 was not leftist purity. In fact, Joseph Stalin was worried that Communist involvement would scare away the centrist Radical Party’s support for the Popular Front. The election of Blum’s socialist government, however, unleashed a massive wave of industrial action. Over two million workers took part in strikes, occupying factories and paralyzing production. Marceau Pivert, leader of the Socialist Party’s radical left, proclaimed that “everything is possible” in the new environment. Business leaders appealed to Blum to restore order. The result was the Matignon Accords, which secured collective bargaining rights, strengthened union protections, and provided substantial wage increases. Separate legislation gave French workers the forty-hour week and two weeks of paid vacation. Exhausted but overjoyed, millions flocked to the countryside and the sea for the first time that summer. The dignity these reforms afforded to working people was undeniable. Though they were the product of grassroots rebellion, not Blum’s program, they couldn’t have been implemented without the Popular Front in power. The reforms also contained the seeds of their undoing. The upsurges of May and June 1936 triggered capital flight and a business counteroffensive over the implementation of the reforms. With political instability growing, Blum’s middle-class coalition partners abandoned the fight. The leader had neither the support nor the resolve to pursue more radical measures or to offer adequate aid to his fellow socialist-republicans fighting a bloody civil war against fascists in Spain. Blum was pushed out of power again in 1938, all the while pleading that he had attempted to be a “loyal manager of capitalism.” Blum might have been selling his reforms and radical vision short. But unable to protect its policies, the Popular Front in France was little more successful, in the end, than the first two British Labour Party governments. It was only in Sweden that interwar socialists were able to mount a serious challenge to fiscal orthodoxy. Swedish economists had long pursued heterodox economic ideas, and starting in the 1930s, the SAP put them into practice. Building the Swedish Model Accounts of the rise of social democracy in Sweden often focus on the country’s exceptional features. Its civic culture, limited state repression, and even racial homogeneity are commonly invoked. But on the whole, the country’s left faced similar challenges to its counterparts elsewhere; it just managed to find ways to overcome them. One relevant difference was that the nation underwent industrialization relatively late, in the 1870s. Trade unionism expanded in the following decades, and the Swedish Trade Union Confederation (LO) was founded in 1898. This relatively late development gave advocates of industrial unionism greater scope to organize across craft divisions. The late start meant that Swedish unionism developed under the ideological influence of socialism — the SAP was founded in 1889, the same year as the Second International. The party’s founders were drawn to socialism through the example of movements in Denmark and Germany, and the ideology quickly took root in Sweden. Socialists faced the intransigent opposition of elites, with a 1902 New York Times article describing battles between workers and capitalists and fears of the “dreaded red flag” flying in a Sweden that was only rivaled by Russia as “the most feudal and oligarchical country in Europe.” Sweden was described similarly in SAP literature as an “armed poorhouse.” Sweden did not grant universal male suffrage until the early twentieth century, meaning that, like the Bolsheviks, Swedish socialists were forced to focus most of their initial efforts on the shop floor rather than in parliament. In the political struggle for civil reforms, socialists proved themselves abler than the country’s liberals. As in Germany, the limited power of elected office also papered over potential differences between a would-be parliamentarist right and more radical forces. Yet in Sweden, under the leadership of Hjalmar Branting, the Social Democrats were able to maintain a relatively stable broad tent and engage in productive coalition work with liberal and agrarian forces. Despite some reformist inclinations, from early on the Swedish movement was built on socialist ideological grounds: it advocated policies that bridged gaps between craft and industrial workers, and it put emphasis on uplifting the most poorly paid. Social Democrats consistently prioritized universal programs — of benefit to both the poor and farmers — and not just the narrow interests of workers. Instead of pursuing shortcuts in their early years in opposition, Sweden’s socialists began to build a hegemony more durable than that of other Second International parties. By the 1920s, things were going according to plan: the struggle for political democracy had been largely successful, and as a result the party had a growing electoral mandate. Early SAP minority governments, however, proved unable to usher in phase two — the winning of social democracy. That began to change in 1932, when the party embarked on nearly half a century of almost uninterrupted rule. The SAP had campaigned on an expansion of public works and more state intervention in the economy and, after taking power, began to implement some of the countercyclical policies forsaken by socialists elsewhere. The real breakthrough, however, came later in the decade, when the SAP formed a parliamentary coalition with the Agrarian Party, and the LO negotiated the 1938 Saltsjöbaden Agreement, or Basic Agreement, with the powerful Swedish Employers’ Confederation (SAF). Reconciling working-class interests with those of small farmers meant that nationalization was off the table for the time being. The agreement consolidated a compromise whose foundations had been laid in 1906, when the LO recognized management’s “right to manage” — to direct the labor process and make personnel decisions — in exchange for employers’ recognition of workers’ right to organize. Planning was gradually redefined from state nationalization to public investment and economic forecasting. The party had made a significant shift from representing workers’ interests in opposition to constructing a cooperative “people’s home” for the entire population. It recognized that prosperity depended on growth, and that there was no immediate alternative to private enterprise. Way back in 1897, amid pitched industrial battles, Swedish socialists made their stated goal “the fostering and development of intellectual and material culture.” Nationalization, at the time, was their assumed means, but the goal was more open-ended. Unlike MacDonald’s Labour Party, however, they didn’t capitulate to the market as it was but made a radical attempt to change how it operated. The Swedish model matured after the war. During the gestation period of the SAP’s 1944 “postwar” program, Finance Minister Ernst Wigforss argued that the concentration of economic power was the problem, not necessarily private ownership. It was another acknowledgment that Sweden’s main issue was underdevelopment, and that it was better for labor to share part of a growing pie with capitalists than to try to capture all of a small one. Yet the SAP did not rule out immediate socialization if the tamed private market didn’t prove itself compatible with the party’s values and goals. The debates at the time show a party that still took its commitment to socialism seriously. Some socialists even shared the expectation of Minister of Social Affairs Gustav Möller that a wave of revolutions might follow the war’s conclusion and ripen conditions for a more traditional socialist program. With the Communist vote tripling to 10 percent, the combined Left had an absolute majority in the 1944 elections. And even in a period with emergency wage, price, and consumption controls, planning had a great deal of mystique. Despite Wigforss’s assurances about the continued role of private capital, the 1944 program advocated government takeovers of basic industries and finance and an overarching state responsibility for shaping investment and maintaining full employment. Economist and Trade Minister Gunnar Myrdal spoke of a “harvest time” for the labor movement, in which the fruits of recent economic development would fall to workers. Yet a push for nationalization found resistance from a capitalist class that could credibly threaten to withhold investment. That climate, combined with the onset of the Cold War, tempered the SAP, which forsook an alliance with the Communists in favor of a new coalition with the Agrarians. Equality Without Expropriation With the march toward socialization blocked, the party eventually adopted a 1951 plan created by LO economists Gösta Rehn and Rudolf Meidner. They shared the SAP’s desire to promote and influence economic expansion but advocated doing so through centralized collective bargaining backed by an active state. The starting point for the Rehn–Meidner strategy was a commitment to using that sectoral bargaining between the LO and the SAF to help equalize pay levels for all workers. That didn’t mean everyone earned the same, but the gap between higher- and lower-paid workers was reduced. The “equal pay for equal work” principle also meant that differentiated wages should be determined by the type of work performed, not by a particular employer’s ability to pay or an employee’s power on their shop floor. This was done for three reasons. First, because of an ideological commitment to equality: even if wages can’t be equal, at the very least we should lift up the incomes of the worst off and limit the advantages of the best off. Second, because wage compression was politically useful: it reduced divisions within the working class and promoted solidarity across industries. Third, because it played an important macroeconomic role in the Rehn–Meidner plan. Wage demands would be set so that firms at an average efficiency level could survive, but less efficient firms would be squeezed and forced to radically restructure or go bankrupt. The most productive firms, however, would benefit from the wage restraint of their workers and garner excess profits. These profits would allow them to expand their productive capacity and thus generate more wealth. The system helped encourage high-wage, capital-intensive industry. These negotiations were done directly between labor and capital, but the role of the state was crucial: “active labor market policies” helped workers formerly employed in less productive firms get reabsorbed in expanding parts of the economy. Social guarantees — for health care, education, childcare, and so on — meant that there was a growing state sector to help ensure full employment. It was a program for “functional socialism,” in that it made certain socialist priorities clear but would seek them by shaping the outcomes of capitalist enterprise rather than through nationalization. Such a model, however, was one that capitalists would agree to only under duress. As Meidner put it, “management prefers decentralized bargaining” and “wage differentials as instruments for managerial control.” Even though capitalists benefited from the Rehn–Meidner plan in many respects, it was only implemented because a powerful labor movement and social democratic party pushed it through. Nevertheless, it was a “socialism” jointly administered by a powerful employers’ federation, which was able to set redlines around how far private ownership rights could be eroded. This contradiction would only emerge later. Sweden thrived in the postwar period. It had not been ravaged by the war, and a rebuilding Europe needed the raw materials it exported. What was good for Volvo also seemed to be good for Sweden. Under the twenty-three-year tenure of Prime Minister Tage Erlander, companies enjoyed soaring profits, and the spoils of that growth were shared widely. The dream of constructing socialism through nationalizations was abandoned, and as late as 1976, only 5 percent of Swedish industry was under public ownership. But the results were undeniable. The Rehn–Meidner model combined the dynamic power of capital and free trade, on the one hand, with the might of an organized trade union movement and a social democratic state, on the other, to ensure egalitarian outcomes. Beyond distributing income more fairly, the system allowed Swedes to meet basic needs outside the marketplace through welfare-state guarantees. It was what Wigforss called a “provisional utopia.” International observers like Anthony Crosland drew profound lessons from the Swedish example. The British Labour parliamentarian believed that socialism was compatible with the private ownership of industry. His 1956 book The Future of Socialism criticized the traditional socialist focus on means — the preference for nationalization, for example — instead of the end goal of social equality. “The worst sort of confusion,” he wrote, “is the tendency to use [socialism] to describe, not a certain kind of society, but particular policies which are, or are thought to be, means to attain this kind of society.” It was the most influential revisionist work since Eduard Bernstein’s Evolutionary Socialism , perfect for an era in which capitalism seemed dynamic and the scope for reform within it almost boundless. Crosland was explicit about his intention, telling a friend that he was “engaged on a great revision of Marxism, and will certainly emerge as the modern Bernstein.” The comparison was apt. Bernstein had been a coauthor of the Erfurt Program and one of the most respected Marxists of his generation before, in a series of articles in the late 1890s, he began questioning its orthodoxy. Society was not “splitting up into two great hostile camps,” as The Communist Manifesto claimed; intermediate classes and smaller firms were alive and well, and workers’ material standing was slowly improving rather than deteriorating. Capitalism, he argued, had found ways to self-regulate and avoid crises, and the working class had won the means, in the form of parliaments, to shape its development and slowly legislate reforms. “The ultimate aim of socialism is nothing,” he wrote, “but the movement is everything.” Social democracy would reach new heights when it freed itself from “obsolete phraseology” and was “willing to appear what it really is today; a democratic-Socialist reform party.” Kautsky and Rosa Luxemburg led the reply, and the SPD formally repudiated Bernstein in 1898. But the revisionists would have their moment. While Bernstein had inklings of how malleable capitalism was, Crosland had proof. Despite the Depression and war of the 1930s and ’40s, the system had not collapsed but was instead being transformed. Living standards were improving, and private capital increasingly found itself subordinated to both the state and labor. This was the case in Clement Attlee’s Britain and even more so in Erlander’s Sweden. Taxing the gains from capitalist growth and expanding social spending, rather than outright expropriation, seemed to be sufficient. Going further than Bernstein ever did, Crosland wrote, “Marx has little or nothing to offer the contemporary Socialist, either in respect of practical policy, or of the correct analysis of our society, or even the right conceptual tools or framework.” Three years after The Future of Socialism ’s publication, even the venerable German Social Democratic Party abandoned the Marxist concept of class struggle in its Godesberg Program. The reconciliation to capitalism that social democracy had long made in practice was finally codified in theory. An Unlikely Radical Yet in 1969, when Olof Palme took over as prime minister of Sweden, the class compromise that underpinned the system was starting to come undone. That Palme came to prominence was no great surprise — that he did so on the back of working-class politics was. He was born into an aristocratic Lutheran family and spent much of his childhood on estates and in the company of private tutors. His uncles were so rabidly right-wing that they fought in the Finnish Civil War as foreign volunteers supporting the brutal White Army. One of them, after whom he was named, was killed by the Reds at the Battle of Tampere in 1918. Palme might have always had some egalitarian instincts. After his 1986 assassination, an old household servant recalled that he was different from his family members, even as an eight-year-old, “always helping us maids carry the dishes and talking to us as equals.” But from what we know of his days in primary school and in the military, Palme was hardly political and even displayed some conservative tendencies. As unlikely as it seems, he was won over to socialism during his time in the United States. In 1947, Palme received a scholarship to study for a year at Kenyon College, a liberal arts school in Ohio. He came to love America, filled as it was with postwar confidence and a vibrant trade union movement. Palme was a member of a socialist society while at school and studied under progressive professors. He wrote his thesis on the United Auto Workers (UAW), and he even conducted research at a ball-bearing factory near Kenyon. That summer, Palme also talked with UAW leader Walter Reuther in Detroit, admiring what he saw of the American social democratic movement. Free from rigid boarding schools and military discipline for the first time, Palme hitchhiked and took Greyhounds across the country. In the process, he discovered how widespread racism was in Jim Crow America. He recalled sitting in the back of the bus with black patrons in the South. When asked to move to the front by some white men, he refused. As he wrote in a letter home, he probably only avoided a beating because “they took me for a crazy foreigner.” By the time Palme returned to Sweden, the young man who not long ago thought the Swedish tax rate needed to be halved was writing articles about The Communist Manifesto . The newly politicized Palme became involved in the SAP and was elected chairman of the Swedish students’ union in 1952. Many took notice of his charisma and intelligence, and he was soon brought in as an assistant to Prime Minister Erlander. His role in the administration became so vital that the media would sometimes depict Palme as Erlander’s puppet master. The prestige and attention didn’t please some members of his family, however. His grandmother, proud of her own children’s sacrifices against the “Finnish barbarians,” regretted that her beloved grandson was “in the service of a party that is busy destroying our country.” When he became prime minister at the age of forty-two, Palme hoped to take the model built under Erlander and widen its scope. His ambitions were rooted in his own views but also reflected growing pressure from the rank and file of the LO and the SAP. Decades under a strong welfare state, it turned out, didn’t satisfy egalitarian demands but fostered more expansive ones. Following SAP setbacks in local elections, a 1967 conference committed the party to an “industrial policy offensive” that hearkened back to the 1940s planning debate. Previously, Swedish social democracy had engaged in little direct industrial planning, relying on the demands of unions and targeted interventions to shape market forces. But now, the government set up a public investment bank and expanded state enterprises and mechanisms for the coordination of those firms. The changes were not necessarily anti-capitalist: business needed labor and state support to adjust to a changing world market. But though capitalists could accept active industrial policy, the push to expand workplace democracy was bitterly resisted. LO leaders had to contend with a wave of wildcat strikes and were pushed to the left as a result in the early 1970s. The federation began advocating the extension of collective bargaining to noneconomic concerns. The employers organized in the SAF refused any such reform, so labor (both the LO and the white-collar federations) resorted to pushing its demands through social democratic legislation. With the backing of Palme’s Social Democratic Party, employers were forced to negotiate with unions on just about every workplace issue. The balance established by the 1938 Basic Agreement was being challenged, with labor firing the first shots. The Ownership Question The most radical shift was the 1976 LO endorsement of a new Meidner Plan, a proposal for a collectively owned wage-earner fund. Decades of wage restraint in productive firms had curbed inflationary pressures and allowed for expansion. But these policies also led to massive “excessive profits” — the product of centralized bargaining, not weak shop-floor power — which weren’t always productively invested. Many workers, especially those with valuable skills, felt that the wage increases they had been granted were less than they deserved. Those feelings were compounded by the realization that some of Sweden’s largest companies were earning extremely high profits, in large part because of labor’s willingness to limit its demands. A Communist parliamentarian, C.-H. Hermansson, could point out that even after years of uninterrupted social democratic rule, “fifteen families” owned the majority of Swedish industry. The LO proposal, which was not cleared beforehand with the SAP, would address both the ideological and the practical problems of capitalists’ unilateral control of socially created wealth. Wage-earner funds had been floated in Germany, the Netherlands, and Denmark in the postwar years, but the Swedish plan was more ambitious. A small working group set up in 1973 spent two years formulating a strategy to shore up the nation’s solidaristic wage policy. They ended up presenting a report that advocated a form of profit sharing: firms with more than fifty employees would have to issue new shares equivalent to 20 percent of their annual profits to worker-controlled wage-earner funds. Though the proposal was at first received indifferently by LO leaders, they came to see it as a possible solution to the problems facing the Swedish model. The response from the trade union rank and file was more enthusiastic, particularly because of the plan’s anti-capitalist implications. Within several decades, the funds (controlled by workers through union boards) could wrest ownership from private capital. As Meidner said in a 1975 interview, “We cannot fundamentally change society without changing its ownership structure.” It was a left-wing repudiation of “functional socialism.” For their part, many workers who had been indirectly exercising political power through the Social Democratic Party felt they had the skills and expertise to run their own workplaces without compromising with capital. The resolution’s adoption at the 1976 LO convention was followed by a spontaneous rendition of “The Internationale.” The Meidner committee anticipated strong business resistance and stressed that employers were not being expropriated. They would retain their existing shares, but their ownership stakes would be diluted as new shares accumulated in the funds. And since that share of profit wouldn’t be taxed, the state was itself subsidizing the funds. The plan could even have promoted further wage restraint and less adversarial relations between workers and management. Yet after some consideration, business leaders saw the plan for what it was: an existential threat. A More Equal Society Meanwhile, Palme pressed his larger project throughout his first seven years in office. He was fond of using poetry to describe the world social democracy sought to build, a world that acknowledged that “man, not the moon, is the measure of everything. An open city without fortifications is what we are building together, its light shining up against the loneliness of space.” For Swedish women, in particular, social democracy was a great boon in what was once considered the most patriarchal country in Scandinavia. Historically, socialists had acquitted themselves far better on the question of sexual equality than their rivals, as most agreed with August Bebel that there couldn’t be a just society without “equality of the sexes.” But radicals like Alexandra Kollontai and Vladimir Lenin who recognized the “double oppression” that women faced — both from capital and from sexism — thought the scope for reform was limited within capitalism. (In the early 1900s, Kollontai could dismiss the feminist movement itself as “poison.”) Socialists, in general, favored universal suffrage, employment, and other civil rights but were less proactive in other struggles and were suspicious of cross-class feminist causes. Sweden showed just how much sexual oppression could be diminished within capitalism. Child allowances, family leave, childcare, even the provision of school meals — all eased the burdens placed on women. Beyond such legislation, “equal pay for equal work” and industry-level bargaining that favored the lowest-paid sectors disproportionately helped women. Still, in the mid-1960s, roughly half of Swedish women of working age stayed at home. A popular 1961 pamphlet pointed out that now women had the right to compete with men in the labor market but also had to maintain their household duties, making it difficult to do both in practice. Amid debate over this question, the state took steps to facilitate women’s participation in the labor force. An advisory council on sexual equality working directly with the prime minister’s office was created to generate policy that encouraged “free development” for women and challenged traditional gender roles. Palme committed himself wholeheartedly to the effort, writing in a thoughtful essay called “The Emancipation of Man” that women’s struggle for equality meant taking on the “pressure of thousand years old traditions.” By the end of that decade, roughly three-quarters of women in the country were in paid employment — the highest rate in the world. Combined with the wider social and economic transformation underway, Swedish society was upturned — traditionalism declined, secularism grew, and new forms of sexual liberation flourished. What was once a hierarchical nation, a culture still yearning for past imperial glories, could now take pride in being a democracy committed to equality at home and anti-colonial struggles abroad. Capital Fights Back By the time the 1976 general election came around, the Social Democrats had been in power almost continuously for forty-four years, longer than most Swedes had been alive. They had faced a stiff challenge in 1973, and then, in 1976, the socialist bloc fell short of a majority. The immediate cause of the defeat might have been a debate over nuclear power. It was favored by the Social Democrats but opposed by the Center Party, which expanded its scope to green politics. The larger challenge came from the structural dilemma of social democracy. No matter how creatively implemented, it was still dependent on private sector profits and the calculation by business that maintaining the peace with a powerful labor movement was worth the cost. Sure enough, employers enjoyed stability: from 1938 to the start of the wildcat strike wave in 1969, strike levels were lower in Sweden than elsewhere in Europe. But political unrest and the end of the postwar economic boom meant that the truce could not last forever. The Left began to break with aspects of the 1938 Basic Agreement. In some instances, this was an ideological act, such as in the encroachment on management prerogatives and the push for industrial democracy. In other cases, as with the wage-earner funds, it was a mix of practical and ideological imperatives. The employers’ federation, for its part, radicalized as well. For the first time in decades, the SAF began a media campaign against the core pillars of social democracy. It attacked the Meidner Plan, portraying it as an attempt by union bureaucrats to concentrate power in their own hands. The LO rank and file still rallied behind the plan, but the SAP was never much committed to it to begin with. Nor were white-collar professionals who voted for the Left. The charges stuck. Some seventy-five thousand people marched through Stockholm against the Meidner Plan on October 4, 1983, in what was then the largest demonstration in the country’s history. At the same time, employers began resisting even moderate demands for wage increases. Amid broader economic shifts that resulted in the internationalization of the Swedish economy, and developments including the 1973 worldwide oil crisis, the threat of unemployment and inflation grew. Still tasked with absorbing private sector job losses and maintaining broad welfare supports, the state began growing rapidly, with public expenditure reaching almost 70 percent of GDP. Social democracy was always predicated on economic expansion. Expansion gave succor to both the working class and capital. When growth slowed and the demands of workers made deeper inroads into firms’ profits, business owners rebelled against the class compromise. Neoliberalism — a set of policies to use state power to restore employer profits by rolling back regulations and challenging unions — was one way to solve the crisis of the 1970s. Its advocates argued that Keynesian capitalism worked up to a point but had fixed limits: stimulus beyond them would yield inflation without growth, and unleashing growth again meant not spending more but dialing back the welfare state and constricting the bargaining power of unions. In short, the working class would have to accept less. Wresting control of investment from capital was another way out of the crisis. But social democrats were unprepared for this choice. Thinking they had abolished the business cycle through state intervention, they forgot a core tenet of Marxism: that the contradictions of capitalism, and its tendency toward crisis, cannot be resolved within the system. After attempting and failing to borrow their way out of the crisis, social democrats eventually responded to the charge that social democracy itself was at the root of the economic crisis by wholeheartedly agreeing. Perhaps things would have played out differently if Palme and his party had backed the Meidner Plan wholeheartedly in the 1970s. However, they ran into another big dilemma of social democracy: social democratic leaderships have to win elections and build stable institutions. They didn’t necessarily want working-class mobilization outside the voting booth. To maintain electoral stability and their own ability to mediate between capital and labor and to enact reforms, social democrats shied away from leftist solutions to the crisis. In doing so, they ironically ended up undermining their own voting bloc, the true source of their power. Despite the SAP setback in 1976, the basics of social democracy went unchallenged longer in Sweden than elsewhere in Europe. But in the 1980s, centralized bargaining policies and practices began to crumble, and the Meidner Plan was stripped of its transformative ambition. The wage-earner funds introduced in 1984 bought shares on the market rather than requiring firms to issue them, and limits on their holdings curtailed their capacity to challenge private control. After the center-right victory in 1991, the new government moved to dismantle them. Full-employment policies were dropped by a social democratic government as Sweden entered its financial crisis in the early 1990s. Though Sweden still has better social indicators than almost any other nation, its welfare state was reshaped through reforms that privatized key aspects of service provision. Entry into the European Union in 1995 further challenged what remained of the Swedish model. The Great Retreat Though other social democratic experiments sputtered and failed, the Left was not in full retreat everywhere. In France, forty-three years after Léon Blum’s second government fell, François Mitterrand’s 1980s socialist government was an attempt to rage against the dying of the light. His program was the most radical from a mainstream party in decades. “You can be a manager of capitalist society or a founder of a socialist society,” Mitterrand could say. “As far as we’re concerned, we want to be the second.” By the time Mitterrand took power with Communist support in 1981, France already faced growing unemployment, economic stagnation, and an unfavorable international business climate. Despite his rhetoric, Mitterrand’s immediate program was a radical Keynesian one. His “110 Propositions for France” proposed a public works program and the construction of social housing, nurseries, and clinics. The government’s early legislative victories expanded shop-floor union rights, along with codetermination measures. The minimum wage and pensions were increased, while the working week was reduced to thirty-nine hours. A 1982 nationalization bill put five industrial groups, almost forty banks, two steel companies, and much of the armaments and aerospace industries under state control. These nationalizations, decried as Bolshevism in the business press, were made less on ideological grounds than to help maintain employment and oversee economic restructuring. Nonetheless, business resistance grew to unprecedented levels, and $5 billion was lost to capital flight. Just a few years after asserting his revolutionary credentials, Mitterrand was pleading with France’s business leaders: “This will be one of the ways to end the class struggle. We want to develop a mixed economy. We are not revolutionary Marxists-Leninists.” He might have recalled what he had told one of his aides only a few months earlier: “In economics, there are two solutions — either you are a Leninist or you won’t change anything.” Mitterrand’s program did spark a wave of popular support, but he was unable to harness that energy or to deal effectively with employer resistance. His choice of retreat was made easier by restrictions imposed by the European Monetary System, which linked the franc to the deutsche mark and other European currencies within agreed exchange-rate bands. France devalued the franc three times between 1981 and 1983, but Mitterrand ultimately chose austerity to remain in the system (today the eurozone allows for even less monetary flexibility). The bold “110 Propositions” could not overcome the discipline of both domestic elites and the international market. The French socialists were forced into a dramatic U-turn, not just halting their march forward but embracing the politics of austerity. Olof Palme didn’t live long enough to oversee such a retreat in his own country. But within a few years of his 1986 assassination, both state socialism and social democracy were widely pronounced dead. Social democrats still governed in many places. Since mid-century, they had given up the ambition to construct a successor to capitalism, settling for doses of socialism within it. Now they ended up, as they had in the interwar period, following the path of the Ramsay MacDonald governments and, at most, marrying redistributive measures to economic orthodoxy. Tony Blair in Britain, Gerhard Schröder in Germany, and North American counterparts, including Bill Clinton and others in the Democratic Leadership Council, helped codify the social democratic retreat into a new ideology. The newly minted Third Way promised “opportunity, not government” and “a politics of inclusion,” not welfare. The social democrats who had once demanded a middle way between communism and capitalism, French socialist leader Lionel Jospin lamented, now proposed one between social democracy and neoliberalism. The goalposts had moved to the right. As part of this shift, historic labor parties were transformed into social liberal ones, their appeals designed more for middle-class professionals than their long-neglected working-class bases. The substitution was deliberate. After Labour’s loss in the 1992 general election, the Fabian Society published Southern Discomfort , a pamphlet calling for the party to reorient toward professionals in the South of England. Its conclusions — an emphasis on “opportunity,” “individualism,” and fiscal constraint — were embraced by Blair in his successful 1997 campaign. New Labour was, at least in part, a project to turn Labour from a social democratic party of the working class into “the political wing of the British people” — young, cosmopolitan, and dynamic. Clinton did much the same, embracing suburban professionals and “knowledge workers” as replacements for his party’s lost voters and finding new sources of capitalist support in finance and Big Tech. Chuck Schumer stated the logic plainly on the eve of the 2016 election: “For every blue-collar Democrat we lose in western Pennsylvania, we will pick up two moderate Republicans in the suburbs in Philadelphia, and you can repeat that in Ohio and Illinois and Wisconsin.” The consequences show up in the voting data. According to Political Cleavages and Social Inequalities , edited by Amory Gethin, Clara Martínez-Toledano, and Thomas Piketty, in the 1950s the working class in Western democracies voted for the Left at a rate 31 percentage points higher than voters of other classes. By 2020, that margin was 8 percentage points. The rich have maintained their allegiance to parties of the Right, but the professional classes shifted in response to the social liberal turn of social democratic parties. Parties of labor became parties of the educated. Over the course of decades, social democracy abandoned workers; then workers abandoned social democracy. Anthony Crosland’s The Future of Socialism was at its most poignant in its demands for “not only higher exports and old-age pensions, but more open-air cafes, brighter and gayer streets at night . . . brighter and cleaner eating houses, more riverside cafes, more pleasure gardens on the Battersea model.” The welfare state certainly should not be the end point of human ambitions. But those who embraced Crosland’s moderate legacy in bids to modernize their old parties proved better able to enact his dreams of green urban renewal than of social equality. Beyond the Welfare State Does that mean that the decades of effort to build social democracy were in vain? We can recall Rosa Luxemburg’s comparison, in her 1899 polemic against Bernstein, of reformism with the “labor of Sisyphus,” the king in Greek mythology condemned to push a boulder uphill for eternity, only to see it slide down again when it nears the top. Without a decisive rupture with capitalism, she argued, all that reforms could win was the momentary “suppression of the abuses of capitalism instead of suppression of capitalism itself.” But even after years of slippage, the boulder hasn’t come crashing all the way down. In advanced societies shaped by social democrats, key working-class victories have proven durable, and people enjoy protections from the most extreme forms of poverty and insecurity. Democracy prevents capitalism from returning to its “war of all against all” worst. But for those still aspiring to an age of abundance and solidarity, defending existing victories or negotiating terms of defeat isn’t enough. A decade ago, the movements around Jeremy Corbyn and Bernie Sanders looked like a surprising challenge to the Third Way. Corbyn is still the moral voice he’s always been, but that voice is now back on the fringes of British politics. Sanders is in his mid-eighties and destined to go down as a great “what if” of American history. But a “class-struggle social democracy” is emerging elsewhere. It has a mayor in New York City, Zohran Mamdani, a bench of state legislators and city councilors across the United States, and a generation of organizers seeking to use public office to build organized working-class power. But if they succeed in ways they can only dream of now, they will encounter the same structural challenges that the Swedes did: reliance on the profitability of capital and the economic contradictions that accompany empowered workplaces and full employment. The resolution of those issues will lead down one of two paths, albeit different ones from those Palme suggested — back to economic orthodoxy or toward a more radical, democratic socialist tradition. The Meidner Plan was the last serious attempt by a labor movement to take the second path from inside a functioning social democracy. Whether the conditions for another such attempt will emerge through steady reform or revolutionary rupture remains uncertain. But any democratic socialist movement will have to confront the question the Swedish labor movement put on the table: Who owns the wealth workers create, and who decides how it is used?

Why Black Socialists Opposed Marcus Garvey

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Paul Prescod ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-17T16:01:18+00:00

Africa loomed large in the vision of Marcus Garvey and his followers. In May 1920, the Universal Negro Improvement Association (UNIA) Executive Council decided to send a delegation to Liberia to explore the prospects for settlement and development. UNIA Resident Secretary in Liberia Elie Garcia wrote to President Charles D. B. King that they wanted to “establish trade routes, through a line of steamships, etc., to encourage emigration to build up the country, to transfer its Headquarters to Liberia, to bring with it medical and scientific units, etc.” He asked for “written assurance that every facility will be given it to procure lands for business, agriculture, and industrial purposes. In return the Organization with its vast membership will lend financial and moral support to the Government to help her out of her present economic plight.” Hubert Harrison got a copy of the August 1920 “Confidential Report” from Garcia, where he saw evidence of conspiratorial plans against the government of Liberia. Garcia recommended that the UNIA hide its real intentions to get involved in political activity. It also indicated the thorny internal politics at play and how reluctant the Liberian government was to seriously engage with the UNIA’s schemes. Garcia characterized the Liberian elite as “degenerated and weak morally” and wary of “American and West Indian Negroes.” A. Philip Randolph cut through the unseriousness of Garvey’s dream to redeem Africa. He saw no viable pathway toward building up an independent African state by disparate groups of UNIA members. “The redemption of Africa by Negroes who are unarmed, unorganized, uneducated, a minority in numbers to their oppressors, divided, both in and out of Africa by languages, custom, history, and habits, is a will-o’-wisp, an iridescent dream which could only be born in the head of an irresponsible enthusiast,” he charged. The tendency to paper over class and cultural differences among blacks in the United States was even more pronounced in the way Garvey thought of Africa. William Pickens, a prominent National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) activist and Garvey rival, quipped that “there is no more in common between South Africa and North Africa than between Texas and Turkey.” In his eyes, Garvey “had not a grammar grade understanding of Africa.” Randolph wrote, “To him every African is a Negro and every Negro is black. To him there is a racial and nationality homogeneity in Africa; all tribes possessing one mind.” Famed Harlem Renaissance poet Claude McKay rubbed shoulders with Garvey in Harlem’s political scene throughout the 1920s. After a brief involvement with the Liberty League, he became a Communist Party member. Writing for The Liberator in April 1922, McKay lambasted Garvey for the fact that he “talks of Africa as if it were a little island in the Caribbean Sea.” He sensed a combination of ignorance and patronizing arrogance in Garvey’s casual dismissal of the vast diversity of the continent, and his presumption that they wanted or needed him to save them. “Ignoring all geographical and political divisions,” McKay sneered, “he gives his followers the idea that that vast continent of diverse tribes consists of a large homogenous nation of natives struggling for freedom and waiting for the Western Negroes to come and help them drive out the European exploiters.” It wasn’t long before African political figures, including some who were initially involved with the UNIA, began to speak out against Garvey’s designs for Africa. Madarikan Deniyi, who attended the 1920 convention as a delegate from Nigeria, wrote a letter a year later charging that the African Redemption Fund was a fraudulent scam of “poor and needy Negroes” for the purpose of paying his and other officer salaries. At a “Garvey Must Go” rally, Basutoland native Mokete Manoedi remarked, “We are not favorably impressed with the unmitigated presumption of this man Garvey, in electing himself provisional president of Africa.” Though Garvey’s project ultimately centered around Africa, UNIA chapters in cities like Lagos and Sierra Leone were much weaker than those in the United States and Caribbean. He was never able to overcome the great social, political, and logistical barriers to establishing commercial networks in the continent. On June 30, 1924, the Liberian government officially banned UNIA members from entering the country. Thus ended Garvey’s African Odyssey. Trial and Collapse Garvey’s trial for mail fraud began in May 1923. Garvey chose to represent himself, providing yet another media spectacle. He was found guilty on June 18 but was released on bail during his appeal, after which he went on a speaking tour across the country. But in 1925, the higher courts upheld the original decision, and he spent two years in prison. Harrison was convinced that the jury got it right, writing that Garvey was guilty of “swindling members of his race by means of the . . . ‘Black Star Line’ whose fleet of ships to sail the seven seas existed mainly on paper and in the mind of Marcus Garvey.” Of course, loyal Garveyites could blame the subsequent decline of the UNIA movement on Garvey’s persecution and treachery by enemies of the race. This ignores the much deeper issues with Garvey’s social philosophy, political vision, and organizational practices. While UNIA meetings, conventions, and parades gave the impression of a dynamic organization, little of the activity was geared toward dealing with the material issues confronting most ordinary black people. Ultimately, militancy is a posture and not always an indicator of ideology. As the black population became more urban and integrated in industrial life, issues like wages, working conditions, and housing became more salient and required collective solutions. But the UNIA had nothing to offer on this score. At heart, the rank-and-file member was meant to act as a largely passive consumer of UNIA products and ideology. Judith Stein aptly describes the UNIA program as “oscillating without design between the poles of retaliatory violence and individual self-help. . . . Most recommendations required little active collective implementation because they were exhortations to individuals.” McKay captured the curious blend of reactionary and modern politics at work in the UNIA. In one sense, the Garvey phenomenon represented something new, for he didn’t exist as an appendage of the Republican patronage machine or mainstream civil rights organizations. Unlike many black leaders of the day, he understood the sensibilities of black proletarians, “for in the West Indies the Negro problem is peculiarly economic, and prejudice is, English-wise, more of class than of race.” But he admired the old European empires and modeled his dreams of an African empire after them. “His spirit is revolutionary,” McKay wrote, “but his intellect does not understand the significance of modern revolutionary developments. Maybe he chose not to understand, he may have realized that a resolute facing of facts would make puerile his beautiful schemes for the redemption of the continent of Africa.” While his “stupendous vaudeville” may have been somewhat amusing, McKay felt he had “muddied the waters of the Negro movement for freedom and put the race back for many years.” In a similar assessment, Stein describes Garvey as “socially rebellious” but “ideologically conservative. His gestures defied the powerful, white and black, but the consequences of his actions confirmed their position at the top.” After the mail fraud trial, Harrison was reminded of Garvey in Eugene O’Neill’s play, Emperor Jones , “a fine picture of the psychology of the whole Garvey movement,” which depicts a delusional self-proclaimed king of a Caribbean island who eventually succumbs to his own paranoia. Of course, his popular support did not vanish overnight, and in 1927, President Calvin Coolidge received a petition with over 70,000 signatures calling for his release. His sentence was commuted and expired in November 1927, after which he was deported to Kingston, Jamaica. Garvey attempted to continue political activity abroad, but changing political tides hindered his ability to resonate. In the 1930s, workers in the West Indies began to form trade unions, create labor parties, and launch large-scale strikes. In Trinidad, as the oil workers struck, Garvey claimed they didn’t have a right to strike and was told in clear terms he wasn’t welcome there anymore by the president of Trinidad’s UNIA branch. Writing in 1938, C. L. R. James said, “Today he is a staunch supporter of the British government and makes Tory speeches in Hyde Park about Africa, to the great annoyance of revolutionary British workers.” In the United States, various spin-offs from Garvey ideology emerged, like the Ethiopian Pacific Movement and the Moorish Science Temple, but none took off in the way the UNIA once had. Hugh Mulzac expressed sorrow for the “scores of thousands of humble black men and women” who donated money to Garvey’s cause but “received in dividends only a transitory inflation of their racial pride.” Though the movement spread quickly, just as soon, “the torch flickered and was out, leaving us in darkness as before; poorer, sadder, and perhaps wiser. . . . Who knows?”

Southern Gothic Films Take On the Horrors of the Past

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Eileen Jones ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-17T16:01:18+00:00

One of the films featured in the Criterion Channel’s “Southern Gothic” series is Baby Doll (1956). It’s based on Tennessee Williams’s one-act plays 27 Wagons Full of Cotton and The Unsatisfactory Supper , which Williams adapted for the screen in collaboration with the most famous interpreter of his work, director Elia Kazan. It begins with the lurid, sexed-up horns of Kenyon Hopkins’s score vamping as if in accompaniment to a strip show as we approach a grimly dilapidated Mississippi plantation house with grimy pillars like the post–Civil War plantation in Gone with the Wind . And there we have in a single film moment a perfect summation of the Southern Gothic. It’s a subgenre that takes a sensationalistic, yet critical and modern, revisionist approach to the culture of the contemporary American South, countering the romantic narrative of the “Lost Cause” that was invented to salve the wounds of losing the Civil War and, along with it, an economic model reliant on slave labor that made a minority planter class obscenely rich. The South’s collective post–Civil War determination to conjure up something noble and grand out of an evil history of exploitative violence helped create a culture so peculiarly dreadful that it inspired a whole new literary form in the hands of twentieth-century writers who grappled with its shocking effects, led by William Faulkner, Flannery O’Connor, Erskine Caldwell, Tennessee Williams, and Carson McCullers. They were the literary cabal dubbed “Southern Gothic writers,” and they took on the horrors encapsulated in the famous Faulkner line, “The past is never dead. It’s not even past.” Ruined Abbeys and Ancestral Sins Whether the past is dead or not, and whether even a dead past will still remain ever-present in ghastly revenant form, is the prime concern of all Gothic narratives. When the Gothic genre first arose in the late eighteenth century, it was in the form of dark melodramatic thrillers powered by both dread and subversive excitement about the idea of the return of the “Dark Ages,” the deeply Catholic medieval era, which was regarded then as a pre–Age of Reason era of barbarity. The form was launched by Horace Walpole ( The Castle of Otranto ) and Ann Radcliffe ( The Mysteries of Udolpho ). It was carried forward over the next century and a half by Matthew Gregory Lewis ( The Monk ), Mary Shelley ( Frankenstein ), Joseph Sheridan Le Fanu ( Carmilla ), Bram Stoker ( Dracula ), Robert Louis Stevenson ( The Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde ), Nathaniel Hawthorne ( The House of the Seven Gables ), Charlotte Brontë ( Jane Eyre ), Emily Brontë ( Wuthering Heights ), Charles Dickens ( Great Expectations, Bleak House ), Wilkie Collins ( The Moonstone, The Woman in White ), Oscar Wilde ( The Picture of Dorian Gray ), Edgar Allan Poe (“The Fall of the House of Usher”), Henry James ( The Turn of the Screw ), and Arthur Conan Doyle ( The Hound of the Baskervilles ). Early Gothic works, which became enormously popular, explore the obsession with Gothic architecture associated with harsh old religious beliefs, superstitious rites, crypts and graveyards, and barbaric torture, by establishing morbid settings such as ruined abbeys, forbidding castles, and moldering ancestral homes, all rumored to be haunted. Luckless protagonists, often young, inexperienced, and helpless women, experience harrowing entrapment within these settings where they are preyed upon by human evildoers — often powerful, wealthy, aristocratic men. Supernatural forces are another source of terror, sometimes in the form of ghosts, vampires, or demons, sometimes more ambiguously in the form of overpowering psychological torments — dreams, hallucinations, drug-induced visions, and memory-plagued characters reciting tales of past generational sins so great they overwhelm the consciousnesses of the living. Gothic narratives tend to bring with them a harsh critique of entrenched power, taking on the abuses of the aristocracy and the hoarders of generational wealth and privilege. Class, race, and gender become inevitable topics in a form that deals with the plight of those victimized by long-established systems of punishment and exploitation. Later Gothic works — including novels, poems, stage plays, and films — further developed the genre’s central preoccupation with the threatening resurgence of the past in somewhat more prosaic terms. For example, here’s one twentieth-century plot: the deceased wife of a darkly mysterious aristocratic male whose grand family lineage goes back to the medieval era seems to haunt the halls of his ancestral estate house and prey on the nerves of the anxious young second wife. The aristocratic male may or may not have murdered his first wife, see, which doesn’t bode well for wife number two. That’s the plot of Daphne du Maurier’s runaway 1938 bestseller Rebecca , which was adapted as the equally successful 1940 film by Alfred Hitchcock, and it will do nicely to convey the way the preoccupation with the past can be mapped onto contemporary life in later Gothic plots. A More Recent Past The Southern Gothic variation fixates on a far more recent but still remarkably terrible past involving vast cash-crop antebellum plantations run by a minority of pampered white families and worked by vast numbers of enslaved black people who were terrorized by fantastically cruel practices, including widespread rape. The children of white Southern aristocratic males and enslaved black women often looked remarkably like their white half-siblings living in luxury on the same plantations. Cultural authority figures staunchly defended the South’s “peculiar institution.” The clergy used the Bible, citing chapter and verse in innumerable sermons delivered from the pulpit to justify slave ownership and white supremacy. Meanwhile “poor white” Southerners scratched out a meager living but identified with the rich white planter class in order to see themselves as farther above the lowest rung of a society harshly divided by race and class. After the Civil War impoverished the entire region, a deluded sense of victimization and unjustly degraded nobility seized white imaginations. All this tends to manifest in Southern Gothic narratives as a heated stew of psychosexual twistedness: perverse sadomasochistic behaviors festering in squalor; sick, exploitative, and obsessive religiosity; ghosts and eerie atmospherics emerging out of blood-soaked soil; and weird delusions of grandeur. The Child Bride of Tiger Tail Baby Doll , for example, became notorious for its introductory shot of Baby Doll Meighan (Carroll Baker), a nineteen-year-old “child bride” sleeping curled up in a baby’s crib, sucking her thumb, which became the movie’s poster image and most sensational come-on. She’s being ogled through a peephole poked through the disintegrating wall of their desiccated antebellum plantation house by her husband, middle-aged, harried, racist, alcoholic, and desperately horny Archie Lee Meighan (Karl Malden). He’d promised her dying father, a once-wealthy traditionalist whose fortunes had faded, that he’d wait until Baby Doll’s twentieth birthday to consummate the marriage, and he’s being steadily driven crazy by frustrated lust as well as his own waning fortunes and power. It seems Archie Lee also promised Baby Doll’s father that she’d live in the grandest house around, which is why he bought Tiger Tail, the pillared plantation house now going to wrack and ruin, though it was the pride of the county about a hundred years earlier. His cotton gin business has been steadily going downhill to the point that the sordid wreck of Tiger Tail is all he could afford, and even the furniture he bought to fill it gets repossessed. This leads Baby Doll to threaten that the deal is off as far as consummating the marriage on her imminent twentieth birthday, since in her view Archie Lee has already reneged on the deal. Baby Doll, an impoverished girl who was essentially sold off by her downwardly mobile father, has a fourth-grade education but affects grand lady-of-the-manor airs. She’s chaperoned by her fragile, elderly Aunt Rose Comfort (Mildred Dunnock), who clearly suffers from dementia and is almost as incapable of doing any cooking or housework as the pampered Baby Doll. The older female housekeeper-attendant who will offer the inexperienced young heroine no real protection in her hour of peril is a stock Gothic character. But Aunt Rose Comfort is still a necessary chaperone, in part because Baby Doll is afraid to be alone in the plantation house, which she’s convinced is haunted. The whole rural community is in on Archie Lee’s marital predicament, and many shots are devoted to a Greek chorus of laughing black men. They’re clearly members of an impoverished underclass in the Jim Crow South, but in Baby Doll , they’re also unafraid of demonstrating their mocking amusement at the floundering failures of the white overlords. Humiliated, Archie Lee tries a desperate gambit, setting fire to the cotton gin of a powerful outsider in the community, Sicilian American Silva Vacarro (Eli Wallach), whose superior machinery and savvy have put almost all other cotton gin owners in the area out of business. Silva, hated by the xenophobic locals but kowtowed to because of his newly acquired wealth and status, strongly suspects Archie Lee of the act of arson, but offers him twenty-seven wagons full of cotton to run through his rickety cotton gin so Silva can be on the spot at Tiger Tail and get proof of what Archie Lee has done. Silva has his own ways rooted in ancient Sicilian culture that he often brags about, such as an inclination toward vigilante justice that rivals the well-known lynching practices of Southerners. But he’s perceived as a figure of the New South — bringing industry and business savvy, traits once associated with the Yankees, to the supposedly more arcadian Old South. Silva coerces a signed affidavit out of Baby Doll attesting to the fact that Archie Lee burned down Silva’s cotton gin. In the process, Silva also vengefully seduces Baby Doll — a seduction aided by the ghosts in the plantation house that Silva imitates, creeping around making noises in adjacent rooms until Baby Doll is grateful for his company. Archie Lee is only dangerous once he’s driven to the wall by Silva. He’s got his own ties to the entrenched “old boys’” network in the area, and they know how to deal with troublemaking outsiders. That’s when Baby Doll is finally frightened of Archie Lee, enough to urge Silva to flee, because these “old boys” are the dispossessed and ruined cotton gin owners put out of business who are also clearly members of the Ku Klux Klan. Their nighttime approach probably means vigilante “justice” and violent death. Vengeance at the Girls’ School Baby Doll is unusual among Southern Gothic films, in that it incorporates a dark version of screwball comedy in many scenes involving the absurd goings-on in and around the plantation house. Most Southern Gothics are relentlessly grim and melodramatic. Take, for example, the extraordinarily weird and wonderfully dread-filled Don Siegel movie The Beguiled (1971), which is rare among Southern Gothics in being a period film set during the Civil War. The present in this film is the already war-destroyed South, even as the war continues in its last year. A wounded Union soldier (Clint Eastwood) trapped behind enemy lines persuades the headmistress (Geraldine Page) of a girls’ school — located in what was formerly a working plantation house — to take him in and let him recover there in hiding. In order to prevent anyone from turning him over to the Confederate troops camped nearby, he uses all his considerable seductive power on the lonely, poor, and frightened women and girls of the school, including the sole enslaved woman still working there (Mae Mercer). His first transgressive “conquest” is the lingering kiss on the lips that he plants on a twelve-year-old schoolgirl. But soon his arrogance in seducing several young women and girls there, while rejecting the advances of the middle-aged headmistress, backfires on him and instigates a frenzied maenad attack of lust-fueled jealousy and hatred. Under the direction of the headmistress, his wounded leg is amputated in an act of vengeance, and though he’s badly impaired, the stage is set for a contest of vicious reprisals. There’s still a terrible past to be revealed by the Union soldier — it’s the headmistress’s long-term, incestuous relationship with her late brother, who was the rich plantation owner before the war. The deep-rooted psychosexual sickness powers most Southern Gothics, even those films in the series that lack other important Southern Gothic traits. Cape Fear , for example, both the 1962 and 1991 versions, generates terror through the monstrous villain Max Cady, a sadistic, drawling Southern ex-con who seeks revenge on a lawyer he blames for his lengthy imprisonment, and who has studied enough law to master the system and use it to enable his violence. His brutal rape and torture of a local woman — who is too frightened of him and the punishing processes of the legal system to bring charges against him — is only a rehearsal for the ultimate vengeance he has planned. That is, similarly savage attacks on the lawyer’s wife and teenage daughter. The False Preacher Charles Laughton’s 1955 masterpiece The Night of the Hunter is a unique film that’s also on the edge of the Southern Gothic, but it has the subgenre’s familiar figure of the corrupt or false or disturbingly fanatical preacher. In this case, it’s Harry Powell (Robert Mitchum), who has no official place within an established church but follows a bloody religion, “the religion the Almighty and me worked out betwixt us.” The Powell character was based on a real-life West Virginia serial killer named Harry Powers, hanged in 1932, who answered lonely hearts ads placed by widowed women, marrying them and then killing them for their money. Harry Powell also lives off the money stolen from murdered widows, and his entire religion of violence is powered by a twisted misogyny. Observing that there is plenty of killing in the Lord’s own book, Powell decides God only clearly hates the soft, seductive allure of women. He attends a strip show to foster his own rage, and the phallic switchblade he carries in his pocket shoots the knife through the cloth in a grotesque substitute for sexual erection and release. Shelley Winters and Robert Mitchum star in Charles Laughton’s 1955 masterpiece The Night of the Hunter . This is the nightmarish figure who’s coming after the small-town widow Willa Harper (Shelley Winters) and her two children (Billy Chapin and Sally Jane Bruce). He believes they still have the money stolen by her bank robber husband who — before he was hanged for his crimes — briefly shared a cell with Harry Powell. Powell’s terrorizing of the little family is so harrowing an experience you’ll never forget this film once you’ve seen it. The Church Without Christ But perhaps the most unsettling and strangely little known of the great films in the series is John Huston’s Wise Blood (1979), based on the 1952 novel of the same name by Flannery O’Connor. It’s about Hazel Motes (Brad Dourif), a young Southerner and veteran of an unnamed war who returns home bent on an enigmatic religious mission. Himself the grandson of a fanatical preacher who raised him (played by John Huston), shown in flashback, he sets about preaching his own invented religion, the “Church of Truth Without Christ.” Repudiating all notions of evil or an afterlife or Jesus Christ’s redemption of a sinful humanity, Hazel begins to draw a curious crowd in the street only to have an eager con man (Ned Beatty) try to join forces with him as his promoter, seeing it as a great moneymaking scheme. When Hazel furiously rejects him, the con man simply finds a homeless man and dresses him up in the same preacherly suit and hat as Hazel’s and sends him out into the street to approximate the same speech. Hazel is impeded in his serious aims at every turn. A lonely, addled street boy named Enoch Emory (Dan Shor) hero-worships him and tries to earn his friendship, telling him he’s found a better Jesus in the form of the shrunken, mummified corpse of a South American man that’s on display in the local museum. Hazel’s own attempts to befriend an itinerant preacher (Harry Dean Stanton) who is known to have blinded himself in a fit of religious self-martyrdom, run aground when the preacher’s supposed daughter Sabbath Lily Hawks (Amy Wright), an abused young woman who’s come to revel in feeling like she’s in a debased state of sin, falls in love with him. Then Hazel makes a test of the preacher’s blindness and finds out he’s faking it. This event is crushing, because it goes back to Hazel’s own childhood religious agony. His obsession with removing Jesus Christ from religion goes back to his early youth when, apprised of Christ having died for our sins, he muttered “I never asked him to,” and tried to work off the moral debt and sense of guilt by walking around with stones in his shoes. It’s only a matter of time before Hazel is driven to make good on the fake-blind preacher’s outrageous con. Hazel blinds himself with quicklime. He also wraps barbed wire around his naked chest under his shirt and, yes, puts all those stones back in his shoes. But even these gruesome attempts at self-salvation only create more prosaic and entangling obstacles to any serious religiosity. His slightly batty middle-aged landlady, who objects in scandalized tones to his extreme religiosity as “not normal,” nevertheless falls in love with him and becomes determined to take care of him for the rest of his life. He ends up trapped and helpless in a dingy bedroom, barely alive but not allowed to die a martyr’s death because the landlady won’t let him. And I should add, peppered through much of this relentlessly bleak film is the darkest of dark comedy. More dark comedy would’ve helped Wildcat (2023), a biopic about Flannery O’Connor’s short but prolific life as a Southern Gothic writer in her native Georgia. Nevertheless, it’s a good companion piece with Wise Blood . O’Connor herself wound up trapped in a small room in her mother’s house after lupus — which had also killed her father at a young age — began ravaging her system, though the room also became the writing space where she did her greatest work. Ethan Hawke directed and cowrote the ambitious script with Shelby Gaines, and his daughter Maya Hawke plays O’Connor starting off as a young writer having liberating experiences both personally and professionally in New York City before the disease puts an end to her freedom. Maya Hawke also plays several of the female characters in O’Connor’s short stories, including Lucynell Crater of “The Life You Save May Be Your Own,” Mary Grace of “Revelation,” Star Drake of “The Comforts of Home,” and Joy “Hulga” Hopewell of “Good Country People.” Her mother Regina (Laura Linney) tries to be supportive. But she doesn’t understand or approve of the harsh, unsparing qualities of her daughter’s literary portraits of Southern life. And Regina has many maddening traits of “respectable” middle-class Southern white women, including virulent racism and inflated notions of her own virtue, that will be faithfully represented in Flannery O’Connor’s fiction. O’Connor was a serious-as-a-heart-attack Catholic preoccupied by what it would require to attain a virtually impossible state of grace. Her struggles are represented in the film in various ways including her tortured talk with an Irish Catholic priest (Liam Neeson) and her conversations with the bright young atheistic literary set in New York City. A sophisticated young woman, who’s supposed to be ex-Catholic writer Mary McCarthy in a recreation of an actual 1950 party scene, offers the bromide that the rite of the Eucharist in the Catholic Mass should be seen as a “beautiful symbol” of human salvation through Christ rather than a truth to be taken literally. Confronted with this squeamishness about consuming the transubstantiated body and blood of Christ, O’Connor retorts in implacable Hazel Motes style, “Well, if it’s a symbol, to hell with it.” The Southern Gothic series offers an embarrassment of riches when it comes to examining elite abuses of power over several generations. Such long-standing abuses create in many of these narratives an overpowering atmosphere of evil that characters often mistake for something supernatural, or that pushes them toward their own outlandish cruelties, or a morbid and brutal religiosity. Taken together, these films constitute a sensationalistic reckoning with the grim legacy of Southern culture in the United States.

Una pequeña victoria frente a los mapas que sitúan al Norte Global en el centro y empequeñecen a África.

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Prabir Purkayastha ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-18T19:05:02+00:00

Read in English here La semana pasada, el 4 de septiembre, la Asamblea General de las Naciones Unidas aprobó una propuesta africana para un nuevo mapa mundial, que muestra el tamaño real de los países en relación con los demás. Su objetivo es corregir la visión del mundo moldeada por el mapa mundial ampliamente utilizado basado en la proyección de Mercator, creada por Gerardus Mercator, un cartógrafo flamenco del siglo XVI. El problema con el mapa de Mercator es bien conocido; por ejemplo, muestra a la isla de Groenlandia con el mismo tamaño que el continente africano, ¡a pesar de que África es 14 veces más grande que Groenlandia! En pocas palabras, los mapas de Mercator exageran enormemente el norte del mundo —los Estados Unidos y Europa— mientras que reducen África y la mayor parte de Asia. Tampoco trata con benevolencia al resto del hemisferio sur. Sudamérica, que tiene casi tres veces el tamaño de Europa, parece comparable a esta en un mapa típico de Mercator. Aunque no existe ninguna razón geográfica por la que el hemisferio sur deba ser más pequeño que el hemisferio norte, los cartógrafos lo hicieron significativamente más pequeño porque tenía muy poca masa continental más allá de los 40 grados y, por lo tanto, se recortó. Esto significó que, en la mayoría de los mapas de Mercator, debido a que los cartógrafos occidentales recortaron gran parte del hemisferio sur, el ecuador quedó significativamente desplazado hacia abajo. El doble efecto de comprimir las zonas ecuatoriales, donde se encuentra gran parte de África, y desplazar el ecuador hacia abajo hizo que el norte del mundo, particularmente Europa y América del Norte, parecieran mucho más grandes de lo que realmente son. Por lo general, en la mayoría de los mapas de Mercator, el hemisferio norte parece el doble del tamaño del hemisferio sur, aunque ambos hemisferios son idénticos en tamaño. Cualquier mapa, ya sea de Mercator o de cualquier otra proyección, presenta el problema de que se puede ser fiel al tamaño o a la forma, pero no a ambos. Se trata de un problema fundamental que surge al proyectar una superficie tridimensional sobre una hoja de papel bidimensional. No hay forma de hacerlo sin distorsiones. Solo un globo terráqueo puede representar fielmente la Tierra tridimensional. Pero entonces, ¿qué hacemos cuando queremos reproducir detalles geográficos para nuestros lectores en libros o artículos? Las distorsiones evidentes de la proyección de Mercator y su enfoque eurocéntrico dieron lugar a varias alternativas, particularmente entre los cartógrafos de la izquierda amplia o progresista. Otros, especialmente en los Estados Unidos y Europa, se han opuesto a ella, argumentando que el tamaño no importa y que no les molesta que se les muestre mucho más grandes de lo que realmente son. Sin embargo, muchos discreparon e intentaron desarrollar proyecciones alternativas. La proyección de Gall-Peterson, que contó con el respaldo del gobierno socialdemócrata alemán de Willy Brandt, es una de esas proyecciones. La proyección de Hobo Dyer, que se encuentra en el dominio público global —lo que significa que cualquiera puede usarla sin pagar regalías—, es otra. Finalmente, las Naciones Unidas han puesto fin a las guerras de los mapas , con una victoria rotunda a favor de la moción presentada por las naciones africanas para utilizar mapas de área igual. La votación fue de 164 a favor de la moción, con los Estados Unidos como único voto en contra. El objetivo del mapa de Mercator era facilitar la navegación de los barcos en mar abierto, donde ya no es posible orientarse reconociendo diversas características topológicas, como ríos, colinas, aldeas, pueblos, etc. En mar abierto, si identificamos nuestra ubicación en un mapa de Mercator y el destino al que deseamos llegar, podemos viajar en línea recta, tal como se muestra en el mapa. Conserva la dirección, lo que significa que, si trazamos un rumbo en un mapa de Mercator, podemos fijar nuestro rumbo según él. Es por eso que los países marítimos utilizaban proyecciones de Mercator para la navegación cuando contaban con pocos instrumentos, más allá de la brújula y el sextante, para determinar su ubicación con respecto a su destino. Es importante señalar que la dirección que se muestra en el mapa no es la ruta más corta entre el punto de partida y el destino. El camino más corto entre dos puntos en la superficie del globo terráqueo es el arco del gran círculo que va desde su ubicación hasta su destino. Una línea recta en un mapa bidimensional puede parecer la distancia más corta, pero no lo es, ya que no toma en cuenta la curvatura de la Tierra. En pocas palabras, el precio que pagamos por convertir una superficie curva en una plana y bidimensional es que distorsionamos la forma de la superficie. Estiramos ciertas áreas y encogemos otras. En una Tierra plana, la distancia más corta sería la que indica su brújula, pero no así en una esfera. Anteriormente, los barcos utilizaban las estrellas y el sol del mediodía para estimar dónde se encontraban y hacia dónde debían dirigirse. Fijar un rumbo hacia su destino utilizando un mapa de Mercator y una brújula resultaba mucho más sencillo que tratar de adivinar dónde se encontraba en un océano desierto, sin puntos de referencia, y cambiar constantemente de rumbo en busca de una ruta más corta. La línea constante en el mapa se denominaba “línea de rumbo”, y seguirla ayudaba enormemente a los navegantes. Los mapas de Mercator eran populares en Europa, particularmente en Europa occidental, ya que, aparte de los árabes, eran las principales naciones marítimas. Mientras que los árabes se enfocaban en el comercio, particularmente en el Océano Índico, los europeos occidentales se dedicaban no solo a la pesca, sino también, fuera de temporada, a la piratería, las incursiones y el saqueo de otras naciones. Los vikingos, importantes figuras románticas en Hollywood, eran particularmente brutales , ya que combinaban el saqueo con el comercio de esclavos. Por supuesto, como todos los piratas, una vez que se alcanza el poder, se puede convertir ese poder marítimo en conquista. Los hombres del norte, o nórdicos, conquistaron Francia y más tarde Inglaterra, y son conocidos por nosotros como normandos. No muy diferentes de las Compañías Holandesa y Británica de las Indias Orientales, que comenzaron con el comercio y la piratería y más tarde establecieron imperios coloniales. No es de extrañar que los reinos marítimos de Europa occidental, para quienes la guerra, la pesca para alimentarse y la caza de focas, morsas, etc., con el fin de obtener grasa y pieles, constituían las principales fuentes de riqueza, se centraran mucho más en la navegación y la construcción naval. Esto significaba que los barcos construidos para el agitado Océano Atlántico contaban con ciertos avances de los que carecían los barcos árabes e indios, aunque la brújula, el astrolabio, los mapas astronómicos y otros instrumentos náuticos procedían todos de Asia. Los barcos de Europa occidental contaban con cañones montados en cubierta, lo que les permitía disparar “andanadas”, es decir, disparar múltiples cañones simultáneamente, a diferencia de los cañones montados en la proa y la popa. Esa es una de las principales razones por las que un puñado de países de Europa occidental pudo aprovechar los océanos para, primero, hacerse con el comercio marítimo y, más tarde, construir sus imperios coloniales. A diferencia de los fuertes fijos, los barcos actuaban como fuertes flotantes, lo que proporcionaba a los europeos una movilidad en las guerras navales de la que carecían las fortificaciones terrestres estáticas. Como muestra la monumental obra de Needham sobre la ciencia y la tecnología chinas , muchos avances náuticos en la construcción naval y la navegación procedían de China. Los siete viajes de los barcos de Zheng He en el siglo XV (1405-1443), que partieron de China, el sudeste asiático y la India, y recorrieron la costa árabe hasta África, demostraron que los chinos habían construido enormes barcos y una poderosa armada. En aquella época, se encontraban muy por delante de Europa y de los árabes, tanto en la construcción naval como en los buques de guerra. Las diferencias internas entre la aristocracia terrateniente y los comandantes navales condujeron a la destrucción de la armada y a la prohibición de construir cualquier buque de alta mar. Aunque la India suministraba a los árabes la madera necesaria para la construcción naval y en algún momento contó con una poderosa armada, cedió el comercio de alta mar a los comerciantes árabes. Ni China, ni la India, ni los árabes consideraban que la armada tuviera importancia militar. Como dijo un emperador mogol: “Los océanos son para los comerciantes, no para nosotros, los guerreros”. Las divisiones de castas en la India también llevaron, en un momento dado, a prohibir a las castas superiores emprender viajes oceánicos. La travesía del Kala Pani , siempre prohibida por los brahmanes, no se respetó de manera estricta. La India experimentó una gran expansión marítima en el período budista, no a través de la guerra, sino mediante el comercio y la labor de proselitismo de los monjes budistas. Tras el resurgimiento del hinduismo en el sur, el tabú que prohibía a las castas superiores viajar a través de los océanos se aplicó de manera más estricta, dejando los océanos en manos de comerciantes y mercenarios. Por ejemplo, las principales flotas de los reyes indios – ya fuera el rey de Calicut, también conocido como Samundri o Zamorin, o Shivaji – dependían de comandantes navales y buques mercenarios, muchos de ellos al mando de árabes. Como demuestra la historia, los mapas, el comercio marítimo y la guerra están íntimamente relacionados. Los viajes marítimos y las guerras navales confieren a los mapas una importancia estratégica. No es simplemente la ubicación de las fronteras de cada país lo que importa, sino también su visión estratégica del mundo. Un vistazo rápido a un mapa habría convencido a los líderes estadounidenses de que Irán cuenta con una clara ventaja estratégica en el Estrecho de Ormuz, al igual que los hutíes en el Mar Rojo y el paso hacia el Canal de Suez. A small victory against the maps which center the global north and shrink Africa

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Prabir Purkayastha ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-18T19:05:02+00:00

Lee en español aquí On September 4, the United Nations General Assembly adopted an African proposal for a new world map, which shows countries’ true size relative to one another. It aims to correct the view of the world shaped by the widely used world map based on the Mercator Projection, created by Gerardus Mercator, a 16th-century Flemish cartographer. The problem with the Mercator Map is well known; for example, it shows the island of Greenland as the same size as the African continent, even though Africa is 14 times the size of Greenland! Simply put, Mercator maps greatly exaggerate the global north – the US and Europe – while shrinking Africa and most of Asia. Nor does it treat the rest of the Southern Hemisphere kindly. South America, nearly three times the size of Europe, looks comparable to it on a typical Mercator map. Though there is no geographical reason the Southern Hemisphere should be smaller than the Northern Hemisphere, mapmakers made it significantly smaller because it had very little landmass beyond 40 degrees and was therefore cropped out. This meant that on most Mercator maps, because Western mapmakers cropped out much of the Southern Hemisphere, the equator was significantly displaced downward. The double whammy of squeezing the equatorial areas, where much of Africa lies, and shifting the equator down made the global north, particularly Europe and North America, seem much larger than they actually are. Typically, on most Mercator maps, the Northern Hemisphere looks twice the size of the Southern Hemisphere, though the two hemispheres are identical in size. Alternatives to the Mercator projection Any map, whether Mercator or any other projection, has the problem that you can be true to either size or shape, but not both. The problem is a fundamental one of projecting a three-dimensional surface to a two-dimensional piece of paper. There is no way of doing this without distortions. Only a globe can truly represent the three-dimensional earth faithfully. But then what do we do when we want to reproduce geographical details for our readers in books or articles? The blatant distortions of the Mercator projection and its Eurocentric approach gave rise to several alternatives, particularly among broad-left or progressive cartographers. Others, particularly in the US and Europe have opposed it, arguing that size does not matter and that they were comfortable being shown much bigger than they actually are. But many dissented and tried to work out alternate projections. The Gall-Peterson projection, which was backed by the German social democratic government of Willy Brandt, is one such projection. The Hobo Dyer projection, which is in the global commons, meaning anybody can use it without paying royalties, is another one. Finally, the United Nations has ended the map wars , with a resounding victory for the motion moved by the African nations for using equal area maps. The voting was 164 in favor of the motion with the US casting the only nay vote. Read more: US votes against UN resolution promoting maps reflecting Africa’s true size The objective of Mercator map was for ships navigating in open seas, where we can no longer navigate recognizing various topological features, rivers, hills, villages, towns, etc. On the open ocean, if we identify where we are on a Mercator map and the destination we want to reach, we can travel in a straight line, as shown on the map. It conserves direction, meaning if we plot a course on a Mercator map, we can set our course by it. That is why ocean-going countries used Mercator projections for navigation when they had few instruments beyond the compass and the sextant to determine where they were relative to their destination. It is important to note that the direction shown on the map is not the shortest path between where you start from and your destination. The shortest path on two points on the surface of the globe is the arc of the great circle from where you are to your destination. A straight line on a 2-dimensional map may look like the shortest distance, but it is not as it does not account for the Earth’s curvature. Simply put, the price we pay for converting a curved surface to a flat 2-dimensional one is that we distort the shape of the surface. We stretch certain areas and shrink others. On a flat earth, the shortest distance would be what your compass shows, but not on a sphere. Earlier, ships used stars and the midday sun to estimate where they were and where they needed to go. Setting a course to your destination using a Mercator map and a compass was much easier than trying to guess where you were on an empty ocean with no landmarks and constantly changing course for a shorter route. The constant line on the map was called the thumb line, and following it greatly helped the seafarers. Mercator maps were popular in Europe, particularly in Western Europe, because, apart from the Arabs, they were the major ocean-going nations. Trade and piracy in the colonial era While the Arabs focused on trade, particularly in the Indian Ocean, Western Europeans focused not only on fishing but, in the off-season, on piracy, raiding, and looting other nations. The Vikings, major romantic figures in Hollywood, were particularly brutal , combining loot with slave trade. Of course, like all pirates, once you become powerful, you can convert that ocean power into conquest. The northern men, or Norsemen, conquered France and later England, and are known to us as Normans. Not very different from the Dutch and the British East India Companies, starting with trade and piracy and later establishing colonial empires. Not surprisingly, the ocean-going kingdoms of Western Europe, for whom war and fishing for food and hunting seals, walrus, etc., for blubber and skins were major sources of wealth, focused much more on seafaring and shipbuilding. This meant ships built for the rougher Atlantic Ocean had certain advances that Arab and Indian ships did not, though the compass, astrolabe, astronomical maps, and other nautical instruments all came from Asia. Western European ships had deck-mounted cannons, which allowed them to fire “broadside,” meaning multiple cannons firing simultaneously, unlike the front- and rear-mounted cannons. That is one of the major reasons a handful of West European countries could use the oceans to first capture the ocean trade and later build their colonial empires. Unlike fixed forts, ships acted as floating ones, giving Europeans mobility in naval wars that static ground fortifications lacked. Ocean navigation and development in Asia As Needham’s monumental work on Chinese science and technology shows, many nautical advances in shipbuilding and navigation came from China. Seven voyages by Zheng He’s ships in the 15th century (1405-1443), which traveled from China, Southeast Asia, India, and down the Arab coast to Africa, showed that the Chinese had built huge ships and a powerful navy. At that time, they were well ahead of Europe and the Arabs, in both shipbuilding and warships. Internal differences between the landed aristocracy and the naval commanders led to the navy’s destruction and a ban on building any oceangoing ships. Although India supplied the timber necessary for shipbuilding to the Arabs, and once had a powerful navy, it ceded the oceangoing trade to Arab merchants. Neither China, India, nor the Arabs saw the navy as militarily important. As one Mughal Emperor said, Oceans are for traders, not us, the warriors. Caste divisions in India also led, at one point, to barring upper castes from undertaking ocean voyages. Crossing Kala Pani , always forbidden by the Brahmins, was not strictly followed. India saw major maritime expansion in the Buddhist period, not by war but through trade and proselytizing Buddhist monks. After the revival of Hinduism in the South, the taboo against upper-caste travel across oceans was enforced more strictly, leaving the oceans to traders and mercenaries. For example, the major fleets of the Indian kings, whether the King of Calicut, also known as Samundri or Zamorin, or Shivaji, relied on mercenary naval commanders and ships, many commanded by the Arabs. As history shows, maps, ocean trade, and war are intimately connected. Ocean voyages and naval wars make maps strategically important. It is not simply where each country’s borders lie that is important, but also how it strategically views the world. We can see this currently playing out in the Bab-al-Mandeb and the Persian Gulf. A quick look at a map would have convinced US leaders that Iran has a clear strategic edge in the Strait of Hormuz, just as Ansar Allah do in the Red Sea and the passage to the Suez Canal. Neglecting the oceans repeats the mistakes Asia made in a different age, mistakes from which it has learned. We need to bear this in mind today as well. BRICS+ Summit: between promises and contradictions

Lee en español aquí The 18th BRICS Summit, held in New Delhi on September 12–13, occurred amid an exceptional intensification of international contradictions. Wars in West Asia, unilateral sanctions, tariff barriers, and the weaponization of finance and technology have exposed the fundamentally unequal character of the imperialist-dominated international order. Against this background, the continued expansion of BRICS reflects the desire of developing countries for greater strategic autonomy and a more democratic system of global governance. At the same time, the summit demonstrated that BRICS+ remains a heterogeneous grouping whose members differ substantially in their political alignments and economic interests. The New Delhi Declaration, the outcome of the summit, reiterated the demand for reform of the United Nations, international financial institutions, and the multilateral trading system. It opposed unilateral sanctions not authorized by the United Nations Security Council and advocated greater trade in national currencies. The declaration also supported increased representation for developing countries in institutions whose rules continue to be determined primarily by the United States and its allies. These positions have objective significance at a time when Washington increasingly employs sanctions, tariffs, export controls, and control over payment systems to discipline countries unwilling to accept its dictates. Read more: BRICS nations call for end of US blockade on Cuba, condemn Israeli and US violations in Iran Ambiguity Yet the declaration’s carefully-negotiated language also revealed the limitations of the expanded formation. BRICS+ now includes countries subjected to sustained Western pressure, such as Russia and Iran, as well as the United Arab Emirates and other states retaining extensive economic and security ties with the West. Consensus can consequently be maintained only through formulations that acknowledge conflicts without always identifying their political causes. This ambiguity was especially apparent in the summit’s treatment of the widening war in West Asia and the position adopted by Iran. Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian used the summit to denounce unilateral sanctions and the war of aggression against Iran carried out by the United States and Israel. He correctly stressed that economic resilience cannot be separated from peace, sovereignty, and security. Iran also advocated trade in national currencies, greater use of the New Development Bank, and the construction of independent transport and energy corridors (as it has already created through Pakistan, which has provided six roadways for Chinese and Russian goods to enter Iran). For a country subjected to decades of sanctions, exclusion from Western financial networks, and repeated threats of regime change, membership of BRICS+ provides an important diplomatic and economic opening. Iran’s position, however, contains a marked ambiguity. Tehran presents BRICS+ as an instrument for resisting unilateralism and defending sovereignty. Simultaneously, it must seek accommodation with neighboring Gulf states, several of which are deeply integrated with Western capital and security arrangements. Iran signed a declaration calling for restraint and de-escalation even while accusing the United States and Israel of aggression and defending its own retaliatory actions. Its militancy against hyper-imperialist pressure is therefore combined with a pragmatic effort to avoid regional isolation, secure sanctions relief, and preserve channels of negotiation. Read more: No new talks with the US until the previous conditions are met, says Iran This should not be understood simply as inconsistency. Iran confronts a vastly superior military and financial coalition and must maneuver within severe constraints. But it also illustrates the broader problem facing BRICS+. Opposition to Western domination does not automatically produce a coherent anti-imperialist program. The participating states are capitalist states pursuing their respective national interests. Their ruling classes may resist particular aspects of US hegemony while remaining prepared to reach accommodations with global finance or with regional reactionary powers. BRICS+ is consequently an arena of struggle within the changing world order, not an already constituted alternative to hyper-imperialism. Partners The summit’s most consequential political development for Asia was the meeting between Prime Minister Narendra Modi and Chinese President Xi Jinping. Xi’s first visit to India in seven years confirmed the gradual improvement in relations since the military disengagement arrangements initiated in 2024. The two leaders agreed that India and China should regard each other as partners rather than rivals and approach bilateral relations from a long-term strategic perspective. They also discussed the boundary question, trade, market access and supply-chain problems. Any durable improvement in India–China relations must be welcomed. The prolonged confrontation following the Galwan Valley clash of 2020 damaged both countries, increased military expenditure and strengthened the efforts of the United States to incorporate India into its Indo-Pacific strategy. Washington has consistently sought to turn India into a strategic counterweight to China through the Quad and expanding military and technological cooperation. A continuing India–China confrontation therefore serves US strategy far more than it serves the interests of the Indian or Chinese peoples. Peace and tranquillity along the Line of Actual Control remain indispensable. Agreements on disengagement must be followed by sustained diplomatic and military-level engagement, clarification of disputed areas and strict observance of existing arrangements. Neither nationalist rhetoric nor competitive infrastructure construction should be allowed to trigger another confrontation. Differences over the boundary cannot be wished away, but they can and must be managed without subordinating the entire relationship to militarised rivalry. Economic relations present an equally difficult contradiction. China is one of India’s largest trading partners and an important source of machinery, electronic components, chemicals, renewable-energy equipment and other industrial inputs. India’s enormous trade deficit with China, however, reflects both the weakness of Indian manufacturing and the obstacles faced by Indian exports in the Chinese market. Addressing this imbalance requires improved market access, Chinese investment under transparent conditions and, above all, an Indian industrial policy capable of developing domestic productive capacity. Over the past six months, two-way bilateral trade has soared . The Modi government’s approach has alternated between dependence on Chinese imports and politically motivated restrictions on Chinese investment and technology. Its “China-plus-one” rhetoric cannot conceal the failure of its own policies to generate broad-based industrialisation. Nor can closer strategic alignment with the United States supply India with an independent manufacturing base. The US seeks privileged access to the Indian market while using tariffs, technology restrictions and political pressure whenever Indian policies diverge from its interests. The incoherence of the Modi government is best demonstrated in its inability to actually produce a national development agenda that takes advantage of the buoyancy of the Chinese economy and disengages from a United State more geared towards economic nationalism. China, for its part, must recognize that stable relations require sensitivity to India’s legitimate concerns. These include the unresolved boundary, unequal market access and the trade imbalance. The considerable asymmetry between the two economies makes reciprocity particularly important. Beijing’s call for partnership will acquire substance only when accompanied by practical measures enabling Indian goods and services to enter the Chinese market and preventing border tensions from repeatedly overwhelming political dialogue. Cooperation between India and China would give BRICS+ far greater weight in the struggle for democratization of the international order. Together, the two countries represent more than a third of humanity. Their cooperation could advance reform of international institutions, climate finance, technology sharing, public-health initiatives, and a fairer trading system. Conversely, renewed confrontation would paralyse BRICS+ and permit outside powers to exploit divisions throughout Asia. India must therefore pursue an independent foreign policy based on peaceful coexistence, not participate in a US project to contain China. The New Delhi summit produced proposals for institutional continuity, stronger financial cooperation, and greater resilience among developing economies. But declarations will have limited value unless translated into material arrangements. The New Development Bank must expand lending in national currencies and prioritize socially necessary infrastructure rather than reproduce the conditionalities and corporate orientation of the World Bank. Cross-border payment mechanisms must be developed transparently, without creating new forms of domination by the strongest BRICS economies. The strong statements on behalf of the Cuban Revolution and the Palestinian people’s aspirations for self-determination will need practical application. Will the BRICS+ find a way to break the cruel and illegal US embargo on Cuba, and will the BRICS+ provide support for the revival of Palestinian political life? BRICS+ embodies both the movement towards a new world equilibrium and all the contradictions of that movement. The cautious India–China rapprochement is one example of the contradiction, combining recognition of mutual dependence with unresolved strategic distrust. The future of BRICS+ will depend upon whether such contradictions are peacefully managed and whether cooperation delivers tangible gains to working people rather than merely expanding the room for maneuver of national ruling classes. For India, the necessary course is clear: restore normal and peaceful relations with China, resist incorporation into the United States’ military strategy, oppose aggression and unilateral sanctions against Iran, and work for a genuinely democratic multilateral order. A new world equilibrium will only be when it restricts hyper-imperialist domination and enlarges the sovereignty and developmental possibilities of peoples. The New Delhi summit marked another step in that contested transition, but the direction and social content of the emerging order remain matters of political struggle. How to Understand Key Terms in Large Language Models

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: [:en]THE TRICONTINENTAL[:es]TRICONTINENTAL[:] ／ 著者: 不明 ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-18T09:44:48+00:00

When you use AI to write, read reports, or work with files, you may encounter terms such as token , context , and agent . These terms help explain what happens when you give an AI application a task: what information it can use, how it produces a response, and whether it can take actions such as searching for sources or saving a document. This guide introduces the main terms related to large language models (LLMs) through practical examples. It begins with how models process information, then explains how to give them instructions and reference material, how tools and agents carry out tasks, and how to organise ongoing work. It also introduces ways to access models through other software or run them on your own equipment. 1. How Language Models Work Large Language Models A large language model (LLM) is an AI model trained on vast amounts of text and other data to learn patterns in language. ‘Large’ refers to its scale, including the number of internal parameters adjusted during training. When generating text, a typical LLM uses the information available to it – including your instructions and the text already written – to predict what could come next. More precisely, it predicts the next token , a small unit of text such as a word or part of a word. It selects a token, adds it to the text, and repeats the process. In this way, it builds a response step by step. Many people first encounter LLMs through a question-and-answer conversation. But the same process also allows models to summarise documents, translate, and draft text. A fluent response, however, is not necessarily a correct one: generating plausible language does not guarantee factual accuracy. Token A token is a small unit of text that a language model processes. Before your message reaches the model, a tool called a tokeniser splits it into tokens. A token may be a whole word, part of a word, or a punctuation mark. Spaces can also form part of a token. For example, a tokeniser might keep ‘cat’ as one token but split ‘playing’ into ‘play’ and ‘ing’. This is an illustration, not a fixed rule: different tokenisers can split the same text differently. A token is therefore not the same thing as a word, and counting words will not give you an exact token count. Each token is assigned a numerical ID so that the model can process the text as numbers. When generating a reply, the model predicts tokens one at a time. These tokens are then converted back into the text you see on screen. Token counts matter when using a model. Input tokens are the tokens in the material supplied to it, such as your instructions, documents, and any earlier messages included in the conversation. Output tokens are the tokens it generates. Many model providers charge for API use based on these counts, often at different rates for input and output. Models also have limits on how many tokens they can process or generate in one request. Longer texts generally require more tokens. The language also makes a difference: expressing the same meaning in different languages can produce different token counts. To find the exact count for a piece of text, you need the tokeniser used by that model. Pre-trained Knowledge Token prediction explains how a model produces text, but it does not yet explain why a model can answer questions without being given any external files. The answer lies in what it learnt during training. During training, a large language model learns patterns and information from massive amounts of text, code, and other data. This is also why, even if we do not provide the model with any external files, it can still answer a large number of questions about common sense, history, maths, programming, and so on. This content is not obtained by the model through temporary searching. The model has already learnt patterns related to this information during training. This information is often described as the model’s pre-trained knowledge . However, the model does not have a perfect database of facts. It learns patterns from its training data, and these patterns can sometimes be incomplete, outdated, biased , or simply wrong. When the model does not have enough reliable information to answer a question, it may still try to produce an answer based on what it has learnt. This is one reason hallucinations can happen. Multimodality Pre-trained knowledge is still mainly about patterns learnt from text and other training data. But is there a model that can accept different types of input beyond natural language, or generate content other than ordinary text, such as images, audio, and video? The answer is yes. Such models are called multimodal models . Imagine uploading a photograph of a flower and asking, ‘What kind of flower is this?’ The system prepares two kinds of input. Your question is split into tokens and converted into numerical IDs, as described above. The photograph is processed separately into a numerical form that allows the model to work with visual details such as colours, shapes, and the arrangement of petals. The model then uses both the visual information from the photograph and the question you have asked to generate a reply. This ability to combine different types of information is a key feature of multimodal models. Models that support audio or video also convert those inputs into numerical forms they can process, although the methods vary between models. Of course, this does not mean that models supporting only natural language have no external channels at all for understanding images. A common approach is to use image text recognition or audio transcription tools to convert different types of data into general text, which is then input into the model. To some extent, however, this approach involves loss. Imagine that, for a human being, reading a textual description of an image is still inferior to viewing the image directly. So multimodal models are generally better at working directly with these different types of information. 2. Giving Models Instructions and Information Prompt Understanding how a model works internally is useful, but in daily use, the first thing that matters is how we communicate with it. To make a large language model give a high-quality answer, how you express the task to the model matters greatly, in addition to the model’s own abilities. When interacting with a large language model, the instructions, questions, background information, constraints, and examples we provide can generally be called a prompt . So a prompt is not merely a simple question. For example, ‘help me write an email’ can count as a prompt. But if you add the email’s use case, goal, tone, format, and specific requirements, you can form a more complete prompt. The process of designing and refining this information so that the model can complete a task more accurately and consistently is usually called prompt engineering . From the point of view of usage, what we normally type directly into an AI input box is usually a user prompt , that is, a prompt provided by the user. In addition, the system may provide the model in advance with a special kind of prompt called a system prompt . This is used to define the model’s overall behaviour, role, capability boundaries, and answering style. In the usual instruction hierarchy, the system prompt has higher priority than the user prompt. So even if the user gives an instruction that conflicts with the system’s requirements, the model usually needs to follow the system prompt first. This is also why the same large language model may behave differently in different AI applications. Apart from the model itself, the system prompt and other contextual information provided by the application can also have an important impact on the final result. For practical examples of how to structure instructions, provide context, and specify the output you need, see How to Talk to AI – A Prompt Engineering Guide . Context When a model answers a question, it can draw on more than the latest message you typed. The context is the information available to it when generating that response. This can include system instructions, your current question, earlier messages and replies, and any document passages or search results supplied to the model. For example, suppose you provide an article and ask for a summary. You then ask, ‘How does the author support the main argument?’ To answer this follow-up question, the model needs access to the article as well as your new question. The application usually includes earlier material in each new request. However, a message remaining visible in your chat history does not guarantee that its full text is still available to the model. The amount of information a model can handle at once is limited by its context window , measured in tokens. For many models, this window must accommodate both the input and the response being generated. Instructions, articles, earlier messages, and replies all take up space. The limit therefore concerns more than the length of a single question. As a conversation grows, applications manage this limit in different ways. Some ask you to start a new conversation; others remove older material or replace it with a summary. Summarising earlier material to make room for further conversation is often called compaction . For example, a long discussion about editing an article might be condensed into a short summary of the main decisions and remaining tasks. This allows the conversation to continue, but details or qualifications may be lost. If a model appears to have ‘forgotten’ something, that information may no longer be in its current context, or it may have been left out of a summary. However, models can also overlook or misuse information that is still available. For important tasks, restate essential requirements, provide the relevant passages again when needed, and check the answer against the source. Useful context is relevant and clearly organised; adding more material does not automatically produce a better answer. Memory Context is the information a model can use when generating its current response. But some information may also be useful in future conversations. Memory is a feature that allows an AI system to save information for later use. The application typically stores it outside the model, for example in files or saved records, and adds relevant information to the context when needed. Saving a memory does not retrain the model or increase its context window. For example, in an application with memory enabled, you might ask it to remember, ‘Please use British English in writing.’ The application can save this preference and include it in the context of later writing tasks, reducing the need to repeat it. Memory can also preserve project information, such as the intended audience for an article, editorial decisions you have agreed on, or tasks that remain unfinished. What is saved and how it is used varies between applications. Some let you explicitly choose what to remember; others identify useful information from conversations. Saved information may become outdated, may not be brought into a particular response, or may be applied incorrectly. For important tasks, it is still useful to restate key requirements and check that the answer follows them. Retrieval-Augmented Generation A large collection of documents may exceed a model’s context window, while only some passages are relevant to your question. The model may also need information it did not encounter during training, such as internal documents or recent reports. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is a way of finding relevant information and using it to help a model answer. The process has three main steps: the system searches a source or collection of sources for relevant material, adds that material to the model’s context alongside your question, and asks the model to generate an answer using it. The retrieved information provides reference material for that response; it does not retrain the model. For example, suppose you have a collection of research reports and ask, ‘What was the unemployment rate in South Africa in 2023?’ The system searches the reports for passages or tables that could answer the question. It then supplies the relevant extracts to the model, which uses them to formulate a response. Where the system preserves source details, the answer can also include references so that you can check the figure and its context. The sources available for retrieval are often organised into a knowledge base : a collection of documents or other information that the system can search. This might contain research papers, institutional reports, or internal guidance. Web search can also form part of a RAG process when the system retrieves information from webpages and supplies it to the model to help answer a question. RAG can help a model produce answers grounded in specific sources while reducing the amount of material placed in its context. However, it does not guarantee accuracy. The system may retrieve the wrong passage, miss an important qualification, or use an outdated source; the model may also misinterpret the material. For important claims, check the cited source and whether it actually supports the answer. 3. From Answers to Actions Tools So far, we have looked at how models generate answers and use information. But many tasks also require actions, such as finding a report online, updating a document, or playing a song. Tools allow an AI application to carry out these operations. A tool is a function that a model can call to perform a specific operation. For example, a web search tool retrieves information from the internet, while a file tool reads or modifies documents. The model requests an operation, the application executes it, and the result is returned to the model. Suppose you ask an AI assistant to revise an article and save the changes. It can call a file-reading tool to access the draft, use the model’s language capabilities to revise the text, and then call a file-writing tool to save the revised version. The model produces the edits; the tools provide access to the document and carry out the changes. Agents and Workflows Tools allow an AI application to perform individual operations. Completing a task may also require deciding which operation to perform next, checking its result, and adjusting the approach. An agent is an AI system that uses a model to guide this process towards a goal, without requiring the user to specify every step. An agent typically works through a repeated cycle: the model chooses an action, a tool carries it out, and the result is returned to the model. The model then uses that feedback to decide what to do next. This might mean continuing with the task, trying a different approach, or asking the user for clarification. Consider the article-editing example above. You ask the assistant to revise a draft according to an attached style guide and save a new version. An agent can read both documents, identify the changes needed, revise the article, and check the result against the guide. If it finds inconsistent spelling or a missed requirement, it can make another revision before saving. An agent may stop when it judges the task complete, needs your input, or reaches a limit set by the application. Its output still needs to be checked against the original requirements. Tasks can also be organised into a workflow : a predefined sequence of steps, such as reading a draft, checking its spelling, and saving a revised version. In a workflow, the main steps are specified in advance; an agent can decide which steps to take as the task develops. The two approaches can be combined. For example, an editing workflow might include an agent that decides how to resolve problems found during review. 4. Organising and Extending AI Work Workspace Once AI can carry out tasks, the next question is how to organise longer projects. If you only occasionally ask AI a question, a chat window is enough. For longer projects, such as writing a book or developing software, repeatedly explaining the files, background, and progress takes time. Many AI tools provide something called a workspace . You can think of a workspace as a dedicated working area for a project. It may contain files, instructions, previous outputs, and other information related to the project. The application can access relevant materials from the workspace when needed; this does not mean that every file is included in the model’s context at once. For example, we can create a ‘writing project’ workspace and put articles, images, and reference materials into it. Afterwards, when we ask AI to revise an article, organise materials, or continue writing the next chapter, it can work with these materials without our needing to re-upload all the materials every time. For a step-by-step guide to organising project folders and working with an AI agent in Visual Studio Code, see our AI workspace setup guide . Skill When you ask AI to carry out similar tasks repeatedly, you may find yourself explaining the same requirements each time. A skill is a reusable set of instructions and supporting resources for a particular task. It gives the application a prepared approach to follow, reducing the need to explain the process from scratch. For example, suppose you regularly edit articles for a publication. You could create an editing skill that instructs the AI to use British English, preserve the author’s argument, check punctuation, and present suggested changes with brief explanations. The skill could also include the publication’s style guide and examples of previous edits. When you use it with a new draft, the application can draw on these instructions and references to review the article. You can reuse the same skill with different articles and update it when your editorial requirements change. This helps maintain a consistent approach while allowing you to add instructions specific to each draft. To learn how to find, install, use, and create skills for your own tasks, see How to Install, Use, and Create Skills . Model Context Protocol Model Context Protocol (MCP) is a shared standard that helps AI applications connect to other software and use its tools or information. You can think of it as a common connector: when both sides support MCP, they have an agreed way to work together. For example, suppose you are editing an article and the reports you need are stored in your organisation’s document library. With a suitable MCP connection, your AI application can search that library and retrieve relevant passages to help check the article. This can save you from finding and uploading each report yourself. A skill and an MCP connection can support different parts of the same task. The skill provides instructions for reviewing the article; the MCP connection gives the application access to the reference materials. 5. Accessing and Running Models API Key / Auth Token An application programming interface (API) is a way for one piece of software to ask another to do something or provide information. For example, a writing application can use DeepSeek’s API to send an article to a DeepSeek model and request ‘Summarise this text’. The model generates a summary and sends it back to the writing application, which displays it for you. To use that API, the application may need an API key or an authentication token : a credential sent with the request to show that it is authorised to access the service. Depending on the service, this credential may also link usage to an account for billing. Here, ‘token’ refers to an access credential, rather than the units of text described earlier. Open-Source Models An open-source model is released with materials and permissions that allow others to use, study, modify, and share it. Researchers and developers can examine how it works and adapt it for their own purposes, building on existing work rather than training a new model from scratch. Examples include publicly released models from China’s DeepSeek and Alibaba’s Qwen team, which users can download, run, and adapt for their own tasks. For users, one practical benefit is the option to run a model on their own equipment. For example, a research organisation could use a downloaded model on its own server to summarise internal reports, keeping the documents within its own systems. Smaller models may also run on a personal computer, while larger ones require more powerful hardware. Conclusion The concepts in this guide describe different parts of working with AI: how a model generates a response, what information is available to it, and how an application uses tools to carry out a task. Understanding how these parts work together helps explain both the capabilities and the limits of the applications you use. For your next task, consider what information the model needs, which actions the application must perform, and what requirements the result should meet. These questions can help you provide relevant materials, give clearer instructions, and check whether the work has been completed as intended.
