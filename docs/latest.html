What to Do When Your Union Contract Is Voted Down

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Chris Brooks ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-05T20:39:36+00:00

Like stumbling across a yeti or flying saucer, I was experiencing the seemingly impossible: a conversation between HR executives was warming the depths of my heart and giving me hope for the future. It was December 2022, and I was attending a convening of company executives and labor leaders hosted by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology’s Sloan School of Management. A line of corporate bigwigs from long-unionized Fortune 500 companies were heatedly voicing their exasperation over the new bargaining climate in the country. Apparently, all of these companies were suffering from the same affliction: tentative agreements were being voted down by union members. In some cases, contracts went down by large margins several times before finally passing. “It’s especially bad with the young workers, who have completely unreasonable expectations,” said one HR executive from a company that would go on to make nearly a billion dollars in net income that year. “They want the return of cost-of-living adjustments and pensions. It’s completely unrealistic with where the economy is today,” added another executive from a company with a market capitalization of more than $30 billion. “The union leaders are often just as upset as we are about how out of touch their membership is.” For years now, workers’ expectations have been rising across the country. As they should be. Corporate America rebounded after the Great Recession, and corporate profits have soared over the past decade, reaching an all-time high in the first quarter of 2026. In a recent article headlined “Corporate profits were already at historic highs. They shot even higher in Q1,” Yahoo Finance journalist Ben Werschkul writes that “US business profits are booming no matter how you measure it,” with corporate balance sheets remaining above historic norms for more than a decade. For the working class, the story is very different. Our standard of living has declined under the combined pressure of stagnant wages and rising prices. Income inequality in the United States has reached heights not seen since the Great Depression. According to the Wall Street Journal , inflation-adjusted corporate profits increased by 50 percent between the end of 2019 and early 2026. Hourly wages increased by a pathetic 3 percent over the same period. Workers everywhere should be demanding more — and that is especially true for unionized workers at highly profitable companies. I applaud workers who are making company executives wring their hands over their refusal to accept that status quo in bargaining. But just because we demand more does not mean we can easily win it. And we can’t afford to be naive about how asymmetric the power imbalance is between workers and employers, or how unprepared many unions are for a real fight. There are real dangers when a tentative agreement is voted down. Workers might go out on a long and painful strike only to return with an offer little better than the one they originally shot down. The experience can divide and demoralize both the membership and the bargaining team. Bargainers can feel abandoned by the members who elected them. Members can feel betrayed by the bargainers they elected. In the end, the union feels weaker, and the boss appears stronger. One of my proudest accomplishments as the United Auto Workers’ (UAW) top strategist was creating the Department of Bargaining Strategies. We didn’t know it at the time, but one of the new department’s top priorities would quickly become assisting locals whose contracts had been voted down. Time and again, we saw two interrelated problems driving no votes: local bargaining teams were not ambitious enough in bargaining, and they had not built the high-participation campaigns necessary to win more. Thankfully, the road to winning a stronger contract and building a more powerful union is also the road to winning enthusiastic and overwhelming contract ratifications. Through this new department — staffed with a combination of experienced bargainer-organizers hired from other unions and some of the most talented staff from the rank and file — we showed bargaining teams and members that a contract rejection does not have to become a source of division. It can instead become an opportunity to change how the union bargains and fights. By making negotiations more transparent and turning the entire membership into active players in the fight for a strong contract, a local can lay the foundation to win more while building greater trust between the bargaining team and the shop floor. The Three Axioms of Bargaining After more than a decade of bargaining, I have boiled down the basics to three axioms that I repeat over and over when training bargaining teams: Bargaining Axiom no. 1: Bargaining is when we go to the table with proposals to improve our working conditions, pay, and benefits, and management says no. Bargaining Axiom no. 2: What we win at the table reflects the power we build in the workplace. Our power is rooted in our unity and our ability to act collectively. Bargaining Axiom no. 3: Meaningful progress in negotiations most often happens when we create a crisis for the employer — typically through a credible strike threat. The first axiom is my definition of bargaining, but don’t take it too literally. Management rarely just says no at the bargaining table. They’re often more sophisticated than that. Instead, employers prefer to cultivate a bargaining environment of exhaustion and isolation. The company stretches out negotiations, warns that ambitious demands will cost jobs, and portrays a potential strike as reckless. At the same time, management tries to confine the bargaining process to a small room occupied by professional representatives. The more isolated the union’s bargaining committee becomes, the easier it is for management to persuade negotiators that nothing better is possible — and for members to conclude that their own negotiators failed them. The phrase “collective bargaining” is often treated as a legalistic term. It should instead describe how negotiations are actually conducted: collectively, with members shaping the goals, tracking the fight, and taking responsibility for building leverage. Members are far more likely to support an agreement when they helped decide what the union would fight for, understood how management responded, and participated in the pressure campaign that produced the final settlement. Under those conditions, ratification is the conclusion of a collective struggle rather than a blind vote on a document negotiated behind closed doors. Famed UAW President Walter Reuther once said that “power is the ability of a labor union like the UAW to make the most powerful corporation in the world — General Motors — say ‘yes’ when it wants to say ‘no.’” His point is well taken. Ask anyone who has served on a union negotiating team, whether at a zealously anti-union manufacturing plant in the South or a supposedly bighearted nonprofit hospital in New England, and they will tell you that making persuasive arguments to management won’t get you anywhere. Unless unions collectively build the capacity to make management’s refusal costly, the boss can and will keep saying no. And workers may ultimately blame their own negotiators and union leadership as a result. Member participation is not merely a good idea or the right thing to do. It is the source of our power. How to Build a Powerful Contract Campaign The second axiom of bargaining is that what we win at the table depends on the power we build in the workplace. That power is not built in a day and can’t be rushed in the final days before contract expiration. A strong contract is won by a membership that has been involved long before bargaining begins and continues to play a meaningful role until the agreement is ratified. We do this by organizing a contract campaign. A contract campaign is a collective action plan with a beginning, middle, and end, organized around specific demands that workers are trying to win. It has a target: someone who has the power to give us what we want. And it involves a series of escalating actions that progressively turn up the heat on that target and create the conditions for victory. Those actions should be timed to support what is happening in bargaining. Ideally, they build in intensity and participation, culminating at the union’s peak of strength. This is where the third axiom comes in. We build enough power to finally make the boss say yes when they want to keep saying no. We do that by creating a crisis for the employer, typically (though not always) by organizing a supermajority strike. Returning to the UAW’s “stand-up strike” as an example: this strike kept the union at the peak of its strength, using the credible threat of additional strikes over and over again to drive concessions out of Ford, General Motors, and Stellantis. It was a unique strategy that kept the companies guessing, captivated the media’s attention, and forced politicians to pick a side (resulting in then President Joe Biden walking a UAW picket line , a historic first in the United States). Ultimately, though, this strategy brought the companies to their knees because of the power of UAW members who were ready and willing to walk off the job at a moment’s notice — and proves the point that our power in negotiations rests on our power in the workplace. Through the UAW’s Department of Bargaining Strategies, we started training locals to think of the bargaining team and the shop floor like jumper cables: they only work when they are connected. That connection has to be maintained through representative structures, constant two-way communication, and workplace actions that have an effect that members can see at the bargaining table. We can build those jumper cables by democratizing the bargaining process. Don’t Keep Negotiations Behind Closed Doors Keeping members in the dark about bargaining is a recipe for disaster. Bargaining teams should never agree to ground rules that bar them from talking to the membership or the public about the status of negotiations. That language only helps management. Management knows everything that is happening in bargaining — they’re in the room! So secrecy simply denies workers on the shop floor the information they need to organize around what is happening in negotiations. Transparency strengthens trust between negotiators and the membership, and demonstrates to management that the bargaining team genuinely speaks with the authority of its coworkers. And it helps move members to take the actions necessary to win stronger contracts. Survey the Membership Before bargaining begins, use a survey to have members rank their top issues, define their priorities, and generate ideas for new proposals. Surveys should systematically gather input from a large majority of the bargaining unit. Bargainers should not just be collecting ideas for proposals but having their coworkers rank their priorities, so negotiators can accurately assess what matters the most. It is critical to know the difference between a demand that a supermajority of workers will strike over and another that might be popular but most aren’t willing to miss a paycheck over. Crucially, it’s important to not merely pass out surveys but also to use them as conversation starters. The bargaining survey is often the first opportunity to identify and recruit volunteers who can help write proposals, distribute future bargaining updates, and organize potential actions. Recruit Subject-Matter Experts The “official” bargaining team may be limited in size, but anyone and everyone can be involved in developing bargaining proposals. Recruit coworkers into working groups to hammer out proposals addressing issues that are widely and deeply felt in the workplace. Then have those workers present their proposals directly to management at the bargaining table. Is there a particular group of workers — cafeteria workers at the school, photographers at the magazine, lab techs in the hospital — who feel like they are always overlooked in bargaining? Have them collectively present their demands to management, and keep those demands as a highly visible union priority throughout negotiations. This not only can help restitch long-standing holes in the quilt of workplace solidarity but publicly reinforces to the boss that the workforce is united and invested in the bargaining process. Clearly Communicate Bargaining Goals Workers should know what the union is fighting for before negotiations begin. Some unions go so far as to distribute their bargaining proposals to members before presenting them to the company, then keep workers informed about how management responds. During the stand-up strike at the Big Three, UAW President Shawn Fain repeatedly used Facebook Live to explain the union’s demands and the companies’ responses. Members knew why they were being called on to strike and why they were being asked to stay out on the picket line. When management responded to the UAW’s initial proposals with an insulting first counter, Fain famously “filed” them in a garbage can on video. Members around the country began making memes of that garbage can. One local actually built him one and presented it to him for use in future negotiations. This is the kind of buy-in and member-initiated activity that good communication generates. Members come to understand that winning a strong contract does not fall solely on the bargaining team or union leadership. The fight belongs to all of us. Ironically, many longtime bargainers are afraid of publicizing the union’s demands and making ambitious proposals, because they fear their coworkers will vote it down for falling short of the union’s shared goals. This has it all backward. Our coworkers aren’t crazy or unreasonable. When workers are asked to make a decision without any insight into what was being demanded or how hard we had to fight to win it, it makes sense that they would make decisions based on the information they do have: comparing the employer’s massive profits and rising executive salaries against their own paltry wages and benefits. By that logic, it makes total sense to vote down every contract — exactly the situation I was happily listening to company executives complain about at the Sloan School event. The only way to cultivate mature, strategic judgment is through the kind of deep participation that a contract campaign with clear communication provides. And not for nothing, the fear of fighting for more has produced a vicious downward spiral of concessions in many unions. The only way we will raise standards in our industries is by abandoning the old philosophy of “aim low and settle lower” for a new approach: aim high, unite, and fight like hell to get everything we can. Send an Energizing Update After Every Session Bargaining updates — often written distributed through emails and flyers — should be short, informative, agitational, and persuasive. The purpose is not merely to report on a specific article that was tentatively agreed to or describe the boss’s latest hijinks at the table. Every update should help members understand what is at stake and what they can do next. It should be a call to action. Use Bargaining Caucus Time to Communicate With Coworkers Bargaining often involves a lot of downtime. Put it to use. Identify the key message members need to hear, create a phone tree, and use caucus time to call them. Or send out mass texts. Or write that shop paper to send out to everyone. Just make sure these communications are a two-way street. They should allow the bargaining team to gather feedback and help their coworkers understand the relationship between the actions they are taking on the shop floor and the company’s reactions at the table. Bring Members to Bargaining Allowing members to attend negotiations is often called “open bargaining,” an increasingly popular approach to negotiating a contract. Open bargaining shows management that the union is united, it shows members that their union has nothing to hide, and it provides a valuable learning experience for workers by giving them the opportunity to hear firsthand what management thinks of their own employees — and what they think our labor is worth. When possible, bargain at the workplace and invite members to attend before or after their shifts or during lunch breaks. This allows workers to move in and out of the room without losing an entire workday. Members who attend should follow a common set of rules: keep a straight face, do not speak unless it is part of a prepared plan, and follow up with negotiators afterward with questions. Pack Bargaining at Pivotal Moments Even when bargaining is always open, there may be dozens of sessions in a typical campaign. The union should identify pivotal moments when it makes sense to mobilize their coworkers in a show of force. Pack the first bargaining session . Turn the opening session into a bargaining kickoff. Systematically gather commitments to attend from the entire bargaining unit. Use the opening of negotiations for the bargaining team to explain the union’s priorities and why they matter. This demonstrates that the membership is united behind its goals and starts to signal how far everyone is willing to go to win them. Pack bargaining when members present an important proposal . Prepare workers who care deeply about a particular issue to speak at the table, and mobilize their coworkers to attend in support. This shows management that the proposal is not the personal project of one negotiator but a widely shared demand. Pack bargaining when management presents an important counterproposal . Do not allow management to insult only the bargaining team when it rejects workplace improvements or meaningful raises. Give the boss an audience. Force management to look a sea of their employees in the face while explaining why they are not worth what they are owed. What if management refuses to let members attend ? How we bargain is as much a reflection of our power as what we bargain. I’ve seen employers refuse to attend sessions when large numbers of employees were present, then try to turn the membership against their own negotiators by claiming the company would discuss wages if only the bargaining team stopped “irresponsibly” insisting that coworkers be allowed in the room. Unions have the legal right to decide who serves on their bargaining team — even if that means the entire bargaining unit. But anyone waiting for the National Labor Relations Board to enforce that right anytime soon is likely out of luck. If management refuses to enter the room when employees are present, then how we bargain becomes the first test of the union’s strength. Turn that refusal into an organizing opportunity. Bargaining on our terms — at the worksite, open to members, and without unnecessary secrecy — may itself require escalating action. The union has to unite everyone around that fight and keep them moving forward together. When You’re Stuck, Talk to Your Coworkers Bargaining is hard. We cannot always anticipate every trick management will use to confuse, divide, and demoralize us. Whenever negotiations get stuck, put your trust in your coworkers by holding one-on-one conversations, organizing department meetings, or hosting a town hall. Our collective intelligence and creativity are greater than those of any single individual. Collective problems require collective solutions. Strength in Solidarity A tentative agreement is most likely to be voted down when members feel disconnected from the bargaining process, distrust the people negotiating on their behalf, or believe the union settled for less without building the power necessary to win more. The answer is not to lower workers’ expectations. It is to organize those expectations into collective power. Through surveys and conversations, workers can develop proposals that address the most widely and deeply felt issues in the workplace. But winning those demands requires much more than strong arguments or powerful worker testimonies. We have to fight. And the bigger the demands, the bigger the fight, and the more powerful our union needs to be. That’s why contract campaigns are essential not only to winning strong agreements but to building stronger unions. When members help establish the demands, understand the obstacles, participate in the campaign, and experience the fight together, the final ratification vote is not treated like a referendum on how well the bargaining team did. It becomes the culmination of a struggle the membership helped lead.

Yes, the Left Won Big Last Night

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Branko Marcetic ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-05T20:10:11+00:00

Something has shifted among the Democratic electorate. That was already clear from Zohran Mamdani’s trouncing of establishment royalty Andrew Cuomo in the New York mayoral race last year, punctuated by a series of yet more socialist wins over establishment incumbents seven months later. And it was clear from the primary election in Maine, where Democratic voters rejected their own governor in favor of a left-populist firebrand dogged by scandals who had never held political office. But the victory of Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan over the centrist, Israel lobby–backed Haley Stevens may eclipse even these as both a victory for the broad left and for anyone who genuinely cares about democracy. No candidate has been outspent to the degree El-Sayed was and still won, however narrowly, with Stevens’s nine-to-one advantage in outside spending funding an endless torrent of TV ads touting a nonexistent endorsement from former President Barack Obama and scurrilously attacking El-Sayed. Former socialist lawmaker Jamaal Bowman fell in 2024 to a money landslide roughly a quarter of the scale of what El-Sayed faced. Besides a visceral reminder that it is possible for people power to triumph over naked plutocracy, the results should also help, depending on what happens in November, put to bed the very last talking point that party centrists held over their progressive opponents: that socialists and other lefty candidates only have appeal on the coasts or in deep-blue urban areas. This was never true, as we knew from countless indicators: Bernie Sanders’s 2016 primary win in Michigan, for instance, or the fact that two of the earliest “Squad” wins came in Detroit with Rashida Tlaib and Minneapolis with Ilhan Omar, along with the spate of local- and state-level socialist victories in the Midwest over the past decade, including the socialist blocs in the Chicago and Minneapolis city councils. But given the Senate race’s statewide nature and in a state that Trump has won twice, El-Sayed is now in a position to put that talking point to rest in the eyes of the average voter in a way that none of these other electoral milestones could have. Still, denial is a hard thing to shake. “Progressives didn’t get the big election night they wanted,” went an early Wednesday headline from Politico . This morning saw a hurried, collective moving of goalposts by Left-averse commentators to absurdly redefine victory as not actually winning the election, as El-Sayed did, but winning it by a blowout margin — a standard that no doubt would have still been applied if Stevens had been the one who eked out the narrow victory. In reality, it’s hard to make the argument that Michigan handed the Left a bad night. Five out of six of Bernie Sanders’s endorsed candidates won in the state, including Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) member Donavan McKinney, who will almost certainly become what will potentially be the ninth socialist member of the US House from the safe blue seat in Michigan’s Thirteenth Congressional District and expand the left-wing “Squad” to as many as dozens of members. Also notable is Sunrise Movement cofounder William Lawrence, who won his three-way race for the purple Seventh Congressional District — previously held by centrist Democratic senator Elissa Slotkin, whose endorsed candidate for the seat came in third last night. Also victorious among Sanders endorsees was Abbas Alawieh , a cofounder of the Uncommitted Movement and an uncommitted delegate at last year’s Democratic National Convention who led a sit-in there in protest of the Kamala Harris campaign’s position on the Gaza genocide, and who won his primary for state senate with a comfortable 18 point margin. That list also includes two other DSA members: County Commissioner Yousef Rabhi, who handily defeated the sitting, four-term mayor of Ann Arbor, and Chris Gilmer-Hill, who was endorsed by both El-Sayed and the organization’s Metro Detroit branch and won his primary to represent Michigan’s Eighth District in the state house. If this isn’t the night that progressives had wanted, then we can only assume they had wanted to lose. Two Birds With One Stone But it’s El-Sayed’s victory that is deservedly being treated as the most important story of the night. The result marks another high-profile rejection of the Democratic elite by their previously loyal voters, arguably the story of the party during the Trump 2.0 era. Establishment Democrats went all in to try and swing the race to Stevens, with Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer securing donor support for her and Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee Chair Sen. Kristen Gillibrand — who last year falsely accused Mamdani of talking about “global jihad” — reportedly lying to donors that the other centrist in the race was a DSA member. A spate of eleventh-hour endorsements from big Democratic names, including the still- popular Michigan Governor Gretchen Whitmer and the mystifyingly prominent Rep. Jim Clyburn , couldn’t push Stevens over the line. Her centrist colleague, Rep. Hillary Scholten, trashed El-Sayed as a misogynist and “ anti-American ” and even suggested she might not back him in the general. (Scholten, who this year ran unopposed, is now in a potentially vulnerable place, with El-Sayed having won 58 percent of the vote in three Western Michigan counties with parts contained in her district). But arguably, the most significant thing about the race is the sheer scale of right-wing money El-Sayed had to overcome to win and the massive defeat of the pro-Israel lobby that it represents. Only a few years ago, pro-Israel forces were virtually dictating the results of Democratic primaries through unheard-of levels of outside spending against otherwise well-liked progressives. Stevens herself was one of the beneficiaries of this strategy. In 2022, she cruised to victory over the progressive son of one of Michigan’s most famous political families, former Rep. Andy Levin, on the back of a more than $3 million investment from the United Democracy Project (UDP), the super PAC of the American Israel Political Affairs Committee (AIPAC). While UDP funded an anti-Levin ad blitz that never actually mentioned Israel, the then-president of AIPAC openly attacked Levin — a proud Zionist and former synagogue president, whose transgression was pushing to condition US aid to Israel as a way to stop it from destroying the two-state solution — as the “most corrosive member of Congress to the US-Israel relationship.” Stevens ended up winning the district, which contains much of her home county of Oakland, by nearly 20 points. Considered an ominous sign for money in politics at the time, that amount was a small fraction of the nearly $32 million that the pro-Israel UDP invested in Stevens this time around, the largest sum it has ever spent on any congressional race. Together with another $30 million from groups funded by various corporate interests, it made the race the state’s most expensive nonpresidential primary on record and the third-most expensive Senate primary ever held. And this time, the issue of Israel was front and center — not so much in the ads that UDP ran but in the fundamental political divide between the two candidates and the metacommentary surrounding it. Just like Cuomo had in New York, Stevens bet big on bear-hugging Israel at a time when the country has never had a worse standing with the US public and tried to make an issue of El-Sayed’s criticism of its treatment of Gazans and his association with other Israel critics like Hasan Piker — even at one point all but calling him an antisemite. And just like Cuomo in New York, the strategy entirely misread where the Democratic electorate is after 2024. This time, “ Oakland County’s congresswoman ” only won Oakland County — where she was born, raised, and currently lives and that she represents in Congress — by 6 points, well below her winning margin in 2022. Together with the victories of Alawieh and McKinney — who prevailed against an incumbent who became a born-again Israel Firster, dragging his feet on backing a ceasefire and disavowing DSA after October 7 — the race is the most dramatic confirmation we’ve seen so far that the politics around the US-Israel relationship have fundamentally changed, particularly on the Democratic side. Even with a transformed Democratic electorate, it’s unlikely El-Sayed and the forces behind him could have achieved this result without some strategic pragmatism. El-Sayed got the crucial backing of many DSA members on the ground even though he made explicitly clear he was not a socialist. Michigan’s democratic socialists seem to have calculated that the areas where they overlapped, including El-Sayed’s full-throated support for Medicare for All, taxing the rich, and public ownership of artificial-intelligence firms, made his campaign a cause worth supporting — a big-tent approach made easier by the fact that El-Sayed publicly embraced DSA’s support as part of his own broad coalition. El-Sayed, meanwhile, wisely changed his tune in some places even as he held fast to the core policy positions that put him in the crosshairs of big-money, right-wing spenders. He walked back his years-old support for the “Defund the Police” slogan, winning the endorsement of the Wayne County Deputy Sheriff’s Association, much as Bernie Sanders had once done to engineer his own career-making political upset. And he campaigned with a positive , patriotic style, framing his criticism of Israel around a left-wing version of an “America First” pitch, undermining the inevitable attempts to paint him as a violent or dangerous “foreign” extremist. On that note, the history-making nature of El-Sayed’s candidacy, as a proud Muslim and Arab American who leaned into those parts of his background, merits a brief mention. It would be no small thing to have the first ever Muslim US senator at a time of virulent and rising Islamophobia and anti-Arab racism. That bigotry is lately finding physical form not just in people’s daily remarks, attitudes, and random outbursts of violence but also in actual policy and political rhetoric: Trump’s Muslim ban, his various wars on the Muslim world, the increasingly openly racist rhetoric of establishment policymakers. And it’s not just on the Right. El-Sayed weathered one of the nastier dog-whistling campaigns we’ve seen in a while, a Democratic version of the subtly racist campaign John McCain ran against the not-actually-Muslim Obama eighteen years ago, a campaign that similarly saw El-Sayed’s opponent occasionally come face-to-face with the ugliness it had stirred up. This kind of thing is likely not going away anytime soon, even with this result. But El-Sayed’s victory, as well as his chance to overcome the more openly racist version of this that Republicans seem dead-set on running for the general election, will be an important milestone for basic decency and a more tolerant United States. As things move to the general election, we can say two things for sure: we will keep hearing that the Left is headed for almost certain doom; and AIPAC and other vehicles for big money will spend ever more insane amounts to try and make it a reality. But El-Sayed’s primary win is a reminder that, sometimes, with enough hard work, it’s possible to break through a tsunami of cash and come out breathing on the other side.

Ruling-Class Disarray Let the GOP Go Off the Rails

Every day, millions of Americans get out of bed, tune into the news, and ask themselves a simple question: How the hell did the Republican Party become so spectacularly unhinged? The GOP is fully off the rails, unbothered by any seeming concern for basic political economic stability, the empirical reality of things we can see with our own eyes, and the fundamentals of human decency. Donald Trump has remade the Republican Party in his image over the past decade, creating a kind of personalist strongman rule that’s without exact precedent in American history. But Paul Heideman argues that the sorry, scary state of today’s Republican Party cannot be solely attributed to one strange, horrible man. Rather, it’s the product of a range of features of the US political system, some long-standing, others more recent. Heideman’s book Rogue Elephant: How Republicans Went from the Party of Business to the Party of Chaos argues that the United States’ weak party system played a big role in creating the conditions for today’s derangement and the specific forms that derangement has taken. For Jacobin Radio ‘s podcast The Dig , guest host and Jacobin editor Micah Uetricht sat down with Paul Heideman to discuss why business could destroy Joseph McCarthy but not Trump, the service-party model that turned both parties into fundraising networks, Newt Gingrich’s remaking of the House around money, big tobacco’s fingerprints on the Bill Clinton impeachment, the Tea Party revolt against the bailout, and why the coming fight to succeed Trump is unlikely to leave the party any less chaotic. You can listen to the episode here . Micah Uetricht You start this book with a comparison between Senator Joseph McCarthy, after whom McCarthyism is obviously named, and Donald Trump. And your basic point in this comparison is that both of these figures within the Republican Party practice this kind of unhinged reactionary politics that are, in most ways, quite far to the right, but are too unhinged, too chaotic for big business, which we typically think of as the core constituency for the Republican Party. Paul Heideman Trump and McCarthy have a lot in common in that they both, as you say, practiced this demagogic mode of far-right politics. And in doing so, they actually started to threaten some of the core interests of American capital. In McCarthy’s case, this really came through two means. First, among the more sophisticated anti-communists and state planners in the United States, there was always a wariness about McCarthy’s methods and a sense that he might go too far and really discredit anti-communism. It wasn’t that they thought anti-communism was bad. Quite the contrary — it’s that they thought McCarthy might be bad for anti-communism, because he would overreach so far. And this became much, much more intense when McCarthy started going after the army, because, after all, this is the early 1950s. This is the period when the great postwar American empire is really getting built in the aftermath of World War II, at the start of the Korean War. And so to be attacking the army at this time and to be plunging it into bureaucratic chaos as a result of these attacks was really a move that went much too far for much of the American corporate elite, and they really organized against him. That Joseph Welch line [“Have you no decency, sir, at long last?”], of course, made for great drama, but much of the real action was happening behind the scenes in groups like the Committee for Economic Development and the Business Council, which were essentially these organizations of American CEOs that went into action against McCarthy and started organizing other business leaders to move against him, pressuring the White House to really disavow McCarthy. Dwight D. Eisenhower and McCarthy had never been close, but these groups pushed for a much more active stand from the White House against McCarthy’s actions and for organizing the censure motion against him in the Senate that would ultimately seal his fate and leave him politically friendless and without influence after it passed. Nobody Asked for AI

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Grace Blakeley ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-05T14:20:44+00:00

The Big Tech companies have spent $1 trillion on capital investment since the start of the artificial-intelligence boom. Pretty much all that investment has been used to build data centers, purchase the chips that power those data centers, and construct the energy systems that fuel and cool them. That $1 trillion could have been used for anything. It could have been invested in renewables. It could have been used to develop lifesaving new drugs. It could have been used to build housing, public infrastructure, or anything else human beings need to survive and thrive. Instead, it has been channeled into constructing energy-draining, water-absorbing data centers that power large language models (LLMs) built on the theft of millions of people’s creative work, which consistently produce incorrect responses and drive many users mad in the process. It’s hard to think of a worse use of society’s shared resources. Now, you may argue that we live in free-market societies, so investment takes place according to the laws of the market. All that money and investment is flowing into AI because it is simply the most profitable use of resources. And when society maximizes profits, it maximizes efficiency — ensuring that, on aggregate, everyone is better off over the long run. There are a lot of problems with this story. First, we do not live in free-market societies. We live in capitalist societies — that is, societies dominated by capital. Capital is not distributed into lots of little businesses each producing widgets in perfect competition with one another. Capital agglomerates until it is concentrated within a few massive, monopolistic corporations, controlled by a few powerful men. The New Enclosures These massive corporations can afford to ignore what’s profitable in the short run pursuit of what will bring their owners immense wealth and power in the long run. Just take Amazon. It was unprofitable for years while Jeff Bezos channeled more and more money into expansion. Investors continued to funnel billions into Amazon’s coffers because they were betting that Bezos could carve out a powerful monopoly. And they were right. Corporate concentration and monopoly power trump competition and efficiency every time. Despite all the hype about AI, LLMs are not currently profitable. OpenAI and Anthropic — the two companies whose fate is tied entirely to the performance of their LLMs — have yet to make a profit. And yet both companies are trying to list on the stock market chasing valuations of $1 trillion. Why? Because these two companies have successfully enclosed — that is, taken and put walls around — vast areas of human knowledge. Now they are selling it back to us in the form of strange, sycophantic chatbots that put all this knowledge back together in unpredictable — and sometimes completely useless — ways. Still, investors are betting that this innovation will revolutionize capitalist production, and that one or both of these companies will continue to hold immense power over the market. All the other companies in the ecosystem — the ones building the infrastructure, the ones producing the chips, the ones powering the data centers — are dependent upon the performance of the LLMs at the center of the boom. Which is why companies like Nvidia and Oracle are locked into a circular network of financing with OpenAI — they’re funding OpenAI while it loses money, because their profits are contingent upon the company’s success. Crash and Concentration This is where the next correction to our original story comes in. Right now, we are absolutely in a bubble when it comes to AI. That doesn’t mean the technology won’t permanently change the way our economy works — but it does mean that the tech won’t be as profitable as most investors are expecting, especially over the short term. In other words, not only are our markets not free — they’re not efficient either. Investors are betting that this tech cycle will be like the last one — the one where companies like Google, Meta, and Amazon enclosed the entire internet. Lots of those companies took a long time to make any profit, because they were investing to eke out a market-dominating position — not to maximize short-term profits. The companies involved in the AI boom are trying to do the same thing, but it’s not working. First, there’s too much competition among US LLM providers. It’s not just ChatGPT and Claude now — Gemini and Copilot are nipping at their heels. Then there’s competition from China. Chinese companies have already been able to create their own LLMs — they might not be as good as the originals, but it doesn’t matter, because they’re incredibly cheap. And then, there’s the productivity puzzle. For the LLMs to be profitable over the long run, they need to be rolled out by lots of businesses that won’t cancel their subscriptions as soon as a cheaper option is available — which means the productivity gains of introducing the technology have to be very clear. But not many businesses have seen significant productivity gains from the introduction of the technology. In this context, it’s not clear how all these LLMs are going to generate the profits required to produce a decent return on that $1 trillion investment. When investors realize that some of the bets they have made aren’t going to yield much of a return, the cash will dry up. The circular webs of financing will collapse. Companies will go under. And the world will be left with hundreds of massive data centers without enough customers. Compute will become extremely cheap — just like accessing the internet did after the telecom companies spent billions competing to lay fiber optic cables in the 1990s. The boom will get going again after the bust — with valuations returning to more sensible levels. A few of the massive tech companies will buy up the remnants of the others, leaving the market even more concentrated. The technology will become pedestrian as it is incorporated into every area of our lives. People will get real about what AI can do and what it can’t do. Of course, millions of people will lose their jobs and their savings during the bust. Wealth and power will become even more concentrated, as the largest tech companies buy up their defunct competitors, before leveraging their control over the technological infrastructure that most other businesses require to function. They will use this power to further capture our democracies, lobbying politicians to wage war on their foreign competition. When Capital Decides Our environment will also be permanently changed. Erald Kolasi and Jesse Damiani have just written an excellent piece calculating the environmental impact of the AI boom. They estimate that, in 2026, AI will be responsible for 1.1 percent of total global energy demand. AI is already using more energy than the whole of the United Kingdom. Meanwhile, many communities are being forced to go without water , while others experience striking “heat island” effects from the astonishing amounts of heat being pumped out by these megaliths. After years of hype and trillions of dollars, the AI boom will leave us with a captured economy, a corrupted democracy, and a devastated environment. We’ll be told that this is just how markets work — sometimes they go up, sometimes they go down. But this narrative obscures how decisions are really made in capitalist economies. As I showed in my book, Vulture Capitalism , capitalist economies are characterized by pervasive central planning. Monopolistic corporations work with corrupt politicians to decide who gets what, while using “the market” as a veil to conceal their power. The AI boom is no different. A few powerful capitalists decided that we would spend several years devoting all our time and money toward the development and roll out of AI — and so we did. “The market” didn’t decide that AI would be our future. Capital did. And when the devastating consequences of their decisions become clear, we cannot allow them to hide behind disembodied market forces. Capital took us down this route, and capital must be made to pay. The politicians aren’t going to go after their wealthy backers; that part is on us. So block new data centers, strip them for parts, and start organizing your communities and your workplaces for the fightback.

There has been a weird debate over the last few months about whether the United States is better off than Western Europe. The participants of this debate tend to operate under the shared premise that people in the United States are richer than people in Western Europe, and then quibble over whether that means it is better. But this shared premise is incorrect. Once you adjust typical economic aggregates for hours worked and inequality, Western Europe clearly pulls ahead of the United States. Gross Domestic Product Most of these debates start by comparing the GDP per capita of various countries. Below, I have graphed these figures using OECD data. The obvious problem with this measure is that it does not account for how many hours of work each country has to put in to produce its GDP. This implicitly treats all time not spent working as having zero value. Childhood, student life, retirement, vacations, holidays, family time, and socializing are assumed to be worthless. Under such a metric, a country whose workers put in eighty hours a week to produce a certain amount of output is considered to be just as well-off as a country whose workers can produce the same output in half the time. This is nonsense. To account for differences in leisure time across countries, you want to look at GDP per hour worked, which is a figure the OECD also provides. With this single adjustment, the United States no longer looks all that different from peer nations. Denmark and Germany have higher per-hour GDPs than the United States. Sweden is only 3 percent lower. France is only 7 percent lower. The only way the United States could fare much worse on GDP per hour worked than it does on GDP per capita is if these non-US countries work less than the United States. There are two ways a country can work less than another: by having a lower employment rate or by giving its workers more time off. In the below graph, we see that, with the exception of France, these other countries do not have lower employment rates than the United States does. This means that their relatively better showing on the GDP per hour metric is driven entirely by giving workers more time off, generally in the form of holidays and vacations. Germany is the most extreme case in the group, as it has a higher GDP per hour than America, the highest overall employment rate, and the lowest number of hours worked per worker. Germany is not poorer than the United States. It has just chosen to cash out more of its productivity in the form of free time than the United States has. The other Western European countries have made similar decisions. Even with France, which is the only country that has a lower employment rate than the United States, a little digging reveals that this is mostly driven by decisions it has made about retirement and student life. Among the prime-age (twenty-five to fifty-four) population, France actually employs a higher share of its population than the United States does. It runs behind the United States on overall employment only because the French retire earlier, more French young people are in college, and French college students are less likely to work than their American counterparts. All societies, including the United States, make decisions about retirement timelines and the duration and nature of student life. France has merely decided to be more generous about those things than the United States has, just as it has decided to be more generous about vacations and holidays. One can certainly argue that student life, retirement, and leisure are worthless or that France has overvalued them. But GDP per capita does not support that argument. It simply assumes it, by construction. Inequality and Diminishing Marginal Utility In addition to assuming that time away from work is worth zero dollars, the GDP per capita metric also assumes that every dollar of output has the same value regardless of how it is distributed. In reality, money has diminishing marginal utility , meaning that an extra dollar distributed to a millionaire is worth far less than an extra dollar distributed to a low-income or middle-income person. Thus, all else equal, a country with a more egalitarian distribution of income is richer — has more utility — than a country with a less egalitarian distribution. On every conventional measure of disposable-income inequality, the United States runs far behind peer nations. What small edge the United States may still have after accounting for differences in hours worked is completely overwhelmed after factoring in the diminishing marginal utility of money. Note that I am not doing anything particularly exotic in this piece. The fact that GDP is unable to capture the value of leisure time (or the value of nonmarket production), and the fact that this presents a problem when using it as a measure of affluence or general well-being, is totally uncontroversial in the economics world. The fact that money has diminishing marginal utility is also one of the foundational premises of marginalist mainstream economics. Other quality-of-life metrics like relative levels of safety and health can be used to argue in favor of Western European standards of living, but they are ultimately unnecessary. Conventional economic measurements alone get you there.

The Marxist Case for the Technofeudal Hypothesis

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Yanis Varoufakis ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-04T18:46:24+00:00

When my book Technofeudalism was published in 2023, I had not imagined that the likes of Steve Bannon would embrace it. I had, however, fully anticipated a barrage of criticism from Marxist thinkers, especially in response to the book’s provocative subtitle ( What Killed Capitalism ). It is now incumbent on me to present the Marxist case for the technofeudal hypothesis, which, though written for a broader audience, was Marxist in its conception. Karl Marx’s own method sought to bypass moralistic censure of capitalism’s penchant for injustice, monopoly, and sleaze. Instead he studied capitalism in its purest form: as an anarchic market system driven by an alien force — capital — seeking its own proliferation, a task necessitating the commodification of everything and a vicious class struggle over surplus value. Any analysis that claims to continue Marx’s work must do likewise. Today, I contend, a mutation of capital has gained inordinate traction. I call it cloud capital: a network of remarkable machines that produce no commodities but generate enormous rent-extracting powers by interfacing directly with us, outside anything that can usefully be called a market. Studying this novel form of capital should be every Marxist’s responsibility. Before focusing on cloud capital, it is important to clear away three common misunderstandings. First, no, we are not returning to feudalism. History is not going backward. Driven by unbridled capital accumulation, we are hurtling forward into a brand-new socioeconomic mode. According to the technofeudal hypothesis, capital is more dominant than ever. Wealth grows exponentially from gigantic investments in dynamic machines, not static assets. Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta, and Apple are as ruthlessly capitalist as Edison and Ford ever were. Thus, the term “technofeudal” denotes no literal regression, but rather draws an analogy with extractive power practiced outside markets. Second, rents are not new to capitalism. Capitalism never existed in the pure form of mainstream economics. Like our bodies need residual serpent DNA, capitalism needed remnants of feudalism to function. David Ricardo warned that ground rent would grow dangerously, as it did. Paul Baran and Paul Sweezy showed how monopoly surplus was a form of rent. Since the 1970s, financialization grafted mountainous financial rents onto existing forms. So the mere proliferation of rents offers no evidence that capitalism is evolving into something else. For such a claim to be plausible, there must be evidence of a qualitative change in capital itself. Third, data is not the new land. The belief that our stolen data has become the new feudal estate is a form of “data fetishism” that Marxists must reject. “Surveillance capitalism” constitutes no qualitative change of the capitalist system, since legislation granting us property rights over our data would eliminate any rents extracted via its surreptitious harvesting. The real novelty lies elsewhere: in how cloud capital uses data to modify our behavior directly and “transport” us outside anything resembling a capitalist market. In short, the technofeudal hypothesis revolves around the notion of cloud capital. To grasp its nature, we need to start with Marx’s definition of commodities as goods or services produced for exchange in a market, of capital goods as produced means of commodity production (PMCP), and of markets as decentralized spheres of circulation where labor values turn into money and surplus values into profits, rents, and interest. Changing Our Behavior Are Google’s machines conventional PMCP-type capital goods or cloud capital? The answer is: it depends! When Google charges you for a film on YouTube, it sells a commodity — in which case its machines function as standard PMCP. However, when Google’s machines offer you a free search result or directions on a map, they function as cloud capital: cloud-based machines that produce no commodities but enter into a relationship with you to modify your behavior. This is something no machines could achieve until recently. But how does cloud capital modify our behavior? By engaging us in one-on-one, two-way, real-time dialogue. Consider Amazon’s Alexa. It trains you to train it to know your preferences so as to do things for you, offer you good advice, and thus gain your trust. Then, one day, when your coffee maker dies, it suggests you buy a particular model. You click “buy.” What just happened? Cloud capital has sold you a commodity that it played no role in producing, and its owner, Jeff Bezos, pocketed up to 40 percent of the price as cloud rent, a form of absolute rent. Evidently, these machines are not conventional capital goods (PMCP). They have crossed the Rubicon into what I call “produced means of behavioral modification (PMBM)” — or cloud capital . Fascinatingly, cloud capital is, in Marx’s technical sense, unproductive (as it produces no commodities) but hugely powerful, its novel social power extending over consumers, over waged laborers, and over conventional capitalist producers. In the process, cloud capital replaces markets with something I call cloud fiefs, like Amazon.com. Why is Amazon.com nothing like a market? Recall Gosplan, the Soviet planning ministry. It too offered a top-down algorithmic system matching buyers and sellers. That was not a market — it was its opposite. Amazon is similar: a centrally planned allocation system. No one interacts with anyone unless the algorithm matches them. Given that, unlike Gosplan, Amazon is privately owned, it is best defined as a cloud fief, not a capitalist marketplace. Consider Instacart. It practices “perfect price optimization” — every customer gets a unique, noncomparable price. Price competition thus becomes impossible. Retailers and platforms can collude in ways the naked eye never discerns. The system is designed to be an invisible solvent of a market mechanism’s basic traits. Consider Uber. Yes, it looks like a monopoly reseller of drivers’ labor. But Uber also tracks drivers’ debt obligations. It offers higher rates and loans to update their cars to reluctant drivers, then lowers pay rates once they are hooked. ShiftMed does the same with nurses — offering lower rates just before their rent is due. This is algorithmic extra-market despotism, not a capitalist labor market in which, as Marx hypothesized, wages reflect the labor values of the wage goods they buy. And here is another insight that Marxists must grasp: for the first time, free labor helps a variant of capital enhance its powers to modify everyone’s behavior in the interests of its owners. Unlike conventional capital, which only waged labor manufactures, massive free labor helps cloud capital accumulate. With every video we post, every rating we give, every mile we drive, every purchase we make — we augment cloud capital and raise our own costs of quitting. We can leave a platform, yes. But the cost of leaving increases with every click. Formal freedom masks real unfreedom. That is the classic Marxian diagnosis, applied to the age of cloud capital. “But are the platforms not fiercely competing with one another?” many ask. “Are their clashes not evidence of ruthless market competition?” No, it is not. Market competition is based on price and quality and unfolds in decentralized markets. Economic rivalry, in contrast, of the sort we see today in the clashes between TikTok and Instagram, Uber and Lyft, etc., is zero-sum, territorial, pitching one fully centralized cloud fief against another. Unlike the market contest between, say, Toyota and General Motors, platforms race to lock us in their centralized trading schemes, not to offer us lower prices or higher quality. What about successful YouTubers and influencers? They work hard, and some of them earn well. Are they not entrepreneurs? From a Marxist perspective, they are not. Their labor produces no new exchange value and no productive capital (cloud capital itself is unproductive). It generates only use-value and helps lure unpaid users, but it does not create surplus value. Influencers are, in that sense, not capitalists but, instead, a well-remunerated, privileged layer of cloud serfs, their high pay obscuring the general condition: most of us laboring unpaid to augment cloud capital’s power over us. Now let me turn to macroeconomics. Marx showed that capitalism’s accumulation drive produces business cycles and a long-term falling rate of profit. In the longer paper, I extended his analysis to include cloud capital. Following Richard Goodwin’s adaptation of a prey–predator model, the analysis demonstrates the dynamic relationship between the economy’s productive department, where all value is created utilizing conventional capital and waged labor, and the technofeudal department, where cloud rents are extracted. Four phases emerge. At first, the technofeudal department grows at the expense of the productive department. Then, once cloud rent extraction exceeds a threshold, productive capital accumulates again and both departments grow. A third phase kicks in when, as the profit rate exceeds another threshold, cloud capital overaccumulates and its capacity to extract rent wanes. Lastly, in the fourth phase, both the profit rate and the cloud rent extraction rate decline. In the long run, as cloud capital accumulates and unpaid labor replaces more and more waged labor in the technofeudal department, the thresholds themselves decline and the system tends toward a collapse of both profit rates and cloud rent extraction rates. Thus the technofeudal hypothesis generalizes Marx’s falling rate of profit to a world with cloud capital, reaffirming the Marxist case for socialism as the only alternative to system failure and societal collapse. Crisis Response Since my Technofeudalism was published, artificial intelligence has become all the rage. Cloud capital did not need AI to emerge — reinforcement learning algorithms built the first cloud fiefs. But AI massively escalates cloud capital’s power. AI-powered bots will soon know us perfectly, speak to us like humans, and cultivate emotional dependence. Switching platforms will become akin to a heart-wrenching divorce. This creates what I call “artificial social relations of consumption (ASRC),” which dialectically interact with the machines themselves. How did capital respond, in the past, to its intensifying contradictions? Marx’s successors identified three responses: finance capital, imperialism, and legitimizing ideologies. We see the same today: Cloud finance: the merger of cloud capital with traditional finance has ignited a New Cold War between the United States and China. Through stablecoins and Trump’s “Genius Act” cloud finance is privatizing the dollar, giving Big Tech powers once reserved for central banks. Imperialism and war: AI targeting systems that brought mega-death to Gaza are the same cloud capital that Palantir and Oracle sell to Britain’s National Health Service. Technofeudalism is modern war’s handmaiden. Techlordism: a new ideology replacing neoliberalism. As neoliberalism deified the market, techlordism deifies cloud capital, offering a veneer of philosophical legitimacy to the replacement of human labor with rent-extracting machines, integrating Big Tech into state institutions and handing central banks over to cloud finance. And here is the central dialectical contradiction: the more successfully the cloudalist class concentrates all powers — industrial, financial, political, cultural, and state power — the faster it drives down both profit rates and cloud rent extraction rates. Private success breeds system failure. Not Absolving Capitalism Some critics fear that by arguing that something worse than capitalism has arrived, I absolved capitalism or served bourgeois propagandists who want to return to a mythical pure capitalism. This fear is misplaced. My hypothesis is grounded firmly in the Marxist tradition. Capitalism was always monstrously irrational. Technofeudalism is a mutation internal to capitalism: it arose because the socialist transformation was averted brutally. Recognizing this does not excuse capitalism; it sharpens the need for socialism. Perhaps the greatest contribution of the technofeudal hypothesis is its illustration of how private capital has become inconsistent not only with social well-being but even with private ownership — except for a tiny band of techlords. AI-enabled cloud capital is collectivizing our labor, our intellect, our identity, even our audiovisual likeness. The bourgeois state is incapable of regulating Big Tech. The case for socializing both productive and cloud capital — all of society’s capital stock — emerges stronger than ever. Regarding our theory of change, some Marxists are critical of my call for a united front of traditional proletarians, cloud proles (waged gig workers), cloud serfs (unpaid users), and even vassal capitalists (small businesses being crushed by cloud fiefs). This is contentious, naturally. And yet Marxists have always debated alliances — with peasants, or with the petty bourgeoisie. This debate must continue within our tradition, not outside it. What’s in a Word? Let me end with the semantic question. Is this still capitalism, or should we call it technofeudalism? I understand the hesitation. A Marxist economist once told me, with a bitter smile, that he refused to let go of the thought that people like us came to this world to overthrow capitalism. “If capital did that without us,” he asked, “what’s our purpose?” I sympathize. I too grew up hoping the Left would have the honor of felling capitalism. But then there is Rosa Luxemburg’s devastating question: “Socialism or barbarism?” Her point was that socialism was not inevitable — that barbarism was a very real possibility. In her day, barbarism took the form of fascism. Today’s fascism rides on the coattails of technofeudalism and techlordist ideology. So what’s in a word? In the 1770s, Adam Smith celebrated “commercial society” even though Britain’s feudal department was still larger than its capitalist department. In the 1840s, Marx and Friedrich Engels could have called the new era “industrial feudalism.” But calling it capitalism helped people living in the nineteenth century grasp the transformation underway. The safe option today is to assume the cloudalist transformation is internal to capitalism. The controversial option, which I argue for, is to adopt a new term like technofeudalism to jolt our collective imagination. Both are defensible within the Marxist tradition. Neither is a reason for nastiness between Marxists. Dialectics teaches us that reality is built on contradiction. At the time of the original Great Transformation, society was both feudal and capitalist. Today, it is both capitalist and technofeudal. The whole point of Marxist dialectics is to recognize this puzzling coexistence, and to see in it the possibility of overcoming the contradiction through socialist transformation. First, we must see the contradiction. Once we do, the emancipatory potential knows no bounds.

Mexican Sovereignty in the Era of the Donroe Doctrine

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Alexander Aviña ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-04T16:49:06+00:00

When it comes to US-Mexico relations, 2026 thus far reads like a Taylor Sheridan script. Last April, the CIA’s clandestine operations inside Mexico became public when two agents died in a car crash in the northern state of Chihuahua after raiding and dismantling a mountain drug lab. Reporters soon revealed that four CIA agents had participated in the operation, albeit wearing Chihuahua state police uniforms, and that this was their third counter-narcotics raid since January. In May, CNN and the New York Times broke a stunning and seemingly related story. Reporters claimed that the CIA had been running a “secret war on cartels” since last year, assassinating lower- and mid-ranking members to dismantle entire criminal networks. This secret war went beyond the kingpin strategy that prioritized the arrest or killing of “cartel” leaders to “a playbook not much different than counterterrorism missions to destroy groups in the Middle East and elsewhere in the world.” Except that this was taking place clandestinely and illegally inside Mexico. The Mexican government responded to both reports by loudly proclaiming its national sovereignty. President Claudia Sheinbaum initially denied that Mexico’s federal government knew about the Chihuahua CIA operations, placing the blame on a state governor (a member of a right-wing opposition party) gone rogue. Yet contradictions have emerged in this account. High-level conversations about these operations between the US and Mexican governments allegedly took place. And the April raid involved Mexican military personnel in a support capacity. Sheinbaum, along with the CIA, also categorically rejected the existence of an elite spook death squad assassinating narcos inside the country. Indeed, she denied the direct participation of any US personnel in counter-narcotics operations in Mexican territory. The United States’ role, she argued, is limited to intelligence sharing. For Mexico, the question of sovereignty — and its public discursive performance — has long involved a sort of rhetorical vagueness: strategic at best, Janus-faced at its worst. While this is not uniquely Mexican, its deeply interconnected history with the United States and the consequences of sharing a long border with a settler-colonial expansionist polity have produced the dynamic evident also in President Sheinbaum’s responses. This, in practical terms, weak sovereign power relative to the United States necessitates strong public claims to the contrary, alongside delicate negotiations in private. That long shared border — in addition to related questions of economic integration, geopolitics, migration, and internal Mexican stability — has required US political elites to generally tolerate public, nationalist assertions of Mexican sovereignty. That these two countries engaged in nearly $900 billion in trade in 2025 has also motivated such toleration more recently. From both sides, then, Mexican sovereignty has required careful calibration. Yet this profoundly unequal arrangement, deepened since the 1980s with Mexico’s “structural adjustment” and ongoing integration into the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (until 2020, the North American Free Trade Agreement) is in flux today. The so-called Donroe Doctrine, articulated in the Trump administration’s 2025 National Security Strategy, aims to undo, or at the very least create parallel institutions of power to, the so-called rules-based international order and its buttressing institutions created after World War II. The contingent, partly fictional aspect of this order — in its death throes during the global war on terror and buried in Gaza — is giving way to unilateral US power openly based on force, coercion, and domination in the Americas and West Asia, as evidenced by the kidnapping of Venezuelan president Nicolás Maduro. Mexico has been here before. Poinsettias and the “First Gringo in Mexico” In an 1829 letter to President Martin Van Buren, the diplomat and former US Secretary of War Joel Poinsett wrote: “It would lead you into error to compare [Mexicans] with the free and civilized nations of America and Europe in the Nineteenth century. They started from a period nearer to the age of Charles the fifth, and it is even a matter of some doubt whether this Nation had advanced one step in knowledge and civilization, from time of the conquest to the moment of declaring themselves Independent.” The South Carolinian Poinsett had spent some years in Latin America, notably in Chile, prior to his arrival in 1825 as the first official US ambassador to Mexico. He headed there following his vote to execute the last “ringleader” of the 1822 Denmark Vesey–led slave rebellion in Charleston as part of a court commission. He was sent to Mexico by then-President James Monroe on a special mission to assess where the country merited US recognition of its independence. His ship briefly stopped in Puerto Rico, long enough for him to ponder the high probability of a slave insurrection due “to the vicinity of the Republic of Hayti.” Finally arriving in Veracruz in late 1822, during the tumultuous imperial rule of Agustín de Iturbide, the South Carolinian traveled through eastern and central Mexico. He fretted about Britain’s preponderant economic power in the country, scouted promising mining ventures for his personal portfolio, and (correctly) doubted that Iturbide’s unstable monarchy would last. Upon his return to the United States, Poinsett published his writings, reaching an international audience. As historian Lindsay Schakenbach Regele notes, these published “opinions formed the basis for Alexis de Tocqueville’s statements in Democracy in America about Mexico’s inability to sustain democracy.” Poinsett was not the first choice for this new diplomatic posting. Future President Andrew Jackson had previously declined Monroe’s offer. When the South Carolinian returned to Mexico, he immediately began meddling in its internal affairs. By the time Jackson allowed him to resign in 1830, the ambassador had interfered in negotiations between Mexico and the last remaining Spanish military forces located near Veracruz; secretly organized political opposition via a Masonic Lodge to the ruling Centralist party “which he saw as antithetical to U.S. interests because of its close ties to British business”; and, in 1828, even supported a coup to further US political and economic interests in the country. He also used those “Masonic connections” to obtain “favorable plots of land for himself and his friends and establish an American-based mining company.” Poinsett later went on to serve as Jackson’s secretary of war and carry out the genocidal Indian Removal Act. Now remembered more for the red cuetlaxochitl flower that he sent back to the United States than his diplomatic misadventures, Poinsett created the script for future ambassadors. From the beginning of the United States’ diplomatic relations with Mexico, its sovereignty was titular and negotiable. The United States’ neighbors were not equal nation-states. The question, past and present, is not whether the United States should infringe upon Mexican sovereignty, but how much , and how it should calibrate and determine the scale and form of said infringements. The first ambassador’s actions highlight a broader, structural feature of US-Mexico relations. From the US perspective, Mexico merely serves as a strategically important territory, a launching pad for the regional and global projection of imperial power, and a key to ensuring its own domestic stability (particularly in the past decades through the control of migration and illicit drug trafficking). US national security is premised on viewing Mexico as a territory to control, exploit, and subordinate. In that regard, Poinsett set the rule for a type of meddling that combines state and private interests in aid of imperial policy. That rule led to the theft of more than half of Mexico’s national territory in the mid-nineteenth century and the coup plot that murdered the country’s first democratically elected president of the twentieth century. The US architect of the coup that overthrew President Francisco Madero in 1913, Henry Lane Wilson, was a twentieth-century version of Poinsett. When Madero’s wife implored Wilson to save her husband, the ambassador retorted , “Your husband’s downfall was due to the fact that he never wanted to consult with me.” Josephus Daniels represents the rare exception. A southerner like Poinsett, Franklin Delano Roosevelt appointed him ambassador in the days of the New Deal and Good Neighbor Policy. Daniels (like others in FDR’s “brain trust”) viewed Mexico as a revolutionary laboratory from which to learn and draw lessons. In his recent book, America, América , historian Greg Grandin narrates how Daniels — despite his white-supremacist past and ideas — pushed FDR to consider borrowing from the 1917 Mexican Constitution’s revolutionary advances on social rights. “Mexico,” he told the president in 1934, “is looking to do more for the forgotten man than you have been able to do.” It was a brief moment when some progressive US politicians managed to recognize the limits of political liberalism and of US power itself by reading about and analyzing in good faith the social and economic radicalism unfolding south of the border. Mexico — and Latin America — had something to teach the so-called colossus of the north about social justice, noninterventionism, liberal multilateralism, and respect for national sovereignty, and as Grandin argues, an actual “rules-based international order.” There were surely contradictions, particularly for some Caribbean and Central American nations that chafed under the dictatorial rule of FDR’s (and the United Fruit Company’s) preferred “sons of bitches”: the likes of Fulgencio Batista (Cuba), Anastasio Somoza (Nicaragua), Rafael Trujillo (Dominican Republic), Maximiliano Hernández Martínez (El Salvador), and Jorge Ubico (Guatemala). Yet this looks like a brighter moment when contextualized in a longer history of US-Latin American ties. Tragically for the region, the United States has been a terrible, unwilling student, unable to take responsibility for a violent imperial history, or even acknowledge it. This same history continues to this day, as we have witnessed in Venezuela and Cuba. It is as if the region is there only for the United States to control and dominate it, making the imperialist aspirations of the Monroe Doctrine into reality — including in its southern neighbor.
