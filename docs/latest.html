Why Are Most Union Leaders Silent on Booting Chuck Schumer?

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Eric Blanc ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-18T21:34:42+00:00

Why have only four national unions publicly backed Chris Van Hollen against Chuck Schumer for Senate leader? The International Association of Machinists and Aerospace Workers, the Office and Professional Employees International Union, National Nurses United, and the National Union of Healthcare Workers have taken Van Hollen’s side in this urgent fight. But where is everybody else? It’s hard to overstate the stakes. If Democrats once again squander the opportunity given to them by the American people, the lost chance to improve people’s lives — and the ensuing political backlash — could be even worse than what followed the Clinton, Obama, and Biden administrations. Schumer-style corporate politics might as well have been designed in a lab to ensure that Democrats disappoint voters and quickly hand Republicans back the reins of power, perhaps this time under an even more dangerous and effective incarnation of far-right reaction. Missing the Moment There is exactly zero reason to believe Schumer is the man for this moment. He’s a pro-Israel war hawk who is deeply embedded with the same billionaires that Democrats — including every single victorious 2026 Democratic Senate candidate in a battleground state — ran against in these midterms. Want to see the pro-union Protecting the Right to Organize ( PRO) Act and other urgent reforms passed within the first hundred days of the next Democratic administration? So do I. But even if Democrats win the presidency and retain Congress, getting this crucial labor law reform enacted will almost certainly require abolishing the filibuster. Van Hollen has explicitly endorsed doing so. As he put it last Friday , the Senate needs to let majorities govern with “fifty-one votes,” rather than keeping the “antidemocratic” filibuster. Schumer, by contrast, has refused to say whether he’d support getting rid of it. Asked directly after the midterms whether Democrats should abolish the rule, he dodged the question. There’s also plenty a Senate majority leader can do over the next two years, even with Trump still holding the presidential veto. Democrats should aggressively investigate the administration’s corruption and use their control over spending to immediately challenge its worst policies on Iran, Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), and Israel. Even more important, they’ll need to make crystal clear to Americans the ambitious agenda they’ll enact if they win the White House in 2028. Van Hollen is promising to make the Senate a center of political opposition to billionaire power, putting popular reforms on the table and forcing Republicans to defend their opposition. Schumer is itching to return to the familiar routines of Schumerism: decent policy promises, timid negotiations, and endless excuses for inaction. Plus, he’s insanely unpopular. A November YouGov poll found that just 23 percent of voters viewed Schumer favorably, compared with 39 percent for Trump. That’s right: the Democratic leader most national unions are so far refusing to buck is substantially less popular than Donald Trump. An astonishing 57 percent of voters view Schumer unfavorably. Only 10 percent want him to remain Senate majority leader, compared with 57 percent who want somebody else. Why on earth should the party’s future be placed in the hands of someone so widely disliked? Schumer’s supporters understandably point to the Democratic victories this month. He did help recruit some good candidates, and that deserves some credit. But winning a midterm election amid an unpopular president’s economic and political disasters is a pretty different task from governing successfully afterward. The Case for Van Hollen Like Franklin Delano Roosevelt in the 1930s, Van Hollen comes out of the Democratic establishment but is reading the mood of the public and offering a real alternative to politics as usual. He has called for confronting billionaire power, backed Medicare for All, supported ending the filibuster, and taken a much tougher line on using congressional spending authority to end Trump’s war with Iran. He has also pledged to give the new class of insurgent populist senators a greater role in shaping the party’s direction. Obviously, changing the leader won’t automatically overcome every obstacle to reform. Obviously, switching Senate leaders doesn’t mean the rest of us can sit back and watch the Democrats finally deliver. But there’s a big difference between a leader promising to break with business as usual and one whose career has largely been built around it. Van Hollen has also shown that he’s willing to take political risks for workers. Don’t forget the case of Kilmar Ábrego García, the sheet-metal apprentice and International Association of Sheet Metal, Air, Rail and Transportation Workers (SMART) member who Trump illegally deported to a notorious prison in El Salvador. Van Hollen personally flew there, met with Ábrego García, and helped lead the fight that brought him back to the United States. You would think SMART leadership would back a leader willing to go to such a degree for one of its members. Some national unions have so far made clear that they understand the stakes. The Machinists’ president, Brian Bryant, said Van Hollen understood that the fight was one of “Workers vs. Billionaires.” OPEIU likewise sent Schumer a letter urging him to withdraw and make way for another leader, such as Van Hollen. National Nurses United put the point well in endorsing Van Hollen. Its executive director, Puneet Maharaj, warned that Democrats must not squander the opening voters have given them. Politicking on the K Street Corridor The reason why more national union leaders aren’t yet saying the same isn’t complicated. Many of them feel that a significant part of their job is to build close relationships with congresspeople to deliver for their members and fight for pro-worker legislation. If they back Van Hollen and Schumer wins, there’s a real risk that their union might suddenly find that their calls get returned a little less quickly (or not at all), and that important Democratic leaders might no longer listen to their legislative priorities. That’s a valid concern, especially given how Washington currently works. But it’s also an extraordinarily narrow way to calculate political risk, especially at this moment of crisis. For years now, progressive union leaders have rightly insisted that democracy is on the line, that sky-high economic inequality must be reversed, that the Democrats should stand up for the little guy, and that the labor movement must do everything possible to defeat MAGA and greedy CEOs. Now union leaders and Democrats have a rare opportunity to actually move the needle in that direction. And it wouldn’t even take much effort. This Senate fight is one of those exceedingly rare moments when a union press release could make a historic difference for working people and the country as a whole. There’s no excuse for silence. Schumer is seventy-five and faces reelection in 2028 — if he even chooses to run. He’s hardly a permanent fixture of American politics. And Van Hollen is a respected senator who can work across the party’s ideological divisions. The timidity prevailing on the K Street corridor of downtown DC isn’t an inevitable way of doing politics. Just look at how much bolder unions have been in state-level contests this year. In Michigan, the United Auto Workers (UAW), the American Federation of Teachers (AFT) Michigan, National Nurses United, the Association of Flight Attendants, and United Food and Commercial Workers Local 951 were among the unions that took sides in a fiercely contested Democratic Senate primary, backing Abdul El-Sayed against Schumer’s preferred candidate, Haley Stevens. In Maine, the state AFL-CIO and dozens of local unions went all out for Troy Jackson. And in Minnesota, the Service Employees International Union (SEIU), UNITE HERE Local 17, and others backed Peggy Flanagan against the more establishment-friendly Angie Craig. All three of these Senate winners are now supporting Van Hollen. Moving away from this bold spirit, most national union leaders currently appear to want to stay silent, hoping to square the circle between the evident anger of their base and their desire not to break with Schumer. Some of them have framed the issue to me over the past few days as a matter of fighting for the agenda, not the person. But whether they like it or not, it’s precisely that choice of agenda and strategy that’s now being posed by the Senate leadership fight. Everybody knows that who leads the party has a great deal to do with what the party actually does. It’s a bit disingenuous, too, to suddenly insist that the agenda matters but the leadership doesn’t, given how many national unions came out swinging for Nancy Pelosi when her speakership seemed like it might be challenged in 2018. Back then, the AFL-CIO, AFT, the American Federation of State, County and Municipal Employees, SEIU, UAW, the United Steelworkers, and numerous other unions publicly backed Pelosi in her fight to return as House Speaker. Union leaders understand that the choice of congressional leadership matters for the legislative agenda. Not Too Late My suggestion to rank-and-file members and local and state union leaders is to look at this Van Hollen–Schumer fight as a test of whether their national leaders are ready to meet the moment. If they’re not able to take even this relatively mild risk to help save the Democrats from themselves, why should we expect them to take on the much bigger, even more consequential risks, like backing anti-corporate candidates for office in 2028 or massively investing in new organizing to unionize America afterward? National union leaders still sitting this out or leaning toward Schumer should reconsider. In today’s fight for the future of the Democratic Party, national unions have to choose a side. They can stick with the old, corporate-friendly hacks who helped get us into this mess, or stand with those willing to fight against business as usual. There’s no neutral ground here. Labor Mobilized for ‘Many vs. Mighty’ Midterms

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: 不明 ／ 著者: Jenny Brown ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-18T21:01:11+00:00

Something different was in the air as the labor movement mobilized for the midterms. More than usual, unions were willing to support insurgent candidates who refused corporate donations and bucked the Democratic Party establishment. They championed a Bernie Sanders-style list of working-class issues: Medicare for All, a higher minimum wage, union rights, immigrant rights, stopping war, and taxing the rich. Many are union members themselves. And they won. United Auto Workers President Shawn Fain called the election “the many versus the mighty.” In Michigan, the UAW was a key backer of the victorious Abdul El-Sayed, who earlier defeated the party’s depressing primary choice for U.S. Senate. “You had a lot of rich people who tried to… buy a seat, to buy these races,” Fain told Michigan Advance . “Michiganders… are telling these people they’re not for sale.” Logger and union member Troy Jackson won a Senate seat against 30-year incumbent Republican Susan Collins in Maine after the UAW and the Maine AFL-CIO helped defeat the establishment choice in the primary there. In Ohio, labor stalwart Sherrod Brown faced a river of crypto lobby cash but recovered the Senate seat he lost in 2024. In Texas, the Communications Workers (CWA) mobilized for union-member candidates up and down the ticket, asking candidates to commit to a “Contract with Working Texans.” Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa signed on—she’s a labor lawyer, member of Texas State Employees Union/CWA, and the first Democrat elected Texas governor since 1990. Various union members and leaders are headed for Congress. Ironworker Brian Poindexter won in Ohio. Pennsylvanians elected firefighter and union president Bob Brooks. Forest Service smokejumper and local union vice president Sam Forstag won in Montana. VA nurse and union member Mitchell Berman beat a Republican incumbent in Wisconsin. In New York, UAW members and Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) congressional candidates Claire Valdez and Darializa Avila Chevalier defeated establishment Democrats in the primary, then romped November 3. Richard Ojeda, a champion of the West Virginia teachers’ strike of 2018, won a Congressional seat in North Carolina, beating the head of the National Republican Congressional Committee. "We were elected to elevate the lives of working people across this country,” said Ojeda . “We're coming in LASER FOCUSED on the mission. Help people. Hold anyone who has hurt people accountable. Tax the billionaires. The end.” Others lost, but came way closer than expected. In Nebraska, Kellogg’s strike leader and independent Dan Osborn came up short (44-50) for U.S. Senate. He faced ferocious attack ads, with $24 million in ‘independent’ spending. In Florida, former SEIU organizer and DSA member Angie Nixon defeated the establishment Democrat in the primary and almost won (48-50) a Senate race the pundits had written off. She got zero support from the national Democratic Party. CORPORATE DEMS UNDER FIRE Now some unions have come out swinging, demanding more from the party. The Machinists (IAM) sent a letter urging Senator Chuck Schumer to resign as party leader . OPEIU did the same . The IAM “is through with any member of leadership, in any political party, who stops one iota short of being a ferocious, unyielding and unbending champion of working people,” said President Brian Bryant. “This means… sweeping economic policy changes that will recalibrate the vast, immoral, economic injustice in this country.” The United Electrical Workers concurred: “The credit for these wins belongs not to the Democratic Party—whose leadership has offered at best tepid resistance to the Republican agenda over the past two years—but to the working people across the country who have spent the last two years organizing and fighting back in their communities and workplaces.” UE set out an ambitious agenda : a $25 minimum wage, Medicare for All, dismantling ICE, taxing the rich, protecting Social Security, and passing labor law reform including the PRO Act and card-check recognition for speedier union recognition. National Nurses United said voters expect a stop to health care cuts, stat. President Trump can veto most bills Congress passes, but Vermont Senator Bernie Sanders insisted on November 9 that Congress needs to do its job and pass a bold working-class agenda. If Trump vetoes the bills, that’ll raise the stakes in 2028 to elect a president who will sign them. It’ll take a lot of pressure get corporate Democrats to defy their paymasters. So plans are afoot for May Day 2028 , when the Auto Workers and other unions have important contracts expiring. The plan is to keep the focus on working class demands, backed by a season of contract campaigns and strikes.

‘Delay, Deny, Discipline’ Is Amazon’s Real Pregnancy Policy, Workers Say

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: 不明 ／ 著者: Jenny Brown ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-18T21:01:11+00:00

Willamina Barclay was working as a picker at Amazon’s fulfillment center in Rochester, New York, when she felt lightheaded again. “She pleaded with a manager for a seat… He refused: ‘No, because you don’t have a [pregnancy] accommodation. If I give you a chair, I have to give everybody else a chair,’” he said, according to a recent lawsuit. But Barclay, who learned she was pregnant in March 2025, had already asked managers many times for accommodations—a seat, extra breaks to drink, eat, pee (or throw up) in the bathroom, no ladder-climbing or squatting, and no heavy lifting. Amazon—a company known for fast delivery—had failed to answer for weeks, demanding medical documentation. Even after Barclay provided it, Amazon still refused most accommodations, permitting her four breaks a day, but no chair. She was moved to a different position, but still required to lift heavy packages. Amazon counted her new breaks against her unpaid time off (UPT), a leave bank that workers accumulate at five minutes per hour and that maxes out at 80 hours. She went to Human Resources several times to try to rectify the situation, but stopped because they were also counting those visits as UPT, the lawsuit says. The heavy lifting led to sharp pains. At one point she left the warehouse in a wheelchair headed for the hospital. Amazon also counted that time against her unpaid leave bank. On another occasion, she had to leave due to uncontrolled vomiting. Despite a doctor’s note, that was also counted against her. Her UPT thus used up, Amazon fired her. Under the law, workers cannot be penalized for taking pregnancy-related leave (see box at end of article) . Barclay’s ordeal is detailed in a lawsuit filed September 8 by A Better Balance , a nonprofit focused on work policies affecting families. It’s a proposed class action on behalf of all U.S. workers whose rights under the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA) have been violated by Amazon. SYSTEMATIC VIOLATIONS Barclay’s harrowing experience is common, said Sultana Hossain, recording secretary of the Amazon Labor Union (ALU) on Staten Island: Under the law “pregnant workers don’t have to give in paperwork,” she said. “They only have to verbally say, ‘I’m pregnant and I need to be accommodated.’ But we’re seeing Amazon systemically violate that. “Even if workers get an accommodation eventually, it can take several weeks,” said Hossain. “So people really don’t have any choice but to keep working in their regular role, which is really dangerous if they actually need an accommodation.” Key’Asia, a worker at Amazon’s fulfillment center near Wilmington, Delaware, said that Amazon management twice rejected a note from her doctor, once because it had an e-signature, and then again when she got an ink signature. (For privacy to discuss medical matters, some workers asked that last names not be used.) Managers said the signature looked fraudulent and still refused to accommodate her pregnancy, Key’Asia said. “They are making you do the things that your doctor told you that you’re not supposed to be doing,” she told attendees at an online Jobs With Justice event in October. “They’re making you do things that are putting yourself and your baby at risk.” She said Amazon fired her twice, both times when she was pregnant. PATTERN OF ILLEGALITY Workers have noticed a pattern: When they request accommodations, Amazon managers ask if they are “safe to work.” If they say “yes,” they are forced to continue to work without accommodations. If they say “no,” Amazon puts them on unpaid leave , which rapidly runs out. Marissa, who worked at Amazon’s fulfillment center in Rossford, Ohio, said that after her accommodations were denied, she was forced out on unpaid leave, and she ended up losing her apartment. “I was out $1,700… I couldn’t pay for my vehicle. I couldn’t afford to eat. “Amazon doesn’t care about whether or not you’re in pain,” she said. “They care about whether or not you can get their packages out on time.” Hossain said it was typical for workers to get fired for “job abandonment” because “they ran out of time-off options because they were just not given the accommodation.” (A Better Balance has also sued Amazon for this practice on behalf of a disabled worker.) TRAGIC RESULTS Hayat Bzezi was working at Amazon’s Staten Island JFK8 warehouse during the busy holiday season of 2024, when all workers were required to take an additional weekly 10-hour shift. She had asked for lighter duty, and management said she could use a chair, but then she was denied it. She was “lifting heavy products, climbing up and down the stairs, despite having a high-risk pregnancy,” said her husband Wajdy, an elected ALU trustee who works at the same warehouse. Hayat worked through pain, but it got worse and finally she had to go to the hospital, where she miscarried. It was the second time she had miscarried while working at Amazon. After that the couple decided to speak out . Since then, she has stopped working at Amazon and has been able to bear a child. LAW REDUCES MISCARRIAGES When the PWFA went into effect in 2023, a study found that it reduced miscarriages by 9.6 percent. This suggests that many more miscarriages could be avoided if Amazon, the country’s second-largest private employer, started following the law. Pregnant With A Contract Postal workers move packages like Amazon warehouse workers do, but they have a union contract. It makes all the difference when you’re pregnant, said Postal Workers (APWU) national field organizer Lori Cash. She hired into a heavy job at the Postal Service in 1998 while pregnant with her third child. After a couple of months, her doctor told her she needed to stop work, but she had no sick leave or vacation time accrued. “I went to my union steward, and I said, ‘This is what my doctor is telling me, I’m scared.’ I had no idea what my rights were. I’d never been in a union before.” Her steward explained that she could take leave without pay, under the contract, and could not be fired: “We have a just cause process in our contract. They have to prove that they had a just reason to fire you, and just using too much time off without being paid is not justified.” After the birth, Cash came back to work, but it was too hard: “I had two toddlers and a newborn. I was trying to go back to work with all those shenanigans going on at my house with all these babies.” She told her steward she was thinking of quitting. But her steward fought for her to get the option to come back with fewer hours and gradually increase them, which was also guaranteed in their contract. “Pregnancy is stressful,” said Cash. “You have so much to worry about.” But during her next pregnancy, she felt “much more confident that I was going to be able to get the accommodations I needed. I had a whole union behind me, fighting to make sure that those rights were given to me, and to make sure that I had a healthy pregnancy and was able to take the time that I needed for me, and for my baby to be taken care of.” But there is every indication that Amazon is ignoring it. Last year, New Jersey’s attorney general filed a lawsuit against Amazon after reviewing thousands of worker requests for accommodations. Amazon is New Jersey’s largest private employer, with around 50,000 workers. “When Amazon’s pregnant workers and workers with disabilities request accommodations, Amazon stonewalls them,” the suit charges. “It forces them through a byzantine accommodations process, imposing rigid and unnecessary paperwork requirements and subjecting workers to unexplained weeks-long delays.” Rosa, a worker at Amazon’s fulfillment center in Akron, Ohio, said she asked for pregnancy accommodations because she was working in Ship Dock, a role that requires heavy lifting, pushing, and pulling. She was hospitalized for dehydration four times, but managers still kept her in that role, she said. “Eventually my symptoms became so severe that I had to take an unpaid leave of absence,” she said. “I found out afterwards that I could have done paid short-term disability leave, but these options weren’t told to me.” Amazon denies this. “The suggestion that we routinely deny or don’t support pregnancy accommodations is simply untrue,” said Amazon spokesperson Kelly Nantel. She said “99.9 percent” of pregnancy accommodation requests this year had been approved. But New Jersey investigators found that Amazon regularly fired workers for requesting accommodations, in violation of New Jersey’s Law Against Discrimination and the PWFA. New York State has also sued Amazon for this. Hossain said the medical forms that Amazon requires for pregnancy, injury, and disability accommodations are so comprehensive and invasive that doctors have objected that they violate medical privacy laws. Amazon wants such detailed medical documentation, Hossain said, because “they want to claim that if workers ever get injured, it’s because of conditions they came to work with, and it’s not workplace injuries.” Asked about this, an Amazon public relations representative declined to respond. PACE DRIVES INJURIES The worker justice group Warehouse Life has been tracking and protesting Amazon’s health and safety record since 2023, and helping injured workers who have to “fight through a lot of misinformation and lies that they received from [Amazon] managers about workers’ comp,” said Chloe Sigal, campaign manager for the group. Sigal said Amazon’s rate system, along with “time off task” (TOT) policies, make the work unsafe: “Amazon is pushing people to work as fast, as hard as humanly possible—or really outside what is humanly possible. That’s what’s driving this injury epidemic .” In 2021 the California legislature found that injuries in Amazon warehouses were driven by its rate system. Workers were forced to work faster and faster because if they fell into the lowest 25 percent they could experience discipline, and the bottom 5 percent risked termination. California passed a law requiring warehouses to publish stable quotas and not constantly accelerate them. In 2024 the state fined Amazon $6 million for violating it. Workers also accumulate TOT when surveillance detects a pause in their package scanning. Fear of too much TOT pushes workers to cut short their breaks, skip bathroom breaks (and reduce liquid consumption to match), and push on through pain. Dehydration is particularly risky during a pregnancy. “The reason that people are miscarrying at Amazon is because of this rate system,” said Sigal. Doctors agree. Jenny Fish is a Santa Rosa, California, physician who provides prenatal care. “Prolonged standing, going the whole shift without water, lifting more than 15 to 20 pounds, working night shifts regularly,” she said, “all of this has been irrefutably linked to higher risk of miscarriage, preeclampsia, increased maternal fatigue, preterm delivery, low birth weight, and just in general adverse birth outcomes.” Preeclampsia is potentially fatal. IS THERE A POLICY? Sigal said that soon after Warehouse Life started, several Amazon workers came to the group reporting pregnancy issues. But when the group put out a call on social media for experiences working at Amazon while pregnant, it was flooded with stories. Two thousand women from 40 states responded, and 81 described loss of a pregnancy. (To talk to Warehouse Life, schedule a call here .) Now some of these workers are leading the charge to get changes at the company. They have put pressure on New York City’s Mt. Sinai Health System to terminate Amazon Board of Directors member Edith Cooper as a hospital trustee, given Amazon’s dismal safety record. Warehouse Life members have also met with Amazon management. Sigal said that during those meetings Amazon claimed to have a policy on pregnancy that was only available to workers. But no workers were able to access it. “Nobody has it. We’ve heard from managers that they don’t have it either, that they don’t receive training on what to do,” Sigal said. When Labor Notes asked Amazon for its pregnancy policy and any training materials, no documents were forthcoming. Warehouse Life counters that the company’s real pregnancy policy is “Delay. Deny. Discipline.” MARCH ON H.R. The Amazon Labor Union organized on Staten Island in 2022, and in 2024 affiliated with the Teamsters. But Amazon refuses to negotiate with the union at the 5,000-worker warehouse, despite being ordered to do so by the National Labor Relations Board in April. The union can still help, though. “Just a few weeks ago a pregnant worker came to our union hall saying she wanted help getting an accommodation at work,” said ALU’s Hossain. The worker had already told her manager that she was pregnant. The union sent several workers to accompany her to H.R., saying “that should be enough, and Amazon needs to start the process of accommodating her,” Hossain said. “And they actually did listen, but only because a group of workers accompanied her.” A contract could provide additional protections (see box at upper right) . The union isn’t waiting for workers to seek it out. Union activists distribute fliers with a QR code to a reporting form that goes to the union. Half of the reports “are unfair discipline, the other half is workers who are dealing with injuries or the accommodation process or need workers’ comp or whatever,” said Hossain. “It’s such a big issue.” “It really does feel like we’re all just numbers,” said Rosa in Akron. “I want them to take more accountability. They make it so hard when it comes to a life-changing thing.” Pregnancy Rights It has been illegal to discriminate against pregnant workers since 1978, when feminist agitation won the Pregnancy Discrimination Act (under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act), which says employers can’t discriminate based on pregnancy, childbirth, or related medical conditions. Before that, many employers had a policy of terminating workers when they got pregnant, and pregnant women often weren’t eligible for unemployment insurance. In 2023, the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act strengthened pregnancy rights. Now employers are required to make reasonable accommodations. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission has found that reasonable accommodations which don’t require a doctor’s note include: a seat; additional breaks to eat, drink, or use the bathroom; adjusted uniforms and safety equipment; and changes to your job duties or your schedule to make it possible for you to keep working safely while you’re pregnant or recovering from a birth. Additional accommodations that might require a doctor’s note include limits on weight you have to lift, bending, and ladder use. “But you still have the right to get those accommodations quickly and have them implemented effectively,” said Katherine Greenberg, attorney with A Better Balance . “It is illegal for employers like Amazon to force workers to take a leave of absence if there’s an on-the-job accommodation that would keep them safe and allow them to keep working and earning an income,” said Greenberg. Also, employers can’t punish workers for asking for or needing accommodations. “If you need to pump milk for your baby, you have the right to take a lactation break without being punished for too much time off task,” said Greenberg. “Or if you experience a miscarriage and you need to miss work to recover, you have the right to take that time without being deducted UPT or assigned points for your absence.” Your state or city may have additional protections; check here . The American Woman Who Joined Algeria’s Socialist Experiment

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Julia Harte ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-18T18:50:57+00:00

Within days of arriving in Algeria, months after its liberation from France in 1962, Elaine Klein knew she was there for the long haul. “The experiment to create a new socialist country from the impoverished colony, the tremendous task of building a state respected in the international arena, was daunting,” she later wrote. Yet, “My gut told me that I would settle here.” Her intuition was correct. For more than a decade, the Jewish New Yorker was the only American in Algeria’s new government. She assumed a range of roles — reporter, translator, envoy — as the former French colony established itself as an international haven for resistance groups, political dissidents, and anti-colonial organizers. Klein was not the only one taking on new responsibilities. A national program of popular self-management, or “autogestion,” was implemented soon after the revolution, giving the Algerian people ownership of the farms, businesses, and buildings abandoned by fleeing European settlers. A coup led by military chief Houari Boumédiène overthrew the first postcolonial government. The autogestion experiment largely ended, though Boumédiène kept trying to close Algeria’s wealth gap and resist economic subordination by the Global North. Klein also continued to cover progressive and socialist assemblages around the world on behalf of the national press agency, Algérie Presse Service. When the Organization of Latin American Solidarity gathered in Cuba in 1967 to form a united front against US imperialism, she attended a press conference where Stokely Carmichael called “white Western imperialist society” the “common enemy” of all the delegates present and declared, “Our struggle is to overthrow this system which feeds itself and expands itself through the economic and cultural exploitation of nonwhite, non-Western peoples.” Klein took the opportunity to invite Carmichael to visit Algeria later that year: his first trip to the African continent. Into the early 1970s, she served as a sort of domestic ambassador for other Black Panthers and leaders of international revolutionary movements who flocked to open offices in Algiers, a city dubbed the “Mecca of Revolution” by Pan-African Bissau-Guinean poet Amílcar Cabral and the “third world capital” by Klein. Her work brought her under the scope of a vast CIA operation aimed at uncovering foreign involvement in domestic protest movements within the United States. The FBI’s COINTELPRO initiative helped drive a wedge between the international wing of the Black Panther Party, which Klein helped establish, and their US-based counterparts. After more than a decade in Algeria, Klein herself was forced to leave when she refused to spy on a friend for the Boumédiène regime. Her idealism, however, never wavered. Until her death this September, she followed an irrepressible impulse to stand with other humans fighting for freedom and self-government, no matter where they were. And despite the remarkable role she played in some of the twentieth century’s most historic anti-colonial, social ownership, and civil rights movements, she never thought of herself as anything more or less than a world citizen. Revolution in the Third World Klein first encountered the Algerian anti-colonial movement at the 1952 May Day parade in Paris. She had moved to France months earlier, after being pushed out of her job in New York with the United World Federalists (UWF). The UWF, part of an emerging “world government” movement that aimed to eradicate war and establish a system of global justice, had fractured due to internal divisions and external pressure from US authorities, a process that would recur with many causes Klein championed over time. After moving to Paris, she began learning the history of decoloni­zation in North and West Africa through work as a French–English conference interpreter. In 1960, the year that eleven African countries — including nine former French colonies — became independent, she helped organize a congress in Ghana, the first African state to shake off colonial rule. There she developed a fast friendship with Frantz Fanon, whom the Algerian provisional government had recently named their ambassador to Ghana. Back in New York later that year, Klein met the Algerian provisional government’s representative to the United Nations. He hired her on the spot to write promotional material and lobby for Algerian independence at the United Nations. On December 19, 1960, those efforts would pay off when the General Assembly voted 63–8 to recognize Algeria’s right to self-rule. (The United States and twenty-seven other countries abstained.) As Mokhtefi later told me, the unexpected job was a long-awaited opportunity for “digging in” and “getting my hands dirty” in the decolonization cause that she had long supported. When France recognized Algerian independence in the summer of 1962, a brief power struggle ensued inside the National Liberation Front, the party that had led Algeria’s revolution. One of the Front’s original leaders, Ahmed Ben Bella, and Boumédiène, the head of his army, took control of Algiers in September. Klein arrived in October and started working for the tourism authority, a largely theoretical job in a country that would receive no tourists for years to come. Upon learning she was from New York, Ben Bella’s press and information adviser hired her as his assistant. From there, she was transferred to the government’s secretary general, an office that produced agendas, reports, and press releases for meetings of Algeria’s cabinet. Finally, she was moved to Algérie Presse Service, where she wrote daily op-eds and became the entire “English desk.” Ben Bella was an outspoken adherent of Third Worldism, the anti-imperialist, anti-racist movement through which current and former colonies united to resist oppression by European powers as well as, eventually, the American and Sino-Soviet blocs during the Cold War. Third Worldists, as political scientist Robert Malley put it, had an “uneasy coexistence” with the Eurocentrism and cultural superiority embedded in some Marxist theory; Friedrich Engels in 1848 had described France’s conquest of Algeria as “an important and fortunate fact for the progress of civilization.” Yet following the 1962 exodus of French colonists, or “pieds noirs,” socialists from around the world flocked to Algeria, drawn by the revolutionaries’ vision of a more democratic, egalitarian state. These foreigners would become known as “pieds rouges” after Ben Bella’s overthrow ousted many of them from Algeria too. Among them was the Greek Trotskyist Michalis Raptis, aka Michel Pablo. While leading the Fourth International during the 1950s, Pablo had devised a counterfeit-money scheme to buy weapons for Algerian revolutionaries on the world market and had established an arms factory for them in Morocco. Soon after Pablo arrived in independent Algeria, he became an economic adviser to Ben Bella, presiding over the campaign of “autogestion” that put the nation’s resources under popular control. Modeled on similar experiments in Cuba and Yugoslavia — countries that also sent medical teams to Algeria to help replace the European health care personnel who had fled — Algeria’s self-management program established worker-run committees to oversee the farms, industrial enterprises, and estates left behind by the colonial regime. After attending an Algerian congress on industrial self-management in 1964, the Trotskyist French militant Daniel Guérin wrote that the event had been “dominated” by the emergence of “a working class, of a socialist awareness.” Ben Bella himself embraced socialism at public rallies and within his government, opening a National Office for the Support of the Socialist Sector. This enthusiasm was short-lived, however. Ben Bella’s other advisers persuaded him to fold the socialist sector support office into the agricultural ministry and to exert more central control over the self-managed farms, giving rise to a corrupt bureaucracy. While the National Liberation Front continued to publicly tout Algeria as a self-managed socialist state, the country slipped further from that ideal. “Power and increasing adulation isolated [Ben Bella] from a truthful examination of events,” according to Klein. “He launched new national projects every day, but neglected the measures required to implement them; he created offices and agencies that functioned in name only.” Ben Bella also began to take more punitive measures against domestic dissidents, arresting, torturing and banishing those who criticized him. Three years after Algeria had won its independence, Boumédiène led a coup in which Ben Bella was arrested. It was the result of an internal power struggle more than an ideological rift; Boumédiène would continue to pursue some of the same progressive social policies and redistribution measures as his predecessor. But many pieds rouges, including Pablo, were branded foreign atheists and forced out of Algeria. Klein stayed, even though she had joined popular protests against the coup. Her fluency in English and French — a rare combination in postrevolutionary Algeria — had made her useful to the foreign affairs minister, Abdelaziz Bouteflika, a key ally of Boumédiène. “Hope remained that one day everything would be put right,” Klein later wrote. “And we all went back to work.” Panthers, Trotskyists, and G-Men In the summer of 1969, two years after she had invited Carmichael to Algeria and shown him around the country, Klein received a call that another Black Panther leader was in Algiers — and needed her help establishing a permanent presence there. Eldridge Cleaver had fled the United States several months earlier after exchanging shots with Oakland police the night of Martin Luther King Jr’s assassination. For Klein, who had protested racial segregation in the United States since college and reported on the rise of the Black Panther Party for the Algerian press, the opportunity to work for the Panthers was thrilling. As with many in the Third Worldist movement, she saw anti-racist and anti-colonial activism as inseparable. Klein helped formalize Cleaver’s status in the country by involving him in the world’s first Pan-African Cultural Festival, which she organized in Algiers soon after his arrival. She also helped Cleaver and other Panthers establish the party’s international branch there, and accompanied his wife Kathleen on a speaking tour back in the United States. As Cleaver settled into Algeria, he took a more militantly anti-American stance and strengthened the Panthers’ Third World, anti-colonial bona fides through new alliances with figures such as North Korea’s Kim Il Sung and the Palestinian Fatah leader Yasser Arafat. In a 1971 interview with a San Francisco radio station, Cleaver proudly claimed that Black Panther members serving in Vietnam had switched sides, fighting against the US war effort there. These moves clashed with the priorities of the US-based Panthers, who, depleted of personnel and funds by the number of Panthers who had ended up in prison, were trying to put more resources into peace­ful community service programs. In March 1970, as part of its COINTELPRO effort to “expose, disrupt, misdirect, discredit, or otherwise neutralize the activities of black nationalist, hate-type organizations and groupings,” the FBI’s second most powerful official recom­mended that the Bureau send Cleaver a letter “aimed at inflaming the dispute” by telling Cleaver he was being undercut by US-based Black Panther Party leaders. One year later, as Cleaver was on the brink of completely falling out with one such leader, Huey Newton, J. Edgar Hoover endorsed a plan to send Cleaver and Newton spurious correspondence designed to “provoke the existing difficulty” between them. One fake letter purported to show Newton denouncing Cleaver to Boumédiène. Ten­sions between the Algerian government and the Black Panthers, meanwhile, had been building, exacerbated by the Panthers’ disinclination to inte­grate into Algerian society. There was also a fundamental contradiction between the goals of the Algiers-based Panthers and those of the Algerian government. For Cleaver, the Black Panther Party had a singular objective: overthrowing the US government. But Algerian leaders wanted to limit their military aid to freedom fighters trying to liberate their countries from foreign colonization. The mutual frustration between the Algerian authorities and their American guests peaked in August 1972. That month, members of the Black Lib­eration Army, a splinter group started by former Black Panthers who had rejected Huey Newton as too moderate, hijacked a Delta flight from Detroit destined for Miami and landed it in Algiers. They planned to donate their $1 million ransom from the airline to the international Black Panthers, but Algerian authorities confiscated the funds and returned them to Delta. When Cleaver demanded the money, local police raided the Panther villa and took their weapons. Cleaver was never officially deported, but with other Panthers also leaving Algiers, he decided to leave. To extract Cleaver from Algeria, Klein enlisted the help of none other than Michel Pablo, who had fled to France after Boumédiène’s 1965 coup. The former Fourth International secretary and Ben Bella adviser who had once counterfeited money to supply arms to Algeria’s revolutionary army sprang into action once more to help a different kind of revolutionary leave Algeria. Pablo gave Klein the number of a “passer” who could meet Cleaver at the Geneva airport and covertly drive him over the border into France. Pablo also supplied her with a “classy British passport with a photo of a Black man who could have been a younger Eldridge,” as Klein wrote in her memoir. She arranged a safe house for Cleaver and helped him find French lawyers to petition for his asylum there. Klein’s own position in Algeria was becoming more precarious under Boumédiène’s regime. In 1972, secu­rity agents asked her to spy on a friend of hers who had married Ben Bella, and she refused. Two years later, Klein herself was forced into exile from the country to which she had devoted her life. But she had a companion by her side. Two years earlier, a mutual friend had set her up with Mokhtar Mokhtefi: a dashing hero of the Algerian Revolution, who was also rapidly grow­ing disillusioned with the authoritarian turn of the post-independence lead­ership. When Mokhtefi ended up in France, Mokhtar followed, and they lived there for two decades, doing a mix of odd jobs — from making jewelry to writing children’s books — before moving to the United States in 1994. As she forged a new life first in Paris and ultimately in New York City, Algeria underwent its own identity crises and political convulsions, straying even farther from the socialist ambitions that had defined its early years of independence. When Bouteflika — the Boumédiène-aligned foreign affairs minister who had used Klein’s translation services in the 1960s — became president of Algeria in 1999, he shored up his power by enriching a small group of oligarchs at the expense of the general population. Twenty years into his presidency, Bouteflika was forced out of office by the largest popular opposition movement in Algeria since the revolution that had launched his political career sixty years earlier. On November 1, 2019, the sixty-fifth anniversary of the start of the revolution, tens of thousands of demonstrators who called themselves the Hirak (Arabic for “movement”) surged through Algiers to protest Bouteflika’s final efforts to cling to power. “Algeria will take back its independence,” they chanted in Arabic, clapping in time to the syncopated rallying cry. Young and old Algerians waved flags as they marched in the streets or banged on pots and pans from their windows in solidarity. Among the protesters thronging the main streets of Algiers so densely that it was almost impossible to move, Elaine Klein — now Elaine Mokhtefi and ninety years old — could be seen in the crowd, glowing with excitement and cheering on the Hirak with the slogans of the new revolutionaries. “I’ve Started to Talk, and I Don’t Stop” I first visited Mokhtefi at her Manhattan apartment in 2021, when I emailed her out of the blue to ask if I could interview her for a book I was writing about Americans who had traveled abroad to join revolutionary liberation movements. We spoke for several hours about the remarkable trajectory of her life and the causes she and Mokhtar continued to support after moving back to the United States. It was a lonely repatriation for Mokhtefi, at first: “There was nobody to tell what had happened, neither about the Black Panthers, nor about the Algerian war. Everyone seemed very far from all that.” But she and Mokhtar were not idle. “We had to contribute,” she said. “We had to show that we were still responsible world citizens.” They joined Palestinian liberation demonstrations, climate justice marches, and visited encamped protesters at Zuccotti Park several times during the 2011 Occupy Wall Street movement. Writing her memoir, Algiers, Third World Capital: Freedom Fighters, Revolutionaries, Black Panthers , Mokhtefi told me, had saved her from a pit of grief that she feared might swallow her up. But ever since it was published in 2018, “and I went on these tours, and I saw that people were really inter­ested in what I had lived,” she continued, “I’ve started to talk, and I don’t stop.” I stayed in touch with Mokhtefi as I continued working on my chapter about her. In 2022, I went to Algiers to visit prominent sites from her time there and interview former Algerian revolutionaries whose paths had crossed with hers. Eventually she met my partner and daughter, and I got to know many of her friends. Every time we saw each other, I marveled at how busy her social life and traveling schedule remained, even as she grew frailer and memories eluded her more often. In August, I visited her apartment one last time to attend a party she threw to celebrate receiving Algerian nationality in recognition of her service to the nation. By then, I had a copy of my own book to give her. A few weeks later — three days before her sudden death — she emailed me, telling me she had started reading my book from the beginning, but that about halfway through the second chapter, “I couldn’t hold myself back, I had to read about me. I just wasn’t capable of waiting my turn. What an experience . . . Thanks, merci, gracias, saha as the Algerians say, the works. I felt so full of myself, validated well beyond any merit.” She told me how lucky she felt that we had entered each other’s lives. Then she added, “I will read the other chapters and see your perspective through to the end, and with such pleasure. Did Moderate Democrats Really Outperform the Left? No.

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Matt Bruenig ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-18T16:59:55+00:00

Democrats did well in the 2026 midterm elections. According to the current tally, they gained forty-one House seats , eight Senate seats , and five governorships . Democratic candidates of all types had success, making it difficult to easily point the finger at this or that faction for blowing the election. This has generated some frustration among factional partisans because such partisans are heavily invested in the idea that their style of politics is electorally superior to the politics of other factions. They can’t point to winners and losers to support their positions because, in this election, everyone was a winner. So they are forced to make their case by analyzing margins of victory, which is hard to do well and full of uncertainty even when it is done well. In this piece, I walk through various approaches to quantifying candidate-specific performance to provide a sort of primer on how to think about this and successfully navigate the deluge of election pseudo-statistics. I. Raw Vote Share The most obvious, though naive, way to distinguish between winners is to look at their raw vote share. If one Democratic candidate won 51 percent of the votes cast in their race and another won 70 percent of the votes cast in their race, then, at a glance, it seems like you could say that the 70-percent candidate performed better than the 51-percent candidate. Certainly the 70-percent candidate did better, but this is not the same thing as performing better. When pundits talk about performance, they are trying to isolate the effect the candidate’s own characteristics had on the election results. Raw vote shares do not do this because raw vote shares reflect other factors as well, including the underlying tendencies of the voters in that area. In other words, it’s possible that the 70-percent district leans more Democratic, and the 51-percent candidate would have also gotten 70 percent in that district. II. Expected Vote Share To isolate the candidate-specific effects, you have to establish some kind of baseline figure for what the election results would have been in a hypothetical version of the race where the candidate was a “generic” Democrat. The difference between the actual results and these “expected” results is then assumed to reflect the unique characteristics of the candidate. This is not an easy thing to do, and most of the efforts I see in the discourse are ridiculous. A. Uniform Swing as Expected Vote Share The most common approach to quantifying expected vote share is to determine how vote shares changed nationally from the prior election and then assign that change uniformly across every voting area in the country. This is what Kevin Deluca did in the following graph. It may look sophisticated, but, as the caption explains, all Deluca did was go through each of the twelve states and add 7.5 points to Kamala Harris’s 2024 presidential result in that state. This is his entire model for expected vote share. The difference between this expected vote and the actual vote is then used to declare underperformers and overperformers. The simplicity of this model is very charming and no doubt explains why I see it more than any other: anyone who can add and subtract is capable of running the model. But does it really make sense to suppose that, if you ran a generic Democrat in every Senate race in the country, each of them would have received a Harris+7.5 vote share? Even if one were to suppose that all of the noncandidate differences between these states and races could be fully captured by Harris’s 2024 presidential result, why would equal performances by generic Senate candidates show up as equal changes in percentage points? Wouldn’t it make more sense for equal performances to show up as equal changes in percent of the 2024 non-Democratic share that was converted to the 2026 Democratic share? To see what I mean, imagine two House districts, one where Harris got 80 percent of the vote in 2024 and another where Harris got 20 percent of the vote in 2024. If you put equally-performing generic Democrats in those districts, what would you expect the result to be? The uniform-swing approach says that we should expect them to add the same number of percentage points. So, for example, we should expect the 80-percent district to become a 90-percent district and the 20-percent district to become a 30-percent district. In this scenario, both districts moved equally by 10 points. But to move 10 points in the 80-percent district, you have to persuade 50 percent of the non-Democratic voters. Moving 10 points in the 20-percent district only requires persuading 12.5 percent of the non-Democratic voters. Why would we expect one generic Democrat to be four times as persuasive as another generic Democrat? How is that equal performance? The premise of the uniform-swing model, which is that equal performance equates to equal percentage points, is nonsensical on its face. Clearly, the number of points an equal performer can move will vary state to state and district to district by the share of the electorate that is even capable of being moved. Simple linear regressions using the House and Senate results provide some empirical support for this point. There are at least two other big problems with this approach to modeling expected vote share. First, a uniform vote swing would make sense only if you were modeling generic Democrat versus generic Republican. But these Senate races do not have generic Republicans in them. Maine’s Troy Jackson ran against a thirty-year incumbent in Susan Collins, while Texas’s James Talarico ran against a scandal-plagued nonincumbent in Ken Paxton. The difference between actual vote share and expected vote share would capture the combined candidate effects of both the Democratic candidate and the Republican candidate. So, for instance, if Troy Jackson runs 2 points behind the uniform-swing expected vote share, that could be because he has negative candidate effects, because Collins has positive candidate effects, or because of some combination of the two. Likewise, if Talarico runs 2 points ahead of the generic-versus-generic expected vote share, that could be because he has positive candidate effects or because Paxton has negative candidate effects. By construction, the uniform-swing approach assumes that the relative overperformance or underperformance of Republican candidates is uniformly zero. Second, different districts have different demographic compositions, which should generate different swings even with equally-performing generic Democrats. For example, here’s what percent of each racial group voted for the Dems nationally in 2024 and 2026. As we can see, the vote swing varies by racial group. Given this variation, the only way equally-performing generic Democrats would generate uniform vote swings in every voting district is if every voting district had the same racial demographics. They don’t. Race is the most obvious example, but other demographic variables like income and education vary across districts and also drive differential vote swings separate from anything having to do with candidate effects. B. Overlapping Elections as Expected Vote Share The second most common approach to quantifying expected vote share is to compare a specific Democratic candidate’s vote share to the vote share of other Democratic candidates running for other positions within the same voting area. So, for instance, you might compare how one statewide candidate did to how other statewide candidates in that same state did. This approach avoids some of the problems of the uniform-swing approach. It does not require us to clumsily extrapolate from prior election results. By construction, it controls for demographics and other sorts of variation between different groups of voters because it compares how different candidates did among the exact same voters in the same year. No longer are we stuck having to wonder whether Michigan 2024 is comparable to Michigan 2026 or whether Michigan 2026 is comparable to Texas 2026. Instead, this approach just compares Michigan 2026 to Michigan 2026. But this overlapping-elections approach still has some of the same problems as the uniform-swing approach and some unique problems of its own. Like the uniform-swing approach, the overlapping-elections approach effectively assumes that Republican candidates never underperform or overperform. But this is obviously untrue. In Texas, Senator-elect Talarico (Dem) received 55.1 percent of the vote while Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa (Dem) received 52.8 percent of the vote. The overlapping-elections approach would say that Talarico overperformed by 2.3 points, or, alternatively, that Hinojosa underperformed by 2.3 points. But these numbers don’t allow us to reach any such conclusion. After all, another explanation would be that Talarico and Hinojosa performed exactly the same while Talarico’s opponent, the scandal-plagued first-time Senate candidate Ken Paxton, underperformed by 1.15 points and Hinojosa’s opponent, the twelve-year incumbent Greg Abbott, overperformed by 1.15 points. The overlapping-elections approach is also unable to determine the absolute quality of the candidates in a given geography, only their relative positions to one another (if that). The uniform-swing approach presumably uses national averages on the theory that a large sample across many geographies provides a more reliable indicator of what the average candidate quality is. But if your dataset only includes a handful of overlapping races, it’s quite possible that a disproportionate share of the candidates in your dataset (even, potentially, all of the candidates in your dataset) are underperformers or overperformers. A senator who runs slightly above a governor or the combined House vote could be an overperformer, but they might also be an underperformer surrounded by even greater underperformers. This approach cannot distinguish between the two. Another unique problem with the overlapping-elections approach is that it assumes that the only reason a voter would split their ticket — e.g., vote for a Democrat for senator and then a Republican for governor or House of Representatives — is because of the different characteristics of the candidates running in the various races. But this is not true. A voter might also split their ticket because of differences in the powers of the various offices they are voting on. A voter who typically supports Republicans but dislikes Donald Trump and wants to rein in his power may support a Republican for governor while supporting a Democrat for the House. That same lean-Republican voter, while supporting a Democrat for the House, might support a Republican for the Senate, given that senators serve six-year terms while all House representatives come up for reelection again in 2028. Even if only a small percentage of voters think in this (quite rational) ticket-splitting way, it’s enough to completely swamp most of the point differences we see in these overlapping-election results, especially when you add in the other problems identified above. C. Regression Models One of the least common approaches to quantifying expected vote share is also the most sophisticated: regression models. Rather than assuming a uniform swing or looking at overlapping elections, throw a variety of variables into a model that spits out an expected vote share for each geography. The difference between what the model outputs and the actual vote share is then presumed to be driven by candidate effects. As far as I know, the two main sources for this approach are Split Ticket , which is now at The Argument , and Strength in Numbers led by G. Elliot Morris. In this election, the Strength in Numbers approach showed no real difference between the various ideological groupings of House candidates. If anything, the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) candidates overperformed the average Democrat. Split Ticket, which uses a different regression model, reached a similar result, with only the Blue Dogs and Justice Democrat candidates posting performance results (slightly) distinguishable from zero. The Economist also published the results of a model it apparently put together for the election. According to this model and its candidate groupings, moderates outperformed expectation by less than 1 point while progressives underperformed expectation by the same amount. The main problem with this modeling approach is just that it is not totally clear what variables should be included in the models. This is why they all differ from one another. Another problem, which plagues all of these expected-vote approaches, is that not all candidate effects are related to ideology. Candidates might do better or worse because of their identity, e.g., being a Muslim. They might do better or worse because of their personality, e.g., being charismatic or not. Indeed, as we can see in the scatterplots above, the differences between candidates in each bucket far exceed the average differences between the candidate buckets. This problem is especially relevant when it comes to candidate selection, e.g., when voting in a Democratic primary, because it suggests that the nonideological aspects of candidates completely swamp the ideological aspects. Arguably, these problems could be waved away if the various models all reached similar conclusions about candidate quality and did so at margins too large to plausibly deny. When it comes to the progressive-versus-moderates question, these three regression models do basically reach similar conclusions, which is a null conclusion. What departures we see from null in some of the models on some of the candidate groupings are so small that any grains of salt completely eradicate them. III. Where That Leaves Us Obviously, most discourse participants don’t care about any of this and are happy to move around whatever chart they see that shows that science proves their politics is the best politics. But among pro-moderate pundits who do care at least some about maintaining a defensible argument on the merits, what I have seen lately is a proposal of sorts in which pro-progressive pundits agree that moderates do better than progressives in exchange for pro-moderate pundits agreeing that the gap is very small. Both Jonathan Chait and Matt Yglesias have gone this route after the election. In more recent exchanges I’ve had with Yglesias, he seems to retreat a bit further toward acknowledging that the moderate advantage can’t be reliably demonstrated in the data but that it rests on strong theoretical foundations. The problem with these strategic retreats is that the difference between zero and nearly-zero is not going to be big enough to convince anyone to abandon policy initiatives that they think are important, especially given that policy popularity changes over time in part due to the advocacy of policy partisans. If the current electoral penalty of advocating single-payer health care over expanding Obamacare subsidies is 1 point or less, it would be crazy for someone who believes single-payer health care would dramatically improve society to give it up, especially when the alternative of attempting to persuade some voters to change their minds on that, which includes running candidates who argue for it, also exists. Anthropic’s IPO Is a Bid to Manufacture Confidence

Anthropic, maker of artificial intelligence software Claude, is going public this Thursday. The initial public offering (IPO) could top the record SpaceX set earlier this year, once a concurrent private stock placement is counted. In February, Anthropic raised capital at a $380 billion valuation, and it is now seeking buy-in at $1.4 trillion. The rise has only been matched by the demand from investors, as Bloomberg reported the IPO road show was oversubscribed by six times the available shares. In other words, institutions will be crowding out individual investors vying for early gains. Anthropic aims to raise $64 billion from the IPO in a nontraditional coming-to-market event. It has already been widely noted how the IPO’s role has changed over the last decade, with the flipping of public and private functions. Previously companies went public when their growth depended on it, raising capital from the public in order to continue expansion. But today, exponential growth takes place across multiple rounds of buy-in for private credit and venture capital (VC) funds. When those investors are ready to sell, the IPO works, in part, as an exit opportunity. Meaning: the people in the know, inside Anthropic, are rushing to market in order to offload their shares at peak value into your hands. Anthropic is aware of the problem with how this looks. For this reason, out of its IPO of 825 million shares, over 800 million (97 percent) are fresh issuance — newly formed slices of the company that dilute previous shareholders’ stock but have the benefit of providing capital upfront to the company. This is not a normal distribution for a large IPO but does mimic the earlier framework of SpaceX — that is, another company that oriented its price and offering around retail interest. Essentially, these companies are duping investors with the facade of fresh issuance as proof of internal confidence, understanding that selling pressure will spike in the months after the firm goes public. Obscure lockup terms mark out twenty-three liquidity events — days when insiders can sell shares — beginning on the first day of trading. Typically, early investors would wait six months to a year before being able to sell. The thinking is presumably the following: increase public confidence by smoothing out the change of hands, in order to avoid a deluge of shares going live at any one time. Forever Young In preparation for going public, Anthropic filed its S-1 prospectus document with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). The tech-and-VC All-In Podcast with Chamath Palihapitiya and Jason Calacanis opened its post-S-1 commentary with David Sacks by calling this the greatest wealth-creation event in the history of Silicon Valley. Indeed, more than a thousand Anthropic employees are in line to make $10–$100 million post-IPO. Calacanis followed up by questioning whether this was the “greatest business in the history of capitalism.” Irony aside, I found the comment thought-provoking. Could it be that Anthropic is the greatest misallocation of capital and the greatest company capitalism has ever produced? This is one of an array of dialectics defining the development of American frontier AI labs. Together they merge imagery from utopic and dystopic futures: we are told that these developments will either eliminate half the workforce, or “create more jobs than they destroy”; solve the climate crisis, or burst the environmental constraints of water, electricity, and critical minerals asunder; they’re either a word-order prediction machine, or an army of banal geniuses. We might be either in the first inning or the ninth: Who can tell? The element in common is that few people in the public eye hold any position in between these extremes. Bifurcated opinion is an accumulation mechanism for these companies; the unknown is the elixir of their success. The moment they become a known quantity is the moment when corporate accounting standards mean something. Palihapitiya of All-In demonstrates the value of AI by citing the fact that a company like Microsoft has grown its revenue by two-thirds ($198 billion to $332 billion) in the past four years without increasing its workforce. The company recently sent buyout offers to 60,000 employees: revenue per worker has skyrocketed, and yet it still sees dead weight everywhere. It’s a story seen across the tech industry. Torsten Slok at Apollo recently found that revenue per employee in the sector is up around 40 percent since the 2022 turning point in AI and has flatlined in the S&P’s remaining 493 companies. We can assume 2022 really was the turning point, after ChatGPT was introduced to the public that November. Yet what happened a month earlier was, I’d argue, far more important to Slok’s findings. Elon Musk acquired Twitter in October 2022, and within the week he halved its workforce and issued work-intensity ultimatums to thousands more. A 7,500-person company was reduced to a little over 1,000 staff. On Twitter (soon renamed X) and in the media , most speculated about the collapse of user experience and platform viability. Instead, nothing really changed; vulgar and racist tweets were widespread, explicit usage of the platform proliferated, but if we’re being honest, nothing collapsed in the way we expected. Bluesky, save for a cohort of academics, has not supplanted X as the place where news headlines are disseminated. Slok’s headline read “No Signs of AI Payoff Outside the Tech Sector,” but this fabricated an impact where it was yet to be observed. The tech sector, led by Musk and backed up by the Effective Altruism crew thereafter, slashed employees and found an unfortunate truth about “bullshit jobs” — that millions of prestigious white-collar positions weren’t materially involved in the production process. The apparent productivity gains owed rather less to a revolutionary AI workforce. The Spectacle of an AI Economy As Anthropic comes to market, we might look for insight from Guy Debord’s Society of the Spectacle . A Marxist and prominent figure among the French situationists in the 1960s, Debord saw the circulation of images as a final evolution in the accumulation process. Surplus ultimately finds itself reinvested as signage, representation, and aesthetics as opposed to the productive economy. Leading up to the IPO, Anthropic deployed a yearlong advertising campaign stretching from the Super Bowl to the FIFA World Cup and every major TV event in between. The tagline? “Keep Thinking.” Over a Rolodex of apocalyptic images, the voices of everyday Americans can be heard questioning the future of AI, saying things from “If they end up taking all the jobs, then what does it really mean to work?” to “Could AI help people stop feeling misunderstood?” The images shift from catastrophe to connection, showing mothers holding daughters and whales breaching water. Debord wrote in 1967 that “the spectacle is the nightmare of imprisoned modern society which ultimately expresses nothing more than its desire to sleep. The spectacle is the guardian of sleep.” Essentially, the spectacle exists as a way of whispering to society: keep sleeping. In response, a revolutionary leader like Malcolm X used to warn his audiences that they must wake up and think for themselves — the subtext being that society will dictate what thoughts are permissible, in order to control how they act. The irony today is that capital no longer cares what we think. We are free now, liberated (encouraged even) to think whatever thoughts we’d like. Our aggregated impressions, worries, and conundrums are catalogued and commodified. We can all be communists, socialists, or anti-capitalists at heart; anything, so long as we keep thinking using their models. When we fear that AI means the end of critical thinking, we may be fearing the wrong thing. Claude and ChatGPT are not the end of our capacity to critique or analyze. If anything, the opposite is true: we enter a mind-numbing labyrinth of critical thoughts, questions, and opinions. These are anti-relational machines, not anti-thinking ones. I was on the phone with my dad the other day; how cool is it, he said, that he can put an image of his struggling plants into ChatGPT and get back a protocol for caretaking. I asked, what would you have done before? I’d probably have called my brother, he answered. AI and Big Tech capital integrated the lessons of postmodernist discourse with surprising effectiveness. Back in a 2013 blog post , author and media theorist Nathan Jurgenson called this the “anti-capitalism-capitalism” of Silicon Valley. Thus far, the debates surrounding Anthropic chief Dario Amodei’s obsession with extinction-level AI events have been somewhat surface-deep. Skeptics like Ed Zitron and Steve Eisman of The Big Short believe it’s a smoke screen for regulatory capture by a company with no economic moat around it. Others take it literally. It’s both and it’s neither: it’s the construction of a discursive world, a spectacle, that distracts us from confronting the distributional politics of AI’s advancement. Dual-Zone Economy In 1987, the stock market crashed on Black Monday at the behest of newly automated computer-programmed trading algorithms, which simultaneously sold stocks as they began to fall due to the macro environment. Some of the conditions from back then again seem familiar — what was then a skirmish with Iranian forces is today a full-blown war; again today, the dollar is weakening both nominally and politically, and global energy markets are stressed. Still, after crashing 20 percent, the indexes finished in 1987 slightly higher than they started it. The temperature of the times blew hot and cold. That same year, German automaker BMW introduced “dual-zone automatic climate control” into its premium vehicles. For the first time, the driver could dial the heat up as their passenger turned the air conditioning on. By the turn of the millennium, most models included some iteration of this. Industry commentators at the time dubbed it “the marriage saver.” The same moving car could now hold two divergent conditions. Today Anthropic chief Amodei, fellow AI kingpins like Sam Altman, and newly disgraced former Nvidia chief Jensen Huang are sitting on each other’s laps in the driver’s seat and running it hot, even despite last week’s drawdown. But we’re in the passenger seat, and the data says it’s cold as can be in here. These companies are public or will be public soon; they, more than anything, will decide the fates of our retirement accounts; their collapse would spell a more widespread recession. They expect us to join them for the ride, for the sake of the marriage. And yet the recent US midterms saw forty of forty-one ballots related to data centers reject them by wide margins. Capitalism’s greatest business is, as Karl Marx predicted, birthing its own gravediggers. Deskilled white-collar labor, though highly paid, is learning of its own immateriality. Working-class Americans are saying no to having their communities repurposed for the sake of technological progress. AI’s advancement and its discontents, as a particular technological revolution distinct from others, are setting the stage for a once-in-a-century kind of cross-class, cross-identity, cross-party opposition. In recent years, Pedro Sánchez’s Spanish government, formed by his Socialists (PSOE) and the left-wing alliance Sumar, has been a reference point for the global left. Rarely among social democratic politicians, Sánchez has become well-known for his confrontations with Donald Trump and Benjamin Netanyahu, and for his governing coalition’s progressive social and economic policies. However, Spain’s government could soon fall into the hands of a new alliance of the conservative Partido Popular (PP) and the far-right Vox party. At least, this is what almost all polls now suggest, less than two weeks before the general election on November 29. There are many reasons for the electoral weakness of the PSOE and its further-left allies, now grouped in the Frente Amplio alliance. The broad-left government had already lost its majority in parliament in 2023, forcing it to rely on the votes of regionalist parties. But it has also been hit by its own mistakes as well as a ruthless offensive by traditional Spanish elites. Here are six reasons why the snap elections on November 29 could force Sánchez from office. 1. Complicated Parliamentary Arithmetic After the last general election in 2023, Sánchez only remained prime minister thanks to an eclectic coalition of progressive parties and Catalan, Basque, and Galician nationalists, including conservatives. They were united only by fear of a PP-Vox government that would be very hostile to the cultural and national diversity that defines Spain. Because of the tight electoral results in 2023, the current legislative term has been marked by parliamentary instability and by a no-holds-barred war by the Right against a government that it considered illegitimate from the outset due to its reliance on parties who want independence from Spain. That dependence on conservative formations such as Catalonia’s Junts and, to a lesser extent, the Partido Nacionalista Vasco (PNV) has forced the government to lower its ambition regarding some laws and has cost it important votes in Congress, especially on housing issues. The most serious parliamentary defeat came this October, when PP, Vox, and Junts together rejected two emergency decrees on housing tabled by the government following the eviction of an eighty-seven-year-old woman in Madrid and the subsequent massive demonstrations for the right to housing. Much of the mobilization centered on the government’s inability to curb rents. However, Sánchez also called this snap election hoping that the debate on housing would distract attention from the migration crisis in Ceuta. 2. Stormy Relations With Catalan Conservatives Among the numerous parliamentary groups on which the PSOE-Sumar coalition government depended, the one that has set the highest price for its support has been Junts, a Catalan party which supports pro-business economic policies. This party is led from exile by a former president of the Catalan government, Carles Puigdemont, who until last month feared arrest over his role in organizing the unofficial 2017 independence referendum. Following the 2023 general election, Junts set a price on supporting Sánchez’s investiture as head of government. It said it would only vote to keep him prime minister in exchange for an amnesty law that ended the judicial prosecution of Catalan pro-independence leaders. The approval of the amnesty law in 2024 unleashed a nationalist campaign by the Spanish right, which considers the pro-independence supporters “coup plotters.” This conflict, too, fed Spanish nationalist party Vox’s electoral rise. The PSOE lost the support of numerous voters, and the Right exploited to saturation the idea that Sánchez has no principles and is capable of doing anything to stay in power. The Supreme Court, controlled by a judiciary heavily tilted to the right, has battled to prevent the full application of the law, but it has ended up being applied by order of the Constitutional Court. 3. Lawfare and Corruption The justice system has been one of the main arenas of a fierce opposition to Sánchez. Right-wing ideas are clearly overrepresented in the Spanish judiciary: the two conservative judicial associations gather more than 40 percent of judges, while less than 10 percent belong to the only progressive association (the rest are not part of an association or belong to smaller groupings). To this unbalanced picture is added the growing shamelessness of the most reactionary judges in using the justice system for political ends. This is already shown by the judicial harassment against left-wing party Podemos and, more seriously, against Catalan independence supporters. Various conservative judges have initiated proceedings of dubious justification against Sánchez’s brother and wife, confirming, in the eyes of the right-wing electorate, the idea that the Socialist prime minister is surrounded by corruption. The accusation is especially damaging because Sánchez himself reached the head of government in 2018 thanks to a motion of no confidence after the then-ruling PP was convicted in a serious corruption case. Unfounded accusations against Sánchez’s relatives have been mixed with real cases that have contributed to damaging his popularity: his former number two, ex–Minister of Development José Luis Ábalos, has been sentenced to twenty-four years in prison for taking bribes and awarding public contracts for the purchase of masks during the pandemic, and Santos Cerdán, who was also the PSOE’s organizational secretary, is likewise being investigated for corruption. 4. Insufficient Action on Housing and Inflation The PSOE-Sumar coalition government has managed, despite its parliamentary weakness, to approve important measures such as increases in the minimum wage, the expansion of paternity leave, a boost to pension rates, and a law — albeit an insufficient one — to regulate rental prices. These added to the labor reform that has reduced temporary employment and the creation of a minimum living income to fight poverty, both introduced during the previous PSOE-Podemos government (2020–23). In addition, the macroeconomic data is good: Spain has for years been top of the table for GDP growth among the major European economies, and unemployment is at its lowest level since 2008. However, a large part of the working class has seen its standard of living worsen. The minimum wage has risen almost 75 percent since 2018 (from €858 gross to €1,500 per month), but it has not pulled up wages in general: according to the Juan de Mariana Institute , three out of four wage earners earn under twice the minimum. Meanwhile, inflation reached close to 5 percent this October, and food has become 41.5 percent more expensive since 2018. Even more serious is the escalation of rents, rising 43 percent on average in a decade; in large cities, renting an apartment is equivalent to 100 percent of a young person’s income. The housing situation, which is the main problem for Spaniards according to all polls, translates into more than forty evictions per day, despite the fact that Sánchez promised that this parliamentary term would be “the legislature of housing.” The explosion of the housing movement in recent weeks, which has included the first general strike on this issue in the history of Spain, demonstrates the failure of the coalition government in this key area for the Spanish working class. 5. Immigration and the Rise of Vox The worsening of the material living conditions of much of the middle and working classes has opened up space for Vox’s racist messaging, which has only in recent years managed to sink roots among the lower-income electorate. The sudden entry of more than seventy thousand migrants into Ceuta (Spain’s North African enclave) on July 30–31 seemed to confirm Vox’s rhetoric, which warns of a supposed migrant “invasion.” Santiago Abascal’s Vox has turned the rejection of immigration into its absolute priority, with a discourse that connects it with crime, even though the latter has declined while the number of migrant workers has grown. Its radicalism is akin to that of Germany’s ultranationalist Alternative für Deutschland: in 2025, Abascal incited the racist riots in Torre-Pacheco (Murcia), and a spokeswoman advocated the “remigration” of between seven and eight million people, including Spaniards descended from foreigners. As in other European countries, the conservative PP has contributed to normalizing Vox’s discourse, forming governments with it at the regional level (in Spain’s so-called autonomous communities) and hardening its anti-immigration line. Driven by the reactionary wave that began with Donald Trump’s reelection in 2024, Vox is approaching 20 percent in polls, and no one doubts that it will form a coalition government with Alberto Núñez Feijóo’s PP if the results allow it. 6. A Divided and Weak Left Sánchez could reestablish his coalition government in 2023 thanks to the electoral comeback achieved by his PSOE, but also by Sumar , the formation led by Deputy Premier Yolanda Díaz, which scored 12 percent of the vote. Podemos, which split from Sumar, today has only a small parliamentary representation. For several weeks in October, the alternative left starred in a real soap opera about whether it would form a single candidacy. It was even mooted that such a list would be headed by Gabriel Rufián, of Esquerra Republicana de Catalunya (ERC), the most popular politician to the left of the PSOE in all of Spain, despite his pro-independence stance. Finally, a coalition called Frente Amplio was formed, led by former Barcelona Mayor Ada Colau and including fifteen parties. Podemos refused to join the Frente Amplio despite the great similarity between their programs, and now Podemos is polling under 2 percent support. This division has aggravated the disenchantment of the left-wing electorate. It also reduces the chances of a new broad-left majority, since the electoral system penalizes parties with few votes spread across many provinces, such as Frente Amplio and Podemos. Less than two weeks before the elections, Frente Amplio is aiming for 10 percent of the votes, which would give it between fifteen and twenty seats. But if Colau’s candidacy sinks, even if the PSOE has a decent result, there will hardly be a parliamentary majority willing to keep Sánchez as prime minister. Paradoxically, part of his Socialists’ now-scant hopes lie in the good prospects for Euskal Herria Bildu (in the Basque Country), the ERC (Catalonia), and Adelante Andalucía, three left-wing sovereigntist formations that are growing in the polls and would also vote in favor of Sánchez returning as premier. But Nothing Is Set in Stone To these structural factors was added, a few weeks before the elections, a collision between two trains that caused sixty-nine deaths. This was the second high-speed accident in a year, revealing the deplorable state of the country’s railway infrastructure. The disaster prompted the resignation of Transport Minister Óscar Puente, a Sánchez loyalist. In addition, the accident has weakened one of the PSOE’s main lines of argument: the defense of public services against the “chainsaw” of the Spanish far right, which admires the ultra-free-marketeer economic policies of Argentina’s President Javier Milei. At the time of writing, almost all polls show a considerable majority for the right-wing camp, but they also detect a recent rebound by the PSOE. Both the Socialists and the Frente Amplio want to believe in a scenario like 2023, in which the charismatic Sánchez’s effective campaign and fears of the Right ended up mobilizing the broadly left-wing electorate. However, the government parties would today need to recover much more ground during the campaign than they managed in 2023. A long-enduring right-wing campaign to delegitimize the government, Vox’s strength, and the sustained wear and tear of office means that, for now, the most likely outcome is that Spain loses its luster as a model for the Left. Healthcare workers respond to a broken system by unionizing

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: Tempest ／ 著者: Dennis Kosuth ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-18T22:57:14+00:00

C hicago is seeing a burst of unionization amongst registered nurses – and it has become infectious, in the best sense of that word. In May of 2026, 77 percent of nurses at Rush University Medical Center (RUMC) voted in favor of joining the National Nurses Organizing Committee (NNOC). The 1,700 nurses at RUMC are now part of National Nurses United (NNU), a union that represents 225,000 nurses in healthcare settings across the United States. NNU was founded by a merger of nursing unions in 2009 and, in the past 17 years,has grown considerably from its initial strength of 150,000. The latest victory for NNOC took place in the first week of November when nurses who work for Endeavor Health at Edward Hospital in suburban Naperville, voted by a margin of 65 percent in favor of joining the union. By adding another 1,200 nurses to the Chicagoland area, NNOC now represents over 10,000 members in the region. Nurses at Chicago’s Saint Mary of Nazareth Hospital voted 96 percent in favor of unionizing in June of 2026 – days after going on a one-day strike six days before the vote had even occurred- over the unlawful termination of nurses who were advocating for having a collective voice in their workplace. St. Mary Hospital was acquired by Prime Healthcare in March of 2025, and nurses were motivated to take action following drastic changes they experienced in patient care, following cost-cutting measures taken by Prime to boost their bottom line. Nurses at St. Mary struck again for one day on September 3. On September 15, nurses at Olympia Fields Hospital in suburban Chicago, also owned by Prime Healthcare, went on a one-day strike, also voting with an 82 percent margin to join the union about a week afterwards, adding another 400 nurses to NNOC. In early December, 2,400 nurses at four hospitals in the northern Chicago suburbs owned by Endeavor will have an opportunity to vote to organize with Teamsters Union Local 743. Across the United States, there are almost 4.5 million registered nurses, and about one in five of them are in unions – double the overall unionization rate of workers in this country. There are various factors at play behind these statistics, with the most notable ones being a response to chronic understaffing, post-COVID pandemic exhaustion, and the leverage that comes with the relatively higher job security that nurses have. I am a NNOC member, a nurse who works part-time at Cook County’s Stroger hospital, and recently was in conversation with three nurses from St. Mary Hospital and one nurse from RUMC. They had all led successful campaigns to unionize their hospitals with NNOC in the spring of 2026. The testimony of these nurses is useful for understanding what is pushing nurses to organize themselves, the challenges they are confronting in these campaigns, and what the future holds for healthcare workers fighting for justice. Mary, a nurse who works at RUMC, reported on how her coworkers joined the organizing campaign at St. Mary, on the heels of the RUMC vote to join NNOC. She said, “The most inspiring thing was seeing nurses come together for St. Mary’s. It came at the perfect time for us because everyone was so hyped up and excited and ready to jump into action. Seeing people [at RUMC] commit the time, not just for ourselves, but for other nurses in the community to make all nurses in Chicago, and the country, to make work better for them – was really inspiring.” Working conditions were a major driver of nurse unionization. Richard, a nurse at St. Mary, described what motivated him to organize after Prime took over. “Things just got worse and worse. One of the things that happened is my coworker got his ribs broken and he got a black eye from being beaten really badly by a patient because we weren’t staffed properly. I just wanted to be able to have them give us a proper amount of staff under whatever circumstances we have, rather than just applying to some basic rule of one staff to six patients.” Patient safety was a big concern for Jesús, also a nurse at St. Mary, as the employer didn’t want to have “sitters,” someone to watch patients to prevent falls. His unit has “a lot of geriatric dementia patients, and Prime wanted them to be without a sitter, and they were pushing us to admit these patients without one. You can’t really say that patients need a sitter right away, but you’ve seen enough falls and enough adverse complications from falls to know who needs one. They wouldn’t listen to us because the care that patients need, they’re not going to get it, because Prime is trying to squeeze as much juice out of a lemon.” When Mary from RUSH worked as an agency nurse at a unionized hospital, it gave her a glimpse into what conditions might be like. “Nurses wanted to stay and make the hospital a better place to work as opposed to, ‘I’m going to quit and go wherever I feel respected,’ because they do feel respected and appreciated there. Their compensation was on par with other hospitals in the area, and the culture was just more collaborative. Keeping experienced nurses at the bedside was only going to do good things for patients.” Prime Healthcare is a privately held for-profit system, which generates billions of dollars in revenue from its systems across the country, and its assets have only increased since acquiring six more hospitals in the Chicago area. Jesús, who was fired by Prime for advocating for unionization, is directly experiencing their wrath and the wealth they have at their disposal to resist the union. “They have that money. It’s nothing to them because they make billions of dollars off the backs of the patients as well as us.” He continued by saying, “They’re just like, ‘you don’t need any hallway monitors,’ because it just saves them money. They just think, ‘you can take these two patients,’ and they don’t have to pay two nurses. They don’t think about the safety and the potential complications of adverse effects.” Brenda, a nurse at St. Mary, described what provoked nurses to take action. “We decided to go on strike because Prime kept retaliating against our colleagues. They had said that they were going to stop. The day that we received the news of an election date, Prime called two more nurses and left a voicemail that they were terminated because they were, according to Prime, ‘trespassing.’ This was completely false because that’s when we were leafleting, and we had a federal law protecting the right to do that. That’s what made us say, ‘you know what? This is enough.” Richard added, “Our strike was before we had a union, which is unheard of. When our coworkers got fired, a lot of people became very angry. Someone asked me: what was the secret of how you unionized? What was the secret to getting support? The secret to getting support was our company and their war on nurses. A lot of the people became very upset. We won by 96 percent because the company made a lot of nurses very angry.” Even at a so-called non-profit hospital like RUMC, whose policies and practices are nowhere near as nakedly offensive as Prime’s, nurses still overwhelmingly supported unionization. Mary describes how her coworkers saw organizing a union as “having power, having a seat at the table. Having a say in what’s happening to us, what’s happening to our patients. Having a say in our compensation, in our benefits, in our healthcare, in our tuition reimbursement, because that could change whenever Rush wants it. Those benefits could go away at any minute, the only way we can actually secure those is in a contract.” The underlying factor of who ultimately controls the workplace surfaced in both for-profit and non-profit systems. During the unionization campaign, RUMC countered that management had open channels of communication with nurses. Mary described what it looked like: “The hospital’s stance was, ‘We have this great shared governance model. Nurses have so much say. Here are some of the things we’ve taken into account. Here are some of the changes we’ve made.” There was a big push for doing Zoom calls to answer questions, but those questions were very filtered, and we lost our opportunity to talk. Then we lost our cameras. People saw how much Rush was restricting open dialogue, and they were like, “okay.” Jesús described organizing for justice at St. Mary in this way. “It’s a very common issue, and it doesn’t matter what you do, if you’re an Amazon worker or you work in a kitchen, you work. There’s always that power struggle between people trying to make money and you trying to do your job. It’s about the working class coming together.” Brenda reported how workers across different units in the hospital joined together. “It was inspiring how we started to connect unit to unit. Before, ICU [intensive care unit] and emergency room, they are different worlds. In the ICU we’re like, ‘ER is just sending all their patients out to us.’ After getting to know everyone, talking to everyone during the meetings, as well as psychiatry, medical-surgical units… it’s like little towns, right? Getting everyone connected and on the same page, that was one of the best things that we saw.” Jesús went on to describe the importance of links between healthcare workers and their community and how unionization will help stabilize care for patients. “St. Mary has been here a long time. I’ve met people who were like, ‘I was born in this hospital,’ and they have no other place to go, they’re part of the community. Some of them don’t have the pleasure of going to Northwestern or Rush, these fancy hospitals. The community deserves great nurses, but we keep losing nurses to Rush, Northwestern, UIC, and all these other places because St Mary’s cannot compete with them.” Brenda, also from St. Mary, added to this. “With the union, I know that we’re going to get support to fix our staffing grids, and hopefully to retain nurses. Nurses don’t want to do bedside anymore because of the working conditions. During COVID, everyone was a hero, right? All the nurses were heroes. Once that attention went away, they started cutting down. A lot of the things changed about two years ago when Prime took over, and healthcare is now for profit instead of to help our communities.” She further asserted that “healthcare should never bring profit for any company, any government, anything like that. It shouldn’t be a means of profit. In the end, unionization affects our community and affects our patients. With the strike and how much support we had from the community, it was because we’ve taken care of them. We’ve taken care of their kids, we’ve taken care of their families, and themselves. The community knows, and that’s why they were so appreciative and supportive of our movement.” Jesús was one of the multiple nurses that Prime Healthcare had fired during the campaign. Despite this, he shared, “I would still do the exact same thing because regardless of everything that happened, you get further together than just by yourself. We have to organize regardless of where you are, and what you do. At the end of the day you have to work together to make your workplace better. You and your coworkers have a voice which is something that the bosses don’t want you to have.” As was mentioned above, while the 20 percent overall unionization rate of nurses is impressive compared to the average workforce, there is still a lot of room to grow a labor movement of healthcare workers. The overall chronic terrible state of healthcare in this country means there is an ongoing potential to organize patients alongside unions to win significant reforms. A number of newly elected politicians won with a promise of winning healthcare for all. When the Democratic Party majority in Congress gets seated in January 2027, accompanying this event will be severe cuts to Medicaid as a result of Trump’s 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act. These changes will slash about 1 trillion dollars from health services over the next 10 years. Resisting austerity and fighting for the healthcare we deserve will take significant struggle and solidarity between workers and patients – hopefully, the recent victories of nurses in Chicago will help set the stage for successful fights. On Sunday, as Southeast Asian leaders gathered in Manila for the 49th ASEAN Summit, the Philippine police carried out what activists call the largest single-day crackdown in decades of Ferdinand Marcos Jr.’s presidency. At Sucat People’s Park in Muntinlupa, a designated freedom park, police took more than 300 people into custody. Eight more were seized at Liwasang Bonifacio in Manila as they tried to march towards the summit venue. Television footage showed police brutally arresting and detaining them. The National Union of Journalists of the Philippines (NUJP) says at least 22 media workers were harassed who were covering the protests. Who were they ? Farmers, students, indigenous people and workers. What united them was a demand that the guest of honor of the ASEAN Conference would rather not hear: Scrap Pax Silica. President Donald Trump lands in Manila on Tuesday, November 17, 2026. It is his first visit since 2017, and nearly 20,000 police officers have been deployed across the capital to receive him and other ASEAN leaders. He is expected to pressure Southeast Asian governments to join Pax Silica. To understand why so many Filipinos are willing to be arrested to stop it, we must go back to 1991. That year, the Philippine Senate did something few small nations dare to do. It voted to end the Treaty of Friendship, Cooperation and Security that allowed the United States to keep military bases on Philippine soil. That same year, Mount Pinatubo erupted and buried Clark Air Base under ash. By the end of 1992, the last American warships had sailed out of Subic Bay. For a generation, Filipinos told this story with pride. The bases were gone. Thirty-five years later, the Americans are returning to Clark. This time they are bringing data centers. Pax Silica’s first physical project is approximately around 4000 acres AI industrial hub at Luzon Economic Corridor, on part of the old Clark air base. Under pressure from the streets, the US ambassador to the Philippines Lee Lipton, now says no announcement will be made at the summit, and Manila hopes to sign before the year ends. The deal has been delayed. Sunday’s arrests show how far the government will go to keep it alive. A peace written in Washington The US State Department launched Pax Silica in December 2025. It brings together the countries that matter in the “silicon stack”: the mines, power plants, chip factories, computing centers and AI models on which the digital economy runs. About two dozen governments have signed, from Japan and South Korea to India, Israel, and Kazakhstan. The name is no accident. The Pax Romana was the long “Roman peace” that began in 27 BCE, imposed on the Mediterranean world by Rome’s legions and paid for by tribute from conquered provinces. The Pax Britannica was the century after 1815, when the Royal Navy ruled the seas and Britain’s empire stretched from India to Africa. The Pax Americana is the order the United States built after 1945, resting on the dollar and on hundreds of military bases around the world. Those at the center called each of them peace. For those at the edges, they meant conquest, plunder and wars fought far from the imperial capital. Pax Silica openly claims a place in this line. The man who drew up this one is Jacob Helberg, the State Department’s Undersecretary for Economic Affairs. Before taking office, he was a senior adviser to the chief executive of Palantir, the company that builds surveillance and targeting software for armies and spy agencies. Pax Silica was born where Silicon Valley meets the security state. The official story is that Pax Silica protects supply chains from China as evident with the term “coercive dependencies.” This argument gained more strength with the Reuters report revealing a draft State Department letter to 35 countries warning them against “duplicative initiatives whose expectations conflict with our own.” The terms of loyalty are strict. The immediate target is China’s new World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization (WAICO). But the message reaches every partner. Your choices are no longer entirely your own. This is the old politics of the client state, rewritten for the age of AI. That is part of the truth, but only part. Its deeper purpose is to decide who controls the technologies of the coming century, and on whose terms. Inside the bloc, the roles are already fixed. Washington and its tech giants keep the most advanced AI models, write the standards and hold the off switch. Partners provide the land, the water, the minerals and the labor. Critics warn this creates a trap of “ trusted interdependence .” The bases come back Return now to Clark and look at the map around it. The Port of Subic Bay, once one of the largest US naval bases overseas, has been named the official maritime gateway for Pax Silica. Further north lie the military sites that US forces use under the Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement . There are now nine of them. A US Typhon mid-range missile system has been stationed on Luzon since April 2024, and during this year’s Balikatan war games US anti-ship missile launchers were set up at one of these sites. Philippine officials insist that the AI hub is purely commercial . But commerce and war are hard to separate when the goods are chips and critical minerals. Rare earths guide missiles. Semiconductors fly drones and run radars. Manila’s new rules on critical minerals could open about nine million hectares to mining exploration. This is why the people’s alliance BAYAN calls Pax Silica war production disguised as development. China is the obvious rival in this design, and Washington makes no secret of it. Yet the logic of empire runs wider than any single rival. The United States wants Asia’s land, labor, minerals and loyalty organized around its own arms industry and tech monopolies, whoever the enemy of the day may be. The membership list reflects that. Israel, a founding signatory, has used AI targeting systems in its war on Gaza. Kazakhstan holds vast mineral wealth on China’s western border. Singapore guards the Strait of Malacca. These are not simply markets. They are chokepoints, mines and frontlines. Who pays the price The price is being paid first in Tarlac. As reported, in the village of Sapang Kawayan, about 200 Aeta families have received letters from the state developer telling them they must relocate. The Aeta are the Indigenous people of the Pinatubo foothills, and many were driven from their homes once before by the eruption of 1991. “They should just have us killed because we will fight for our land,” said Casamira Capiz Maniego, who is 72. The Bases Conversion and Development Authority (BCDA) insists that only 40 households are affected and that no one “will be displaced.” The land is only the beginning. According to Mongabay , the hub would need about 3 gigawatts of electricity and between 60 and 90 million liters of water every day. Filipinos already pay the highest household electricity rates in Southeast Asia. Farmers nearby fear for their water in the dry season, and environmental groups say the authorities have produced no hydrological studies . Opposition now reaches far beyond the left. Caritas Philippines, the social action arm of the Catholic Church, opposes the hub in its present form. Bishop Gerardo Alminaza says the project is being rushed, with too little consultation with the Aeta. The mayor of Capas, the town that would host the hub, says local officials were never consulted. In September, police arrested five paralegals and detained nearly 30 people outside a hotel hosting a Pax Silica forum. Sunday’s mass arrests are the same pattern on a larger scale. An empire that needs cheap land and quiet communities abroad also needs governments willing to silence their own people. While the land, the water and the labor come from the South, the profits, the patents and the power stay in the North. None of this means the Global South should turn its back on technology. Developing countries need chips and AI. But they need them on their own terms, through technology transfer, public investment and cooperation among themselves, and with the consent of the communities whose land and water are at stake. Pax Silica offers none of this. Southeast Asia knows that refusal is possible. Of the eleven ASEAN members, only Singapore and the Philippines have joined . In 1991, Filipinos showed the world that foreign bases could be sent home. The farmers, students and Aeta who filled the jails of Metro Manila on Sunday are carrying that memory forward. They are not against technology. They are against being made the raw material of someone else’s empire. Atul Chandra is the Asia co-coordinator of Tricontinental: Institute for Social Research . ‘The cry of the earth and the cry of the poor rise up together,’ says Pope Leo XIV

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Pablo Meriguet ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-18T05:35:54+00:00

Pope Leo XIV just concluded his extended visit to Latin America. Amid crowds and speeches that interwove theological discourse with suggestive political comments, the pontiff traveled through Uruguay, Argentina, and Peru. An anti-neoliberal Christianity It was likely his speeches in Argentina that had the greatest repercussions in the media, precisely because it is the country governed by the ultra-orthodox neoliberal Javier Milei. “The human being is the author, the center, and the end of all economic and social life; therefore, the economy must be at the service of the person, and not the person at the service of the economy,” said the first North American pope. He also spoke of the importance (from a Christian perspective closer to that of his predecessor Francis) of work as an essential dimension of the human condition, which stands in contrast to the neoliberal doctrine that has become so prevalent thanks to far-right governments in the region: “Depriving a person of work is not simply denying them an income; it is a serious blow to their dignity, nullifies their ability to contribute to society, and plunges them into social exclusion.” However, little has been said about the significance of his words in Peru, the country he chose to tour extensively. The choice was not accidental. The pope worked for several decades in the Andean nation, where he pursued an ecclesiastical career more aligned with the Church’s “option for the poor” than with the elitist, right-leaning thinking that has been so prevalent in recent years among the upper echelons of the Peruvian Curia. Justice for the crimes of the past In Peru, Leo XIV said , “The events of the past deserve to be treated with justice,” referring to the human rights crimes that occurred in Peru during the armed conflict that hit the population hard – especially the poorest in rural areas, who witnessed human rights violations committed in the midst of a civil war. The Pontiff added: “The Church is aware of the suffering caused by violence and wishes to stand in solidarity with those who endure it, encouraging the efforts of all who work to protect the life, dignity, and rights of individuals through law and justice.” In support of Indigenous peoples He also referred to the violence that many Indigenous leaders must endure in a society like Peru’s, which has historically been built based on unequal economic development based on ethnic origin: “In recent years, many Indigenous leaders have been murdered for having protected their own territories. The blood of these brothers cries out to God and demands justice from mankind.” He added: “The Church’s social doctrine is an ever-evolving instrument that helps us read the signs of the times under the guidance of the Holy Spirit. Through it, the ancestral knowledge you possess can come together with the Church’s heritage of wisdom, to protect your rights, acquired over generations.” In this regard, he highlighted some of the criticisms raised by Indigenous communities who have managed to resist the ruthless exploitation of their territories by the market: “The cry of the earth and the cry of the poor rise up together, unmasking the logic of the market and exploitation that threaten the very balance of creation … These are not mere accidents, but the bitter fruits of arbitrary exploitation of natural resources that undermines the very existence of the people.” “It is impossible to separate the stewardship of the Amazon rainforest from concern for the peoples who inhabit it; integral ecology, social justice, and spiritual life go hand in hand… These are not mere accidents, but the bitter fruits of an arbitrary exploitation of natural resources that undermines the very existence of the population,” said Leo XIV in Pucallpa on November 15. Against the inequality that creates misery Finally, the Pope emphasized the contradiction inherent in Christian doctrine (which supposedly aspires to universal humanity) when it comes to material injustices: “It is unacceptable that, in many communities, the right to access health care and education is denied due to physical distances and the neglect of institutions … We feel in our hearts and souls the vulnerable situation in which so many women, girls, and adolescents find themselves, as well as the excruciating pain of those who suffer violence and sexual abuse, which cause deep wounds, often amid the silence of complicity and impunity.” These statements may seem futile to someone unfamiliar with the reality in Peru. They might well appear to be empty words in the face of a predatory economic system that does not stop even at faith. But the truth is that in Peru (one of the countries with such a significant and numerous Catholic majority) the Pope’s words may have an effect that few could foresee. Will the Pope’s visit have any impact on Peruvian politics? Peru has not strayed from the neoliberal path since the 1990s, when dictator Alberto Fujimori (father of current President Keiko Fujimori) rapidly introduced sweeping economic policy reforms known as the “Fujishock.” These statements, however, could help strengthen certain voices that, like the Pope (without advocating a revolutionary discourse) insist on reforming the Peruvian state to steer it away from the neoliberal path. If the Pope can say these things in Peru, Argentina, or any other country, it is because he has tacit permission to say whatever he wishes. The last president to insult a Pope was Javier Milei himself, when he called Francis – his fellow countryman – “the representative of the devil on earth,” which led him, upon assuming the presidency, to apologize countless times for the public affront. Perhaps this is why neither Keiko Fujimori nor Javier Milei has dared to question the words of a Pope who is clearly challenging a state of affairs that harms the most vulnerable and exalts the power of the most powerful, revealing how these two phenomena reinforce one another. The effects of the Pope’s visit are, among other things, political as well – a fact that Leo XIV will surely reflect on during his flight back to Europe.

Ebola deaths in DRC cross 5,500 as armed attacks and funding shortages impede containment

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Pavan Kulkarni ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-18T05:35:54+00:00

The fastest-spreading Ebola outbreak on record has killed 5,568 people , nearly half of the 11,376 confirmed cases are in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) as of Saturday, November 14. “If we don’t stop this outbreak now, it will be the worst Ebola outbreak that the world will have,” Jean Kaseya, director-general of the continent’s highest health body, the Africa Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), told Reuters on Sunday. The world’s worst on record so far is the West Africa outbreak, infecting 28,600 people and killing more than 11,000 over a period of two years from 2014 to 2016. The current outbreak – caused by the rare Bundibugyo strain of Ebola virus for which there is no approved treatment yet – has already claimed half as many lives in just six months since the outbreak was declared in DRC on May 15. However, later genomic sequencing showed that the virus was already transmitting among communities in Ituri province by mid-February. The health response, which began late – three months after the outbreak – is severely underfunded. ​ Kaseya said international donors have only released $1.3 billion, less than half of the $2.9 billion they have pledged. Much of the funding made available is not translating into frontline capacity.​ Doctors Without Borders (MSF) described the dysfunction on the frontline: “vehicles and ambulances without fuel; surveillance teams that exist on paper but are nowhere to be seen on the ground; community workers without phones or mobile data to share alerts; and health care staff left without the supplies or technical support needed to handle suspected cases.” Unpaid for months, many have been forced to strike, while many others “are left trying to stop the outbreak with empty hands and empty pockets,” said MSF International President, Dr. Javid Abdelmoneim.​ The medical charity added that inadequate surveillance is most concerning, “with entire areas poorly covered or even completely neglected.” Even six months after the outbreak was declared, 80% of the new cases are still emerging outside the contact lists of the confirmed cases, Kaseya said. 65% of the deaths are occurring outside health centers, further increasing the risk of community spread. Armed groups impede health response The disease has spread to eight of DRC’s 26 provinces. All are in the mineral-rich eastern and northeastern region, where about a hundred armed groups operate.​ The most prominent is the M23, which, backed by Rwanda, has occupied several cities in the region, including large swathes of the North Kivu province, which now accounts for half of all the new infections. Lacking water and sterilization capacity, some healthcare facilities in this province have themselves become sources of transmission, the MSF said. In North Kivu’s Beni health zone, nearly three-quarters of all infected patients contracted the virus in a health facility. ​ About 50 km from the city of Beni, Allied Democratic Forces (ADF) – an armed group originating in Uganda and aligned with the Islamic State – attacked Cantine-Aloya locality, killing 33 civilians. “The attack comes at a critical moment in the response to the ongoing Ebola outbreak. Health facilities have been attacked and patients, including hundreds of people with confirmed or suspected Ebola infection, have fled in search of safety, raising serious public health concerns,” Africa CDC said in a statement condemning the attack. Christian Kakule, medical director of the Kelekele Hospital Center in Cantine, told AP , “High-risk contacts and other individuals under monitoring have all fled,” he said. “Our concern is that when probable cases end up out in the open like this, they can become a source of transmission.” “Following up contact tracing in areas where ADF [is] operating is a very hazardous idea,” said UN Senior Ebola Response Coordinator, Julien Harneis, adding that in recent days, the ADF has been killing more people than Ebola.

Monday, November 16, marked another horrific night for Palestinians in the occupied West Bank after illegal Israeli settlers launched simultaneous assaults against civilians in the eastern governorate of Jericho, and the southern governorate of Bethlehem.
