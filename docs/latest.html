From Daryl Gates to Digital Surveillance: Is Los Angeles Really Protecting Immigrants?

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Sharon Kyle ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-30T22:46:40+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. In the 2026 Los Angeles mayoral race, City Councilmember Nithya Raman has accused Mayor Karen Bass of failing to adequately protect immigrant communities from federal immigration enforcement. That is a serious charge. It deserves serious examination. But Raman's criticism has raised another question, one that connects directly to Los Angeles's history of policing. During a mayoral debate, Raman invoked the LAPD's 1979 Special Order 40 as a model of immigrant protection, describing the policy associated with the Daryl Gates era as a “gold standard.” She subsequently clarified that she was praising the policy, not Gates himself. That distinction matters. So does the extraordinary difference between the world of 1979 and the world of 2026. If we are going to evaluate which candidate can best protect immigrant communities, we must look beyond historical comparisons and political accusations. We must examine how government power operates today—and whether our leaders understand the technological capabilities of the institutions they oversee. What Special Order 40 Actually Did Special Order 40 was adopted in 1979, during Mayor Tom Bradley's administration, when Daryl Gates was chief of the LAPD. It prohibited officers from initiating police action solely to determine someone's immigration status. The purpose was important. Immigrant residents needed confidence that reporting crimes or cooperating with police would not automatically expose them to immigration enforcement. But Special Order 40 was not an absolute prohibition on cooperation with federal immigration authorities. Nor did it eliminate the broader problems associated with policing under Gates. As discussed in Part 1 , Gates presided over an era marked by aggressive policing, Operation Hammer, and profound mistrust between the LAPD and Black communities. A policy offering meaningful protections to immigrants could coexist with practices that inflicted serious harm on other populations. That contradiction deserves recognition. But there is another problem with treating Special Order 40 as a contemporary standard. It was developed for a world in which the collection, storage, and exchange of personal information were far more limited. Technology has fundamentally changed what cooperation between government agencies can mean. The Difference Between 1979 and 2026 In 1979, police generally gathered information about someone's identity or movements through direct encounters, investigations, and comparatively limited record systems. Today, government agencies can collect, retain, search, and exchange enormous quantities of information. Automated license plate readers record vehicle movements. Booking fingerprints can enter national databases accessible to multiple agencies. Commercial databases can assemble information about people's addresses, associations, and activities. Information collected for one purpose can become useful for another. An LAPD officer does not necessarily have to question someone about immigration status—or personally contact an ICE agent—for information collected through local policing to become accessible to federal authorities. That does not mean every database connection constitutes an unlawful disclosure. It means effective immigrant protection requires more than restrictions on individual officers' conduct. A city can comply with an older policy while leaving residents exposed to dangers its authors could scarcely have imagined. That is why I find Raman's invocation of Special Order 40 as a contemporary “gold standard” inadequate. The question is whether Los Angeles's protections have kept pace with the institutions they are intended to restrain. The Surveillance Warnings Los Angeles Has Already Received These concerns are not speculative. For years, the ACLU of Southern California and the Stop LAPD Spying Coalition have documented and challenged the LAPD's expanding surveillance capabilities. In litigation reported in 2017, the ACLU and Electronic Frontier Foundation documented that the LAPD and Los Angeles County Sheriff's Department were collecting approximately three million license plate scans weekly and had accumulated roughly half a billion records. Those historical figures reveal the extraordinary scale surveillance had already reached nearly a decade ago. The records concerned ordinary motorists, not simply people suspected of crimes. Such databases can reveal where people travel, worship, seek medical treatment, or participate in political activity. Stop LAPD Spying has examined what it calls the architecture of surveillance : the interconnected technologies, databases, agencies, and institutional relationships through which police gather and use information. Its research has connected data-driven policing to racialized enforcement and the displacement of marginalized communities. In its report Automating Banishment , the coalition examines relationships among policing, surveillance, real estate development, and the regulation of urban space. The coalition has also raised concerns about real-time crime centers, automated license plate readers, and private surveillance vendors. Not every allegation made by advocates has been conclusively established. Nor does every surveillance technology necessarily violate the law. But Los Angeles has received detailed warnings about these capabilities for years. That history creates responsibilities for elected officials. When evaluating Bass, we should ask what her administration has done to investigate these concerns, independently assess surveillance systems, and ensure compliance with city protections. When evaluating Raman, we should ask whether her proposals address the broader surveillance infrastructure or only particular relationships with federal immigration authorities. Both candidates should explain what they would do when a surveillance technology cannot be adequately controlled. Would they restrict it, suspend it, or end the city's use of it? Raman's Criticism of Bass Deserves Scrutiny Raman has accused the Bass administration of allowing LAPD practices that potentially expose immigrants to federal enforcement. She has raised concerns about information sharing and questioned whether the city has done enough to prevent cooperation with ICE. Those concerns deserve examination. But political accountability requires factual precision. Reporting has raised questions about LAPD booking fingerprints entering national systems accessible to immigration authorities. That is troubling, particularly if information collected during routine policing can later facilitate immigration enforcement. But federal access to information does not, by itself, establish that LAPD officials deliberately transmitted it to ICE in violation of city policy. Similarly, concerns about automated license plate readers require examining who controls the information, who can access it, how long it is retained, and whether vendors or other agencies can disclose it. The LAPD has said it found no evidence that its license plate reader information was shared with immigration enforcement agencies. That assertion deserves independent verification. It should neither be accepted uncritically nor disregarded without contrary evidence. Raman is entitled to challenge Bass. Holding an incumbent mayor accountable for the police department is precisely what a challenger should do. But which practices does Raman believe violate sanctuary protections? What evidence supports those allegations? What corrective measures would she implement? Bass must answer equally specific questions. Has her administration independently audited information-sharing systems? Has it examined vulnerabilities created by technology vendors? How does it verify compliance? What consequences follow violations? These questions matter more than competing claims about historical commitment. What Los Angeles Has Actually Done Los Angeles has not been standing still. In 2023, Councilmembers Raman, Eunisses Hernandez, and Hugo Soto-Martínez introduced a motion directing the city to develop stronger sanctuary protections. Following Donald Trump's reelection in November 2024, Bass worked with City Attorney Hydee Feldstein Soto to accelerate the legislation. The City Council adopted the ordinance, and Bass signed it into law in December 2024. The ordinance restricted the use of city personnel, property, resources, and information to support federal immigration enforcement, subject to legal exceptions. In July 2025, Bass issued Executive Directive No. 12, instructing city departments to reinforce those protections. Both Raman and Bass deserve recognition for their contributions. Raman helped initiate the legislative effort. Bass helped accelerate its completion, signed the ordinance, and subsequently directed its implementation. But adopting protections and ensuring their effectiveness are different responsibilities. The mayor oversees city departments, appoints leadership, and influences enforcement priorities. The City Council also exercises legislative, budgetary, and oversight authority. Neither candidate can reasonably claim that immigrant protection is solely the other's responsibility. And neither should be judged solely by legislation supported or directives issued. The question is whether those measures work. A sanctuary designation cannot substitute for effective administration, meaningful oversight, and adequate resources. The Foothold: When Surveillance Expands Beyond Its Original Targets This is where my Foothold Theory becomes relevant. The theory begins with a proposition: Those seeking to accumulate power often find their easiest opportunities among populations least able to resist. They are the low-hanging fruit. Immigrants facing detention or deportation may have limited ability to challenge government practices. Public officials can justify extraordinary measures by portraying targeted populations as dangerous, undeserving, or outside the circle of people whose rights merit protection. When the public accepts those justifications, those exercising power may gain more than an immediate advantage. They gain a foothold. In surveillance, that foothold may take the form of databases, expanded monitoring capabilities, information-sharing arrangements, or weakened expectations of privacy. Once established, those systems need not remain confined to the populations originally used to justify them. A system capable of tracking an immigrant's movements can track a citizen's movements. A database revealing where one vulnerable population lives, works, or travels can reveal the same information about others. And a surveillance capability created under one administration can be inherited by another with different priorities and fewer restraints. This does not mean every official supporting surveillance intends to abuse it. But it would be dangerous to assume that the first population subjected to an intrusive government practice will necessarily be the last. That is the difference between examining surveillance as a collection of technologies and examining it as an accumulation of institutional power. What the Miner's Canary Is Telling Us In Part 2, I described the disappearance of Black Los Angeles through the metaphor of the Miner's Canary. When the canary began to suffer, miners knew the air had become dangerous. The warning was not simply about the bird. It was about conditions threatening everyone. Immigrant communities may now be revealing another danger. When government can monitor movements, assemble personal information, and connect databases across agencies, those capabilities create risks extending beyond immigration enforcement. The Miner's Canary identifies the warning. My Foothold Theory asks who is accumulating power, how they are doing it, and what prevents them from expanding its use. Together, these ideas explain why defending vulnerable populations is not merely an act of compassion. It is also an act of collective self-preservation. Martin Luther King Jr. warned that “injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere.” I understand that warning not only as a moral principle but as a practical observation about power. Those willing to exploit one population's vulnerability have not necessarily demonstrated that they will respect the rights of others when circumstances change. Why should we assume they will stop? What a Mayor Must Understand The debate over immigration enforcement returns us to the central question running through this series. What understanding should we require of someone seeking to serve as mayor of Los Angeles? The mayor cannot control federal immigration law or prevent every federal enforcement action. But the mayor can direct city departments, appoint leadership, propose budgets, oversee compliance, and work with the City Council to strengthen safeguards. Those responsibilities require more than good intentions. They require understanding how modern institutions accumulate information, how technology expands their capabilities, and how authority granted for one purpose can be used for another. Raman is right to demand accountability from Bass. But invoking a policy adopted in 1979 does not establish what protections are adequate in 2026. Bass is right to point to the city's sanctuary commitments. But issuing directives does not establish that those protections are effective in practice. Both candidates must explain how they would identify and close gaps between policy and enforcement. Both must demonstrate an understanding of the technological realities confronting immigrant communities. And both must recognize that inadequate safeguards threaten more than those currently targeted by immigration enforcement. The measure of leadership is not merely whether a mayor supports immigrant rights in principle. It is whether the mayor understands the powers threatening those rights and can use the authority of the office to restrain them. The Danger of Failing to Recognize Power Los Angeles's history offers an important lesson. Government power can expand through policies, practices, and technologies that initially appear limited in purpose. The most vulnerable may experience the consequences first. But they are not necessarily the last. This is why historical ignorance is dangerous in political leadership. A mayor who does not understand how institutions acquire and exercise power may fail to recognize new threats until they are deeply embedded. A mayor who disregards vulnerable communities may overlook the warnings those communities provide. And a public that assumes those warnings concern only immigrants, Black residents, or other marginalized populations may discover too late that the protections it neglected were protections everyone needed. The question confronting Los Angeles is not whether Special Order 40 was progressive in 1979. It was. The question is whether our leaders understand what it takes to protect people in 2026. If Los Angeles cannot prevent powerful institutions from using modern surveillance systems against its most vulnerable residents, what assurance do the rest of us have that those systems will not eventually be used against us? That is the question both candidates should be required to answer. Part 3 of the four-part series “Who Gets to Lead Los Angeles? Where Did Black Los Angeles Go? The Disappearance of a Community That Built the City

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Sharon Kyle ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-30T20:56:32+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. Part Two of Who Gets to Lead Los Angeles? Los Angeles celebrates its diversity. Its leaders routinely invoke the contributions of the many communities that have shaped the city. But something extraordinary has happened to one of those communities. Black Los Angeles has been disappearing. In 1980, approximately 496,000 Black people lived in Los Angeles, representing nearly 17 percent of its population. By 2020, that number had fallen to approximately 367,000, or about 9 percent. The city lost more than one-quarter of its Black population even as its overall population grew. Those numbers represent families leaving neighborhoods, businesses losing customers, churches losing congregants, and communities losing political influence. But the disappearance of Black Los Angeles is more than a demographic story. I believe it was an early warning of the economic insecurity now threatening millions of Americans. And the warning concerns not only what happened to Black Angelenos, but also what their experience reveals about how power and wealth accumulate. A Community That Helped Build Los Angeles People of African ancestry were among the city's original settlers in 1781. Of the 44 settlers who established the Spanish pueblo on Indigenous land, 26 had some African ancestry. Generations later, Black migrants from the South helped transform Los Angeles into an industrial powerhouse. They worked in aircraft manufacturing, defense production, transportation, and other industries while confronting discrimination in employment and housing. Black entrepreneurs established businesses, newspapers, churches, and cultural institutions. Central Avenue became a nationally significant center of Black music and commerce. South Los Angeles and Crenshaw became centers of political organizing and community life. Black leaders, including Mayor Tom Bradley, helped reshape city government. Black Angelenos were among the people who built modern Los Angeles. So why did so many leave? When Economic Security Disappeared For much of the twentieth century, industrial employment offered working-class families a path toward stability. Many jobs provided wages and benefits that made homeownership and a measure of financial security possible. Union contracts established wage scales, seniority protections, grievance procedures, and rules governing promotions and dismissal. Unions were not free of racial discrimination. But where Black workers gained meaningful representation, collective bargaining helped limit employers' ability to make arbitrary decisions. As manufacturing jobs disappeared, that economic foundation weakened. Factories closed, production moved elsewhere, and technological changes reduced demand for labor. Employers and policymakers also embraced arrangements that diminished workers' bargaining power. Black workers, already disadvantaged by discriminatory employment practices and unequal access to accumulated wealth, were especially vulnerable. For families with limited savings, losing a stable job could mean losing the ability to remain in Los Angeles. Meanwhile, housing costs continued rising. Many Black families moved to the Antelope Valley, the Inland Empire, and other communities where homes were more affordable. Some left voluntarily in pursuit of new opportunities. Others faced economic pressures that made remaining increasingly difficult. The distinction matters. But so does the larger pattern: a community that helped build Los Angeles was finding it harder to afford living here. Housing Discrimination Did Not End When It Became Illegal Housing discrimination compounded these pressures. For decades, racially restrictive covenants, discriminatory lending, and exclusionary real estate practices prevented Black families from accessing neighborhoods and building housing wealth available to white families. But it would be misleading to suggest that discrimination ended when fair-housing laws were enacted and only its economic consequences remained. The discrimination itself continued. I know this firsthand. In 1973, when I was 16, I participated in a high school experiment in Lakewood, California, designed to test racial discrimination in apartment rentals. A white female classmate and I posed as recent college graduates seeking apartments. We visited the same four apartment complexes separately, with me going first. At every location, I was told the apartment had already been rented. When my white classmate visited those same properties, all four accepted her application. At one complex, the manager who had told me the apartment was unavailable invited her inside to see the vacant unit. This happened five years after Congress passed the Fair Housing Act. The managers had been polite to me. Their discrimination was concealed beneath ordinary courtesy. My classmates were excited that our experiment had produced compelling evidence. But I was beginning to understand something far more personal. We had documented obstacles I could expect to encounter throughout my life. My white classmate received opportunities denied to me, although our supposed qualifications were identical. She had done nothing to earn that advantage. I had done nothing to deserve the disadvantage. The experience taught me that making discrimination illegal does not eliminate the willingness to discriminate. And when discriminatory conduct goes unchallenged, those responsible can continue benefiting from it. Black Los Angeles Was the Miner's Canary In the book, The Miner's Canary , Lani Guinier and Gerald Torres used the canary once carried into coal mines as a metaphor for racial inequality. When the canary showed signs of distress, miners knew the air was becoming dangerous. The problem was not confined to the bird. It was a warning to everyone in the mine. I believe Black Los Angeles served a similar function. For decades, Black families experienced disappearing industrial jobs, weakening labor protections, persistent housing discrimination, and rising living costs. Many eventually left the city. Today, in 2026, Americans across racial lines are confronting versions of that same insecurity. Stable employment no longer guarantees financial stability. Homeownership is increasingly difficult for younger generations. Housing costs consume household incomes while workers struggle to keep wages aligned with living expenses. The experiences are not identical. Black Angelenos faced particular forms of racial exclusion that cannot be erased by comparisons with broader economic hardship. But the underlying vulnerabilities should look familiar. The warning signs were visible long before economic insecurity became so widespread. Why didn't more Americans recognize them? The Foothold: How Power Gains More Power Martin Luther King Jr. wrote in his Letter from Birmingham Jail that “injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere.” For much of my life, I understood those words primarily as a moral appeal: we should care about injustice even when it happens to someone else. I have come to understand King's warning in a more expanded way. It is also a warning about the nature of those who perpetrate injustice. People willing to exploit others, who demonstrate little regard for the suffering they cause, do not necessarily possess an internal boundary preventing them from extending that exploitation to others. Why should we assume they will stop? That question sits at the forefront of my mind when I think about Donald Trump. In 1973, the same year I discovered racial discrimination in apartment rentals through my high school experiment in California, the U.S. Department of Justice sued Donald Trump, his father Fred Trump, and their real estate company for allegedly discriminating against Black apartment seekers in New York. Federal investigators documented Black applicants being told that apartments were unavailable while white applicants were offered vacancies. The case ended in a 1975 consent decree requiring changes to the company's rental practices, although the Trumps denied wrongdoing and admitted no liability. I find the historical parallel difficult to ignore. More than fifty years later, Donald Trump occupies the most powerful political office in the country. I cannot claim that the housing discrimination allegations of 1973 shed light on every decision he has made since. But I can ask why Americans should disregard that history when evaluating how he exercises power today. For me, this is not simply a question about whether someone harbors racial animus. It is a question about what we should learn when a person or institution is credibly accused of exploiting people with limited power—and what happens when that person subsequently acquires vastly greater power. The people who are easiest to exploit today are not necessarily the only people who will be vulnerable tomorrow. That is precisely why I believe Martin Luther King Jr.'s warning deserves to be understood not only as a moral principle, but as a warning about power itself. That question is at the heart of what I call my Foothold Theory . Related: Foothold Theory: How Authoritarian Power Erodes Rights by Targeting the Least Protected First Those seeking to accumulate power and wealth often find their easiest opportunities among people with the least ability to resist. These populations are the low-hanging fruit. They may be politically marginalized, economically vulnerable, or already regarded with suspicion or contempt. Existing prejudice makes their mistreatment easier to justify. The public may be persuaded that the victims deserve their circumstances, exaggerate their suffering, or pose a threat requiring extraordinary measures. Once those justifications succeed, the exploiters gain a foothold. They establish that protections can be weakened, rights disregarded, and certain people made to bear burdens others would never accept. They also discover what the public will tolerate. The immediate victims suffer, but those responsible may gain something more enduring: wealth, political influence, institutional power, or precedents that enable further exploitation. And there is no reason to assume their ambitions will remain confined to their original targets. Those people were simply the easiest place to begin. This does not mean every injustice is part of a coordinated conspiracy or that every economic decision causing harm reflects malicious intent. But we should judge powerful people and institutions by their conduct, what they gain, and whether they seek to weaken safeguards protecting others. When those entrusted with power demonstrate a willingness to exploit people who cannot effectively resist, we should not mistake the vulnerability of their first victims for a limit on their ambitions. The Danger Does Not Stay Contained Consider housing. For generations, racial exclusion enabled some Americans to accumulate property wealth while denying others similar opportunities. Today's housing affordability crisis has additional causes, including insufficient construction, restrictive land-use policies, and the growing importance of inherited wealth. But the history reveals what happens when powerful interests protect economic advantages while others are denied a basic necessity. Labor offers another example. When workers lose collective bargaining power, employers gain greater discretion over wages, benefits, and working conditions. Black workers were especially vulnerable to discrimination and industrial displacement. But weakened labor protections do not threaten only Black workers. They affect employees across racial lines. The 2007–2008 foreclosure crisis offers an especially stark illustration. Black and Latino borrowers were disproportionately exposed to predatory and high-cost mortgage lending. When the housing market collapsed, those communities suffered devastating losses. But the consequences spread throughout the national economy. The crisis had multiple causes. Nevertheless, the treatment of vulnerable borrowers revealed dangers within a financial system willing to profit from risks others would ultimately bear. The warning was there. Too many people treated it as someone else's problem. Why This Matters to Political Leadership This article is part of a larger examination of political leadership, historical knowledge, and responsibility in Los Angeles. In Part 1, I questioned what it means when someone seeking to become mayor demonstrates an inadequate appreciation of the history of Black Angelenos and their relationship with the LAPD. The issue was never simply remembering names or dates. It was understanding how institutions acquire power, how that power has been exercised, and who suffers the consequences. The disappearance of Black Los Angeles raises the same question in the economic sphere. A mayor cannot single-handedly reverse decades of racial discrimination, restore manufacturing jobs, or eliminate economic inequality. But the mayor of Los Angeles is not powerless. The mayor helps determine the city's budget priorities, appoints department leaders and commissioners, influences housing and development policies, and oversees agencies responsible for enforcing important local protections. Those responsibilities have real consequences. A mayor who understands the history of housing discrimination can strengthen the city's response to discriminatory practices and support policies that expand access to affordable housing. A mayor who appreciates the importance of labor protections can prioritize enforcement of wage laws, protect workers from retaliation, and support stronger standards for employers doing business with the city. And a mayor who recognizes how powerful interests exploit vulnerable populations can scrutinize development decisions, public contracts, and policies that transfer economic benefits to the powerful while imposing costs on those least able to bear them. None of these powers is unlimited. The City Council, state and federal governments, courts, and private economic forces all shape what a mayor can accomplish. But those limitations make sound judgment more important, not less. Historical knowledge is not an ornament of political leadership. It is a tool for recognizing danger. Without it, leaders may mistake exploitation for ordinary economic activity, discrimination for individual misfortune, and the concentration of wealth and power for inevitable progress. The question is not whether a mayor can solve every problem. It is whether the person entrusted with the city's executive authority understands these problems well enough to recognize when government is helping perpetuate them—and has the judgment and determination to use the powers of the office to challenge them. The Warning We Failed to Hear The disappearance of Black Los Angeles is not simply a story about a community losing its foothold in a city it helped build. It is a warning about what happens when economic security erodes, discrimination persists, and those benefiting from unequal arrangements accumulate still more wealth and power. What if we had recognized the struggles of Black communities as warnings about the direction of the entire society? What if we had paid attention not only to those suffering, but also to those benefiting from their vulnerability? The Miner's Canary tells us to pay attention when vulnerable populations begin to suffer. My Foothold Theory tells us to pay attention to those willing to cause or tolerate that suffering—and to the power they gain when society allows them to proceed. One identifies the warning. The other helps explain how the danger expands. Ignorance of how power gains more power, and how wealth concentrates in the hands of those willing to exploit others, endangers us all. The question is no longer simply where Black Los Angeles went. It is whether America will recognize what its disappearance was telling us before still more communities experience the same loss of economic security. And whether the people we entrust with political power understand enough about that history to prevent the pattern from continuing. Minneapolis Refused to Take Trump’s Bait. It Paid Off.

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Eric Blanc ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-30T18:36:03+00:00

All signs point toward a resounding rebuke of Donald Trump in Tuesday’s midterm elections. Democrats have opened up a commanding lead in national polls , Trump’s popularity has sunk to historic lows, and even many of his former supporters are turning against him. There are plenty of reasons for this collapse, from rising prices to Trump’s disastrous war with Iran. But we shouldn’t forget what was arguably the single most important turning point of his presidency: the defeat of his militarized assault on Minneapolis. In January, Trump’s administration set out to terrorize immigrants, crush resistance, and establish the precedent that federal forces could occupy American cities against the wishes of their residents and elected officials. Instead, it encountered a mass movement whose courage and discipline forced Washington into a humiliating retreat. The political fallout was immediate. By late January, a Reuters poll found Trump’s overall approval had fallen to 38 percent, matching its lowest point of his second term to date. His approval on immigration, previously his strongest political asset, had sunk to 39 percent. Minneapolis had helped turn Trump’s signature political strength into a liability. In retrospect, this outcome can seem inevitable. Surely Trump’s attacks were so excessive, his agents so brutal, and the killings of Renée Good and Alex Pretti so shocking that a national backlash was bound to follow? But nothing was predetermined. Indeed, there’s compelling evidence that Trump and his advisers had a plan with a real chance of working: provoke Minnesota into a social explosion, whose grassroots violence and property destruction would serve as a pretext for polarizing the country and escalating Trump’s crackdown on dissent and democracy nationwide. Similar strategies have worked for authoritarian governments in other times and places. In Minneapolis, the trap failed because of the movement’s exceptionally high level of organization and nonviolent discipline. The Provocation It’s clear that ICE, under administration orders, was consciously provoking the people of Minneapolis this past winter. Thousands of heavily armed, poorly trained federal agents flooded the Twin Cities. Masked officers conducted aggressive raids, detained residents, confronted legal observers, and deployed chemical weapons against protesters. On January 7, an Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) agent shot and killed Renée Good, a thirty-seven-year-old mother. And rather than de-escalate, Washington doubled down. On January 13, Trump warned Minnesota that a “DAY OF RECKONING & RETRIBUTION” was coming. Two days later, he threatened to invoke the Insurrection Act unless Minnesota officials stopped the supposed “professional agitators and insurrectionists” confronting ICE. Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem confirmed that military intervention had been discussed. And by January 18, the Pentagon had placed roughly 1,500 active-duty soldiers on prepare-to-deploy orders. The administration was preparing the ground for deploying the military. Nor do we have to guess how some White House officials hoped events would unfold. On January 17, Zeteo reported that two administration officials acknowledged that figures inside the White House would welcome significant violence by Minneapolis protesters. One senior official said they “would not mind it” if such violence provided a pretext for invoking the Insurrection Act. In other words, widespread disorder wouldn’t have been a setback for Trump’s strategy. It could have provided exactly the excuse he needed. The logic was straightforward: Provoke confrontations through extraordinary federal aggression. Wait for enraged protesters and community members to retaliate with widespread violence or property destruction. Then portray the movement as an anarchic threat to public safety, rally Americans around the need for order, and justify an unprecedented expansion of federal military power. Recent revelations make the administration’s methods even more disturbing. As Wired reported this week, federal investigators infiltrated activist organizations and communications networks on an extraordinary scale. Attorney Kevin Riach, representing one of fifteen Minnesota activists facing federal charges, has argued in court that undercover agents participated in organizing groups while seeking to entice participants into criminal activity. The strategy was hardly irrational. Research shows that property destruction, even on a limited scale, can sharply undermine public support for protests. It would not have taken much for Trump to transform a confrontation about federal repression into a national argument about supposedly violent leftists and blue-city disorder. Imagine the aftermath of Good’s killing had Minneapolis descended into sustained rioting, widespread vandalism, and attacks on ICE or local police. How long before the national conversation shifted away from the shooting itself toward whether Trump should restore order and whether movements should oppose property destruction and violence? Why Minneapolis Didn’t Take the Bait Trump’s central miscalculation wasn’t underestimating the anger his agents would generate. It was choosing to stage this provocation in one of the most deeply organized progressive metropolitan areas in the United States. Minnesota has a rich, decades-long tradition of labor, immigrant-rights, and religious organizing. Groups including Unidos MN, ISAIAH, Service Employees International Union (SEIU) Local 26, and the Twin Cities teachers’ unions had spent years developing relationships, training leaders, winning reforms, and building the capacity to act together. As SEIU Local 26 President Greg Nammacher explained , their January resistance would not have succeeded without years of preparation: “We would not have met it if we had not built a capacity beforehand.” These organizations saw clearly that a violent confrontation on Trump’s terms was one the movement was likely to lose. As JaNaé Bates Imari, co–executive director of the faith-based organization ISAIAH, explained in an interview on The Dig : Part of what is happening in Minnesota is a needling by the federal government to try to get us to respond in a particular way. And Minnesotans across the spectrum have said we will not take the bait . . . . No one [is] confused about who the peacekeepers are in the midst of what’s taking place. Crucially, organizers went beyond merely preaching restraint by building the organizational capacity to turn restraint into a collective practice. Unidos developed mass trainings for legal observation of ICE operations, ultimately training approximately thirty thousand people. ISAIAH trained hundreds of clergy, while allied groups trained thousands in nonviolent civil disobedience. Neighborhood Signal chats sprang up. Residents organized to document immigration raids, distribute whistles, support families sheltering at home, and mobilize neighbors. There were genuine pressures toward escalation. Some protesters threw objects at officers, and instances of vandalism occurred. After witnessing federal violence, not everyone agreed about the limits of confrontation. But breaking with unstrategic leftist norms about a “diversity of tactics,” the movement’s leading organizations had developed the relationships and legitimacy needed to establish collective norms about disciplined nonviolent mass action. The Minnesota Star Tribune reported that protesters confronted a suspicious flag-burner and that participants in organizing chats rebuked and expelled people proposing violence. By cultivating shared expectations, training participants, challenging provocations, and giving people effective alternatives, the Minneapolis movement provided opponents of ICE with something more powerful to do than express their fury. On January 23, an estimated 75,000 or more people marched through downtown Minneapolis in subzero weather during the Day of Truth and Freedom strike action. According to organizers, roughly a thousand businesses, overwhelmingly small businesses, participated in the work stoppage. The movement’s militant resistance and discipline helped keep the attention on federal agents’ terrorization of Minneapolis’s undocumented community, as well as the heroism of two innocent Americans who decided to stand up for their neighbors. Renée Good and Alex Pretti were ordinary people who resisted injustice and paid with their lives. But their deaths alone would not necessarily have been enough to defeat the administration’s offensive. Because without an organized movement committed to preventing mass violence and property destruction, their killings could have become entangled in a debate over which side was responsible for the disorder. Instead, Trump lost control of events and the narrative. The Warning From France We don’t have to rely entirely on hypothetical scenarios to see what might have happened otherwise. France’s growing protests this fall against the cost of living and the government have recently provided a troubling counterexample. What began with high school students protesting deteriorating educational conditions in September grew into a broader movement drawing in unions, Yellow Vests, and other groups demanding economic and political change. But on October 17, huge demonstrations across the country were accompanied by extensive violence and destruction. Shops were looted in Paris, public buildings attacked, and Marseille’s city hall vandalized. Remarkably, Le Monde reported that the government had deployed only about sixty thousand police nationwide — tens of thousands fewer than normal — even though intelligence services had warned of a high risk of violence and looting. Before October 17, much of the national discussion had focused on police repression and the legitimacy of the protesters’ grievances. Afterward the government seized the opportunity to recast the confrontation as a battle against disorder. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu attacked the radical-left La France Insoumise, while politicians on the Right demanded harsher policing. The events on October 17 were a gift to the right. Far-right leader Marine Le Pen received the most positive assessments of any major political figure’s response. And an Ipsos survey found that 55 percent of French respondents believed the leftist party La France Insoumise had contributed to the tensions and violence. As historian Luca Provenzano has argued , allowing disorder to spread can itself be a highly effective state strategy. During France’s May 1968 upheaval, for example, authorities shifted from highly visible repression toward a more restrained police posture that created space for student escalation. A subsequent Interior Ministry assessment concluded that protesters’ excesses had helped restore support for order. The Right went on to win a sweeping electoral victory . Whether the French government deliberately allowed the October 2026 violence to unfold is not yet established. But the danger doesn’t depend on proving a grand conspiracy. A state can benefit from disorder whether it consciously encourages it or simply takes advantage when it erupts. That’s precisely the trap Minneapolis avoided. The Fight Ahead Of course, Trumpism is far from defeated. We don’t know exactly how badly MAGA will lose on Tuesday, and even a decisive electoral rebuke won’t automatically stop his political prosecutions, authoritarian ambitions, or continued mass deportations. Trump could still try to manipulate or reject unfavorable election results, whether next week or in 2028. He could again seek to provoke widespread disorder and use the ensuing fear to justify repression. To defeat Trumpism nationwide and to transform our country, we’ll need big, durable, welcoming organizations, new recruits beyond the ranks of existing college-educated activists, trusted leaders, and the ability to launch rapid, disciplined nonviolent action when necessary. The best way to honor Renée Good and Alex Pretti — and to thank the people of Minneapolis for their courage and sacrifice — is to learn from what they achieved. Mass nonviolent resistance doesn’t happen automatically. It has to be organized. And when it is, even the most powerful government on earth can be forced to retreat. Socialists Need Our Own Foreign Policy Agenda

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Max B. Sawicky ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-30T17:35:40+00:00

It is common for mainstream news outlets and political commentators in the United States to describe socialists as “very liberal” or simply “progressive,” or to puzzle over how to draw ideological lines between democratic socialists and others on the broad Left. There are, in fact, serious differences — differences that socialists would do well to sharpen and articulate for the public. At the same time, as the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) grows by leaps and bounds, some commentators prefer to highlight those DSA positions that are furthest from common agreement. I have previously tried to distinguish between socialists and liberals when it comes to domestic economic policy. But what about foreign policy? Here I propose a socialist agenda for this field. Again, one of my objectives is to show how a socialist approach would differ from a liberal one when it comes to US relations with other countries. As socialists work their way into positions of governing power in the United States in the form of urban mobilizations and the growth of the Squad and the DSA-allied bloc in Congress, we could expect the organs of “national security” to be among the last to open up. Historically, they have enjoyed considerable autonomy and have been the least susceptible to democratic accountability. The ease with which presidents of both parties have launched armed hostilities against other countries without the approval of the Congress, rumored to be a constitutional prerogative, has become notorious. Still, with growing political power will come opportunities for the Left to play a serious role in foreign policy. Understanding US Foreign Policy Since World War II There was a moment in the 1970s when the fabled Church Committee exposed an endless series of dirty deeds committed by the Central Intelligence Agency, the Federal Bureau of Investigation, and others. That exposé was in 1975–76, and Senator Frank Church hailed from Idaho — these days as far right as a state can get. Going back further, there was a moment in 1956 when a Republican president, Dwight D. Eisenhower, blocked US support for British and French imperialist machinations against Egypt after it nationalized the Suez Canal, which was surely Egypt’s right. Other than these anomalies, however, US-led Western imperialism has enjoyed free rein since World War II. The basic dilemma for the Left is that the good deeds we might hope for our government to perform with the aid of its military would be the responsibility of a state that gives no sign of interest in beneficent acts. A libertarian friend impressed upon me the inevitability that any intervention by the (capitalist) state would be poisoned by craven, ulterior motives. My thesis is that the overriding ulterior motive in US foreign policy since World War II has been world hegemony, enshrined in the infamous George Kennan “ X Article ,” which has superseded narrower economic concerns. It is this to which the quasi-autonomous institutions of US national security have been dedicated, hastening to fill the gaps left by the dissolution of the British and French empires. Liberalism is compromised by sympathy for the hegemonic imperative. One notorious example was given by President Bill Clinton’s secretary of state, Madeleine Albright, who referred to the United States as “the indispensable nation.” In other words, hegemony was essential. Another Democratic foreign policy maven and former human rights activist Samantha Power played the genocide card in her celebrated book, A Problem from Hell , to argue for a more interventionist US foreign policy. But when hell settled over Gaza and Ms Power had a chance to say something, her response to the carnage in 2024 was to send tents . The Biden administration fiddled while Gaza was being blown to smithereens. It also dithered on the revival of Barack Obama’s deal with Iran, which likely made it easier for Donald Trump to initiate hostilities with the country when he returned to office. Of course, under the George W. Bush administration, we had the flaming lies about “weapons of mass destruction” (WMDs) in Iraq, now being replayed in absurdist form by President Trump with regard to Iran. Meanwhile, the US withdrawal from Iraq has left that field open to our supposed enemy, Iran. Nobody said foreign policy had to make sense. Socialists and Interventionism The narrower, default stance on the Left has been blanket opposition to military intervention by the United States or its allies. The posture of liberal pro-interventionists is to exaggerate the horrors of a failure to intervene. These justifications have usually been spurious. Historically, every assault by a great power is justified by claims, usually fallacious, of a prior injury or of an overriding humanitarian purpose. Complicating the picture, there are ways a powerful nation can commit aggression against a weaker one without resorting to military means. One has been the predatory use of indebtedness, about which much has been written . Another is the imposition of sanctions on the movement of capital and trade goods. The current weapon of choice is the tariff, not to mention the actual, lethal use of force by the United States and Israel currently on display in the Middle East. The effectiveness of sanctions and tariffs is open to question. And now the Trump administration is discovering in Iran that even its military might cannot always wrest concessions from a recalcitrant government. Socialists, given the opportunity, would at the very least place a high bar before any proposals for US military intervention. We must reject the default to liberal excuses that skirt the underlying motivations for aggression. We saw many of these in the debates about Iraq: The Bush administration would not competently prosecute the war. The search for WMDs should have been allowed to continue. We failed to gain the cooperation of allies. Bush failed to get the proper approval from Congress. Now the critique of choice is that “President Trump has no plan”; we also see efforts to use the spike in gas prices to score political points. We should be wary of putative anti-Trump voices with thin critiques of the Iran attacks. The issue is not the lack of an end game, Israeli connivance in the war, the shifting excuses for objectives, or the carnival of MAGA national security leaders. And Trump does in fact have a plan: it is to flex, blow things up, kill people, and pose as the second coming of Douglas MacArthur. It may seem to boil down to the president’s deranged personality and feeble intellect, but the fact is that the US foreign policy establishment, guiding both parties, has had Iran in its sights for decades, ever since the rise of Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh in the 1950s and later the fall of the US puppet regime led by the shah. War-skeptical liberals hoping for criticism of Trump’s Iran adventure from less militaristic US allies have been disappointed by the repetition of the bankrupt mantra that Iran must be blocked from possession of nuclear weapons. The allies have also been weak in their criticism of the machinations of Trump and the Israeli state in the Middle East, much like centrist or corporate Democrats in the United States. Wars for Oil? One theory for US aggression that is bandied around is the notion that the international reserve currency status of the dollar would be endangered by oil exporters such as Iraq or Iran. But world trade is already conducted in a variety of currencies, not just the dollar, and this will increasingly be the case as the rest of the world — particularly the People’s Republic of China — continues to grow economically compared to the United States. Nations with large stocks of dollars also face the dilemma that if the dollar declines in value, which would happen if they tried to reduce their holdings in favor of another currency, their own wealth would take a hit. Reductive economic explanations have plagued left-wing foreign policy debates more broadly for a long time. Back in the day, it was suggested that the Vietnam War was about the quest for control of rubber exports from Southeast Asia. In Kosovo, there was talk of vast mineral resources for which Western powers lusted. With Afghanistan, it was said that a pipeline was really what was at issue. Now everything is supposed to be about oil. This may be so for Trump himself, but we know he is ignorant. The relevance of oil can be exaggerated. First, the world is weaning itself off fossil fuels, since wind, solar, and even nuclear can be more economical and secure. Second, the United States itself is a net exporter of fossil fuel. Third, much oil extraction sits uneasily on a knife-edge of profitability: a limited shift in its world price puts the higher-cost extraction operations out of business, which naturally limits investment. And fourth, investment in new extraction takes time and is prey to large, up-front, fixed costs that become sunk costs. Investment in oil and gas extraction is discouraged by the obstacles and uncertainties noted above; it can’t be turned on and off like a spigot. This classic economic explanation for US aggression has been given new life by Trump’s nakedly acquisitive Venezuelan venture. We should not doubt that commercial opportunities existed in all of the targets of US interventions, including opportunities for fossil fuel exploitation. But the politics of hegemony, behind which stand the broad interests of capital, looms above them all. This basic analytical framework distinguishes socialists from liberals. Today Donald Trump seems to have reduced the overarching, historic priority of Western imperialist hegemony, strongly founded in economic factors, to his personal dominance, wealth, and unrestrained freedom of action. We still lack a comprehensive story that explains the current regime, other than loose talk about nutty billionaires who want to go to Mars. Perhaps their influence, along with that of crypto bros, Gulf monarchs, and Israel’s rampages, exercise more leverage on Trump and US foreign policy than ordinarily understood. Exception to Anti-Interventionism? We could imagine exceptions to default anti-interventionism. The big one now is Ukraine. Nobody proposes to send North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) troops up against Russians in Ukraine, but other types of assistance have been supported. I won’t try to resolve the Ukraine aid debate here, except to claim that there is a legitimate anti-Russian-imperialist rationale for aid. Socialists will probably remain divided on this question. There is a thin line between sending Ukraine missiles and sending US soldiers to fight alongside Ukrainians. (I don’t doubt the latter is also in progress, sub rosa, in the form of quasi-official trainers and operators.) The broader issue is the fate of Europe, threatened on one side by the Russian Federation and on the other by Trump’s disdain for the NATO alliance. Europe is the chief redoubt of social democracy in the world, with all its faults, in sharp contrast to neofascist trends in Russia, India, Turkey, Hungary, Israel, the Philippines, and the United States itself. The fixation of the Left on NATO tends to gloss over this fact. Europe might also be considered a preferable rival for US hegemony; it would certainly be an improvement over Trumpist governance. The other elephant in the room is Israel. By now the case for a liberal, much less a socialist, Zionism is dead. There can be no arguing about a death toll of seventy-thousand-plus, with a count that grows with new atrocities every day. The political collapse of Labor Zionism opens the way for a recovery of the history of Bundism, celebrated in the fabulous new book by Molly Crabapple. The Right to Self-Determination and Its Limits A common theme for Ukraine, Palestine, and Iran is the right of a people to self-determination . This is a principle socialists ought to keep at the forefront; it was good enough for V. I. Lenin . Socialists have always been pioneers in the furtherance of democratic rights in capitalist countries, and democratic rights in domestic affairs logically extend to democracy in foreign policy — hence national self-determination. We may not like how some nations exercise such rights, but we often regret how other individuals exercise their freedoms, too. That doesn’t mean we are against freedom. If an imperial hegemon is going to be meddling in affairs outside its borders, it should be limited to sending money and material assistance where it is needed, without conditions. In Ukraine, we could say that nation’s right to self-determination overrides concerns over the dangers of US-centric hegemony. In any case, the chief beneficiary of support for Ukraine is Europe. An implication of the right of nations to self-determination, which in principle would logically, unavoidably apply to Israel, is the rightful sovereignty of nations. The dark side of sovereignty is the implied freedom of national governments to commit crimes against their own residents. There can also be a collision of national self-determinations, again vividly illustrated in Israel versus Palestine, so the principle cannot be an absolute one. But it can’t be ignored either. Israel has arguably forfeited its right to self-determination, just as could have been said of Cambodia under the Khmer Rouge. One good rationale for the rights of nations and peoples (among which I would include Palestine) to be free from aggression is for the sake of world peace. I would designate peace the second socialist principle for foreign polic y . Socialists like peace; the working class always bears the greatest costs of war. Against “Realism” Once military force is put to the side, the other sorts of economic aggression can be dealt with by diplomacy. Setting aside how Trump’s addled mind conceives of them, tariffs are ideally a device to more fairly apportion high value-added manufacturing, which every country seeks, among nations. So too can disagreements that lead to sanctions be resolved, short of the use of force. Predatory international lending can be reformed. Debts can be relieved and payments rescheduled on more bearable terms, or canceled outright. Support for international institutions of diplomacy and aid such as the United Nations, which Trump is trying to supplant with his “Board of Peace,” can be strengthened. Most of this is consistent with a liberal perspective, but that doesn’t make it useless. Regarding tariffs, liberals tend to swing against the working class in favor of a mythical “free trade.” For socialists, tariffs ought to serve a fair trade agenda that assists economic development in the poorer nations and promotes economic sustainability, especially with respect to climate change. One debatable off-ramp from foreign policy liberalism is the so-called realist school in international relations. In recent years, the most visible public faces of this thinking have been John Mearsheimer and Stephen Walt, mostly for their bracing criticism of Israel and the Zionist lobby. Yet underlying “realism” is a chauvinistic, foundational prioritizing of US national security — hence it is not a suitable framework for socialists. The realists do tend to have better arguments than liberals, as the latter are caught between the impulse to do good deeds and the realities of national power. The realists’ skepticism and hardheadedness about military adventurism have been more persuasive. This restraint could prove to be reversed, however, in the case of escalated conflict with China, which is an unambiguous rival to the United States for superpower status. International working-class solidarity is my proposal for a third basic socialist principle of foreign policy. But that idea is bedeviled in the near term by the reality that presently workers rely on their own governments for security. In that light, the straightforward posture is to stand for national self-determination, which means noninterventionism and peace. Why Socialism Beats Liberalism Liberals tend to be animated by a desire to see the government right wrongs elsewhere. Consequently, they are suckered by appeals for humanitarian interventions. They are also reluctant to call a crackpot excuse an outright falsehood motivated by bad intentions. And out of patriotic reservations, they gloss over foreign policies rooted in exploitative economic schemes. They have yet to figure out the disastrous trend, not least for Jews themselves, in Zionism. (Some on the Right have, it seems: former US Ambassador to South Africa Patrick Gaspard pointed out that there was no resignation from the Biden administration over Gaza equivalent to the recent protest by the MAGA neofascist Joe Kent.) In this respect, socialists, going against stereotype, are often much more realistic about government and politicians. Liberals are the dreamers, and US foreign policy is the nightmare from which we all have to wake up. The three basic socialist principles of national self-determination, peace, and international working-class solidarity offer a way out. The liberal, on the other hand, is caught between patriotic hopes for humanitarian interventionism, invariably dashed, and a desire for peace; they also fail to reckon with the relevance of trade policy to working-class interests. Incidentally, trade policy could conceivably benefit workers in the countries of our trade partners by upholding labor-rights components of trade agreements, a purpose for which liberals are usually asleep at the wheel. It seems possible that socialist principles for foreign policy would even have some currency among Trump voters. The divisions among them over the Iran war are well known. Their neo-isolationism dovetails with socialist opposition to the hegemonic imperative, not to mention “forever wars.” Neither is the Democrats’ embrace of free trade doing them any good among working-class Trump voters. The biggest obstacle to a peace-oriented foreign policy is the susceptibility of voters of all political types to paranoia. For that, a ready antidote is at hand: the manifest, recent failures of bipartisan-cum-Trumpian foreign policy in Libya, Syria, Iraq, and Iran. Aside from the usual, scandalous neglect of ongoing genocides in Africa and Palestine, the key fact undermining the usual US foreign policy nostrums is that its adventurism has failed in its own terms. This event served so many purposes and hit each nail square on the head. May all of these folks get positions in unions, and hopefully join DSA. Part One of a Four Part Series A friend recently asked me what I thought about the Los Angeles mayoral race. Before I could answer, she brought up something City Councilmember Nithya Raman had said during an October debate with Mayor Karen Bass. Raman had invoked former LAPD Chief Daryl Gates while criticizing the city's cooperation with federal immigration enforcement. For many Black Angelenos, hearing Gates's name associated with a supposed “gold standard” of police policy was deeply disturbing and for some it was triggering, I understand that reaction. I also believe the controversy has been used for political advantage. Both things can be true. It's possible that a candidate can make a historically insensitive remark that causes genuine pain, and then an opposing campaign can exploit that misstep to strengthen their electoral position. That's politics. But I am interested in something beyond whether Raman should have mentioned Gates (more on that later in this series). I want to know what we should reasonably expect someone seeking to become mayor of Los Angeles to understand about the people she hopes to serve. Daryl Gates Was Not Just Another Police Chief Gates served as chief of the Los Angeles Police Department from 1978 until 1992. His tenure is remembered for aggressive policing, racial profiling, battering rams, police sweeps, and an adversarial relationship with Black communities. Under Gates, Operation Hammer brought massive police deployments into predominantly Black and Latino neighborhoods. Thousands of residents, many of them young people, were swept into anti-gang enforcement operations, including individuals who had no gang affiliations. In 1991, the videotaped beating of Rodney King exposed LAPD brutality to a national audience. But the beating itself was hardly an isolated incident. For generations, Black Angelenos had known about and experienced police violence, and resentment over abusive policing had helped ignite earlier civil unrest, including the 1965 Watts uprising. What made the Rodney King beating different was not the brutality but the technology that allowed an ordinary citizen, George Holliday, to capture it on videotape and make the evidence available to the public. What Black communities had long described, often without being believed, could now be witnessed by millions. When four officers were acquitted on state charges the following year, the verdict helped ignite the 1992 Los Angeles uprising. These events were not abstractions to the people who lived through them. They shaped relationships between Black families and law enforcement, influenced how parents prepared their children for encounters with police, and left wounds that extended well beyond the individuals directly subjected to police violence. I know this from personal experience. In 1991, I was living in Lake View Terrace, the Los Angeles community where Rodney King would be brutally beaten by LAPD officers. My son was a Black teenager, coming of age and eager to enjoy the independence that teenagers naturally seek. He wanted to go out with his friends without adult supervision. Like so many Black parents, I had already given him “the talk”—the painful but necessary conversation about how to navigate an encounter with police, how to protect himself, and how to survive a situation in which simply being a young Black male could place him in danger. But having that conversation did little to ease my anxiety. I knew all too well that no amount of caution, respect, or compliance could guarantee his safety when confronted by a police officer willing to abuse the power of the state. One day, I shared my concerns with a white friend. Rather than understanding why I was frightened, she dismissed my fears, suggesting that I was taking things too far. I immediately regretted confiding in her. Something changed between us that day. I felt a distance that had not existed before. I had shared one of my deepest fears as a mother, and she had treated it as an overreaction. Less than a month later, the videotaped beating of Rodney King was broadcast across the country. It had happened less than a mile from my home. For me, the recording did not reveal a danger I had never imagined. It documented the very danger I had been trying to explain to my friend. That is one of the less frequently discussed consequences of racial inequality. It can create a schism even between people who consider themselves friends. When one person's lived experience is dismissed by another, the resulting injury is not simply disagreement. It is the realization that someone you trusted does not fully recognize the world you and your children must navigate. The Rodney King videotape allowed millions of Americans to witness police brutality that Black communities had described for generations. But for many Black parents, the question had never been whether such violence was a reality. The question was whether our children would survive an encounter with it. Mayor Bass was a community organizer during this period. She has spoken about her own experiences with LAPD surveillance and being labeled a terrorist by Gates. So when Raman described Los Angeles as a “gold standard” for non-cooperation with immigration enforcement during the Gates era, it is not difficult to understand why Bass and many other Black Angelenos reacted with anger. To be fair, Raman was referring to Special Order 40, a policy adopted in 1979 that limited LAPD officers' authority to initiate police action solely to determine someone's immigration status. She was not necessarily endorsing Gates's overall record. That distinction matters. But so does the historical significance of invoking his name. Raman subsequently apologized. An apology was appropriate. Whether the mistake should disqualify her from office is a separate question. The Difference Between Ignorance and Indifference I do not believe a person's failure to know a particular historical fact automatically demonstrates indifference. People have different interests, experiences, and educational backgrounds. Someone can be deeply interested in science and relatively uninterested in political history. Another person might spend decades studying literature without developing much interest in economics. We cannot reasonably expect everyone to know everything. But the standard changes when someone chooses a profession in which certain knowledge is essential. If Raman had become a veterinarian, I would have little reason to question whether she had developed a deep understanding of the history of Black Los Angeles. But she did not choose that profession. She studied Social Studies at Harvard and earned a master's degree in City Planning from MIT. Her professional work has involved housing, poverty, urban development, and public policy. She has served on the Los Angeles City Council since 2020. Now she is seeking to become the mayor of Los Angeles. Those choices create responsibilities. Urban planning is inseparable from the history of racial inequality. Redlining, restrictive covenants, discriminatory lending, exclusionary zoning, freeway construction, urban renewal, and unequal public investment shaped the geography of Los Angeles. We live with the consequences of that geography today. These were not simply mistakes made by individual property owners or prejudiced neighbors. They were often policies implemented or sanctioned by public institutions. Anyone whose professional life centers on urban planning should be prepared to explain how those decisions shaped the communities that exist today. And anyone seeking to become mayor of Los Angeles should understand the particular consequences of Los Angeles policies for Black Angelenos. What Education Should Produce I recently interviewed Robert Corsini, a white, Ivy League-educated city planning expert. Corsini spoke about the racial hierarchy embedded in American urban planning. His observations demonstrated an understanding of how technical seemingly racial neutral decisions about land use and development could reproduce racial inequality. I do not know whether he acquired that understanding through his formal education or through his own curiosity about civil rights and racial justice. But the conversation stayed with me. Why do some highly educated professionals recognize the racial hierarchy embedded in American institutions while others with comparable educational opportunities appear less attentive to it? Part of the answer may be personal curiosity. People naturally gravitate toward some subjects and not others. But that cannot be the entire answer when professional responsibility is involved. Curiosity is a personal inclination. Competence and diligence are professional obligations. A mayor need not personally experience every injustice that residents have endured. But a mayor should understand how those injustices shaped the city she has been elected to serve. What Raman Knows—and What Remains Unclear It would be unfair to suggest that Raman knows nothing about structural racism. She has publicly discussed discriminatory housing and zoning policies. Her work on housing and homelessness reflects an engagement with systemic inequality. Raman has also released a Black Agenda for Los Angeles addressing housing, homelessness, economic opportunity, and policing. But the document raises an important question beyond the merits of its proposals: Which Black community leaders and organizations participated in developing it, and how much influence did they have over its priorities? These distinctions matter. Recognizing structural racism is not the same as understanding the particular history and experiences of Black Angelenos. And developing policies intended to benefit a community is not necessarily the same as developing those policies in meaningful partnership with that community. None of this establishes that Raman is indifferent to Black Angelenos, nor would it be fair to draw that conclusion from a single debate remark. Nevertheless, I remain troubled. The most concerning possibility is not simply that Raman failed to appreciate the historical significance of Daryl Gates's name during a debate. It is that someone could advance through elite education, professional training, years of public policy work, and elected office without developing a sufficiently deep understanding of the particular history of Black Los Angeles—or without recognizing the importance of learning that history directly from the people who have lived it. I cannot establish that Raman has failed to acquire that knowledge. But her remark gives voters a legitimate reason to ask her to demonstrate it. How does she understand the relationship between policing and Black political life in Los Angeles? What does she know about the decline of the city's Black population? How does that history inform her proposals on housing, homelessness, economic development, and public safety? These are reasonable questions for someone seeking the city's highest elected office. A Standard for Every Candidate I do not believe Raman's mistake should automatically disqualify her. Nor should Black Angelenos be expected to dismiss their concerns because the candidate's intentions were different from the impact of her words. We should evaluate what she says, what she knows, what she has done, and what she proposes to do. The same standard must apply to Karen Bass. A candidate's lived experience can provide valuable insight, but it does not exempt that candidate from scrutiny of policies, performance, or results. The media has a responsibility here as well. Journalists should explain why Gates remains such a painful figure in Black Los Angeles, while also investigating the substance of Raman's criticism about immigration enforcement. Otherwise, a legitimate historical grievance risks becoming another campaign spectacle that leaves voters less informed. The question is not whether Raman is a good person or whether Bass benefits politically from the controversy. It is whether our expectations for political leadership are sufficiently demanding. What should we expect a candidate for mayor to understand about the history, experiences, and needs of the people she seeks to serve? That question belongs at the center of the election. And it leads to another, perhaps even more consequential question: What happened to the Black Los Angeles that helped build this city? A Midterm Rout Will Hurt the GOP, but Trumpism Will Endure

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Joseph G. Peschek ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-30T14:39:07+00:00

Observers across the spectrum expect Democrats to make significant gains in the upcoming midterms, in House and Senate races and beyond. There are good reasons to trust in their predictions. Nonpresidential elections in the US are increasingly “nationalized,” meaning that evaluations of presidential and national politics strongly influence down-ballot choices. And President Donald Trump, having behaved cruelly and erratically while presiding over a weak economy and an unpopular war with Iran, has not set Republicans up for success. With inflation rates high, wages stagnant, and borrowing costs elevated, economic issues are central to the GOP’s current quandary. In a recent Washington Post –ABC News–Ipsos poll, Trump’s economic approval rating stands at just 28 percent. By a three-to-one margin, respondents said the state of the economy was either not so good or poor. Forty-eight percent said they were financially worse off than when Trump returned to office in 2025, the highest percentage saying this about any president in Post -ABC polls since 1986. On other issues, 65 percent said it was a mistake to take military action against Iran, and 68 percent disapproved of the way Trump has dealt with US allies. Tellingly, 56 percent said Trump had gone too far in deporting undocumented immigrants, compared with 48 percent in April 2025. But whatever happens in November, Trump will hold office for two more years, and the politics he represents won’t evaporate when he exits. Anyone expecting a midterm rout and Trump’s departure in 2029 to restore the old normal — and for the old normal to be sufficient to prevent another far-right resurgence — needs to think again. Instead, we must reckon with the political force that has been unleashed in American life, the aftershocks of which will no doubt be felt for generations to come. In my new book , Class, Power and the State in the Trump Era: A Critical Analysis (De Gruyter Brill, 2026), I try to sketch an early picture of Trumpism. Below I draw on it to describe what Trumpism is made of, how it changed between Trump’s two terms, and what that means for the Left. Authoritarian Overreach One unmistakable aspect of Trumpism is brazen authoritarianism. This dimension is on full display right now, as Trump works diligently to undermine confidence in the electoral system. The Supreme Court checked his attempt to use the US Postal Service to restrict mail-in ballots in September. Since then, the administration has engaged in “systematically laying out unsubstantiated and false claims in an effort to sow doubts about the safety of the country’s decentralized election system,” as a New York Times analysis put it. For example, as part of Trump’s big-lie campaign about illegal voting, the Justice Department claimed without evidence that twenty thousand people on California’s voter rolls were “likely noncitizens.” The department then charged twenty-eight California residents, all legal immigrants, with violating federal election laws. Despite Trump’s disinformation campaign, most Americans are confident the midterm elections will be conducted fairly, though seven in ten lack confidence that Trump will accept the results if Republicans lose. Recent weeks have brought stepped-up repression against those Trump regards as enemies. Most notably, Department of Homeland Security officials raided the Washington home of Congresswoman Ilhan Omar, the Somali American representative of Minnesota’s Fifth District, seizing her phone, passport, and other documents. Trump has long displayed a fixation on Omar, whom he has called “garbage,” and on Somali Americans, who reside in large numbers in Minnesota, especially in the Twin Cities area. Several days before the raid, Trump wrote on Truth Social , “The once Great State of Minnesota is being taken over by Somali Immigrants. There is Violence, Murder, and Mayhem all over the State. The people of Minnesota must fight back.” According to several reports, those familiar with the probe believe that its goal is to revoke Omar’s citizenship and eventually deport her. In my book, I characterize Trumpism as a distinct form of right-wing politics, blending (pseudo-)populism, nationalism, and authoritarianism with uninhibited, even predatory or patrimonial, pro-capitalist economic and social policies. While these political forms have existed in various shapes throughout American political history, they combined in new ways in the Trump era. Trumpism marked a departure from the pre-2016 Republican ideological consensus and signaled the arrival of an alternative form of right-wing politics, one that played out in other countries as well.The goal of the MAGA movement is to weaken rival forces and establish hegemony in American politics based on a reconfigured national-conservative bloc enjoying both popular and elite support. The attack on the election system and the raid on Omar’s home make sense in that light. A movement bent on dominating American politics has every reason to discredit the elections that could check its power and to punish the people it treats as enemies. The Evolution of Trumpism If these features were plainly evident in Trump’s first presidency, they only deepened their hold after he left office in 2021 and during his second presidency. From 2021 to 2025, between Trump’s two terms, Trumpism underwent four important shifts. First, Trump’s reactionary political worldview grew more radical, marked by an unremitting commitment to the stolen-2020-election lie, intensifying authoritarianism, vows of retribution, and hysterical rhetoric about the threat of socialism, communism, and the “radical Left.” Second, the Trump campaign worked with right-wing think tanks to draw up blueprints for a second Trump administration. The purpose was to go beyond such traditional conservative objectives as cutting taxes and reducing spending to a wholesale assault on the administrative state that would give the president complete control over the executive branch. As Russell Vought , now Trump’s budget director, put it in 2023, “The president Day One will be a wrecking ball for the administrative state.” The best-known plan for a second Trump administration was the Heritage Foundation’s Project 2025, released in 2023, of which Vought was a key architect . Third, Trump and the MAGA movement aligned closely with the preoccupations of right-wing culture and political thought, such as Christian nationalism, anti-wokeness crusading, “pro-natalism,” and concerns about “civilizational erosion” that draw on the far-right great replacement theory. Such thinking is common among Trump 2.0 figures like JD Vance, Pete Hegseth, and Stephen Miller. Fourth, after leaving office in 2021, Trump almost entirely abandoned the economic pseudo-populism aimed at the working class that was a prominent part of his 2016 campaign. Trump’s 2024 campaign openly courted Wall Street, Silicon Valley, and other parts of corporate America, such as oil and gas, tobacco, and the cryptocurrency industry, which he had previously called a scam. Trump vowed to cut corporate tax rates, reduce environmental and other regulations, and support fossil fuel drilling. In May 2026, Trump was asked if “Americans’ financial situation” motivated him to make a deal with Iran. “Not even a little bit,” he replied . “I don’t think about Americans’ financial situation. I don’t think about anybody. I think about one thing — we cannot let Iran have a nuclear weapon. That’s all.” He added, “Every American understands.” An Opening for the Left But not every American understands. The war with Iran is unpopular, especially in the context of Americans’ domestic economic difficulties. Those difficulties are largely of the president’s own making. Trump’s signature One Big Beautiful Bill Act, enacted in July 2025, has been called “the most regressive tax and budget legislation in modern American history” by Yale scholars Patrick Sullivan and Jacob Hacker. The inflationary pressures squeezing the working class stem largely from misguided Trump policies, including the war on Iran, which has driven up the price of oil and oil-based products; across-the-board tariffs, which have raised prices of imported goods and inputs used in domestic production; and anti-immigration and deportation measures that have led to labor shortages in construction, agriculture, meatpacking, and other sectors. So the conditions exist for a large-scale repudiation of Trump and the MAGA movement, which are increasingly authoritarian and even fascistic. As early as 1921, more than a year before the March on Rome, Antonio Gramsci theorized the cross-class, psychologically intense appeal of fascism, noting that fascism “has become an unleashing of elemental forces which cannot be restrained within the bourgeois system of economic and political governance.” The challenge for the Left is to construct a politics that can leash, redirect, and de-energize these “elemental forces” to forestall further drift toward authoritarianism and fascism, something centrist and corporate liberalism has been unable to do. What does this mean for politics after the midterm elections? First, the Left needs educational efforts to counter McCarthyite attacks from the Right on progressive and socialist ideas so that they can be discussed rationally and without stigma. Second, it should build on the successful electoral work by the Democratic Socialists of America and other groups seen in the Zohran Mamdani campaign in New York and elsewhere. Third, it needs progressive populist economic plans that serve working-class interests and cut through right-wing pseudo-populism. Economist Isabella Weber’s proposals for strategic price controls, which have been discussed in Jacobin , are one starting point worth considering. Gains by the Left in areas such as these will help counter not just the remainder of the Trump 2.0 presidency but Trumpism as an increasingly malignant form of right-wing, antidemocratic capitalist politics. There’s a story doing the rounds. A woman is sitting in a bar in tourist-filled Turin; you can imagine the glass panes looking out onto the square, the little tables for spritzes and olives. Kids and chitchat. Then, out of nowhere, everyone starts coughing and wheezing; she feels a burning sensation in her eyes. The air is filled with white fumes, tear gas engulfing the little tables. Everyone rushes inside, they lock themselves in. Only afterward do they spy, through the fumes, a few hooded protesters running towards the piazza. Only afterward — when they can breathe more easily and slip into the side streets to make their way home — are the squares and streets fully taken over by skirmishes and barricades. The great Italian critical theorist Furio Jesi , thinking of his native Turin, wrote in 1969 that “you can love a city, and recognize its houses and its street in your deepest and dearest memories: but it’s only in the moment of revolt that you feel like the city is truly yours.” Revolts, Jesi argued, offer up strangely telling moments of symbolism. In the story above, the tear gas arrives before the protests. It smothers the emblematic scene of Italy’s commercial place in the world — its food and monuments, the great Italian hope. The image is engulfed by clouds of authoritarianism. The protesters arrive afterward, few and scattered, but the damage precedes them. The protests that have rocked northern Italy over the past two weeks represent the outcome of years of repression and criminalization by the political and media classes. On the one hand, they take inspiration from a remarkably international moment. R., an activist from Cantiere — one of Milan’s historic social centers — tells me that young people of North African descent in Milan’s suburbs look to their French equivalents like their big brothers, with their expansive culture of hip-hop, film, and militancy. And similarly, she notes, throughout the street protests you could see not only the ubiquitous Palestinian flags, but also Moroccan and Albanian ones, symbols of other cousins in other streets but in the same struggle. On the other hand, interviews with kids involved in the clashes and protests in Milan and elsewhere underscore the desperation of Italy’s economic conditions: families that simply cannot get by on poverty wages. It is entirely normal in Italy for a month’s salary to be less than €1,000, barely covering rent and bills. Add to this the rising costs of food and gas, and it is surprising that the protests didn’t break out sooner. While the windows of high-end shops caved in across the city centers in the north, supermarket shelves have been going empty across the south. Motorways and ports have been blocked by thousands of truckers striking against rising fuel costs — with the result that basic produce hasn’t been getting from farm to table. The self-employed truckers have found wide public support, M., an activist from Porco Rosso , a left-wing community center in Palermo, tells me. The social centers and grassroots trade unionists have been coming out to support, but also small business owners from the tourism industry as well as from across the agricultural sector. No fuel, no food and supplies, no business. Less economic miracle than economic mirage. Making Crimes Doesn’t Pay The government’s principle reaction to the protests has been to criminalize them. A few days after the aperitivi tables went flying in Turin, Giorgia Meloni announced that she would be pushing through a new law removing legal protections for young people, and establishing harsher sentencing. At the same time, thousands of truckers have been identified by police and hundreds served with fines for blocking the road. Searching for a scapegoat to offer up to the right-wing electorate, decolonial activist Youssef Siher was arrested on “terrorism” charges so fabricated that the courts immediately ordered his release. Giving short shrift to the separation of executive and judicial branches, Meloni attacked the judge’s decision as “shameful,” an affront to the police’s hard work. Responding to protest with new criminal measures, mass arrests, and undermining the courts are standard elements in the repertoire of any right-wing government, not least in Italy. But these tactics have become a leitmotif of Meloni’s stint as prime minister in particular, for reasons that can be explained through a brief look back over the past five years of Italian politics. Back in 2021, in the wake of the economic crash and burn of the plague biennial, the European Union once more installed a technocratic government to manage the billions in fiscal stimulus that was to be invested in the country’s economy. Capitalist powers were determined that the cash was not going to be wasted on handouts and kickbacks. Nearly all the main parties, including the hitherto ruling Democratic/Five Star alliance, backed Mario Draghi’s broad coalition, lining up behind the promise of a financial miracle. It was a disastrous political strategy. Only two parties stood out of line, Nicola Fratoianni’s Sinistra Italia (center left) and Meloni’s Fratelli d’Italia (far right). Casting itself as the underdog and riding a wave of racist anti-immigrant hatred, Meloni’s party picked up enough votes to become the largest single party. Fast forward to 2026. The EU’s post-COVID stimulus money, predictably squandered in half-baked projects and through a chronic lack of civil service staffing, was directed to Italy more than any other country but failed to save it from stagnation . The result is not only that gas prices are soaring, but that the Italian state lacks the funds, and the will, to dig the working classes out of the hole it has plunged them into. Lacking the economic clout or courage to introduce policies that respond to grievances expressed across every part of society, the Meloni government has consistently turned to boasting about new laws to criminalize dissent. The sheer range of activities and people targeted by the government through new laws and decrees over just four years is truly astounding. Ravers, anarchists, people smugglers, youth gangs, families employing surrogates, environmentalists, squatters, anti-Zionists, protesters in prisons, protesters in detention centers, the parents of protesters — and, of course, street protesters in general — have all been targets for new legislation threatening administrative fines or harshened prison sentences. To this growing list of subversives we can also add a whole series of criminal proceedings and investigations that have effectively been spearheaded by the government, against sea rescue missions, striking trade unionists, the Palestine solidarity movement, and, most recently, doctors supporting patients threatened with forced deportation. The vast majority of these laws and investigations have not actually led to social activists and other rebels being given longer sentences or thrown in prison at a greater rate. This is largely thanks to the relative independence of the Italian courts (along with constant work by lawyers’ networks and solidarity movements), that have challenged these laws at every turn, often leading to pernicious reforms being blocked by judges. The government’s response has been to support a smear campaign against the “red robes” — i.e., left-leaning judges — culminating in March’s government-driven referendum to reform the judiciary. This led to the first real chink in Meloni’s public image, however: the reforms were voted down . Despite the failure of the strategy on any practical level, the government has little to lean on by now except for such threats and rhetorical attacks. It came as little surprise, then, that the main response to the riots in the north and the strikes in the south was a wave of arrests (including schoolchildren arrested at the school gates), high-profile investigations, resurrection of the old boogeyman of “organized crime,” and the announcement of yet another security law. Announcing the new law on TV, Meloni focused on the petty looting. “I want to be clear: no political ideal, no opposition to the government, justifies destroying a shop to make off with the goods. Behind that window there’s a family who has made lifelong sacrifices, workers who risk losing their jobs, citizens who have the right to experience their own city without fear.” But you can look through a window from two sides. From one side you see the commodities; from the other you look out to the street, where there are indeed workers, families and citizens — the very same people who form that grand litany of subjects that the government has threatened and bullied over the last four years. People who, in a moment of revolt, perhaps really did experience their city without fear. Wartime Elections The underlying causes of — and future prospects for — the current conflagrations cannot be separated from international politics, including Italy’s relationship to the United States. If, in 2022, the watchwords had been the job market and the EU stimulus program, now they are the old couplet of fuel prices and global warfare. And though once a thorn in the EU’s side, it is now the Meloni government that has tried to appease European politicians and capitalist forces by backing the Israeli-US war against Iran and the genocide in Palestine — while also attempting to second guess Donald Trump’s military erraticism, whipping between support for Ukraine and Russia like a tattered flag in a storm. The fuel dispute that has been burning all year strikes at the heart of the problems facing Italy and Italians. The US-Iraeli war on Iran has shot gas prices up by 25 percent since January. Attempting to soften the blow, the government’s response was to cut fuel taxes across the board, lowering the prices at the pump. This has been an extremely costly solution for a government desperately trying to avoid an EU infringement procedure: the fuel tax cuts cost the budget half a billion euros every month — which is why Meloni was winding the policy down, to the truckers’ ire. That policy has now been replaced — thanks to the truckers’ wildcat strikes — by subsidies specifically aimed at supporting diesel-dependent businesses. While this may appease some of the more militant and organized logistics workers in the south, it does not provide a response to the impact of fuel costs more generally. Meloni is aware of this, and promptly flew to Kuwait to smooth out a deal on price capping fuel imports in exchange for military assistance to the US-allied petrostate. Leveraging Italy’s geopolitical position to resolve economic problems at home has been Meloni’s horizon throughout her premiership. But, as I have argued before, the gamble hasn’t been paying off. In large part, this is because these alliances are being rejected by the electorate. Despite the opinions of the mainstream press and industrialists alike, Italians increasingly oppose arming Ukraine, and — as last year’s vast if ephemeral demonstrations proved — are generally supportive of Palestinian liberation. Much depends on the turnout for the upcoming national strikes by Italy’s small but militant grassroots Unione Sindacale di Base as well as the giant left-wing Confederazione Generale Italiana del Lavoro, both of which rightly demand higher salaries and criticize arms spending. Still more, next year Italians will go to the polls. The Italian left has a historic opportunity: to unite around an internationalist antiwar platform and propose radical economic change that resolves the structural problems of Italian society — rather than knee-jerk policies that just shove cash into one worker’s hands while handcuffing another’s. Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 on the Protection of Personal Data treats identifiable images of people as personal data, so recorded footage falls within its scope. The UAE Data Office is named as the federal regulator. Businesses in the DIFC and ADGM follow their own data protection regimes instead. The second layer is local. In Dubai, Law No. 12 of 2016 regulates the security industry and created the Security Industry Regulatory Agency (SIRA), which licenses and supervises security service providers, including firms that install surveillance systems. It also lies in the political and organizational experience that helped make it possible, above all the ability of diverse left-wing and progressive forces, with different programs and ideological outlooks, to forge a broad electoral alliance around common ground in confronting the far right. A broad left-wing and progressive alliance despite differences Lula contested the election with the support of the “Brazil Ready for More” alliance, comprising seven left-wing and progressive parties organized into two party federations and two separate parties. These forces agreed on common electoral objectives while preserving their identities and organizational autonomy. This is one of the most important lessons of the Brazilian experience. Left unity does not require ideological or organizational uniformity. It means being able to work together on common ground while respecting pluralism and differences. Disagreements over Lula’s economic and social policies will naturally persist, including among the forces that supported him at the ballot box. Unity does not mean abandoning criticism or autonomy The Brazilian experience raises an important question for left-wing forces around the world. Why do ideological and organizational differences become barriers to cooperation, despite so much common ground, even when these forces face shared political and class challenges? Pluralism among communists, social democrats, the radical left, environmentalist and feminist currents, and independent leftists can be a source of strength and intellectual and political richness, provided it is managed democratically and constructively, without monopolization, exclusion, or claims to possess the absolute truth. Unity does not require abandoning criticism or offering unconditional acceptance of the policies of governments led by left-wing and progressive alliances. Confronting Capitalism and the far right requires a broad social front The victory demonstrated the importance of connecting the defense of democracy, rights, and freedoms with the economic and social demands of working classes and poor and marginalized communities. Lula drew broad support from lower-income groups and proposed expanding social assistance, improving living conditions, and defending democratic institutions. Confronting capitalism and the far right therefore cannot be confined to elections and party alliances. It requires sustained mass organizations in workplaces, neighborhoods, universities, trade unions, and social movements, together with policies that tackle class inequality and improve the lives of the majority. Digital autonomy: the fifth pillar of the left’s independence The Brazilian election revealed the growing importance of the digital sphere in political struggle. Social media platforms, algorithms, and artificial intelligence have become powerful tools in the hands of capitalist interests for shaping public consciousness and steering political debate. During the campaign, Lula’s supporters submitted complaints and evidence supporting allegations that Meta, the company that owns Facebook, Instagram, and other platforms, showed digital bias in favor of his opponent. The problem is not confined to Brazil. During Colombia’s most recent presidential election in 2026, journalistic investigations uncovered coordinated accounts and AI-generated fake videos targeting the left-wing camp , as well as shortcomings and bias in the platforms’ oversight of political advertising and content. In Germany, Elon Musk, the owner of X, publicly endorsed the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) during the 2025 election. He used his platform to promote the party’s positions and host its leaders, while several studies found a clear bias in the reach of the party’s content on the platform . Proving deliberate algorithmic bias is difficult because proprietary algorithms give capitalist corporations exclusive control over information about how they work and how content is ranked, amplified, or restricted. Without transparency, independent public and legal oversight, and access to the data needed for investigation, it is difficult to determine whether bias arises from algorithm design or deliberate intervention. Companies can easily attribute such problems to a “technical glitch”! The Brazilian experience also highlighted the importance of combining three interconnected arenas of struggle: mass struggle on the ground, legal action to hold digital platforms accountable and regulate their operations, and organized digital activism. Mass mobilization in the streets was accompanied by legal challenges against Meta and the development of a wide-ranging digital campaign that used social media effectively to reach millions of voters. This experience shows that the digital left’s struggle cannot replace grassroots struggle. It can reinforce and complement it. This is where digital autonomy emerges as the fifth pillar of the left’s independence, alongside ideological, organizational, financial, and media autonomy. Genuine political independence is no longer possible without a meaningful degree of autonomy over digital tools, platforms, and infrastructure, the protection of data, and the capacity for mass communication and organization free from the monopolistic control of capitalist corporations and major powers. Digital autonomy does not mean withdrawing from digital platforms. It means using them today with a critical, progressive awareness while working to develop open-source, democratically-governed digital alternatives and to build and strengthen independent technical capacities within the left. A left that fails to develop its digital autonomy and technological tools will remain at the mercy of its class adversaries in one of the most important arenas of contemporary political and social struggle. Lessons for the left worldwide The Brazilian experience cannot be reproduced mechanically. Every society has its own conditions and balance of class and political forces. The Brazilian approach to building alliances and working together can nevertheless offer valuable lessons. The unity the left needs is neither a single party nor a centralized leadership imposing its vision on everyone. It is flexible, democratic cooperation among diverse forces, grounded in common programs, respect for autonomy and pluralism, and meaningful participation by the grassroots, young people, and women. Such cooperation can be developed locally, regionally, and globally through different left-wing fronts and alliances on the ground, as well as digital left networks and internationals that contribute to the exchange of experience, coordinate shared initiatives and struggles, and work toward progressive digital alternatives. Lula’s victory offers an experience worth studying and learning from. It demonstrates the possibility of cooperation among left-wing forces despite their differences, while recognizing the other factors that contributed to the result. The left does not need to erase its diversity to unite. It needs to turn that diversity into an organized and effective force, strengthen its independence across all five pillars, and pursue the struggle for freedom, equality, and social justice while developing democratic, humanist socialist alternatives to capitalism. The disputed election and the violence that followed exposed deep tensions over Tanzania’s political realities, democratic participation, and the state’s response to dissent. A disputed election and a deadly crackdown The violence of October 2025 did not erupt out of nowhere. In the months before the election , opposition parties faced mounting restrictions, rallies were disrupted, and political activists were arrested. Opposition leader Tundu Lissu was arrested in April 2025 and charged with treason, which kept him out of the electoral process. These developments narrowed the space for political competition and raised serious questions about the credibility of the vote. When Tanzanians went to the polls on October 29, demonstrations broke out in several parts of the country, including Dar es Salaam, Arusha, and Mwanza. The opposition has maintained that the death toll was higher, with concerns also raised about people who remain missing and the circumstances in which bodies were removed from hospitals and mortuaries. The commission submitted its report to President Samia Suluhu Hassan on April 23. Without public access to the findings, it remains difficult to scrutinize the evidence, understand the circumstances surrounding individual deaths, and determine what action the government intends to take against those responsible. President Samia Suluhu Hassan and other government officials have repeatedly portrayed the protests as a foreign-backed attempt to overthrow the government, a coordinated plot to destabilize the country. Activists argue that this narrative serves to deflect responsibility, evade justice, and excuse the authorities’ slow response to the killings. Tanzania today is marked by silence, not reconciliation. And a nation cannot simply move on from an atrocity of this scale without truth and accountability.

The world once again calls for an end to the US blockade on Cuba

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Pablo Meriguet ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-29T23:05:02+00:00

Last Wednesday, October 28, the United Nations General Assembly voted (for the thirty-fourth time) in favor of ending the economic and commercial embargo – which many refer to as a blockade – that the United States has unilaterally imposed on Cuba for more than sixty years. With 130 votes in favor, 17 against, and 25 abstentions, the international community once again demanded that the United States end an embargo that has caused unimaginable hardship for the Cuban people and has slowed the Caribbean nation’s development. One of the countries that voted against the embargo was South Africa, whose ambassador emphasized his concern about the negative consequences the Cuban people are suffering as a result of Washington’s decisions. He also called for Cuba to be removed from the list of countries that allegedly sponsor terrorism, a designation that prevents many companies from doing business on the island. In recent years, the Cuban Ministry of Foreign Affairs has denounced that high-level US diplomats had pressured several of its partners to vote against such resolutions concerning the embargo on Cuba, under threat of serious consequences. The denunciation was repeated this year by Cuba’s top diplomat. Despite these efforts, the overwhelming majority of countries once again called on the United States to end the embargo. Washington appeared to have successfully influenced only its closest allies (those fully aligned with its geopolitical ideology) to vote against it. Thus, among the countries that voted against the resolution are several signatories to “Shield of the Americas,” the new international military initiative led by Washington, to which several right-wing and far-right governments in Latin America have aligned themselves. For its part, the UN news portal reported : “The Assembly expresses its concern that, after decades of votes and appeals, the measure remains in force and continues to negatively affect the Cuban population and Cuban nationals residing abroad, without the expectations of economic normalization having been met.” A multimillion-dollar blockade According to Cuban Foreign Minister Bruno Rodríguez , Cuba’s annual losses are estimated at about 8 billion dollars. The total economic losses since the embargo began amount to the astronomical figure of 178 billion dollars, which is a huge sum considering the relatively small size of a country with a population of 10 million. Cuban Foreign Minister Bruno Rodríguez on October 28, showing a calculation of economic losses from the blockade since the UN debate began. Photo: Zoe Alexandra In this regard, the US economic and trade embargo – the longest-lasting in recent modern history – has been denounced by Cuban authorities as a perverse mechanism employed by Washington to boycott, almost from its inception, the Cuban Revolution, which proclaimed itself anti-imperialist and socialist. Added to this prolonged damage are the recent energy restrictions that Washington has imposed on Cuba. Since early 2026, when Washington targeted Venezuela (Cuba’s main energy partner), the possibility of sending oil to Cuba has been cut off by a unilateral US order; the United States warned it would increase tariffs on any country that sends crude oil to Cuba. Only one Russian tanker managed to navigate the waters and reach Cuba – a resource appreciated by Cubans but one that ran out quite quickly. The Caribbean nation depends almost entirely on petroleum for generating electricity, production, trade, and providing hospital care to its population. Added to this are the difficulties in distributing water and meeting other basic needs of the Cuban people, who are the true victims of a new strategy aimed at overthrowing the revolutionary government. In this regard, the support of the international community – the vast majority of which has called for an end to the embargo, considering it a measure that undermines the sovereignty and self-determination of the Cuban people – provides a platform to remind the world of the situation Cuba is facing. Cuban President Miguel Díaz-Canel said on X : “Today at the UN, the international community’s overwhelming support for Cuba was evident in its condemnation of the blockade and the US government’s policy of maximum pressure. Most of the speakers, some representing regional blocs, were clear in their condemnation.” He added : “It is a despicable act on the part of the empire’s representatives to claim they are defending collective punishment – which exacts a high human cost – against the Cuban people, in violation of human rights. It is a futile effort. They are not convincing, and they achieve nothing except through gangster-like coercion.” Exchange of accusations between Havana and Washington But the vote was not without controversy. During his address, the US representative to the UN, Mike Waltz, said that concentration camps existed in Cuba where “thousands of homosexuals, Christians, and Afro-Cubans” were allegedly detained. In response to the statement, the Cuban delegation, visibly upset, began banging on the table and requested a “point of order.” When the Assembly president did not respond, Foreign Minister Rodríguez and the rest of the Cuban delegation approached the presiding officers and Waltz and demanded that he stop lying. Cuban delegation calls for a point of order during US Ambassador to the UN Mike Waltz’s speech. Photo: Zoe Alexandra Following the protests, the Assembly president gave the floor to Rodríguez, who said that Waltz was “blatantly lying” and that his “attitude is disgraceful.” He further stated: “Mr. Waltz is lying again, and I wish him a better future in other roles, but at the United Nations and on the Security Council, his performance leaves much to be desired … He is undermining the dignity of the Cuban people and the Cuban government.”
