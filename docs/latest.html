The Rod Shape Changes Where the Curl Sits Standard lifts typically use a rounded silicone rod that curls the whole lash evenly from base to tip. The Korean method uses a different rod. It’s flatter and has a distinct bend point. This rod lifts from closer to the root while leaving more of the length relatively straight before it curves up. Processing Time Gets Adjusted for Different Lashes A big part of what separates this method from a standard lift is how the perm solution’s processing time changes based on someone’s actual lash texture, instead of applying roughly the same timing to most clients. Open Versus Enclosed Transport This decision affects more than cost, though it does affect cost greatly. The War in Ukraine Is Spiraling Out of Control

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Branko Marcetic ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-10T17:31:57+00:00

War is in the air in Europe. Over the four years and counting that it’s raged, the Ukraine war has often been compared to the two world wars, being the largest land war the continent’s seen since the second of those and having surpassed the length of the first this past June. But unlike those disastrous conflicts, the fighting in Ukraine has not become a true continent-wide affair that’s dragged the rest of Europe’s powers into the fray — so far. It is becoming alarmingly uncertain how much longer that will hold. For nearly five years, both Russia and European North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) states have been at pains to avoid being pulled into direct war with each other. But as Ukraine’s war effort falters, there are mounting signs of the conflict spiraling into generalized warfare. Between escalations, accidents, and a thick haze of sometimes purposeful misinformation, the risk that the conflict may spin out from yet another European bloodbath to something even more cataclysmic has perhaps never been higher. It’s a bitter vindication for antiwar voices who warned for years that NATO member states were sleepwalking into a wider, more dangerous confrontation with Russia. The question now is what can be done to forestall their darkest predictions. Threats and Accidents The stakes of a direct Russian-European war are high. Experts have long argued that any such conflict would quickly turn nuclear , precisely because, as a 2022 Brown University paper argued , Russia’s conventional military weakness versus NATO makes it more likely to reach for the nuclear option. “I think the mainstream media has downplayed these risks, because it would severely undermine support for Ukraine,” says Lyle Goldstein, senior fellow at the Watson Institute for International and Public Affairs at Brown University, and the author of that report. With Russia and NATO’s non-American member states holding nearly five thousand active nuclear warheads between them, any nuclear exchange between the two would not just likely kill millions but imperil the very future of humanity. This is why world leaders for decades painstakingly restrained any conflict between the alliance and the then–Soviet Union, right down to their public rhetoric. And yet hardly a month goes by these days without European and Russian officials openly threatening one another. This past April, Vladimir Putin ally Dmitry Medvedev, the former president of Russia who now serves as the deputy chair of its security council, publicly declared a list of European drone-making facilities a “register of potential legitimate targets”; a month later he warned EU citizens that their “peaceful sleep is over” and that their leaders had “unilaterally entered into a war with Russia.” Meanwhile, the head of Germany’s air force has vowed to swiftly bomb Russia in the event of war, while Poland’s foreign minister, Radosław Sikorski, has boasted that Europe could destroy Russian fuel refineries in “six days” and defeat it in a war “quite quickly.” These verbal salvos hit an alarming peak last week, after Moscow — which two years ago officially lowered its threshold for the use nuclear weapons — formally warned NATO that it was prepared to use nukes against the alliance, which it charged with taking steps that risked “the outbreak of direct armed conflict.” It came in the wake of statements and leaked plans identifying Kaliningrad — the Russian exclave between Lithuania and Poland that serves as a key Russian military outpost — as a top early target in the event of a war between Russia and NATO. All the while, a monthslong drumbeat of warnings has sounded from NATO state officials and intelligence agencies that war with Russia may be right around the corner , or is already underway . The Baltic states have put in place mass evacuation plans, German hospitals are bracing for future mass casualties, and the British government wants citizens to start stockpiling food and water. European capitals are hearing talk of military conscription to meet what seems like an almost inevitable conflict. This war of words is a function of what increasingly resembles a literal war. European officials accuse Russia of a spree of sabotage incidents at defense industrial facilities and other sites across Europe, just as Medvedev warned in April — most infamously, a set of explosive-laden drones discovered at and around Leipzig airport, a logistics hub for NATO’s supplies to Ukraine. One German commentator described the incident as the country’s version of 9/11 — a questionable comparison, since no one died at Leipzig, but an ominous one given the US foreign policy choices that followed those attacks. This adds to a growing list of accidental incursions. During Russia’s now monthslong air assault on Ukraine, Russian munitions, drones, and other aircraft have crossed into the airspace or onto the soil of NATO states dozens of times, including just last week . Russian drones have reportedly violated NATO member Romania’s airspace twenty-three times this year: more than all such incidents over the previous three years combined . So far none have reached the danger of November 2022, when an errant pair of rockets wrongly blamed on Russia killed two farmers on the Polish side of the border, triggering calls for war and an urgent NATO meeting to discuss a response — an emergency only defused when it turned out that Ukraine had been accidentally responsible. “If any one of the incidents that we’ve seen in the past four months had happened in 2023, it would have been game-changing, everyone would’ve been freaking out about it,” says Emma Ashford, senior fellow with the Stimson Center’s Reimagining US Grand Strategy program. “We’ve become so inured to this conflict going on, there was not a huge reaction to any of these.” But it may be a matter of time: in another instance last month, a Russian warship fired two emergency flares at a Danish helicopter conducting surveillance, narrowly missing it. “As these hybrid incidents get more frequent, their kinetic nature intensifies,” says Jennifer Kavanagh, director of military analysis at Defense Priorities. “Without a dialogue channel, there’s always a risk that Russia inadvertently steps over a line and triggers escalation.” Tit for Tat for Tit for Tat That risk became viscerally real last month, after a Russian drone destroyed part of a train near the Ukrainian-Polish border mere minutes after a group of US and European officials were shuttled away. Seemingly calibrated to be a near miss, the attack could have easily triggered what one ex-CIA official described as an “escalatory chain” leading to war had it been slightly off. “The Russian motivation here is to impose costs on Europe, to retaliate for the costs that Ukraine is imposing on Russia,” says Kavanagh. “The Russians understand European money is funding Ukraine’s war effort and that Europe is giving it drones.” Those drones have, by Putin’s own admission , been used to inflict damage on Russia worth 1 percent of its GDP, or, according to Kyiv, to knock out half of its oil refining capacity. NATO states have not just furnished the arms to do this but, through the US and French governments, provided the intelligence that Ukraine has used to select targets and help its drones evade Russian air defenses. As heartening as this might be for Ukraine’s war effort, it has come with real risk, as the line blurs between simply providing military support, which NATO states view themselves as doing, and becoming full-on cobelligerents. To get a sense of how potentially inflammatory such support might be in Moscow, consider the outrage sparked in Washington and Brussels when it was revealed earlier this year that Russia was providing Iran with a similar kind of support to what NATO states are now giving Ukraine. Consider, too, that this support blows well past the lines that Western officials themselves drew earlier in the war to limit the risk of escalation. Long gone are the days when US and European officials thought that merely helping Ukraine obtain fighter jets was too risky, let alone approving long-range strikes into Russian territory. Instead, convinced that the lack of Russian retaliation for these moves over the years meant that “Putin’s red lines are just a bluff intended to scare the West,” as the hawkish Atlantic Council put it , Western leaders appear to be throwing caution to the wind. “It’s notable how far escalation has progressed without a real Russian response,” says Kavanagh. “There is definitely a school of thought in the Western securicratic community that suggests that Russia’s a paper tiger, so we can cross as many red lines as we want,” says Leonid Ragozin, a Russian journalist and Putin critic in exile. “They believe Russia can be provoked endlessly and at some point it will reach a breaking point. But if you hear the signals from Moscow, I think they are quite unequivocal, they are going to retaliate.” In other words, Moscow’s sabotage campaign currently threatening to draw Europe directly into the conflict may well be a Russian attempt to belatedly restore deterrence. “That is absolutely the viewpoint in Moscow, that they needed to demonstrate that Europe could not continue to do the things it was doing with impunity,” says Ashford. That Moscow is now making good on the warnings it issued for years should be sobering, given how frequently and consistently it has similarly warned about its willingness to reach for the nuclear option — and how often those, too, have been dismissed in the West. The Financial Times ’ Gideon Rachman recently observed that Putin’s failure to follow through on past nuclear threats perversely means they “no longer work” as a deterrent. Yet both former CIA Director Bill Burns and former Under Secretary of Defense for Policy Colin Kahl say Russian leaders were dead serious about reaching for a nuke in fall 2022, when then-President Joe Biden told a group of donors the world was hurtling toward possible “Armageddon.” Since then, Russian military strategists have continued to talk about the need to keep nuclear use on the table against a complacent West and have carried out tests and military exercises to that end. “For many years I’ve been concerned that Russian admirals are making the argument to the Kremlin, ‘The West has really lost its mind, we need to take out a French frigate to teach them a lesson about what happens when you mess with the Russians,’” says Goldstein. “I really feel that we’re close to that moment.” Fog in a Spiral This would all be fraught enough in a perfect information environment. But a combination of the fog of war and deliberate misinformation has made it especially hard to sort what’s true from what isn’t. “We know both sides distort the truth pretty systematically,” says Goldstein. The intelligence agencies and leaders of Finland , Denmark , Estonia , and Lithuania as well as the NATO secretary general and the EU defense commissioner , have all come out in the past week to say there is no imminent threat from Russia nor intelligence suggesting one. Yet, for months, many of these same voices were saying the exact opposite, sowing public fear. “When I talk to sources particularly in Ukraine, they connect the hype to the adoption of the long-term budget of the EU for the period 2028–2034,” says Ragozin. “They believe it boils down to a battle for funding for the next six years.” “Some of the language coming out of Germany and the UK seems to me mostly driven by generating the political appetite for defense funding,” says Kavanagh. Sure enough, some European governments appear to be using the specter of war with Russia to lay the groundwork for a pump-priming policy of military Keynesianism, including in Britain, whose new chancellor has promised a “new age of industrialization” centered on rebuilding naval infrastructure. In Poland, meanwhile, a top official in the president’s office accused Sikorski and Donald Tusk, who is prime minister, of exploiting fears of Russia for their own political purposes. Experts are skeptical about the appetite of either side to actually make good on their most hawkish rhetoric. “I think they know in the backs of their minds how devastating this would be,” says Goldstein. Some European officials have effectively acknowledged outright they’re not yet ready to take on Russia. Meanwhile, for a Russia that believes it’s winning what has become a grinding attritional war, getting Europe involved could be a complication it can’t afford. Still, as countless military crises have shown, intentions don’t always matter. One reason is the way that adversaries tend to misread both each other’s actions and the way their own actions appear to the other side. If Moscow’s sabotage campaign was intended to send a message of deterrence to NATO, European and US officials are instead reading it as a deliberate “ test ” of, or attempt to weaken , the alliance’s solidarity — one that calls for their own efforts to restore deterrence against Russia , since in their view, this behavior confirms that “ strength ” is the “ one language ” that Putin understands. To that end, the EU is now exploring a NATO-like way to respond with their own form of “hybrid warfare” that, like Russia’s, theoretically falls short of a full-scale attack but proves “painful in a similar way.” “The Russians think they’re signaling that Europe is getting too comfortable with the idea that they can aid Ukraine, and Europe is seeing it as a Russian attempt to escalate conflict with them,” says Ashford. “Europe wants to believe they’re not combatants, that what they’re doing is normal and non-threatening,” says Kavanagh. “Of course, the Russians don’t see it that way and want to make that clear. Europe sees all the escalation from the Russian side and don’t seem to recognize the ways in which their own behavior is contributing to the escalation spiral.” The other reason is the extent to which European leadership has staked their own prestige on an increasingly unlikely outcome: Ukrainian military victory. The Best Outcome for All The level of physical destruction inflicted on Russia notwithstanding, Ukraine’s drone campaign this past summer proved to be just the latest costly military gambit that failed to achieve its goals: when the dust had settled, they had not succeeded in pressuring Putin to end the war or in engineering political unrest within Russia. And while Ukraine has regained some territory since, the overall state of the battlefield hasn’t fundamentally changed in its favor. “There has been tangible success, but at the tactical not strategic level,” says Ragozin. “Ukraine is showing resilience on the battlefield, but there is no breakthrough. The picture isn’t rosy for the Russians, but it’s pretty dark for the Ukrainians.” The Ukrainian government is now mired in a mushrooming political crisis of its own, suffering from serious (and sometimes literal) political infighting and looking at a massive budget shortfall — all as it weathers the most brutal Russian bombing campaign yet with little ability to defend itself, the US war with Iran having drained global supplies of missile interceptors. After years of European leaders insisting not only that Ukraine would win the war, but that it must , predicting a dark future for all of Europe if it didn’t, Ukrainian officials are now starting to openly say that their country is losing. “The leadership in Europe is so invested in this narrative of Ukrainian victory, to even slightly walk back from that and say, ‘Well, everything we’ve said for the last five years is over the top’ — they’re not going to do that,” says Goldstein. The alternatives are to escalate further — with all the risks that come with it — in the hopes of some future breakthrough or an option that European officials have tended to frame as being just as unacceptable as defeat: making painful concessions in peace talks. “It’s been clear for years what Russia wants, what its conditions are,” says Ragozin. Those conditions include Ukraine formally relinquishing its aspirations to joining NATO and, most contentiously, giving up Crimea, the Donbas, and other territories that Russian forces have illegally occupied since 2022. While Zelensky has publicly acknowledged that Ukraine lacks the means to militarily retake the Donbas and, especially Crimea, the idea of formally relinquishing these territories in negotiations remains a nonstarter in Kyiv. “The problem with Russia is it’s absolutely entrenched in those positions and not moving an inch,” says Ragozin. “Russia is ready to negotiate, but it’s not ready to compromise.” Yet the longer the war goes on in the hope of avoiding this unjust outcome, the bleaker Ukraine’s future looks. According to Poland’s Tusk, Ukraine is suffering a staggering casualty rate of 27,000 killed or wounded per month, prompting talk of drafting women and leading to aggressive and unpopular recruitment tactics that have met with increasingly violent resistance from Ukrainians. Together with a mass exodus to avoid both the war and the prospect of fighting in it, the country’s population has dramatically shrunk , and its birth rate now lags way behind its mortality rate, foreshadowing future demographic, pension, and labor crises. Under air strikes that it can no longer repel, the country’s iron and steel industry — about 5.5 percent of its GDP last year — has effectively shut down . In other words, seeking a negotiated end to the war, however, unfair the terms, could not just help shut off the current escalatory spiral and avoid a Europe-wide catastrophe but may also in the long run benefit Ukraine. “We should recognize any peace is going to be bad. No one is going to like it. It’s still better than continuing the war,” says Kavanagh. Can economic inequality be eliminated without confronting racism, patriarchy, and colonial domination? And can political revolutions produce genuine freedom if they merely replace one ruling elite with another? These questions animated an intellectually ambitious panel at the 2026 Left Coast Forum, held October 2–4 at California State University, Los Angeles. Titled "From Marx to Fanon: Class, Capital, Race, Gender, and Colonialism," the discussion brought together Kevin B. Anderson, professor of sociology, political science, and feminist studies at UC Santa Barbara; socialist feminist writer Rocio Lopez; and historian and political theorist Lyndon Porter. Their presentations challenged a familiar misconception: that Marxism concerns economic class alone, treating racial oppression, colonialism, and women's liberation as secondary issues. Instead, the panelists explored a more expansive revolutionary tradition—one that places human emancipation, rather than simply economic redistribution, at the center of political transformation. Beyond Class Struggle (36:37) Marx's Revolutionary Vision Was Bigger Than Class Kevin B. Anderson opened by examining Karl Marx's later writings, including research that remained unpublished during Marx's lifetime. Anderson argued that these writings reveal a thinker increasingly attentive to racial domination, colonial resistance, Indigenous communal traditions, and alternatives to capitalist development. One particularly revealing example concerned Marx's analysis of Irish workers in Britain. Marx recognized that English workers' hostility toward Irish immigrants served the interests of the capitalist class. Dividing workers through national and ethnic antagonism weakened their capacity to organize collectively. Anderson connected this analysis to Marx's observations about slavery and the American Civil War. Marx understood that the liberation of enslaved Black people was inseparable from the emancipation of the broader working class. Racial domination was not merely a moral injustice existing alongside capitalism. It was a mechanism through which economic and political power could be preserved. Anderson also highlighted Marx's evolving ideas about where revolutionary transformation might begin. Rather than assuming socialism would emerge first in highly industrialized Western European countries, Marx began considering how resistance within colonized societies and traditional communal agricultural systems might contribute to a global movement beyond capitalism. His research into Indigenous societies, communal landholding, and the historical position of women suggested that alternatives to capitalist social relations could emerge from multiple cultural and historical traditions. Drawing on Rosa Luxemburg's socialist feminism, Lopez argued that women's emancipation provides an essential measure of society's overall freedom. She examined how sexual violence and harassment within universities can undermine women's ability to pursue education and participate equally in public life. Lopez described institutional responses that prioritize reputational protection over the well-being of students who report abuse. For her, these failures cannot be separated from the corporate transformation of higher education, where universities increasingly treat students as customers while protecting institutional financial interests. She also examined how student debt disproportionately burdens women and how restrictions on reproductive autonomy reinforce unequal power relationships. But Lopez did not limit her criticism to conservative institutions. She discussed allegations of sexual misconduct within socialist organizations and the failures of political groups that profess equality while reproducing patriarchal relationships internally. The contradiction is fundamental. A movement cannot credibly promise universal emancipation while tolerating women's subordination within its own organizations. Lopez argued that progress against sexual violence has been uneven but real, with feminist organizing forcing some powerful political figures to face consequences. Her message was that women's liberation must be integral to socialist transformation—not something postponed until after an economic revolution. Related: War, Fascism, and the Revolutionary Roads of the Late Marx Frantz Fanon and the Psychology of Colonial Domination Lyndon Porter concluded the presentations by exploring the relationship between Marx's revolutionary humanism and the work of Frantz Fanon. Porter emphasized that Marxism, at its deepest level, concerns more than ownership of factories, economic planning, or redistribution. It asks what kinds of human beings emerge under different social arrangements. Capitalism does not simply exploit workers economically. It shapes relationships, consciousness, identity, and people's understanding of their own possibilities. Fanon extended this analysis into the colonial world. Colonial domination, Porter explained, does not merely seize land and exploit labor. It also attacks culture, identity, psychological well-being, and the humanity of colonized populations. Fanon's concept of the "zone of non-being" describes a condition in which racialized populations are denied full recognition as human beings. Within colonial societies, race and class cannot be separated into independent categories. Racial hierarchies organize employment, movement, policing, access to resources, and even how people perceive themselves. Porter connected Fanon's analysis to contemporary struggles, including Palestine, arguing that colonial relationships continue to structure unequal access to rights, land, security, and political power. He also emphasized Fanon's warning about the limitations of national independence. A colonized country can obtain its flag, government, and formal sovereignty while leaving the underlying economic and social inequalities largely intact. If a new national elite simply inherits the privileges of colonial rulers, political independence has not produced genuine liberation. Related: Orban’s Fall: Huge Ideological Defeat for Far-Right Sectors and Global Fascist Liberation Means Transforming Human Relationships Across the three presentations, a common argument emerged. Economic exploitation, racial domination, patriarchy, and colonialism cannot be understood adequately when treated as disconnected problems. They reinforce one another through institutions, political arrangements, and systems of power. Anderson showed that Marx's own revolutionary thinking developed beyond a narrow focus on industrial workers. Lopez demonstrated why women's freedom must be central to any emancipatory movement. Porter explained why Fanon's understanding of colonialism requires liberation movements to transform human relationships as well as political institutions. The panel's larger challenge was directed toward movements seeking fundamental social change. Greek Life Endures by Offering a Simulacrum of Solidarity

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Katie Gatti Tassin ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-10T16:27:49+00:00

Four thousand two hundred and twenty-nine dollars. That was the cost of joining the University of Alabama’s Delta Delta Delta Panhellenic sorority, affectionately known as “Tridelt” to its members, for the 2025 fall semester. The spring semester will run you another $4,005, and if you’d like to live in the 44,294-square-foot sorority house that sleeps seventy-eight, add another $2,886 in the fall and $2,490 in the spring. This is, as Alabama sororities go, on the affordable side. The most expensive sorority, Zeta Tau Alpha (or simply “Zeta” for those in the know), estimates that a full school year of dues and in-house living totals $19,898, or nearly double the average in-state tuition at an American public university. The cost includes room and board, which is fitting, because Greek life tends to be all-consuming. You will learn to eat, sleep, and breathe it. I was a ’Bama Tridelt from 2013 to 2017, a few years before the “Bama Rush” phenomenon barnstormed TikTok like a wealthy, white, womanly NFL draft of indistinguishable twangy freshmen. In my day, the dues were closer to $6,000 per year if you lived out of the house. I remember this clearly because, after learning the cost, my parents told me in no uncertain terms that the only reason I could join a sorority was that my scholarship paid for tuition. I suspect they regretted this almost immediately when I called them, eighteen years old and very drunk, on a bus to our “pledge retreat” — at the four-star-resort-cum-convention-center in Birmingham where we were whisked hours after receiving our “bids,” or invitations to join — crying about how I felt homesick. But they must have estimated, despite not participating in Greek life themselves, that the economic opportunity it would afford me would be worth the money and trouble. That the $6,000 per year was in some sense an investment in my future. They were right. As the nation reels from yet another fraternity rape story, this time out of Cornell , some interlocutors have wondered whether banning Greek life outright might solve the problem, eliminating the most fetid hotbeds of sexual assault on college campuses. The debate raises plenty of worthwhile questions about institutions’ role in shaping microcultures, the legality and efficacy of outright abolition, and whether fraternities should be understood as a symptom of misogyny and sexual violence or the virus itself. But I have a simpler question: Why, in a culture defined by atomization and largely devoid of any semblance of associational life, has Greek life — at times deadly and destructive — endured? Every year the ranks of those belonging to churches, clubs, leagues, and unions shrink, and yet, as evidenced by the fervor of RushTok, the number of new recruits to Greek life continues to rise. According to a now-archived web page from, ironically, the Dean’s Office at Cornell University, only 2 percent of the US population is or was involved in Greek life. But, the page notes, “80% of the top executives of Fortune 500 companies,” “76% of current United States Senators and Congressmen,” “40 of 47 Supreme Court Justices,” and “all but two” presidents were fraternity members. (The page claims the information comes from an undated and unlinked New York Times article that I was unable to locate. If you poke around enough university agitprop for Greek life, though, you’ll find these phantom stats repeated.) The Cornell missive approvingly dubs this “the power of the 2 percent,” commending fraternities for their “leadership, independence, scholastic achievement and service to their various campuses and communities.” As a former-participant-turned-skeptic, I think fraternities and sororities are better understood as subscription services for access and advantage, a hyperreal allegory for the promises and problems of American capitalism writ large. They are rare, membership-based organizations that bolster, rather than threaten, the power structures that dominate American life, which explains much of their staying power. Elite Reproduction Within just a few weeks of joining my sorority, an older member approached me in the campus Starbucks — “the largest Starbucks by square feet in the world,” the student tour guide had boasted on my campus visit, to my awestruck approval. No doubt the older member had noticed me sitting in the pumpkin-spice latte holding pen fondling my newly treasured Tridelt sweatshirt. She heard I was a public relations major, she said, and wanted to help integrate me in the enormous communications school. It is hard to overstate the impact this woman had on my college experience and later employment prospects. At the time she was the “agency director” of the campus’s premier communications extracurricular, a student-run PR firm that glistened on résumés. Her approval fast-tracked my acceptance into the program, I’m sure, which accepted only a few dozen students. By senior year, I was the agency director and had — I can only assume now — three solid job offers because of it. This type of networking is a major function of sororities and fraternities, but that wasn’t why I joined. At eighteen, I joined for all the most humiliating and human reasons — my concerns were shallow, selfish, superficial. I was not thinking about my labor market prospects or elite reproduction. I was thinking about the fact that I wanted to be at once special and assimilated. I wanted acceptance from and association with people who I sensed — in some proto-class-conscious way, nascently alert to its hierarchy but not yet disgusted by it — were better than me, a kid from a no-name town in Kentucky known only for its sprawling Amazon regional fulfillment center. And I wanted to get drunk with boys! But my experience — being plucked out by a high-achieving and generous older member then ushered along the path to accolades and social capital — is indicative of the particular privilege inherent to the Greek system, which can transform the metaphoric big city into a close-knit small town. The mechanism is far less organized than outsiders may assume. There’s no formal process by which alumni committees funnel applications through the side door of Fortune 500 companies. The power of membership is softer, more discreet — less outright patronage than fast-tracked human resources. For those with hiring power, it’s a sanctioned way of assuring “cultural fit,” identifying applicants who’ve been exposed to the same milieux, traditions, and, it should be said, socioeconomic background as yourself. It’s telling, then, that one of America’s last enduring civic institutions in an era otherwise characterized by alienation is a social club predicated on reproducing class and racial hierarchy, culled from an already-elite subgroup of the population with the time and money to spend on a college education. Greek Mythology But some scholarship on the topic suggests the correlation between participation and career advancement is spurious; that kids with the sort of wealth and access that can lead to illustrious job prospects also tend to be the kids who end up in a sorority or fraternity. It was a reduction in grade point average that was associated with membership, the researchers found, and they were unable to identify any causal “Greek salary premium.” My experience demonstrates how it can grease the wheels of campus involvement, but the benefits are far more tenuous at a population level. And the oft-repeated-by-universities statistics about the success of members (e.g., “all but two presidents”)? Also vastly overstated . The real figures, across every vaunted category listed above, are closer to one in four, not three in four. Still disproportionate representation, to be sure, but far from the majority. These realities — scant evidence that membership itself actually improves career prospects; a battery of self-aggrandizing, invented statistics that make fraternities look like an impenetrable fast track to a congressional seat or executive boardroom — are all part of the carefully constructed myth. The relationship between Greek life and American privilege is obvious and undeniable, yes, but overstating its benefits, power, and impunity risks further boosting the self-mythologizing. That it is above both the law and grubby proletarian economic concerns is a powerful component of the legend that persists, in large part, because it has been permitted to. Time after time, the system’s worst offenses are deemed acceptable-if-unfortunate downsides for all that perceived membership upside. So much about the realities of Greek life and its influence was fuzzy even from the inside, mixing the real with the unreal as mythology often does. Before rush week even began, I heard rumors that Phi Mu’s initiation involved nakedly sitting atop a running washing machine and having whatever jiggled circled with a Sharpie. Tridelt’s supposedly involved swallowing a live goldfish. Those were both false. Other rumors swirled about a shadowy cabal-like meta-fraternity called The Machine that drove political outcomes in both student government and Alabama state politics. This was true: Senator Katie Britt, famed owner of an enormous diamond cross necklace and an unsettlingly bare kitchen , was the Machine-backed student body president at the University of Alabama in 2003. It was and is an insular world of bizarre rituals and rigid intragroup hierarchies, and it surprises me in retrospect how seamlessly I became fluent in its language and assented to its terms and conditions; how quickly a sort of Capitalist Realism –style foreclosure of alternatives made it seem like the only viable option. This disorienting blend of fact and fiction seems most relevant to me now: that Greek life brings advantages but fewer than you might assume; that it is uniquely harmful, and yet is, in many ways, no different from every other elite institution with too much money. After all, a poisonous cocktail of nepotism, grotesque wealth, and unpunished sexual violence aptly summarizes the president of the United States, a man who has turned all of Washington into the fraternity he never had. I had been permitted to enter this world, I can see now, but only conditionally. That acute sense of conditionality thrummed beneath my time there — my membership conditional on my parents’ continued willingness and ability to pay and my acceptance conditional on my continued ability to conform to social norms that were harder to define, most of all a slippery but narrow vision of modern Southern womanhood. But what I remember most about that time, humming beneath the raucous roar of tailgates and lifted Chevy Tahoes, was the loneliness. It was usually muffled by the bluster and grandiosity my participation seemed to afford me, but sometimes, on the rogue Sunday night, it crept in. In other words, the broader cultural alienation existed within this structure, too, a simulacrum of solidarity that sent me searching in adulthood for the real thing. In this way it would come to resemble my experience navigating the economy writ large, the creeping, inescapable knowledge that the advantages I was being sold were more often than not predicated on many more people’s exclusion from them. This unshakable discomfort is what led me to embrace a socialist politics, a vision for the world that offered a sturdier and more scalable means of belonging and flourishing conditional only on something none of us can afford to lose but that the status quo so creatively degrades: our humanity.
