Rust Belt Rising

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Rochester Red Star: News from Rochester DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-07T20:21:52+00:00

by Hannah W. The first ever Rust Belt Rising conference was held on August 15th and 16th here in Rochester. This was organized by Workers Assembly of Rochester (WAR), an independent organization of local labor activists, including DSA members, union staffers and organizers. Local labor leaders, rank and file members and members of community organizations gathered to discuss and learn about the role of the labor movement in Rochester, and how we can move it to be more militant and left. Opening Keynote The opening keynote was a panel containing Colin, an organizer with the Teamsters Local 118, Christina, President of the Federation of Social Workers and Max from Buffalo DSA and Field Director for the Adam Bojak Campaign. The topic was How to Build an Independent Worker Program, which I think is a relevant topic given the current political state. Comrade Max talked about the role labor plays in elections, citing the work he and other comrades have done for Adam Bojak for the 149th assembly. While many unions did endorse Bojak, “We want labor to be a partner, not just a stamp”. Max posed to the audience what difference does union endorsement make, especially if they’re not turning out members for electoral races. While they may provide money for mailers, having people to knock doors is the most effective way to reach voters. He also spoke about the need to move away from the Democratic Party and not waste time and resources trying to take over local Democratic committees. Starting to run your own candidates, and that a sense of “ownership” will be felt by the members. We’ve seen this with Clarie Valdez as an example of a rank and file union member and DSA member who ran for state assembly and now Congress. Colin spoke about the union endorsement process that candidates go through and asked if that process is democratic. Do members have the ability to ask questions of the candidates? Do members have the final vote for the endorsement? Colin noted that this gatekeeping of democracy comes from entrenched union leadership wanting to keep power, rather than allow the rank and file to make decisions. We’ve seen this play out with unions endorsing candidates like Joe Morelle. Colin also spoke on how unions think we’re organizing to support candidates but oftentimes, the politicians are the ones “organizing us.” Christina spoke about beating apathy by having continuous conversations with membership. From engaging them in endorsements to participating in co-governance with endorsed candidates. This looks like holding town halls with electeds, starting mobilization committees, and shifting the union perspective to an organizing mindset. While not all members have the same perspective, this will lead to a more engaged membership. She also talked about the need to work with community groups to build strength and solidarity. Overall I was impressed with the panelists’ perspectives on building out an independent program. This is a long term goal to achieve, but I think the first step is educating the rank and file. In doing so, we can hopefully have members active in electoral campaigns, and end the cycle of endorsing candidates who don’t stand with the working class. Workshops Throughout the conference there were 5 different blocks of workshops with 16 unique workshops. Overall the variety in the programming was impressive, from union contracts, to organizing history and current politics. Get a Job to Organize: Salting This was about salting a workplace. For those unfamiliar with the term, Salting is getting a job with the explicit purpose of unionizing the workplace. Doing so is not an easy task as you have to build out a list of who would want to join in this effort. The workshop went over what it takes to salt, the qualities of a good salt and what the process may look like. The main skill being communication, as salters will have numerous conversations with their co-workers. Colin mentioned a learning on his end of making sure the timeline is communicated to the salter before taking a job. An example is Amazon as this is a long term operation that is 15 – 20 years and a salter should be willing to commit for several years. In addition, he spoke to the desire to have salts stay through the unionization effort and become shop stewards. In doing so, it can ensure the union stays militant and left-leaning. DSA has its own Workers Organizing Workers program, which will train members on how to salt. If you are a member of DSA and interested in salting, please reach out to the chapter and we can get you connected. Secrets of a Successful Organizer: Beating Apathy Kari from Labor Notes gave this training on how to beat apathy. When we think about organizing, we need to think about our list of members as a bullseye (pictured below). This helps determine the level of membership involvement in a given union/organization. The training covered different reasons members wouldn’t get engaged and proposed solutions to counter that. Once we’ve built out our list, the training went over common barriers as to why people might not engage. These four barriers were fear, hopelessness, division and confusion. The room was ask to split amongst those four barriers. In each group, we were asked to give an example of our word and then provide solutions to beating this. Secrets of a Successful Organizer: Assembling your Dream Team Assembling your Dream Team is a continuation from the previous session of Beating Apathy. This workshop went over how to identify leaders within a workplace and bring them into your organizing committee. From there building out a list of your workplace and identifying your members/coworkers who are core, active, disengaged, supportive and hostile. Kari stressed the importance of listwork to identify members that could be leaders. The workshop had us go through two scenarios. One where we were trying to organize a workplace to wear stickers. This was to get us to think of mapping out our workplace, who works there, who talks to who. From there we went over ways to identify leaders in the workplace. Lastly we went through a scenario of identifying leaders for an organizing committee at a hotel. My work with member engagement has familiarized me with the bullseye method. While it is tedious to go through around 550 records, it is important work. Identifying where members lie helps us determine who we need to reengage with and potentially turn into active members. In doing so, we can build leadership and bring members up within the chapter. What NOT to do if you want an active membership The final workshop I attended was things not to do if you want membership engaged. Colin led this workshop and was mainly posing questions to the audience. The first was about involvement and why members don’t engage or get involved. Answers ranged from cliques and gatekeeping to lack of understanding from membership. Second was phrases used that people have heard. Responses included “That’s how it’s done,” “That’s not how it works.” Of the responses, it was noted that sometimes these are valid. On the other hand, we also must think about what message that conveys to membership. Does it allow them to engage or eventually solve the issue on their own? The session ended with asks for all the organizers present to: Invite Listen Delegate Train Support Move on The workshop for me helped change my perspective about how I answer questions members may direct at me. Instead of taking on the work myself, am I able to delegate and empower said members. In doing so this can help create a more involved membership and also build the skills members need to become leaders. Day 1 Closing: New Union Organizing The Day 1 closing was a panel about new union organizing. This included RUNAP, Webster & Penfield EMT (Teamsters 118?), U of R Grad Labor Union (SEIU 200) and Home Healthcare Workers (1199 SEIU). Each of the panelists were given the same set of questions to answer. One question that stood out to me was about tactics they tried that fell flat. For the Webster & Penfield EMT, it was them not anticipating how aggressive the union busting campaign would be. While they did prepare, it was far more hostile than what they expected. The same was also said for the U of R grad union, as UofR changed tactics once Trump was re-elected in 2024. Matt from RUNAP mentioned ensuring that people stay involved and enthusiastic, but their expectations don’t go beyond what the union can actually do. This leads to needing to educate membership. Lastly, Mike from 1199 SEIU emphasized the importance of tools and speed for organizing the ~200,000 home healthcare workers in New York who are now eligible for union membership. Through organizing, workers can create power and pressure on the boss and win. While some, like the Grad Union are still waiting to be recognized, they were able to win wage increases through organizing. With the continued organizing of unions in the Rochester area, we can build worker power and fight for what is rightfully ours. Day 2 Closing: Upcoming Labor Fights The final panel of the conference was on upcoming labor fights here in the Rochester area. This included 1199 SEIU, SEIU 200 and RUNAP. This covered the two largest employers in the area: University of Rochester and Rochester Regional Health. As stated during the panel, these employers set the economic health of the Rochester region. If they underpay their workers, it brings down wages across the entire community. Part of the panel discussed bargaining units and what the main concerns of their members are. Overall, the top concern was health care and benefits. It’s no secret that companies have moved the American population over to high deductible plans to allow wealth transfer to insurance CEOs. I asked the panelists how the NY Health Act (Single payer health care) would benefit them during bargaining. The panelists were all in favor of the bill noting it would allow them to not spend the fight on health care, and bargain for other benefits such as child care. Overall Thoughts First I want to recognize the organizing effort that went into this by my fellow WAR members. From reaching out to unions, to getting panels and booking the venue. I appreciate the work that was done to make this a successful event. I think for the first ever conference I enjoyed going to it and did learn from it and view it as a success. From what I talked with others, some of the workshops could be improved. However I think that is somewhat standard for conferences given that not every workshop will be a success. Perhaps more vetting and walkthroughs of the workshops might be helpful prior to the conference. In addition, evaluations of the workshops can also help the presenters as well. Outside of ROC DSA there were a handful of other DSA members that attended the event. The beginning panel had two DSA members on it calling out socialism explicitly. I think as DSA we should continue to build relationships with members of unions. I think conferences like this are a great opportunity to learn more about local organizing efforts and have direct conversations with union members. To me, the best part was the opening panel as it spoke to the need to divert resources and power from the establishment. In doing so, we can elect people who will actually support and fight for the working class, rather than use us for a photo op. This conference helped reshape the way I view myself as an organizer and the actions I take. The people I talked to were in some form of elected leadership or staffers, with some unelected rank and file members. My hope with this conference is to have more unelected rank and file members join in the coming years. I was impressed with how the conference went and I’m looking forward to the next one. Disclosure Day and the Ultimate Other

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: DSA Religion and Socialism Working Group ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-07T14:25:24+00:00

As someone with a space science background and a long-term interest in the search for extraterrestrial intelligence ( SETI ), I could not avoid the recent film on that topic, Disclosure Day . The film fits within the genre of UFO flicks dating back at least to the classic The Flying Saucer (1950) , the first film on the topic. The film’s release also coincides with recent testimonies of government officials claiming that the U.S. government is secretly studying and reverse-engineering extraterrestrial technology and the recent study by NASA on Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAPs). In Disclosure Day , the character Daniel Kellner (Josh O’Connor) goes rogue against his company, the Wardex Corporation, a government contractor that works with extraterrestrial technology and experiments on captured extraterrestrials with the goal of reverse-engineering their technology. Kellner, believing his company is on the wrong track, steals a device called the Archive, which. contains records of extraterrestrial technology dating back to the Roswell incident . Kellner is almost caught, but escapes with his girlfriend, Jane (Eve Hewson). Meanwhile, in Kansas City, news meteorologist Margaret Fairchild (Emily Blunt) has an incident where she speaks in an unknown language. Unbeknownst to her, Margaret is speaking in an extraterrestrial language. After this incident, Margaret is pursued by Wardex agents and eventually encounters Daniel. The two eventually realize that they were both abducted as children by extraterrestrials and given complementary powers. Near the end of the film, they team up with ex-Wardex agents to reveal the truth of the existence of extraterrestrials to the world. Disclosure Day fits in with recent films in which the extraterrestrials are portrayed in such a way to make them seem more mysterious. This includes films like Arrival (2016) and Interstellar (2014) where the extraterrestrials are portrayed as mysterious, even godlike, but ultimately benevolent and films where the extraterrestrials are portrayed as malevolent or Lovecraftian , such as the 2016 film adaptation of The Three Body Problem , a science fiction novel by the Chinese author Cixin Liu. In a post-secular world, it could be argued that godlike extraterrestrials, whether benevolent saviors of humanity or Lovecraftian monstrosities, are an example of the current appetite for the re-enchantment of the world. One of the ways this type of re-enchantment overlaps with politics is a curious left-wing political tradition that sees advanced extraterrestrials as playing a key role in advancing human progress. The Argentinean revolutionary Juan Posadas , founder of Fourth International Posadism, wrote an essay in 1968 arguing that spacefaring extraterrestrials, being more technologically advanced than humans, would also need to be more socially advanced and therefore socialist. Posadas believed that the socialist countries should call upon extraterrestrials to help them in the fight against capitalism . Another example of a space religion that significantly influenced left-wing movements is Russian cosmism . Russian cosmism was founded by the Russian Orthodox mystic Nikolai Fyodorov, who was also a mentor to Konstantin Tsiolkovsky, the father of Soviet and Russian spaceflight. Fyodorov believed that science would one day resurrect the dead and that space colonization would be required to make room for everyone who was to be resurrected. Russian cosmism was initially suppressed in the Soviet Union. Nonetheless, it continued to influence the Soviet space program. The initial Soviet headstart in the space race with launching Sputnik 1 , the first satellite , into space in 1957 and launching the first human, Yuri Gagarin on Vostok 1 , into space in 1961 were both hailed in the Soviet Union as the victory of socialism over capitalism, proof that space exploration was intrinsic to progress. While Russian or Soviet cosmism did not necessarily involve extraterrestrials, it did form the background of Soviet speculation about life on other planets. This included science fiction authors, such as the Russian revolutionary and scientist Alexander Bagdonov, who wrote Red Star about a Martian civilization that had achieved socialism. It also included scientists, such as the astronomer Nikolai Kardashev, the inventor of the Kardashev scale , which classifies hypothetical civilizations by energy capacity output, and the astronomer Iosif Shklovsky, the pre-eminent SETI research scientist in the Soviet Union and co-author of the book Intelligent Life in the Universe in 1966 with the U.S. astronomer Carl Sagan. Like their U.S. counterparts, Soviet scientists and science fiction authors interested in the search for extraterrestrial intelligence showed an openness to learning from intelligences other than their own. In the NASA report Anthropology, Archaeology, and Interstellar Communication , extraterrestrials are referred to as the “ultimate others” in an essay by Ben Finney and Jerry Bentley. This perhaps unintentionally echoes more traditional religious movements that emphasize hospitality and love of “the other.” The late Pope Franics wrote about “the sacred ground of the other” in his apostalic exhortation Evangelii Gaudium. An older example of such thinking in religious contexts is the tradition of Marguerite Porete (c. 1258-1310), a medieval mystic and member of the Beguines , an all-female quasi-monastic lay religious order that was part of the larger heretical Brethren of the Free Spirit. The Brethren were known for antinomianism and libertinism , but also a form of radical hospitality and egalitarianism that is described by the situationist author Raoul Vaneigem in the book Movement of the Free Spirit as a precursor to later revolutionary and socialist movements. Porete, in her book The Mirror of Simple Souls , taught that a person needed to go through stages of increasing self-denial and realization of God’s love in order to become truly free to submit to the Holy Spirit. One stage of this was to see God as the ultimate other. In seeing God as the ultimate other, the person would then become more open to the other in general and be able to truly love others. The current political scene not just in the United States but around the world is defined by increasing hostility toward the other as well as hostility toward immigrants and refugees across the United States; Europe ; and, recently, South Africa and Australia . The underlying issues behind these conflicts are complex and solutions are still unclear. But one thing that is clearly needed is greater openness to embracing and cooperating with the other to create a world that is less unequal and more peaceful, one in which we can solve problems such as the climate crisis and the risk of misaligned artificial intelligence . Perhaps an encounter with the ultimate other, whether divine or extraterrestrial and whether good or bad, would enable us to have better encounters with the “others” who live next to us and are not all that different from us in the cosmic scheme. The Canton–Hong Kong Strike Changed China’s History

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Promise Li ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-07T13:01:26+00:00

A century ago, workers in Hong Kong and Guangzhou — then generally known in English as Canton — waged one of the longest continuous general strikes in history. At the time, foreign powers such as Britain and Japan had carved China into spheres of influence. Wave after wave of students’ and workers’ movements struggled against this imperialist control, especially the May Fourth Movement of 1919. This anti-imperialist upsurge reached a fever pitch on May 30, 1925. That day, the police opened fire on students who were marching toward the British-controlled part of Shanghai to protest the murder of a Chinese worker at a Japanese-owned cotton factory during a recent strike. The May 30 massacre spurred workers in Hong Kong, then under direct British colonial rule, to strike in June. The Canton–Hong Kong strike lasted sixteen months, shutting down a central British commercial outpost. It was also the impetus for China’s national reunification for the first time outside of monarchical rule. Aided by the organizing acumen of a nascent Communist Party of China (CPC), then operating within the big-tent Kuomintang (KMT), Chinese workers governed Canton and paralyzed Hong Kong. China’s political context has completely changed since the strike. The KMT brutally crushed the CPC six months after the strike’s end, and the Communists retreated from the cities to rebuild in rural areas over the next two decades, eventually managing to defeat the KMT in 1949. The CPC has since led China as a one-party state, the People’s Republic of China (PRC), changing ideological direction in the 1980s and leading China’s turn to capitalism. Today Beijing has become a global superpower rivaling Washington. The PRC has prohibited workers’ movements from coalescing into independent organizations that can contest for political power. Its official histories have converted the experience of workers’ revolt a century ago into merely an expression of patriotism, and we need to reclaim the memory of that experience. Those workers built democratic unions to fight imperialism while transforming bureaucratic unions along the way. The strike showed that the working class led Hong Kong’s first struggle for universal suffrage — a demand ignored by the British and still refused by today’s PRC. The strike’s legacy thus offers an alternative to the PRC’s great-power nationalism and chauvinism that is grounded in workers’ independent organization and democracy. China’s Warlord Era China underwent a massive political transformation throughout the 1920s. The decade began with disarray and fragmentation. Rival military cliques backed by different imperialist powers divided the country starting in the 1910s. They jockeyed for power after the fall of the Qing Dynasty and former Qing General Yuan Shi-kai’s rule. By 1928, the KMT had unified the entire country under its rule by defeating these warlords. The party traced its roots to anti-Qing revolutionary groups in the 1900s linked to Cantonese revolutionary Sun Yat-sen. Sun and his followers were mainly intellectuals, supported by some triad-linked brotherhoods and secret societies, advancing a vision of modernizing and unifying China. In the 1910s, the KMT’s organizational infrastructure and political program were loose and ill-defined. It also lacked a well-organized military unit. This changed when a Cantonese general, Chen Jiong-ming, took power in Canton and welcomed Sun back from exile to his home region in 1920. Shortly after, Sun broke with Chen and set up a government in the region under a reorganized KMT. The main foreign power that backed him was the Soviet Union. The Bolshevik international strategy at the time rested on supporting Communist forces in alliance with broad nationalist movements to overthrow colonial empires. With the help of Bolshevik advisers, Sun transformed the KMT into a cohesive political party with a regional base in Canton in the early 1920s. The reorganized KMT allowed Communists to join as individuals while also incorporating China’s weak but emerging capitalist class under its political umbrella. Anti-imperialism became a bedrock of the KMT’s nationalist program, unifying the Left and the Right within the KMT. When Sun died in early 1925, his successor, Wang Jing-wei, continued his alliance with the Communists as the first president of the KMT’s Canton government. The CPC, formed by revolutionary intellectuals in 1921, began organizing workers in northern and central China. This period saw the organized working class emerging for the first time in China’s political arena. Workers resisted rampant labor abuses and exploitation, exacerbated by the competition between foreign firms and local employers as industry started to develop in China. Although anarchists and Communists led some of these struggles, traditional guilds and other conservative labor organizations dominated many workers’ organizations. The influence of reactionary labor forces was particularly acute in Canton and Hong Kong, where craft guilds, corrupt “yellow” unions, and triad-linked secret societies were the main tendencies organizing workers. The KMT enjoyed great prestige in these regions during the early 1920s, especially among militant workers deeply inspired by their kinsman Sun’s nationalism. All this made it hard to organize workers independently. Canton had little by way of large-scale heavy industry, unlike China’s other major cities, like Shanghai. Most Cantonese workers were scattered across small-shop handicraft and household industries. Hong Kong had more modern industries, mainly British monopolies, like transport, utilities, commerce, and shipbuilding. Though divided in terms of sovereignty, workers in Canton and Hong Kong shared strong bonds. The Chinese in Hong Kong were mostly migrants from other provinces. The great majority came from Canton, which was adjacent to Hong Kong. These links were crucial for workers’ solidarity during the strike. Nonetheless, compared with the north, conditions in the south did not seem as favorable to the growth of an industrial union movement. The Strike’s Outbreak When the CPC sent its best labor organizer, Deng Zhong-xia, to Hong Kong to organize a general strike in solidarity with Shanghai’s anti-imperialist movement, he thus had few expectations of success. Deng came from a different region and spoke a different dialect: he relied on a Hong Kong Communist worker, Su Zhao-zheng, who had led a seamen’s strike in 1922, to build trust with Cantonese workers. Still, Deng was not completely unknown among the Cantonese workers. As the early CPC’s main labor leader, Deng had just organized China’s second national labor congress. This congress proved pivotal for the party in clarifying a labor strategy that it put into practice in the Canton–Hong Kong strike. Held just before the May 30 massacre, this gathering called on workers to form their own organizations independent from the bourgeoisie. It also established that workers’ economic and political struggles were inseparable. In other words, Communists had to extend the scope of workers’ struggles for better working conditions to fight the alliance of the capitalist class with warlords and imperialists. Some of Canton and Hong Kong’s most powerful unions, such as the employer-friendly mechanics’ union, boycotted the congress and protested its militant tone. Deng knew he would face the difficult task of dealing with these unions as he arrived in Hong Kong. But when Deng and Su held a cross-union meeting to call for a strike, a majority, including these yellow unions, voted to authorize it. In a later reflection , Deng suggested that even corrupt union leaders believed they could benefit from a strike at that moment. They were keen to siphon money from the strike fund and increase their prestige and power in the labor movement. The CPC leaders also helped persuade the yellow union leaders by inviting them to participate in the strike committee. But there was another crucial element to the strike vote. The massacre in Shanghai was a turning point for Cantonese workers’ hatred of British imperialism. Sensing this opportunity, Deng, Su, and the CPC’s Hong Kong branch — then only a handful of dockworkers and students — went directly into the factories to agitate the workers. Though the seamen’s union was taken over by corrupt leaders after the 1922 strike, seamen and dockworkers still revered Su, who easily rallied them for the general strike. As historian Daniel Kwan has documented , another CPC cadre, Yang Yin, passionately appealed to a sense of patriotism in seeking to persuade even triad and secret society labor leaders that they had a role to play in a time of national crisis. Thus, as Deng explained, the CPC had a two-pronged strategy: persuading union leaders directly while also agitating among workers to push their own leaders to act. Despite their support, many conservative labor leaders dragged their feet, resulting in the CPC-friendly unions — led by tram workers, seamen, typesetters, and foreign-firm workers — striking first on June 19. But the feelings of anger and restlessness among the union’s rank and file were too strong for Hong Kong’s conservative union bureaucracy to contain. Rank-and-file mechanics struck on their own without official approval from their union leadership. The seamen even physically threatened their union leader to issue an order to strike. If he refused, the workers declared, they would “greet him with their fists.” The Canton–Hong Kong strike uplifted the anti-imperialist demands coming from the movement in Shanghai to abolish China’s unequal treaties with imperialist powers. But its horizon did not stop there. The strike demands were as follows: absolute freedom of the Chinese to gather, organize, speak, publish, and strike; equal treatment of all Hong Kongers, whether Chinese or Western, under the law, and the immediate abolition of all laws and actions targeting Chinese residents for deportation and cruel punishment; every Chinese worker must be granted the right to vote, to satisfy the basic principle of universal suffrage; the introduction of labor laws, including an eight-hour day and a minimum wage, the abolition of contract labor, improvements to the livelihoods of women and child workers, and the implementation of workers’ insurance, with representatives from labor unions included when drafting these laws; the cancellation of proposed rent increases on July 1, with a 40 percent cut to rents instead; freedom of residence to the Chinese in Hong Kong. The strike combined economic and political demands that identified the roots of the workers’ exploitative conditions in colonial rule. Most significantly, it was the earliest moment at which Hong Kongers demanded universal suffrage in a mass movement. This echoed the early CPC’s demand for a wider struggle for democracy across China. The strike showed that the working class was the vanguard of Hong Kong’s fight for decolonization and democracy. A massacre by British troops transformed the strike into a mass movement. On June 23, one hundred thousand people from Canton held a march to Hong Kong in solidarity with the strike. When they passed by Shaji Road, British soldiers in the occupied district of Shamian across the river opened fire and killed dozens of protesters. The massacre drastically broadened the scale of the strike. Workers demanded that London compensate and apologize for the killings. Tens of thousands of workers in Hong Kong then joined the strike and left for Canton. Striking Through Workers’ Democracy The strike had a major impact on Hong Kong. The central strategy was to block access to Hong Kong for sea traffic. Two months in, the strikers decided to only target British ships, a boycott that lasted until October 1926. This strategic shift discouraged Cantonese merchants from opposing the strike and prevented a united front among imperialist powers from crushing it. Meanwhile, Hong Kong was in a state of shambles. Deng recalled that the number of ships passing through Hong Kong in 1925 was only 16 percent of the 1924 level. Hyperinflation reigned, and the city’s major industry, shipping, almost ground to a halt. Hong Kong’s financial institutions nearly went bankrupt. Rocked by its own wave of labor unrest back home, London had little will to amass a military invasion force that could end the strike. CPC cadres organized workers to maintain the strike with a highly disciplined formation governed by a robust democratic structure. The highest decision-making body was the Strikers’ Delegates Congress, which featured eight hundred delegates from unions, each representing one hundred workers. This body met every second day for more than a year to discuss the strike’s direction and organization. The committee openly published its minutes and resolutions in a magazine, Workers’ Road , that the strikers closely followed. The other main leadership body was an elected strike committee, chaired by Su, that was responsible for implementing the decisions made by the congress. Beneath the committee and congress were a host of departments in charge of different types of organizing, from coordinating finances and other logistical tasks to political education. The CPC built influence by helping to design and administer this efficient structure. It also provided political leadership by enabling strikers to participate in these institutions of workers’ democracy. Deng and other party cadres led an advisory committee that helped develop resolutions for discussion and debate in the congress. CPC cadres mobilized workers’ representatives to put forward motions and win others over to their tactics and positions. They organized worker leaders to read articles from Workers’ Road to strikers patrolling to enforce the boycott before their duties each morning, so workers could stay informed about political affairs. This guidance provided a mostly illiterate population of workers with an invaluable opportunity to practice expressing themselves politically and making decisions on how society should be run. All of this was still being organized under the aegis and financial support of the KMT regime, but Deng hoped that these experiments in workers’ democracy could develop into the seeds of a workers’ soviet. He even called the Strikers’ Delegates Congress the “herald of the future Chinese workers’ government.” For over a year, the scale of the strike expanded so much that the strikers’ organization became nearly indistinguishable from regional governance itself. The congress tasked workers with forming groups to patrol provincial borders and ports, making sure that British goods did not circulate. This “patrolling” often involved violent battles, as the strikers clashed with smugglers and other disrupters. Over a hundred workers lost their lives throughout the course of the strike in these pickets. One early threat to the strike came from Chen’s troops, who now received British support. But the workers’ patrols were so militarily effective that Wang could mainly rely on them to defeat Chen. These patrols practically became a workers’ militia that defended the authority of the strikers’ congress and the jurisdiction of the KMT itself. The success of the strike and the anti-Chen campaign initially strengthened the influence of Wang and the KMT’s left wing over the Canton KMT government. Wang largely left the congress in charge of the strike, and the strike committee commandeered and repurposed civic spaces to conduct its daily affairs. It ordered gambling houses, brothels, temples, and clan buildings to be turned into hostels and dining halls for strikers. The congress even set up its own court to arbitrate any disputes that arose during the strike. This court became a democratic judiciary that partly ran the city. An American correspondent from The Nation declared it to be “the only court in China where civil cases were properly tried.” The strike was an occasion for Cantonese workers to practice governing through workers’ democracy in a region long mired in conservative trade unionism. The congress empowered workers to elect their leaders, formulate structured political proposals, decide on political priorities, and act collectively to execute these decisions. Workers debated and passed nearly three hundred political resolutions within a year of the strike. The resolutions broke with many long-standing Cantonese union practices. For one, they mandated unions to be organized democratically with an elected committee, hold regular meetings, and act as vehicles for class struggle. As historian Jean Chesneaux noted , workers formed over eighty unions within a year of the strike, and elected committees ran all but twelve of the 168 Cantonese unions at its height. A Cross-Class Alliance? At the strike’s height, as Chesneaux described, “the strategy and organization of the strike were essentially in the hands of Communist cadres, and neither the nationalist government nor the people of Canton had any illusions about this.” But the inability of the Communists to form genuinely independent workers’ organizations ultimately contributed to the strike’s failure. At the beginning of the strike, the CPC was able to contain the ambitions of Canton’s merchants. It would have been impossible to gather broad union support for the strike without some compromises. The decision to narrow the scope of the boycott to just British goods meant there would be less competition and more profit for the local bourgeoisie. Deng summed up the CPC’s approach toward this conditional alliance as such: “Not yielding a foot in important matters, but being flexible in secondary matters . . . we will find a way to divide their forces later.” The power of the Communists lay in being the most organized force in the strike committee. Deng argued that the best way to combat the reactionary unions was through workers’ democracy. This meant centralizing major decisions in the congress, where striking workers were most concentrated and had the most power to decide on their own affairs. They opposed their corrupt union leaders by contesting their tactics in open view of the elected representatives of the working class. At the same time, the Canton CPC was not visible enough to organize workers independently from the KMT. The KMT was responsible for a significant amount of the funding of the strike, supporting the strike committee with a sizable monthly stipend to run its affairs. Though the KMT played a passive role in the strike, it nevertheless ran the Canton administration and backed the strike committee. The dual party affiliations of the Communists and their deep involvement in the day-to-day spadework of the strike made it hard to concentrate on recruitment into the CPC. Even when striking unions represented over 150,000 workers one year into the strike, there were still only around three hundred party members. Despite the power of the congress, it did not express a political infrastructure independent from the KMT into which the CPC could rally workers. This lack of class independence was the key weakness of the Communist strategy. The Communists were unable to organize workers under their own banner and program. This left an opening that the capitalist class and the KMT right could exploit. A Turn of Fortune The Left’s hegemony was never quite secure. The cracks were showing even at the height of the strike committee’s power. Less than two months into the strike, Liao Zhong-kai, one of the top pro-Soviet and left-wing leaders in the KMT Executive Committee, was mysteriously assassinated. Many suspected the KMT right, but no proof was ever found. Liao’s assassination did not immediately benefit the KMT right. Wang ousted a few leaders of the KMT right such as Xu Chong-zhi, who led the KMT armed forces. By early 1926, however, the KMT right discovered a new champion — Chiang Kai-shek. Chiang was a KMT military officer who rapidly rose in the KMT ranks after taking leadership of the KMT armed forces after Xu’s dismissal. Publicly, Chiang supported Wang’s maneuvers against the right after Liao’s assassination. He helped unify newly trained KMT military forces and the strikers to defeat Chen. But privately, Chiang sympathized with the KMT right’s anti-communism and waited for his moment to act against the Left. Chiang found his first opportunity on March 20, 1926. He accused a pro-Communist KMT navy captain of starting a coup. With this excuse, he abruptly declared martial law in Canton and mobilized KMT military units loyal to him to round up and detain Communist strike leaders. The Communists were caught completely off guard. However, Chiang knew it was not yet the right time to fully smash the Left. The national priorities of both parties were shifting. Thanks to the strike, the KMT had grown into a strong regional power with a base in southern China. Popular fervor against the imperialists and warlords continued to stir across the nation. Both the KMT and the CPC agreed that it was an opportune moment to launch a national military campaign heading north to defeat the warlords and unify China. The KMT had a different conception of what fighting imperialism meant than the CPC. For the former, breaking with imperialism was necessary only insofar as it allowed the Chinese bourgeoisie an equal chance to develop and compete in capitalist markets. For the latter, the goal was to overthrow the imperialist system, summon a national people’s assembly, and eventually found a workers’ state. The Communists in Canton were caught in a difficult position. They knew that much of their support with the working class still rested on a united front with the KMT against the British. The workers still overwhelmingly identified with the KMT, whose stature was further enhanced by its support of the strike. While Chiang wanted to eradicate the Communists from the KMT, he needed them to mobilize the masses for the campaign, so both sides compromised for now. Chiang backed off and released the Communists who had been arrested. He publicly criticized the KMT right and promised to preserve the alliance with the Communists. At the same time, he pressured Wang to resign and temporarily leave the country and decisively solidified his control and that of the military over the KMT. Chiang also required the Communists to hand over their list of members in the KMT and agree not to control more than a third of any KMT city, regional, or national party committees. The End of the Strike Chiang’s show of force was the beginning of the strike’s drawn-out end. The CPC’s reaffirmation of the united front exposed their weaknesses to the KMT right, which rapidly gained power over the KMT in the ensuing months. Without organizing workers to oppose the KMT’s attempted assault on their ranks, the Communists let the KMT right push their limits, and Chiang began disarming the workers’ pickets as early as April. By May, the KMT right controlled the KMT Executive Committee. The Chiang-led KMT began openly calling for a strike settlement and strengthened KMT police authority over the congress. The government curtailed the strike committee’s ability to make arrests and reopened some businesses that had been used to house strikers since the previous June. Yellow union leaders began pushing back against the strikers’ authority. By the summer, the CPC itself was divided over whether to end the strike. Some saw the strike as a distraction from the Northern Expedition, while other cadres, like Huang Ping, argued against the view the striking during the expedition was “counterrevolutionary”: “We might as well tell workers that they should not eat during the expedition, as eating would be counterrevolutionary.” The negotiations with the British, led by the KMT rather than the strikers, stretched on until October. In the end, the strikers won few of their original demands. The Northern Expedition was a success, at least by the KMT’s standards. In March 1927, the KMT army, supported by the CPC, entered Nanjing. But many of the original Cantonese strikers who formed the popular base for the Northern Expedition did not make it that far. The KMT military dismissed many along the way and left them with few provisions. But the worst was still to come. Shortly after entering Nanjing, Chiang’s KMT finally purged the Communists for good. It hunted and shot thousands of Communists in the following weeks. The CPC organized scattered rebellions in different cities, including Canton, to resist. But all failed disastrously. Wang soon also joined Chiang and helped purge the Communists (Wang later turned further to the right and led Japan’s puppet regime in China during World War II). Only one in six CPC members survived 1927. The CPC’s connection to the urban working class was severed for two decades. At first, Deng evaded arrest and continued to organize underground. But in 1933, the KMT finally captured him in Shanghai and executed him. A Legendary Tale The CPC’s rebirth is now a legendary tale. Throughout the 1930s, the remnants of the party rebuilt its forces under Mao Zedong’s leadership in the countryside, eventually defeating both Japan and the KMT. But the CPC that eventually took power in 1949 fostered a different relationship with China’s industrial working class than before. By then, the party needed the working class for nation-building and sovereign development. The idea of workers’ democracy that the 1920s CPC had cultivated gave way to the practice of a ruling party that alone claimed to represent the entire class, permitting no alternatives. The Canton–Hong Kong strike remains a part of the PRC’s nationalist mythmaking. Last year, the Hong Kong Federation of Trade Unions’ centennial event mainly praised the strikers as “patriotic,” with little attention to how they organized. Hong Kong’s labor secretary, Chris Sun, declared that the strike’s legacy involves Hong Kong “fully taking advantage of its privileged position as an international financial center.” A hundred years ago, the CPC did not see patriotism as an end goal, but a way to bridge workers’ national consciousness to a larger fight for socialism. Ultimately, Deng and his comrades failed to move the workers toward a socialist movement beyond national reunification. The strike’s failure shows that nationalism is not enough to build workers’ movements to dismantle capitalism. This lesson is salient for Chinese workers today, at home and overseas. China’s emergence as a capitalist superpower means that Chinese national consciousness is no longer simply an expression of the oppressed. It has a progressive side, when Chinese and Chinese diaspora workers resist US imperialism and chauvinism. It also has a reactionary side, when the PRC subordinates workers’ power and democracy to great-power nationalism. The strike’s centennial should remind us that Hong Kong’s long and incomplete fight for democracy began with workers’ struggle. Like the European working classes in 1848, Chinese workers did not win power. Still, they once governed on a regional scale, with little at their disposal. When they did, they dealt a major blow to imperialism and unified their country. Today, in a world wrought by new imperialist powers and a crisis of democracy, the Chinese working class has no dearth of world-historic tasks it can still accomplish.

Unions have more public support than just about anything else in the United States, and yet their membership has cratered. This poses an immense challenge to left-wing politicians who won’t be able to enact lasting change without a robust labor movement beside them. On this latest episode of the Jacobin Radio podcast Confronting Capitalism , Vivek Chibber addresses the age-old question of whether unions are inherently political, the importance (and limits) of internal democracy within unions, and why socialists have always been at the heart of the labor movement. Vivek Chibber I think it’s an absolutely central question, because, as we’ve said so many times on the show , unions are the lifeblood of the socialist movement, and organizing labor is the one essential precondition for a revitalized Left . Vivek Chibber Really awful. The term “union density” is a technical term that refers to the proportion of the labor force that is actually dues-paying members of unions. So when we talk about union density, what we’re asking is how much of the working class is actually organized. And that number is at a historic low right now. Overall union density is just below 11 percent. But that masks the fact that, in the parts of the economy where labor’s power really matters — which is in the private sector, where profits and value are created — it’s at a historic low. They are left to the tender mercies of their employers, whose main interest, of course, is not in the workers’ welfare but in profits, which typically come at the expense of those workers. So right now in the United States, the union movement is basically flat on its back. Despite calls and efforts for years on the part of the Left and many union leaders to revitalize it, the trend continues to be downward. What Do Unions Do? Cale Brooks If workers on their own are at the employer’s mercy, as a result of the class structure of capitalism, what is it about unions that allow them to fight back? Vivek Chibber What unions do is change the conditions on which employees bargain with their employers around the conditions of work. By that, I mean the wages that they get, the hours that they work, and the pace and intensity of their labor. It’s the union that allows them to bargain better. The question is: Why? Here, the classical economists from Adam Smith to David Ricardo and down to Karl Marx were pretty clear. The reason is that in that bargain, there is a basic inequality of power. And that inequality of power comes from the fact that one party is already in possession of money and wealth and all kinds of resources. That’s the employer. And the other party is desperate because it lacks all of those things. As we’ve said many times on the show, what makes somebody a worker is not that they choose to work, but they have to work. Why do they have to work? Well, it’s because they don’t have those things that could enable them to get economic independence. They don’t have land of their own. They don’t own their own means of production. They don’t have their own factories or their own restaurants or own hotels. All they have is their capacity to work. So workers have to go out and use that capacity to work in order to get the money that they need to survive. Now, without the job, without having some kind of employment, most workers can’t last even a couple of weeks, much less a month. So when they’re out looking for a job, they come to the employer, the employer offers them some kind of condition, some kind of wage, some kinds of terms for employment. They’re not in a position to say no. Employers, on the other hand, are in a position to say no to the employee. Why? Because there are a lot of people looking for work, way more people looking for work than there are jobs. So if one employee doesn’t take the conditions that the employer is offering them, they can just close the door and wait for the next person to come who’ll be as or even more desperate. This makes it look like the basic problem is one of numbers, that is, that there are not very many employers and there are a lot of employees. And there are now sections of neoclassical economics that have started to realize that there’s a bargaining asymmetry, but they focus on the numbers. What they say is that, because there are few employers, they have what’s called “monopsony power.” And that monopsony power means that they’re the only buyers of a good. It’s the opposite of a monopoly, right? Monopoly is when there’s only one seller of a good. Monopsony is when there’s only one buyer of a good. So the idea is, because there are not that many employers and there are lots of employees, employees end up having to form a queue outside the employer’s door, and employers are in a position to pick and choose as they want. The implication is that if you had way more employers, it would wipe out that bargaining inequality between the worker and the employer. There’s an element of that that’s definitely true. When there are lots of jobs going around, when there are lots of firms hiring, it is true that workers can get better terms for themselves. But it masks an underlying reality, which is that the inequality in bargaining isn’t really driven by the difference in numbers. It’s driven by the difference in wealth. One way to get your mind around that is imagine that there’s only one employer and one employee on earth. Now each one needs the other. Each one has the ability to say no. And you could think that each one is kind of a monopsonist, each one needs the other. But Adam Smith acknowledged and recognized this: even if there was only one worker and one capitalist, there would still be an inequality. And the reason is the following. As Smith said, if they come to a standoff and they can’t come to an agreement, and neither one is able to utilize the other’s services, the worker will always give in first. And the simple reason is that if the capitalist doesn’t have the labor that he needs, he still has his assets and his wealth to fall back on, which he can use to buy his food, to pay for his rent, to buy whatever necessities he needs. Push comes to shove, he can liquidate his assets and get the money for it. Cale Brooks And he has more time on his side. Vivek Chibber Because of the things I said, he has more time on his side. That’s the point. He has more time on his side because if he cannot find workers, he can still survive, as Adam Smith said, for months, maybe even a year. A worker, on the other hand, can’t survive more than a week or two, a month at the most. That means it’s not a numbers game. It is what Marx recognized. It is an inequality that stems from the property relations, from the fact that one group of people owns assets and the key means of production and the other one does not. So this means the inequality in bargaining cannot be changed as long as you’re in capitalism. As long as there’s one group of people who has way more wealth and assets than the group of people who are coming to bargain with them, the inequality in assets will show up as an inequality in bargaining power. And that has all the consequences we know about. In fact, if you could summarize the evils of capitalism in one sentence, this is it. Most everything else we know about capitalism flows from this. The inequality in assets generates the inequality in power, which then generates inequality in income and all the other consequences of power imbalances that we see in society today. So then, what does the union do specifically? What the union does is it makes it easier for the workers to hold out when they’re bargaining with the employer. Remember, what makes the worker vulnerable to his employer is that A) he’s easily replaced, and B) he doesn’t have the means to survive a long standoff, like when there’s a strike or when he’s unemployed. So if he refuses to take a job, it means he’s going to be out on the street desperate. What unions do is they change this situation a bit. First of all, unions make it more costly and harder for the employers to fire employees and to replace them. It’s easy to fire one person and to replace them with somebody standing outside the door. But if you fire a hundred people, now you have to find a hundred replacements. If you fire a thousand people, you have to find a thousand replacements, and so on. So because unions enable workers to bargain with their employers collectively, not as individuals, they force the employer to make concessions that they wouldn’t otherwise make, because unions make it costly for the employers to be jerks, essentially. The second thing that they do is that they enable workers to survive when there’s a standoff. Unions have what are called strike funds. They sometimes provide insurance to workers. They have health benefits, things like that. So when a worker is without a job, being a member of the union means the union resources can tide them over in between jobs and when they’re unemployed. That reduces the fear of unemployment, which in turn reduces the fear of standing up to the boss. Notice there are two things that make workers weak. One is that they’re desperate for jobs. And even when there’s just one worker versus one employer, they can’t last without that job. Unions change both of these equations. They don’t neutralize the imbalance. What they do is they reduce it. And that makes a big difference in workers’ lives. So the function of a union for workers is that they reduce their weakness vis-à-vis their employer. They don’t get rid of it. They don’t erase it, but they do reduce it. Are Unions Political? Cale Brooks In other words, what the union is doing is helping workers get a better price for their labor services. Does that mean that unions are inherently political institutions? Vivek Chibber Well, let’s define political. What we mean by political is that they don’t just haggle over the terms of a trade, but they’re trying to change the power balance between the people who are engaged in the trade. So once you’re trying to change power and power balances, we call that politics, right? So are unions inherently political? Well, I think there is an intrinsically political component to what unions are doing. Because when unions bargain for a better wage, better work conditions, better pace and duration of work, or for things like pensions and benefits, they are not just changing the price for which they’re working, they’re also changing the power balance between themselves and the employer. When unions make it a rule that you can’t just fire workers at will, or that you have to give them higher wages and pensions and things like that, when you have to negotiate with them over the pace of work, you are taking some of the unilateral power that employers have away from them. You can say that is a political act. That said, you can do all of these things without really touching the deep sources of power that employers have. Because what unions do is they don’t question the wage bargain itself. They don’t question the fact that one person iss strong, the other weak. One person has all the power, the other doesn’t. They don’t question the fact that this is built into the system. They take the system for granted. They take the actual social relations for granted, and they’re just saying, “Let’s make it so that we are not as weak and as miserable as we would otherwise be without the union.” In the lexicon of left politics, Vladimir Lenin coined a term called “economism.” And what he said was that there are two kinds of struggles that the social democratic left and the socialist left needs to take up. One is a struggle around the everyday conditions of work that the working class faces. The other is a struggle over the very fact that one group of people has to work for another group of people. And that cannot be changed without deep structural changes within capitalism, without taking away some of the underlying unilateral power that employers have over work and over wages. At some point, you actually have to start addressing the fact that employers should not have this kind of power at all. That he called a political struggle. Now, there is a very strong logic for unions to focus on just the economic facts, what’s called economism. And that generates a kind of conservatism. And the conservatism is that you just narrowly focus all your attention on these very day-to-day material concerns that workers have. And it’s possible to just engage in that, focus on that, and be pretty good at it without ever questioning the fact of capitalism itself. Not the degree of power that employers have, but the very fact that employers have such a power and nobody should have that kind of power. The essence of the socialist movement has always been to say, “It’s not that employers treat their employees badly and we want to fix it. It’s that nobody should have the power to treat people badly in this way at all.” Unions do not, of their own logic, question the system. What they question is the terms on which workers are treated in the system. That makes unions essentially economic institutions, and it requires a partnership with more political entities, like political parties, to bring their focus away from the purely economic and to join in what’s called the political struggle. Cale Brooks Right. There’s no automatic slippery slope from the economism to the political struggle. That has to be activated. Vivek Chibber We’ve got 150 years of experience now to show this. It’s very, very rare when unions on their own come to inherently political demands. They tend to focus on economic demands. Why Employers Fight Unions Cale Brooks It could be argued that this assessment makes unions sound kind of tame. Why should employers oppose them? Vivek Chibber Well, because as I said, even though unions don’t question the basic logic of capitalism, what they do is put limits on the unilateral power of employers. And employers hate that for good reason. When they look at their situation, they say, “Look, it’s a cutthroat competitive market out there. And what I need is the maximum flexibility over my labor force, over my venue of employment, over how I pay them, how much I pay them, how I make them work. And a union ties my hands. Not only is it tying my hands, it raises my costs. When I’m forced to pay higher wages, when I’m forced to pay into a pension fund, my costs go up. And if I want to recoup those costs and make the rate of return that’s worth my while, I’m going to have to do something: either raise my prices, which makes me uncompetitive or eat the losses that come from the higher costs, which may not make me uncompetitive, but it does make me go out of business sooner or later.” So they see the union as doing two things: raising their costs and reducing their flexibility moving forward. That’s unlike any other kind of organization. Employers don’t care if workers form a social club — if they’re in a rotary club, even if they have something like tenants’ associations and that kind of thing — they don’t care. What they care about is one specific organization, which is that organization that gets in the way of their profit maximization and in the conditions that they think are essential for profit maximization, like their flexibility at work. And that’s unions. Whenever they see even a whiff of a union coming around, the first thing they do is try to eradicate it, fire the person who’s doing the organizing, figure out ways of making unions harder to organize, try to craft laws that makes it more difficult to organize them and more difficult to sustain once they’re organized. You don’t see this kind of single-minded hatred of any other kind of organization on the part of employers. And so, even though from the socialist standpoint, unions fall short in some ways — they get you only part of the way, not the whole way where you’d like to be — from the employer standpoint, they tend to be disastrous. And while employers can find ways of living with them, they would always and everywhere prefer a world in which those unions didn’t exist. Do Union Gains Come Out of Profits? Cale Brooks This reminds me of a quote in the second volume of Capital , where Marx says that if it were in the capacity of capitalists to raise their prices and tuck away a greater profit whenever workers pushed for higher wages, then they would never oppose unions and never wait for those wage demands to raise their prices. Do you think union gains in wages and other working conditions always come at the expense of capitalists’ profits? Vivek Chibber In the short run, yes. Because unions raise the price of labor in direct and indirect ways — either directly through wages or indirectly through forcing capitalists to buy better machinery that’s less dangerous, scrap the machinery when it’s starting to break down and create injuries, pay into pensions and into unemployment insurance — all these things raise the cost of business for employers. Now that’s a short-run thing. Over the middle to long run, employers can adjust. Once they know that there’s no other way around it, and they’re going to have to live with a union, it forces them to become more productive, to buy better machinery, to reduce their unit costs, even though their overall costs have gone up, which comes through raising productivity. So they manage in some ways to figure it out. But this is why they prefer a world in which they didn’t have to. Sometimes you see progressive economists or social scientists trying to make a case that we can have unions without having them injuring profits. But it’s a fact that the immediate impact of a union is going to be to injure a firm’s profits. And labor militants have to understand that. And indeed, sometimes you want that. There are lots of employers who are going to tell you that, “Look, even if there’s a 10 percent increase in wages, we’re going to go under.” Well, one of the things that the union movement everywhere always said was that those employers shouldn’t exist. If a venue of employment requires paying people substandard wages, wages below what’s needed for a minimal standard of living, they should not exist. Obviously that’s easier said than done, because when you say that such a venue shouldn’t exist, what you’re also saying is that people are going to lose their jobs. And a union movement has to be very cognizant of that. So there are a couple of things to keep in mind. One is that it shows you that a union movement on its own is going to be really hamstrung. Unions are in the business of improving their members’ lives by getting them better conditions of work. That necessarily means raising the costs of business for employers. That means a successful union movement is actually going to create some degree of job loss in the short run. That doesn’t have to be terminal, precisely because unions force employers to become more efficient, more productive, to recoup those losses. Those gains in efficiency also generate economic growth overall. And that overall economic growth means that there are also jobs expanding. We previously talked about productivity and AI , and I said that new innovations do create job loss. But if you just let it play out a bit, people usually find new employment because new technology also means faster economic growth. One of the things unions do, interestingly, is because they shut down what’s called the “low road” — that is to say they make it hard for employers to just go on paying really low wages with really bad work conditions, and they put a floor on what employers are paying people — they force employers to become more productive so that they can afford those higher wages, so that they can afford those better work conditions. That is a social good. The society as a whole and the working class as a whole benefits from this. That higher productivity, the faster pace of growth, also means that people being laid off are going to find new jobs. Economists love to talk about this, but there’s a catch, which is you don’t know how long it’s going to take for the faster growth to come about from productivity increases. There are instances in which a lot of people fall through the cracks even when there’s growth. On a human side, even if people are going to find new jobs, that can mean weeks, sometimes months of transition from one job to the other, and workers are bearing all the costs of that. Now, this has an interesting effect. Unions on their own are very, very hesitant to just give a green light to technological change and are hesitant to make demands that they know might result in some layoffs, because they feel a responsibility to their constituency. So on the one hand, it makes unions somewhat conservative. On the other hand, there’s a real positive aspect to it, which is that everybody is benefiting from higher productivity. What you want is institutions that enable society to benefit from technological change but shield workers who are in those venues who are going to get laid off from those negative effects. This is one of the things that European social democrats worked out. They had labor market policies where unions and governmental institutions would work together to funnel workers into the expanding sectors of the economy, sometimes to retrain them, to give them new skills. None of that eradicates the costs of technological change, but what it does do is that it enables unions to insist that employers pay decent wages and have decent conditions of work without worrying about the unemployment effects of that quite as much as they would have to if they didn’t have the institutions working with them. And if you want to see an example of that, this was the tragedy of the American labor movement. American unions, especially the autoworkers, knew that if they made really ambitious wage demands, the result would be job loss for some of their members. And the problem was they didn’t have any assurance or any kind of institutions to try to funnel workers who were being laid off into new employment. And that made them hold back on some of their demands on employers. If you look at the Scandinavian countries, this is the big difference. They were able to make much more ambitious demands on their employers because they had social institutions that helped funnel workers from the industry shedding labor and venues that couldn’t afford to pay labor the kind of wages they needed, and got them jobs in the expanding sectors and in the firms that were making the higher profits. The lesson here is precisely because unions do raise the costs for employers, they need to be surrounded by other institutions — labor market institutions and fiscal institutions — that work in a complementary fashion. Because if they don’t, unions themselves will have a lot of pressure to hold back on their demands. And as one of the chief strategists for the United Auto Workers said in the 1970s, if we don’t make the demands for decent wages, we are rewarding employers for being jerks, for essentially taking the low road and benefiting in a way that actually ends up harming the workers much more. When Socialists Met Unionists Cale Brooks Let’s now go back in history. When did socialists and unions first come into contact, and what were these relationships like? Vivek Chibber They were present at the birth. Whichever country you look at, you find that socialists were involved in the formation of the trade union movement just about everywhere. Now it wasn’t always Marxists. In many countries, Marxists actually were in a minority or sometimes even absent from the formation of trade unions. But the Left more broadly was a pillar, not only once the movements got going but in forming the trade unions writ large. And there’s a reason for that, which is that, as I said, forming a trade union is difficult. And it’s difficult because employers hate them. Employers don’t want to have anything to do with them. Most workers therefore tend to be hesitant. And when they do come together, it’s over very mundane, very short-term demands. In Europe, this was carried out by the more skilled workers who had some kind of intellectual ambitions, who read a lot, who were reading pamphlets, who were around the Left. It took a kind of intellectual orientation and a moral orientation to say, “We know it’s risky, we know it’s going to be hard, but if we’re able and willing to bear the risks and the costs of bringing these people together, there are going to be immediate payoffs. It’s going to be better for all of us.” And so it took a certain kind of horizon, a certain kind of outlook to be willing to do the arduous work of bringing people together into unions. It was the Left that did that. Now it wasn’t always pointy-headed intellectuals. A lot of these people who were socialists, who started the union movements, were themselves workers, more skilled workers. But there was a very noticeable participation on the part of journalists and intellectuals as well, who essentially committed class suicide and who devoted their lives to forming unions. And they were all on the Left. So the answer to your question is that right at the beginning, the Left was essential to the rise of unions. Cale Brooks We often talk about what socialists gain from the institution of unions, but what do unions stand to gain from socialists being a part of their movement? Vivek Chibber Historically, what socialists did was point unions away from pure short-term and purely sectional demands into class-wide demands that had deeper and more lasting benefits for the working class. Because of all the risks involved in forming a union, the inclination of every worker is to opt for fighting on their own, individually, as subtly as they can without getting caught, and to stay away from the more dangerous tactic of lining up with other of their peers to form a union. Because the moment they start doing that, it becomes visible. And the moment it becomes visible, they risk being fired. So that’s the first obstacle . Workers tend to kind of avoid these risky actions and strategies. But when they do form unions, it is also the case that employers are going to be lined up against them. And the more ambitious their demands, the more employers are going to be resisting. So the first instinct is to make demands that are easier to achieve. Then there’s a second problem, which is that organizing workers is hard. Organizing workers who are of different ethnicities, different races, who don’t trust each other, don’t have a history together is even harder. Now, because it’s harder, it means it’s even riskier, and it gives employers even more opportunities to undermine the organizing strategies. When workers are of different ethnicities, employers can play them off against each other. When they don’t have the same language and the same history, it’s harder to build bonds of trust, which means that when there’s a strike or a job action, it’s harder for them to stay together, because they don’t have a camaraderie that comes naturally from being of the same culture and the same ethnicity. All of these things actually make the material costs of union organizing and union struggles higher and therefore harder. That being the case, under what conditions will people undertake the more arduous, the more risky, the more cost-bearing strategies? Well, it’s when they have a kind of moral commitment to it. They’re not going to ask workers to be altruists, to become saints, to ignore all these risks, to ignore all the costs, but their own moral commitments as organizers will incline them to look for strategies that will work in the face of these costs. If you don’t look hard, you’re not going to find them. And what we saw historically was that the unions that had a real commitment to interracial organizing, unions that had a real commitment to making militant demands, to take bigger risks, to find solutions for workers so that they were able to envision a kind of union that was over time engaging in more far-reaching and more militant strategies, those were always leftists. Those were always socialists because of their outlook. It was the socialists who led the movement away from craft unionism to industrial unionism, from ethnically segmented unions to interracial and multicultural unions. It was a socialist who did this because they were the ones who were ideologically committed to figuring out solutions to very costly ventures. Had it not been for the socialists, the union movement would probably have remained much more sectional, short-termist, and ethnically and racially segmented. So it basically comes down to this. The secret to capitalism is that the structures of the employment relation make it much more appealing to either avoid organizing altogether or do the kind of organizing that’s the least risky and the easiest to achieve. And the least risky and easiest to achieve strategies are always the ones that do the least for workers. It helps them keep their heads above water, but it doesn’t question employer power very much. And it doesn’t give workers the more far-reaching gains that they would like to have. Because of that, it takes an ideological and moral commitment to find ways around all of those costs, all of those risks. And that moral commitment has historically come from people with outlook and the orientation of the socialist left. The Height of Union Power Cale Brooks So we’ve talked in a previous episode about how the American labor movement reached the apex of its power shortly after World War II, when approximately one in three workers was part of a union. We just talked about the role of socialists in union organizing. What other social ingredients made that moment possible? Vivek Chibber It’s really hard to say. When we look back, we can see that there were some aspects of the workplace and the wider environment that made organizing easier than it is today. One obvious one was that workplaces were large. So imagine two scenarios, one in which you have five workplaces in a city that employ just about everyone. They’re quite large. That means that if you are a group of people who want to organize unions, you have five places to go to. Now imagine a situation in which there are five hundredvenues in which people work in a city. Now you have to have an army of people going into each of those five hundred venues to try to organize them. Each one is going to have different groups of people, sometimes with different ethnicities, sometimes with different conditions of work. So when you have fewer work venues bringing lots of people together, there are ways in which organizers can enjoy economies of scale. You’re at one place and you can, with the same strategy, bring in lots and lots of people. The second thing is that you also had a very close connection between workplaces and communities. Workers lived in slums and ghettos that were very close to the place where they worked, which means that the people you’re working with are also the people you see when you go home and you go out to a bar or you’re having your social engagements, marriage circles, things like that. So social ties and class ties overlap tremendously. That’s very different from the world today, when a lot of workers live in suburbs and drive to work. And when they go back home, they don’t see anybody who they’re working with. A third factor that was really important when the union movement got going was that much of the working class didn’t have political rights at all, which means that they didn’t have the right to vote. And lacking the right to vote also meant that you were treated like second-class citizens in a variety of ways. And that created a kind of social identity. It’s not just that I’m an employee who’s being treated badly by my boss. I also am disenfranchised because of my boss. The boss is the one who gets to vote. I don’t. My boss lives outside the ghetto in a decent neighborhood, whereas I live in a place where I don’t have a sewage system. I don’t have basic garbage collection. I’m treated really badly, not just in the workplace but also outside the workplace. All of these things made a class identity much easier to achieve, because your class oppression overlapped with all kinds of social oppressions. Now, by the 1940s and ’50s, what do we see? Workers have democratic rights. You’re starting to see the rise of suburbs. There’s a separation of work from home — much, much bigger than there used to be. There’s the invention of mass media, which means that capitalists and workers share in the same culture way more than they used to in the 1910s and ’20s. All of which is then accentuated by the fact that the workplace becomes a lot more fragmented. Smaller workplaces, inside the workplace, there are all kinds of strategies involved that separate workers and atomize them. In the service sector, you’re working in cubicles where you don’t talk to the next person. Even in factories today, rules are that you are not allowed to talk to the person next to you because cameras catch you doing it. Which means even though I’m standing right next to you, I don’t even know your name. When I was in Delhi last year, teaching at a school that we hold for workers and for union organizers, one of the most shocking stories we heard from somebody who works in an auto factory was that the pace of work is so intense, and the rules around talking to your coworker are so egregious, that there was a worker who fainted in the production line and the people standing next to him did not know for an hour or two that the person had fainted because they couldn’t avert their eyes from the production line where they were working. This stuff didn’t exist in the 1920s. And so it means that not just the workplace is different. It’s not just that the rhythm and the internal regime of work is different. Everything about it is different. That doesn’t mean that we’re in an unresolvable situation. You know, if you looked in the 1920s and ’30s, a lot of unionists were saying, “We can’t win this because the modern factory was so different from the small shops of the 1880s and 1890s where the union movement was born.” And the new environment of the ’30s was one in which a lot of unionists said, “This is going to be hard to navigate.” It’s only after they solve the problems that we were able to come in as intellectuals, as academics, and theorize it and say, “Ah, that’s why it worked.” So it looks simple in the aftermath. My guess is that because this is capitalism, because profit-making still relies on workers, on workers showing up, and because there are still all kinds of vulnerabilities in the capillaries of the system, we just need to find the new ones. We just need to figure out how this particular instance of capitalism is vulnerable in its own way, know how to work those vulnerabilities. And thirty years from now, when people look back, they’ll say, “Oh, well that was pretty easy to do,” because once you’ve done it, of course it looks easy to do. It’s just that right now, as the saying goes, it’s darkest before the dawn. We’re in that moment where we just haven’t figured it out. Once we do, it’ll seem a lot simpler than it does right now. And underneath all this was a further enabling condition. A fourth factor was the rapid economic growth. The period from the late 1920s right after the Great Depression into the 1960s was the most rapid economic growth the world has ever seen. That rapid economic growth was really a kind of a hidden sauce to the growth of the union movement. Because as long as profits were high, the economy was expanding. It was easier to take risks because you could find another job. But the second thing is because profits were going up, employers could afford to give workers all those great things that the unions were demanding. What starts from the 1980s is you still have growth, but it’s much lower. You have expansion, but it’s less rapid. And that means then that employers are going to fight a lot harder against a union than they would have in the 1930s and ’40s. What you have in your lifetime and mine is a capitalism in which the kind of conditions of work and home have changed enormously. And the Left hasn’t figured out how to get beyond them, how to find the weaknesses. And it’s a much more slow-growing capitalism, which means employer resistance is so much greater. There’s other stuff I haven’t mentioned, like outsourcing and globalization and all that. And that all figures into it as well. I just was trying to be illustrative of the ways in which capitalism then and now is different. And the way it’s connected to social and political institutions is different. There are all kinds of ways in which this difference has made it a lot more difficult to organize now. But we shouldn’t lose sight of the fact that profitability is really the deep underlying constraint to all this. And again, that doesn’t mean that it’s going to be impossible, but it does mean that the strategy for A) organizing workers and B) maintaining those unions is going to have to be different. Any union movement that comes up now is going to have to figure out a way of getting growth going again. And in my opinion, they can’t rely on private capital to do it. There’s going to be a very important role for the state in getting growth going again as a precondition for maintaining the union movement itself. Reforming the Unions Cale Brooks You’ve been talking about the kinds of forces that push unions toward conservatism. But what are the forces within a union that make it effective at fighting for its members? Vivek Chibber There are a couple of things that are really important. When a union is functioning right, it’s not just a representative organization but a fighting organization. That means that whatever is going on inside the union has to better enable it, not just to represent the workers and articulate their interests but to fight for them effectively. There are a few things that feed into this, which are really important. And again, it was the Left that recognized this. And this is why it’s not just the unions have been important for the socialist project, but socialists have been the lifeblood of the union movement. One key thing is unions ask their members to undertake risks. They make demands that employers don’t want to agree to, which means that there’s always a danger that the employer is going to dig in his heels and there’s going to have to be a strike or some kind of job action. No worker wants to see that. And depending on the demand you’re making, the battle against the employer will be more or less arduous. And as I said, the more ambitious demands are the ones that will be harder to effectuate, right? That’ll be harder to achieve. So how do you get people to buy into that? Well, if you’d simply order them, like in an army — “do this or else” — it’s not going to work. And the reason it’s not going to work is that in an army, if somebody doesn’t follow your orders, you can court-martial them. Maybe you can execute them. There are all kinds of bad consequences. Unions can’t do that to their members. They can only achieve some kind of success on the strenght of the voluntary sacrifices that union members are willing to make. Now, in order for me, as a union member, to undertake any kind of action that involves some degree of risk, if somebody just orders me from on high to do it, I’m not going to do it. I’ll be like, “Man, I wasn’t part of this. I didn’t say I want to do this. So you’re just telling me to do it.” How do you get around that? Well, if everybody’s part of the decision-making process, and we all collectively come up with the idea that we’re going to take job action x or job action y , then I feel that, “Yeah, it’s risky, but I agree to it.” And because I agree to it, I have something of an obligation not to renege. I still might renege, but at least now I feel like I have an obligation to do it. So one way to bring that about is to have a healthy internal culture of debate and discussion, what we call democracy . A second thing is that you need to have some kind of internal identity and culture of the union, so that you, as another member of the union, are not just somebody I know from a kind of formal affiliation, but someone I really know. We hang out together. We go to the bars together. We see movies. Our kids go to summer camp or something like that. That means that unions are more effective when they have a kind of internal social life and democratic, interpersonal culture that people have toward each other. A third thing is that people who are organizing unions have to understand that while you can reasonably ask people to undertake some risks, you cannot ask them to be saints and altruists. You can’t ask them to commit suicide. You can’t ask them to take up demands that are looking great on paper as super radical, super militant, but they’re going to fail. Because once the union members paint you as someone who can’t be trusted, then they’ll never follow you again, which means that there has to be a realism about what you can and cannot do. Once you make demands that are achievable, people see that they have real power. Then in the next round, you might be able to make more ambitious demands. When you win those, more ambitious demands again. And each cycle deepens the feeling of solidarity, of commitment, of trust, of mutuality, and a confidence in yourselves. So there are these three things that are very important. Internal democracy, a healthy culture of people identifying with each other and being committed to each other, and thirdly, realism on the part of the leadership. That sense of realism will be much more robust if there’s democracy, because people aren’t fools. If you’re asking them to do things that are crazy, they will tell you, and they will vote it down. These three things are important. Do the Leaders Sell Out? Cale Brooks That sounds like a recipe for success, but there’s a critique on the radical left that says that the main threat to that kind of democratic, solidaristic culture within the union comes from the leaders of the union themselves in a kind of power grab, looking out for their own interests against the members. Do you think that there is a conflict of interest between the leadership and the members? Vivek Chibber I think that there is a real danger for a divergence of interests between union leaders and their members. And that divergence of interest comes from the fact that being a union leader can be an awfully cushy job. There are lots of unions in which the union leaders make way more money than the union members, in which the terms are very long, in which they are sitting on top of huge pools of money over which they have discretionary authority. And once you have that kind of power . . . I mean, if you’re a worker who gets elected off the shop floor, off the everyday conditions of work into a very cushy position, there’s a tendency to not to want to give it up. And since you don’t want to give it up, you’re not going to be wild about elections. You’re not going to be wild about opening up decisions to debate and discussion. And so you’re going to subtly, in all sorts of ways, start shutting down normal avenues of deliberation, debate, and interaction within the union. We’ve seen that in country after country after country. There’s no doubt about that. That said, there is a tendency on the Left to underestimate the difficulty of creating a culture of democracy and debate inside the union, such that whenever they see that it isn’t where they’d like it to be, the automatic assumption is that the leaders are shutting it down. But even well-intentioned leaders face a problem in any union, which is that coming to meetings, being in debates and discussions, following events, having an opinion on what to do takes time and takes effort. And there is a tendency on the part of union members to say to leaders, “Hey, look, we elected you to do this work. You do the research. You figure out what’s right. You figure out which strategy works. And when you need a vote, we’ll come in and vote. And we’ll give you the benefit of the doubt and the authority to do what you want to do.” There is sometimes a tendency on the Left to think that ordinary workers are dying to be in meetings, to have long debates, to be on top of everything. But the fact is most people work eight- or ten-hour days, and they want to go home and then do all sorts of other things — socialize, be with their family, be with their friends. And if you are around unions, even very left-wing unions, one of the things that union leaders, the militants will tell you is it’s really hard getting people to come out, to pay attention, and to get them to come to meetings. So there are two images of union leadership on the Left, both of which are correct. One is an image in which union leadersusurp authority and shut down debate, discussion, etc., because they fear losing power. That’s true. It happens. The other one, though, is also true, which is that union leaders work their butts off to try to get the union members to come in and to participate. And it’s just really hard to do. In this case, what’s happening is they’re not usurping authority. The authority is being ceded to them. Both of them are true. And I don’t know if there is a solution to the problem of ceding authority. I don’t know what it is other than exhortation and voluntarism and getting people to come in. There are solutions to oligarchy and bureaucratization.If it were true that ordinary workers are bubbling over with enthusiasm, but these dastardly leaders outmaneuver them and usurp power, you can devise solutions to that. You can put roadblocks in the way of people taking power for themselves. But I think it’s actually a lot harder when people cede authority to leaders, and in so doing open the door to further kind of malfeasance on the part of the leaders to then take advantage of what they’ve been given. Both things are true. And I think that a union movement that’s effective needs to be cognizant that bureaucrats do sometimes become oligarchs, but a lot of the bureaucracy inside a union comes from the fact that you can’t rely on voluntarism. You have to create offices. You have to create new tasks. People have to be paid to do it because otherwise they won’t do it. And that’s a real dilemma inside the union movement. If you don’t understand this, you run into the problem that every time there is a rank-and-file movement, a desire to democratize the union, and they win, you have this bizarre pattern that after winning, they continue with the bureaucracy or they have power that you didn’t think they ought to have. And it’s a mystery to you as to why it’s happening. Cale Brooks “They sold out.” Vivek Chibber Right, and so one of the things that left-wing unions find themselves doing is every time a left-wing rank-and-file caucus wins, you find that they “sell out.” Well, if people are selling out over and over and over again, it means that it probably isn’t an instance of selling out but actually structural pressures. And so we have to figure out what those pressures are. In my opinion, one such pressure is free-riding. People just would rather have the officers do all the work than do it themselves. And the officers have no choice but to create new offices. And that makes the bureaucracy proliferate. Rebuilding the Labor Movement Cale Brooks Without naming names, it seems like many of the unions that are guilty of what you’re talking about are some of these giant legacy organizations that have been around for nearly a century or more. Is the solution actually to scrap them and start new unions? Vivek Chibber I don’t think so. First of all, the legwork it takes to start a new union is so enormous. In the United States these days, it’s almost impossible to start a new union. Labor law is written in such a way that employers have so many advantages to block unionization efforts that if your strategy is to just do an end run around the existing ones and create new ones from scratch, you’re setting yourself up for failure. As it happens, when you only have 6 percent union density, you basically have to start new ones anyway. But it is a fact that these existing unions do still cover a lot of workers, and they’re sitting on huge pools of money that can be used to extend organizing drives and to actually get into parts of the economy that pure voluntarism won’t allow you to do. But a second problem is this: if there are deep structural forces that push unions toward conservatism and bureaucracy, unless you analyze why it happens, what those forces are, and figure out ways of actually undermining them and neutralizing them, creating new unions won’t solve anything. In a few years, they’re going to become bureaucratic, conservative, plotting unions, just like these ones are. So for both of these reasons, I think you have to start where the unions are. You have to try to reform them, democratize them, make them fighting organizations again, utilize their resources to start new organizing drives, figure out what kinds of organizing drives work, and then just throw money at them. Make it inefficient if it has to be. Have the failures. But right now, unless existing unions really pour money and resources into new organizing, I don’t see how we’re going to get more unions. Secondly, of course, if you don’t have an analysis of what’s behind the conservatism of the unions, you’re just condemning yourself to repeating those same things over and over. So my view is that wherever there are any institutions of the working class, even if they’re morbid, even if they’re corrupt, you go in there and you fix them. And then when there’s no other choice, you try to create new ones. But why would you willingly give up on existing resources when you could better utilize them? Cale Brooks One might think that this would actually be a fairly popular idea, because when you look at opinion polling, unions have the highest approval rating since the 1950s. Well into the majority of Americans support unions. Does that support actually matter? Vivek Chibber It matters a lot, because one of the things that weakened union organizing efforts in the last thirty to forty years was this feeling that the general public wouldn’t support them; that they were isolated and therefore were a sectional interest. And now we understand that most of the American population sees unions as a good thing. And what that means, then, is that we have to focus on the one factor that accounts for the absence of unions, which is employer hostility and the unrelenting class war that they’ve been waging on working people. Once people who are working for living understand that they have the community support, the population support, the broad cultural support, it helps them overcome the feeling of dejection and the feeling of hopelessness that a lot of union members have felt after the onset of the neoliberal era. Cale Brooks Given that we’re in a situation where unions are flat on their back, as you said, do we think unions are still going to play a key role in the future for either rebuilding social democracy or something more ambitious ? Vivek Chibber My view is either the union movement is revived or there is no future for the Left. It’s a pretty straightforward view. The reason for that is you can win elections the way Zohran Mamdani has, and other people have, and you can pass a lot of progressive legislation without the unions. That’s great. But if our analysis of the state is correct, that states basically are beholden to the wealthy, and that states are even more deeply constrained by the investment decisions in capitalism as a system, then it is the case that without a union movement, the legislation you’re going to be able to pass is the legislation that capitalists are okay with. Social democracy has many components to it that will come up against employer resistance. When you do come up against employer resistance, the question is: How are you going to overcome it? And as your policies become more ambitious, as they become more pro-worker, that resistance and the animosity on the part of the wealthy and employers is going to only increase. So how are you going to get around it without some kind of power of your own? Now, I might be wrong. We’ll find out, because the near-term future is a future without labor. We know that. Maybe we’ll find that a Bernie Sanders or a Mamdani type of politician can pass all kinds of great legislation without any real support from an organized labor movement. I hope that’s true. I would love to be wrong, because what I’m asking for is something very hard, which is rebuilding a labor movement in an era where we haven’t figured out how to do it. But in my view, if social democracy gets off the ground, it will have to take up policies that employers oppose. And I don’t know any way around it. We’ve never seen any way around it anywhere, in any capitalist country, without an organized labor movement. So my view is that it’s great to see the culture shifting. It’s great to see people winning elections on a democratic socialist ticket. But all of this right now is hanging in midair because none of it is with the assistance of a labor movement. And it’s no surprise that we’re ten years into the Sanders era, and we really have not seen the needle move on policies at all. We’ve seen the needle move in electoral outcomes, but people should be aware that nothing has been achieved yet. There’s no lasting achievement yet of the Sanders era, nothing on class issues. And I think that is utterly predictable based on the state of labor. So I think people inside the Left and Democratic Socialists of America have to understand that we should use the elections to the best of our ability. But once we have office, unless we do everything we can to build a labor movement, I don’t think we’re going to get beyond certain social measures and certain very useful, but nevertheless tepid economic measures.

Zohran Mamdani Just Created an Office of Worker Power

On Thursday, Mayor Zohran Mamdani signed an executive order creating the Mayor’s Office of Worker Power (MOWP), the nation’s first municipal agency built to help workers organize. Tony Perlstein will run the MOWP, reporting to Deputy Mayor for Economic Justice Julie Su. A former longshoreman and union organizer born and raised in New York, Perlstein served as an organizing director for the United Auto Workers, where he helped lead a national campaign to lift pay and protect jobs from automation. As part of its mandate, the MOWP will regularly bring union leaders and rank-and-file members together to discuss worker issues and organizing drives. It also aims to reach unorganized workers, like immigrants and gig drivers, and connect them to worker centers and other resources. The office plans to hold public hearings, teach workers what protections they already have, and steer them toward organizations that can help. Mamdani spoke about the new office with Jacobin ’s Spencer Snyder. Spencer Snyder Tell me a little bit about the Office of Worker Power. Zohran Mamdani This is a first-of-its-kind office in the country, and we’re setting it up to help workers organize in an economy where more and more is stacked against them. This is a way we can help workers fight for the kinds of protections workers won in the past that we now celebrate as just a part of life. Like the weekend. Spencer Snyder How did the idea come about? Is this something you were thinking about on the campaign trail? Zohran Mamdani On the campaign, we talked about the cost-of-living crisis in this city and the need for an affordability agenda. Workers feel that every day. This office is born of our belief that we can make the nation’s most expensive city affordable. It’s also informed by the success of our Mayor’s Office to Protect Tenants and how it’s delivered for tenants across the city. Now we have the opportunity to do the same for workers. Spencer Snyder What kinds of issues is this office meant to address? Zohran Mamdani At the heart of it is the fact that what used to create a middle-class life no longer does. Many workers are grappling with the emptiness of the promise that working nine to five can be enough to live in this city. They’re having to take on two or three jobs. They’re having to engage with a gig economy that is actively growing while also failing to provide a dignified life for so many of the people who participate in it. What we’re going to have in this office is a commitment to assisting workers wherever and however they want to organize — and to doing that in more languages than just English. Spencer Snyder How do you hope this office will affect the work landscape in the city more broadly? Zohran Mamdani I hope it will help workers not only understand the power they have but build more of that power. We’ve seen that when workers come together, it’s something that benefits everybody across the city and across this country. We want to make sure that we at city hall are using every tool at our disposal to help workers do exactly that. Spencer Snyder Can you tell me about the public hearings? Zohran Mamdani One of the many things this office is going to do is hold public hearings and conduct research, to bring out into the open a lot of the struggles that workers are facing privately. No, Seriously, Democratic Socialists Want to Abolish the Senate

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Rochester Red Star: News from Rochester DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-06T18:34:34+00:00

by Gregory Lebens-Higgins On July 14, 2026, the national body of the Democratic Socialists of America released Workers Deserve More, a program outlining the organization’s goals and values. Workers Deserve More was drafted by a committee consisting of members of the National Political Committee, leaders of other national committees, and rank and file members following a process outlined by a resolution passed at DSA’s 2025 National Convention . Upon its release, Workers Deserve More received significant attention in mainstream media. The coverage has provided free advertising for DSA’s popular demands such as universal healthcare, improved working conditions, and replacing the two-party system with a multi-party democracy. Appearing on Fox News , DSA Co-Chair Megan Romer was pressed on the earnestness of these demands. Host Shannon Bream projected DSA’s proposals on screen and asked a series of true/false questions – Replace the presidency and Supreme Court as we now know them? “Yep,” replied Romer. Abolish ICE? “Absolutely.” Among these demands, the call to abolish the Senate has been met with a particularly strong reaction. On newspaper opinion pages, in exchanges with the public at chapter tabling events, and across the family dinner table, it is greeted with a tone of disbelief: “No, seriously, democratic socialists want to abolish the Senate,” exclaims USA Today . As Red Star contributor Jean Allen recently noted , “It is easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of America’s undemocratic institutions.” Our indoctrinated attachment to American institutions and the mystification of their undemocratic nature limits our possibilities for creating something better. James Madison, architect of the Constitution, wrote clearly of his antidemocratic intent. In the tenth of The Federalist Papers , a series of arguments published to support the ratification of the Constitution, Madison considers the problem of “control[ling] the violence of faction.” It is not mere difference of taste or opinion to which Madison is referring, but opposing class interests. “The most common and durable source of factions,” he writes, “has been the various and unequal distribution of property.” Madison continues, “Those who hold and those who are without property have ever formed distinct interests in society. Those who are creditors, and those who are debtors, fall under a like discrimination. A landed interest, a manufacturing interest, a mercantile interest, a moneyed interest, with many lesser interests, grow up of necessity in civilized nations, and divide them into different classes, actuated by different sentiments and views.” The “principal task” of the Constitution is to suppress the struggle between these opposing interests, or at least to ensure the property-holding faction is never on the losing side. The task is achieved by dissipating the popular will and by impeding the ability to reform entrenched power structures. The Senate carries out both of these functions. The United States Senate website ( Senate.Gov ) explains the “Idea of the Senate” by adopting the remarks of British Liberal politician and later ambassador to the United States James Bryce, from his 1888 work The American Commonwealth . Bryce explains the purpose of the Senate as fivefold: (1) to reconcile the independent spirit of the states by giving them equal representation; (2) to create a council to advise and check the President; (3) to “restrain the impetuosity and fickleness of the popular House, and so guard against the effects of gusts of passion or sudden changes of opinion in the people”; (4) to create a body of experienced men as an element of stability; and (5) to establish a court of impeachment for abuses of power by the executive. The Senate has failed on each of these counts. The equal representation of states in the Senate was reached as a compromise between large and small states at the Constitutional Convention. This arrangement leads to wildly undemocratic results, with most senators representing more empty land than people. A majority of senators (fifty-two) from the smallest twenty-six states represent only 21% of the United States population. Though California holds sixty-six times the population of Wyoming, both are entitled to only two senators. The subversion of democratic will to states rights should have been decided by the Union defeat of the Confederacy, yet it remains enshrined in Article I of the Constitution. Bryce’s “body of men [with] greater experience [and a] longer term of membership” has failed to guarantee stability for the country, but has cemented itself as a ruling class. With six-year terms and no recall process, senators are rarely held accountable for selling out their constituents. By amassing wealth and political connections, incumbents in the Senate are reelected at a rate above 80%, and about one-third of senators are currently above seventy years old. As for meritocratic qualification, it is impossible to view Senate press briefings and sustain the belief that America serves up the best and brightest leaders. The Senate is an instrument of obstruction. Its most important tool is the filibuster, requiring sixty votes to end debate and bring most legislation to a vote. Thus, policies with majority support are unable to become law. Since the end of Reconstruction, the procedure has frequently been used to kill Civil Rights legislation and anti-lynching laws. More recently , the Senate filibuster has stifled bills on election integrity, LGBTQ protections, workers rights, immigration reform, climate change, healthcare affordability, and more. Constitutional checks and balances more often subdue challenges to power than restrain the powerful. The inability of the Senate to pass legislation has further empowered the Presidency, which now enacts change rapidly through a flurry of executive orders. The Senate has ceded its role in holding the President accountable. Party loyalty transcends inter-branch responsibilities, and the high bar for impeachment and removal (two-thirds) makes it a toothless and undemocratic remedy. The Supreme Court has also taken on the task of legislating, issuing sweeping rulings on matters that Congress has failed to resolve. Abolishing the Senate is not something that will happen in a vacuum. It is a maximalist demand for achieving a working-class democracy. To reach this horizon, other significant measures must be implemented. These include getting money out of politics by breaking the lobbying power, overturning Citizens United, and publicly funding elections. It includes creating a more representative Congress by implementing proportional representation and ranked-choice voting, and ending gerrymandering to create fair districts. It also includes expanding democracy by protecting voting rights and ending voter suppression. Each of these demands is contained in Workers Deserve More. These changes will help transform Congress into a deliberative body that is more responsive to the democratic will. In this context, the concept of an upper house responsible for checking democratic decisions would seem even more ridiculous. The failures of American institutions arise not from too much democracy, but too little. It must be the mission of all democracy-minded activists to throw off the vestiges of upper-class rule embodied in the Senate. Inside Tacoma’s ICE detention center: worm-infested food, suicide, and neglect

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Sofie Villa ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-07T11:28:23+00:00

At the Northwest ICE Processing Center in Tacoma, Washington, thousands of complaints have been lodged by immigrant detainees regarding dangerous living conditions: abuse, inedible food, prolonged isolation, and medical neglect. Despite more than 3,500 complaints being submitted to the Washington State Department of Health, GEO Group, operating the facility under a contract with ICE, has repeatedly refused to admit state inspectors. Opened in 2004 , the facility formerly known as the Northwest Detention Center (NWDC) has a stated bed space capacity of 1,575 people – one of the largest immigration prisons in the country. Its population hovered around 800 for a couple years before rising sharply after Trump’s second term began, with immigrant rights groups estimating that 1,400-1,500 people were detained there in March. Food budgeted at 97 cents per meal Food, water and air quality account for more than 990 of these complaints. Detainees report finding burned plastic, metal string, rope, wood splinters and even worms in their meals. In one case, raw meat was served to a unit of 54 people. Some were so hungry they ate the food anyways – causing 15 of them to become sick. Other complaints describe drinking water as “foul” and that air coming from the vents smells like sewage. One detainee told immigrant rights group La Resistencia that food served at the NWIPC contained rat feces. The evidence of the poor quality of food and water is not limited to detainee complaints. During an unannounced inspection in 2022, the Department of Homeland Security found long-expired food and boxes of meat left open and exposed to the air. In May 2025, detainees reported stomach pain, bloating, and bloody diarrhea after eating collard greens served at the facility. The Tacoma-Pierce County Health Department later detected Bacillus cereus , a bacterium associated with food poisoning, in the supply of collard greens. A former GEO kitchen supervisor testified that the company budgeted only 97 cents per detainee per meal, and admitted that GEO could not provide adequate meals within that budget. In 2024, GEO reported more than $1.6 billion in revenue for its secure service facility operations in the US. The facility’s problems extend beyond food. More than 650 complaints concern unsanitary living conditions, including black mold in showers, rarely cleaned bathrooms and dirty floors. At one point, approximately 100 people reportedly had access to only 2 working bathrooms. Detainees also report receiving wet or dirty laundry, underwear previously worn by others, and bedding that had not been changed or cleaned after being used by people infected with COVID-19 and chickenpox. Medical neglect accounts for more than 900 additional complaints. Detainees report untreated strokes, paralysis, internal bleeding, heart conditions and asthma. In one case, a man returned from a hospital with prescribed medication, but facility staff withheld it and gave him ibuprofen while his condition continued to deteriorate. The complaints also contain allegations of physical and sexual abuse by officers. Deaths and prolonged isolation Reports also include one death by suicide, several suicide attempts, and extreme isolation. Mergensana Amar, a 40-year-old asylum seeker, committed suicide in November 2018 after nearly 11 months in the NWIPC. Charles Leo Daniel was found dead in March 2024 after spending over 3 years combined in solitary confinement over 2 separate periods in 2020 and 2023. University of Washington researchers also obtained 911 records documenting at least 6 suicide attempts at the facility between January 1 and March 18, 2024. The UN’s Nelson Mandela Rules define any solitary confinement lasting longer than 15 consecutive days as “prolonged solitary confinement,” which is prohibited. UN human rights experts have warned that prolonged solitary confinement may constitute cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment – and can amount to torture. Charles Leo Daniel spent a combined 1,244 days in solitary confinement. GEO repeatedly blocks state inspections Despite thousands of complaints and mounting evidence, GEO has kept these conditions hidden from the public – the company has repeatedly blocked attempts by the state to examine conditions inside the facility. Washington health inspectors were denied entry 10 times, including twice after a federal appeals court affirmed the state’s authority to inspect private detention centers. Finally, on July 9, 2026, a federal judge intervened, ordering GEO to admit state inspectors into most of the facility, on the basis that complaints had raised “compelling concerns” for the health and safety of detainees. But federal findings extended beyond health and hygiene concerns, revealing that GEO also relied on detained workers to perform the labor necessary to operate the NWIPC, paying them as little as 1 dollar per day. A federal jury found that GEO had violated Washington’s minimum-wage law, and the company was ordered to pay $17.3 million to more than 10,000 formerly detained workers. Organizing to shut down the NWDC Immigrant rights groups have been organizing to shut down the NWIPC for over a decade. La Resistencia recently joined with Tsuru for Solidarity outside the NWIPC to host a Solidarity Day on August 15, demanding the facility’s closure. La Resistencia was founded in 2014, after more than 1,200 detainees launched a hunger strike against their confinement and living conditions. Led by undocumented immigrants, La Resistencia has supported hundreds of hunger strikes and protests organized by detainees in the facility – including holding daily vigils in solidarity with a recent hunger strike . As organizing continues inside and outside the detention center, these groups are calling on the community to join the fight to shut down the NWIPC and end the deportation flights from King County International Airport.
