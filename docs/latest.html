There’s No End in Sight to Yemen’s Destructive War

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Helen Lackner ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-23T20:36:22+00:00

Yet again, the world is failing to give Yemen the attention it deserves. Well over a month into the renewal of the war between Ansar Allah, known as the Huthis, and the internationally recognized government (IRG), there have already been thousands of military and civilian casualties on both sides. Fierce fighting is going on in different parts of the country, and more than 250,000 people have been displaced, most of whom are moving from one active front line to areas that might be swept up in the conflict within hours. The humanitarian situation is disastrous, with famine threatening in some parts of the country. One of Yemen’s largest cities, Taiz, is under siege by the Huthis, with hardly any medical supplies, food, or water arriving for the population, now swollen by the 120,000 or so people displaced within the governorate. All of this deserves to be the focus of international concern. The return of full-scale fighting in Yemen was expected. Throughout the country, few favored continuation of the “no war, no peace” situation that had prevailed since the 2022 temporary truce. Despite this, the main events to have grabbed the headlines are the few Huthi drones and missiles that successfully attacked airports and some oil facilities in Riyadh and other locations in Saudi Arabia. Admittedly, these attacks have killed close to twenty people and wounded more than three hundred, and they are an important political development for the kingdom. They also demonstrate that the regional conflict has expanded way beyond both Gaza and Iran, thanks to Donald Trump’s recklessness in going to war in support of Benjamin Netanyahu’s projects. While there is a clear link between the Iran war and what is going on in Yemen and Saudi Arabia, the issues in the two centers of conflict are distinct, and each could exist without the other. But are lives lost in Riyadh worth more than those in Taiz or Sana’a? Back to War Since July 2026, the war in Yemen has resumed at a fiercer and more destructive tempo than its original outbreak in 2015. Fighting on the ground is costing hundreds of lives, mainly among the Huthi troops whose leaders are sending waves of young and inexperienced fighters, many of whom were forcibly recruited, to the front lines in pursuit of their offensive. Fighting continues in the northern areas of Marib and Sa’ada bordering Saudi Arabia. In early September, Ansar Allah took control of the southern part of the Yemeni coast, including both the Bab al-Mandab Strait and Mokha. It achieved this rapid victory thanks to three main factors: its own command efficiency, continued division and lack of coordination among its opponents, and the use of advanced technology, including artificial intelligence misinformation systems. The period since the 2022 truce allowed the Huthis to strengthen and improve their strategy and acquire even more sophisticated weaponry. Their intervention against Israel between late 2023 and 2025 gave them an international popularity boost. Links and support from Hezbollah and Iran contributed to their military skills, assisted by the purchase of advanced technological elements on the international market. They have also benefited from AI advances, as was demonstrated recently by their attempted use of Anthropic’s Claude to develop a missile and then to identify the cause of its failure. The Huthis have long since demonstrated the absurdity of dismissing their movement as a bunch of wild, sandal-wearing tribesmen armed with handguns. Partly as a consequence of the military battering suffered in 2025 from the fifty-two-day US bombing campaign and a number of well-targeted Israeli air strikes, Ansar Allah initially chose to stay on the sidelines of the US-Israeli war against Iran. It expressed support for Iran but only launched a few missiles against Israel in late March, thus maintaining the image of being anti-Israeli rather than pro-Iran. It is only since the breakdown of the “Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding” in late June that the Huthis have taken an active role in the Red Sea. Instead of closing the Bab al-Mandab Strait altogether, following Iranian statements that this option was an additional weapon in the struggle to control the world hydrocarbon market, the Huthis limited their blockade to Saudi shipping. They made a mutual nonaggression deal with the United States , stating that they would not interfere with shipping from any other country. One should note that this implies Israeli shipping is also welcome to use the Red Sea, contrary to the Huthi basic slogan “Death to America, Death to Israel!” and raising questions about their ideological commitments. Between March and July, the Saudis had transferred most of their oil exports through the East–West Pipeline to reach the sea, thus minimizing the disruption to their trade resulting from the Gulf blockades. Attacks on Saudi shipping in August reduced the amount of oil exported from Saudi Arabia’s Yanbu port from five million barrels per day to fewer than two million. This was in addition to the considerable additional cost and time needed to reach the East Asian market, as oil has to be transported all the way around Africa. This has contributed to the world oil crisis. The Huthi Offensives The Huthis had a number of reasons to initiate the fighting at this time. The twenty million people under their rule are suffering worsening poverty due to the growing economic impact of the US designation of Ansar Allah as a “foreign terrorist organization.” This has been combined with the almost complete withdrawal of humanitarian aid to Huthi-ruled areas. Although popular suffering is not a major motivation for Ansar Allah, poverty reduces potential sources of taxation, which is needed to finance military salaries and the purchase of weaponry. The closer relationship of Saudi Arabia with the IRG that followed the exclusion of Emirati-backed southern separatists in early 2026 was also an emerging threat. This was linked to efforts to overcome the remaining divisions within IRG-linked forces. Internationally, the Huthis were concerned about Saudi Arabia’s nascent initiatives, such as the Mecca Defence Alliance with Pakistan and Turkey and the Multinational Maritime Defense Coalition. The latter is a defensive naval alliance formed in July 2026 to protect shipping, international trade, and energy supply lines from Huthi attacks. The international situation will also have encouraged Huthi leaders, suggesting to them that neither the United States nor Israel was likely to focus on Yemen or Saudi Arabia while preoccupied with forthcoming elections and their ongoing, stalemated wars. Combined with a widespread sense of exhaustion over the “no war, no peace” situation, this will have motivated the Huthis to go on the attack while they were still clearly the stronger military entity. In July, the Saudis bombed the runway of Sana’a airport to prevent an Iranian aircraft from landing. Ansar Allah used this as a pretext to justify escalation. It demanded an end to all blockades on Yemen and described Saudi Arabia as its main enemy, reviving the decade-old discourse and barely mentioning its Yemeni opponents. Starting in August, the offensive rapidly shifted from the better-defended Marib region, where the Huthis had failed to break through prior to the truce, to the southern Red Sea coast. Their rapid successes here made it possible to take Mokha and reach the Bab al-Mandab Strait and its islands by September 10. Although fighting persists in Marib and Saada, the main focus is now Taiz governorate and its capital, which is under siege and suffering both military and humanitarian emergencies. While conditions in Taiz are extreme, the city reflects a wider pattern of IRG fragmentation and offers the best site from which to examine Yemen’s political divisions. Taiz has been the focus of fighting between the Huthis and their opponents throughout the decade, with parts of the city and governorate under the control of either side. Long considered the country’s intellectual capital, it is home to the strongest elements of all political forces, from Salafis at one extreme to the Yemeni Socialist Party at the other, as well as a multiplicity of armed groups. As well as resisting the Huthis for the past decade, these forces have also fought against each other. At different times, military groups connected with al-Qaeda, the Islah party, Tareq Saleh’s National Resistance forces, and other elements have fought for control of the city and its sources of income. Today, in the face of a possible complete takeover by the Huthis, their opponents face an urgent need to overcome these differences. The IRG’s Weaknesses The rapid failure of Saudi-backed IRG forces in September came as a shock. Lack of coordination into a single effective command and rivalries between units demonstrated that the process of military restructuring that had been implemented since February was far from complete. As elsewhere in Yemen, patience was running thin with the “no war, no peace” situation, and the IRG was getting ready for the resumption of fighting. In response to the Huthi offensive, it launched the “Dawn of Yemen” counteroffensive in early October. This move successfully recovered some territory in the vicinity of the strait, but it has stalled without being able to break the siege of Taiz, despite some gains in the far north. There is no doubt that the IRG seriously underestimated Huthi technological capacities, and it may also have failed to develop its own skills in that sector. The absence of ground-based cadres certainly contributes to strategic failures, such as the one that resulted in the massacre of seventy-three new recruits and the wounding of more than two hundred in Shabwa on October 19. The “Dawn of Yemen” offensive aims to defeat the Huthis. To achieve that task, it would require unity, a sophisticated overall perspective, and preparations for a long-term struggle. Yemenis throughout the country desperately long for an end to the war with a reasonable agreement that would allow them to focus on recovery and development. The IRG is not the Saudi mercenary force that the Huthis speak of in their propaganda. But it cannot win the war on its own for both financial and political reasons, so it needs Saudi backing. Unfortunately, Saudi political influence remains a fundamental feature of the scene in Yemen that we cannot ignore. Saudi Dilemmas Ansar Allah has also been launching missile attacks on Saudi Arabia that are regular and often lethal. This has had a considerable impact on the Saudi economy. The latest highly publicized international event to have been postponed to better days was the annual “Davos in the Desert” investment conference. This followed a series of less prominent cancellations, all of which have affected the regime’s reputation and its ability to attract investment. Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman must bitterly regret his initial decision to go to war against the Huthis in 2015 and has been trying to extricate his country from the Yemen quagmire since 2020. Negotiators were close to reaching a deal in late 2023, but the Gaza genocide and the US-Israeli war against Iran put paid to those efforts. While Iran is unlikely to be directly responsible for Huthi attacks on Saudi Arabia, the contribution of those attacks to pressure on world hydrocarbon supplies helps Tehran in its stand-off with the United States. In the long run, Saudi vulnerability also weakens its potential for regional domination. The kingdom’s malaise has revealed the failure of the massive bribes it has dispensed over the course of decades to buy support in its hour of need. In spite of the recent international agreements, the rulers of Egypt and Pakistan are only offering limited assistance. British and French contributions will not change the course of the war, and the billions Riyadh has promised to Trump have not persuaded Washington to throw its weight on the scales. Having spent billions more on high-tech weaponry, mostly from the United States, the Saudi military finds itself unable to stop Huthi missiles, due to a lack of interceptors and the vastness of their country. Saudi Arabia now stands alone, facing the Huthis directly within its borders. Saudi Arabia’s experience since 2015 has confirmed that air strikes alone do not win a war. This has certainly contributed to the regime’s hesitation about deploying its military in Yemen once again — a military that already has a reputation as a “parade” force. But the IRG ground forces will need aerial support if they are going to reverse the Huthi gains of recent months. Huthi calls for major financial and political concessions from the Saudi regime become more and more unrealistic as they continue their strikes on Saudi infrastructure, raising the issue of Ansar Allah’s objectives. The dilemma for the Saudi regime worsens by the day: if it wants to reach a deal with the Huthis, that will require unacceptable and unfeasible concessions, but if it continues launching air strikes against their forces in Yemen, there will be more retaliation against targets within the kingdom. It is difficult to see Iran’s offer to mediate with the Huthis as anything other than an attempt to persuade the Saudis to make major concessions. Quiet mediation by Oman is also unlikely to achieve much while the Huthis believe they are on a winning streak. Meanwhile, millions of Yemenis are now in a state of deepening crisis, with hunger and the lack of medical supplies compounded by the risk of bombing, and without any end or hope in sight. Milwaukee’s Proud History of Baseball — and Socialism

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Peter Dreier ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-23T18:30:36+00:00

Organizers like to say that all politics boils down to a battle between organized money and organized people. Candidates with the largest war chests tend to win, but contenders who refuse to take donations from corporate interests can prevail if their supporters are well-organized, inspired, and strategic. Likewise, big employers can spend huge sums on union busting, but employees can win union organizing drives if they know how to mobilize workers to stick together and not be intimidated. The same is true in baseball. Teams in big markets with large payrolls have a decided advantage when it comes to recruiting talented players who command huge salaries, but feisty, scrappy teams with fewer stars can sometimes win championships. Since 2000, more than 80 percent of the teams that have advanced to the World Series ranked in the top half of league payrolls. Teams with the five largest payrolls (out of thirty teams) account for roughly 38 percent of all World Series contestants and nearly half (48 percent) of all World Series champions. But this year is an exception: the World Series contestants — the Milwaukee Brewers and the Tampa Bay Rays — are both among the poorer teams in baseball. Milwaukee is Major League Baseball’s smallest market in terms of population size and TV audience and ranks nineteenth in payroll ($143.5 million). Tampa Bay is twenty-fifth in market size and ranks twenty-sixth in payroll ($98.3 million). The top-spending teams are the New York Mets ($346.8 million) and the Los Angeles Dodgers ($338 million). Despite the disparity, the Brewers swept the Dodgers in four games in the National League Championship Series to make it to the World Series, thwarting LA’s hopes to win three consecutive World Series. The Brewers and Rays won their respective leagues by combining strong pitching, agile and savvy “small ball” play, and because of managers who utilized their rosters to get the most out of teams without many highly paid superstars. During the first half of the twentieth century, in both politics and baseball, Milwaukee’s have-nots often beat the odds. And indeed, those two histories are intertwined in ways not often appreciated. Milwaukee’s Sewer Socialists In the early 1900s, Milwaukee’s socialists came to power by forging an alliance with the city’s labor movement. The city’s culture was heavily shaped by German immigrants, whose athletic and gymnastic clubs embedded the ideal of a “sound mind in a sound body.” One of them, Turner Hall, built in 1883 near the city’s downtown, served as a gathering place for Milwaukee’s radicals and socialists. It provided its members a place to socialize, listen to music, drink beer, and discuss politics. Eugene Debs gave his first speech as a Socialist there in 1898 and spoke at Turner Hall on many other occasions. (It remains a popular site for meetings and performances, with a gym in the basement.) One of Turner Hall’s members was Victor Berger, an Austrian Jewish immigrant who spearheaded the Socialist Party’s rise to local power. Berger was a schoolteacher, an editor of two newspapers (one in German, another in English), and a skilled organizer. He and Debs helped found the Socialist Party of America (SP) in 1901. Berger built the Milwaukee branch into a formidable political machine. He secured the backing of the unions, including the powerful brewery workers’ locals and most German and Polish immigrants. The SP sponsored carnivals, picnics, singing societies, and even Sunday schools, conducting their campaigns in English, German, and Polish. In 1910, Milwaukee voters sent Berger to Washington as the country’s first socialist congressman. He lost his seat in 1912 but was elected again in 1918 and in a 1919 special election; both times the House refused to seat him because of his opposition to World War I. He returned to Congress in 1922, served three terms, and lost his seat in 1928. Berger sponsored bills for old-age pensions, government ownership of the radio industry, abolition of child labor, self-government for the District of Columbia, and a system of public works to provide relief for the unemployed. He also sponsored resolutions to withdraw federal troops from the Mexican border, for the abolition of the Senate (which was then not yet elected directly by the voters and was nicknamed the “millionaires’ club”), for women’s suffrage, and for federal ownership of the railroads. While none of Berger’s proposals gained much traction in Congress, his comrades in Milwaukee were a dominant force in the city’s civic life. In 1912, some 1,200 SP members held public office in 340 cities and towns across the United States, including Socialist mayors in Schenectady and Berkeley; Socialists would later win the mayor’s office in Minneapolis (1916) and Reading (1927). But Socialists’ most enduring success emerged in Milwaukee. Between 1910 and 1960, Milwaukee was led for nearly forty years by three Socialist mayors — Emil Seidel (1910–12), Daniel Hoan (1916–1940), and Frank Zeidler (1948–1960) — and also boasted a number of city council and school board members. “The workers of our city are its most valuable asset,” Seidel, a woodcarver and union leader, said in his inaugural address. During his two-year term, the city adopted an eight-hour day for municipal employees and increased their minimum wage from $1.75 to $2.00 a day. In 1910, the city added chlorine to the lake water, which sharply reduced the incidence of typhoid. A few years later, following a huge outbreak of diarrhea, the city stopped mixing raw sewage with drinking water. Milwaukee Mayor Daniel Hoan, circa 1930 ( San Francisco Examiner / CC BY 4.0 ) Opposed by Milwaukee’s business establishment, Seidel lost the 1912 election. That year he was the SP’s vice-presidential candidate, running alongside Debs. They gained 6 percent of the national vote, 8.4 percent in Wisconsin, and 27 percent in Milwaukee County. In 1916, the Milwaukee Socialists made a dramatic comeback, catapulting city attorney Daniel Webster Hoan into office. Hoan served as mayor until 1940. Detractors initially mocked them as “sewer socialists,” because they focused heavily on solving practical problems, not on revolutionary Marxist ideas. In 1936, Time magazine put Hoan on its cover and praised Milwaukee as “one of the best-run cities in the U.S.” Milwaukee’s voters kept reelecting Hoan because he ran efficient and humane administrations, showing it was possible to both expand public services and balance the city’s budget. The socialists expanded public education for the city’s working-class children (including free textbooks, lunches, and dental and medical programs), created an adult education program for workers, expanded the public libraries and playgrounds, and improved teachers’ salaries. In 1923, Socialist Charles Whitnall — a onetime city treasurer and founder of the Commonwealth Mutual Savings Bank for workers — created a master plan for a network of beautiful parks and protected the city’s Lake Michigan lakefront from development, to ensure the working class would have access to the beaches and other recreation areas. As a result, Milwaukee had the nation’s best municipal park system. In 1923, the Socialists built the country’s first city-sponsored public housing project, Garden Homes. They inaugurated a public bus system to replace the street trolleys that ran down the middle of the road, causing accidents and injuring pedestrians. Under Hoan, the Socialists adopted tough factory and building regulations and inspections, reined in police brutality against striking workers, improved working conditions for rank-and-file cops, and sponsored public markets. They gave preference to union shops for city contracts for everything from printing to horseshoeing. Hoan pushed for a law that allowed the mayor to close any factory if the employer refused to negotiate with the workers. The Socialists forced the city’s private streetcar company to pave the streets, lower fares, improve service, and pay fees to the city in exchange for its monopoly. They opened a municipal quarry to provide crushed rock for street projects, built a municipally owned water system, and created a pioneering sanitation infrastructure. In 1925, the city built the Jones Island water treatment plant, one of the first facilities of its kind, and still in operation. From the start, Milwaukee recycled its sewage sludge as Milorganite (for Mil waukee Org anic Nit rog e n), a fertilizer used on parks, golf courses, and lawns around the country. The treatment plant is now run by the Milwaukee Metropolitan Sewerage District . It sells about 47,000 tons of Milorganite each year, which generates over $12 million in annual revenue for the city, turning a waste management cost into a major revenue stream. In 1932, voters elected Socialist labor lawyer Max Raskin — great-uncle of Jamie Raskin, currently a progressive congressman from Maryland — as city attorney. A close Hoan ally, Raskin was attacked by a conservative newspaper for refusing to prosecute “communistic rioters” — meaning strikers and protesters. Raskin required his assistant city attorneys to end any employment in private practice, which had been a source of corruption and conflicts of interest. Zeidler, the third Socialist mayor, was elected in 1948. He was a popular figure who shared the progressive values of his socialist predecessors, but by then the business community had gained greater influence; at least one major employer threatened to fire employees who voted for Zeidler. During his twelve years as mayor, the city doubled its geographic size by annexing nearby areas. In 1957, Fortune magazine called Milwaukee one of the two best-governed large cities in the country. During his time as mayor, Milwaukee’s black population nearly tripled, reaching 8 percent of the population by 1960. Among other measures, Zeidler pushed for the construction of racially integrated public housing. His political enemies tried to use his strong support for the civil rights movement against him, including spreading false rumors that he had erected billboards in the South urging African Americans to move to Milwaukee to take advantage of its liberal welfare programs. Business Ascends, Milwaukee Declines After Zeidler left office, the city was governed by several business-friendly mayors, which coincided with the city’s dramatic decline starting in the 1960s, from the eleventh to the thirty-first largest city in the country. Its population dropped from 741,000 people in 1960 to about 562,000 today, 23 percent of whom live below the poverty line. Banks fostered white flight to the suburbs by redlining Milwaukee’s neighborhoods. Today the Milwaukee area is one of the most racially segregated regions in the country. Milwaukee’s economy has never recovered from the massive job and population decline. A. O. Smith, the giant manufacturer of water heaters, boilers, and car frames, founded in 1874, still has its global headquarters in Milwaukee, but its production plants are now located in the American South as well as China, Mexico, India, and Turkey. Almost eight thousand union employees once worked at its factory on the city’s northwest side. After being abandoned for many years, the site is now an industrial park, but the number of employees is a small fraction of the former A. O. Smith workforce. Milwaukee was once home to brewery giants like Pabst, Schlitz (which promoted itself as “the beer that made Milwaukee famous”), Blatz, and Miller. In 1905, Milwaukee had 3,809 brewery workers in just eight major companies. It reached its peak in 1953, when Milwaukee had over 7,100 unionized brewery workers. Today a conglomerate called Molson Coors dominates the local beer industry. It employs about 1,200 people in Milwaukee in its main Miller brewery and its corporate offices. Many local draft breweries dot the area, but they produce a relatively small volume per worker compared to the large factories that once called Milwaukee home. Bill Veeck, Baseball’s Radical Owner Milwaukee’s working class embraced baseball as well as socialism. The city was home to minor league, employer-sponsored, semi-pro, and amateur teams. It was a hotbed for talent, particularly during the first half of the twentieth century. Since 1900, at least sixty-three Milwaukee natives have played in the majors, including such stars as Al Simmons (originally Aloysius Szymanski) and Tony Kubek (both of Polish descent) and Ken Keltner and Harvey Kuenn (both from blue-collar families of German ancestry). About another two hundred major leaguers have come from the rest of Wisconsin. Bill Veeck, pictured in 1944 while recovering from injuries sustained in World War II (US Marine Corps) Starting in 1878, Milwaukee sponsored several teams in the early days of professional baseball, none of which lasted for more than a few years. Teams called the Brewers participated in the Western Association from 1888 to 1891, the American Association in 1891, the Western League from 1892 to 1899, and the American League in 1900 and 1901. The longest-lasting franchise, from 1902 to 1952, was the Brewers team in the top-level minor league, the American Association. The Brewers, as well as the Negro National League’s Milwaukee Bears (1923) and the Milwaukee Chicks of the All American Girls Professional Baseball League (1944), played at the quirky, wooden Borchert Field, a privately owned stadium on the city’s North Side. Built in 1888 to accommodate five thousand spectators, its capacity grew to eighteen thousand after several expansions. Ironically, baseball’s greatest maverick, Bill Veeck, then only twenty-seven, purchased the bankrupt, last-place minor-league Brewers in 1941, the year after Hoan was defeated for reelection. Born in 1914, Veeck grew up in baseball. His father was a sportswriter who wound up as president of the Chicago Cubs. He dropped out of college after his father died and did odd jobs for the Cubs. He had an unusual appreciation (for a white fan) for Negro League players. He put together a syndicate of investors to buy the Brewers, a struggling Triple-A team in the American Association. He was the last owner to purchase a baseball franchise without an independent fortune. During his five seasons as Brewers owner, Veeck revolutionized the game. Veeck was both a shrewd businessman and a radical reformer. He voted for perennial Socialist Party presidential candidate Norman Thomas even after Thomas had died. “I’d rather vote for a dead man with class than two live bums,” he later said. Veeck loved to sit in the bleachers and hang out with the working-class fans. He packed Borchert Field during World War II by scheduling Brewers’ games in the morning so that workers on the late shift at war plants could attend games. He also served a breakfast of cornflakes at the game. He staged free days for cab drivers and bartenders. In Milwaukee, Veeck began giving away promotion prizes — caps, a keg of nails, bottles of soda, pizza, and even animals (turkeys, geese, pigs, lobsters, and rabbits) — to attract fans to the park. Veeck modernized and cleaned the stadium’s restrooms specifically to make the ballpark more welcoming to female fans, a vastly underserved market at the time. He gave away orchids to women fans on Mother’s Day. He staged weddings at home plate. Satchel Paige in 1947 (ACME Newspictures) Sportswriters dubbed him “the [P. T.] Barnum of Baseball.” But he wasn’t just a showman. Despite a shoestring budget, he took the Brewers from last place in 1941 to a second-place finish in 1942, followed by three consecutive American Association pennants from 1943 to 1945; the Brewers’ attendance shattered minor league records. Veeck used similar promotional strategies when he purchased the Cleveland Indians in 1946. He owned the St Louis Browns (1951–53) and bought the Chicago White Sox (1959–1961, 1975–1981). Throughout his life, Veeck was an ardent anti-racist. When he purchased the Brewers, he inherited the team’s spring training facility in Ocala, Florida. During one game, Veeck decided to sit in the “colored” section of the stands. The local police showed up, told him he couldn’t sit there, and ordered him to move, threatening to arrest him for violating Florida’s Jim Crow laws. He told the town’s mayor that he’d move the team’s lucrative spring training program out of Ocala if they arrested him. They didn’t, but he moved the Brewers anyway. In 1947, Veeck’s Cleveland Indians hired Larry Doby, the first black player in the American League. The next year, he made Negro League star Satchel Paige, then forty-two-years old, the first black pitcher in the league. That team won the 1948 World Series. He joined the local NAACP and appeared, along with Doby and Paige, on a poster designed to recruit new members to the civil rights organization, featuring the phrase, “The NAACP gets the ball for you.” When he owned the White Sox, he canceled the team’s spring training reservations at Miami’s McAllister Hotel when it refused to accommodate black players, moving the entire team to another hotel. In 1970, he testified in federal court on behalf of Cardinals’ African American outfielder Curt Flood, who was suing major league baseball to overturn the reserve clause, baseball’s version of indentured servitude. Veeck expressed his socialist views when it came to baseball’s business side, too. At meetings of owners and in public statements, Veeck argued that wealthy teams in big markets should share revenue with teams in small markets to level the playing field and create a competitive balance. He contended that when his lowly St Louis Browns played the prosperous New York Yankees, they were entitled to a 50 percent share of the Yankees’ local television revenue, observing, “We’re half the show. They can’t play the game without us.” The other owners hated Veeck and his ideas. Boston Red Sox owner Tom Yawkey called Veeck “that damned socialist.” Corporatization Comes for Baseball After Veeck sold the Brewers, the team stayed in Milwaukee as a minor league franchise. In the late 1940s, the corporate-backed Greater Milwaukee Committee pushed for construction of a new publicly funded stadium with the explicit goal of attracting a major league franchise to the city. Their plan had some elements of the socialists’ focus on public works and recreational infrastructure, but it was hardly on behalf of the city’s working class. County Stadium opened in April 1953 as the first major ballpark in the United States financed entirely with public funds. The new ballpark enticed the Boston Braves to Milwaukee. In 1952, the minor league Brewers had attracted 195,839 fans to Borchert Field. The following season, the Braves lured 1.8 million fans to County Stadium. To increase revenue, the stadium was also used for Green Bay Packers football games, religious services, concerts, ice skating, and other large events. In 1957, the Braves, with stars like Hank Aaron, Warren Spahn, and Eddie Mathews, won the National League pennant and defeated the Yankees in the World Series. That season, attendance at County Stadium reached 2.2 million, first among the sixteen major league teams. The next season, the Braves again faced the Yankees in the World Series but lost four out of seven games. The team’s on-field performance and attendance declined steadily after that. In 1965, only 555,584 fans showed up, as the Braves slipped to fifth place in the National League, while Milwaukee suffered from the loss of jobs and population. The following year, the team moved to Atlanta. In 1970, as part of MLB expansion, car dealer Bud Selig brought together a group of investors and persuaded the other owners to approve another major league team in Milwaukee. He purchased the bankrupt Seattle Pilots, moved them to Milwaukee, and named the new team the Brewers after the minor league team he grew up watching. The Milwaukee Braves play at County Stadium in September 1960. (Robert Jahnke / CC BY 2.0 ) Starting in the late 1980s, Selig threatened to move the team out of Milwaukee if the state government didn’t build a modern, retractable-roof stadium. He claimed that the aging County Stadium couldn’t generate the revenue need to keep the team competitive, such as sales of luxury suites. In 1995, with the support of local business leaders, the politicians complied, enacting a 0.1 percent regional sales tax across five Wisconsin counties, in addition to funds from the state, county, and city. Like its predecessor, the new stadium was built with public funds. Although business and civic leaders called it a public-private partnership, it was little more than crony capitalism. Taxpayers paid for the stadium but, under the lease agreement, didn’t share in the Brewers’ profits. The public paid $609 million to pay off the building costs, borrowing interest, and maintenance. The Milwaukee-based Lynde and Harry Bradley Foundation, known as a right-wing philanthropy, provided a $20 million loan. The Brewers’ contribution was less than $40 million, almost all of it from the Miller Brewing Company, which paid the team that amount for naming rights. As a result, the stadium was called Miller Park from its opening in 2001 until the conclusion of the 2020 season. Despite investing so little in the ballpark, the Brewers own 36 percent of the facility, while the state-created Southeast Wisconsin Professional Baseball Park District owns the rest. In 1998, Selig’s fellow owners named him baseball commissioner, and he put his financial stake in the team in a trust managed by his daughter Wendy. The family sold the Brewers in 2005 to Los Angeles investor Mark Attanasio, who still owns the team. Twenty-two of Major League Baseball’s stadiums currently have corporate naming rights deals. In 2020, the Brewers switched their ballpark’s name from a brewery company’s to that of an insurance corporation. American Family Insurance, ranked 226 on the Fortune 500 list, with $19.6 billion in revenues, signed a fifteen-year agreement to pay about $4 million a year to change the stadium name to American Family Field. Milwaukee’s early socialists would certainly be outraged that even though the stadium is publicly owned, the Brewers control corporate sponsorships and stadium naming rights and have used their political clout to block efforts to get the legislature to split sponsorship revenues equally between the team and the state. If the socialists still ran the city, or if Bill Veeck still owned the team, the Brewers might be playing in Victor Berger Field or Daniel Hoan Stadium. Miller Park, now American Family Field, in 2005 (Jeramey Jannene / CC BY 2.0 ) In 2023, the Brewers persuaded Wisconsin lawmakers to subsidize renovations of American Family Field for around $500 million. Ryan Clancy, a Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) member who serves in the Wisconsin State Legislature and was previously a member of the Milwaukee County Board of Supervisors, was an outspoken opponent of using public funds for stadium renovations, which he calls “corporate socialism” and “corporate welfare.” Clancy thinks tax revenues should focus on local services such as public transit, parks, public safety, and housing, rather than subsidizing Attanasio’s team. Attanasio paid $223 million to buy the Brewers in 2005; by 2016, the franchise was worth $875 million. Today the team is worth $1.9 billion, according to Forbes . Baseball has been totally corporatized since Bill Veeck’s day. His entire career was devoted to baseball, and he was always in financial distress. In contrast, Attanasio, the latest Brewers’ owner, made his initial fortune with the private equity firm Crescent Capital Group. He not only owns the Brewers but is also majority shareholder of English football club Norwich City and part-owner of the American Hockey League’s Milwaukee Admirals. He is also on the board of directors for LA28, which oversees plans for the 2028 Olympic Games in Los Angeles. Although he’s hardly a progressive, much less a socialist, he is one of the few baseball owners who primarily contributes to Democratic candidates. Although he has a net worth of $1.9 billion, Attanasio is among the poorest of the thirty MLB team owners. The wealthiest is Mets owner Steve Cohen, a Wall Street hedge fund tycoon with a net worth of $22 billion. Second is Edward Rogers III, who owns the Toronto Blue Jays and is worth $11 billion, according to Forbes . He inherited the telecommunications and media empire founded by his father, which includes Rogers Wireless, Rogers Cable (Canada’s largest cable TV provider), forty-six radio stations, seventy consumer and trade magazines, and the Blue Jays. Mark Walter, the Dodgers’ largest shareholder, ranks third, with an estimated net worth between $7 billion and $13 billion. Walter is cofounder and CEO of the financial conglomerate Guggenheim Partners. Last year, he bought a majority stake in the Los Angeles Lakers basketball team and sold it just fourteen months later to former Disney CEO Bob Iger and Thrive Capital founder Joshua Kushner (Jared’s younger brother) at a $12.5 billion valuation — $2.5 billion more than the team was valued at when he bought it. Walter is also co-owner of the WNBA’s Los Angeles Sparks and is a major investor in England’s Chelsea football club. The Brewers didn’t win their first pennant until 1982, when they played in the American League, and lost to the St Louis Cardinals in a seven-game World Series. The team shifted to the National League in 1998. It finally won another pennant this year, leading the majors with 103 wins. Out of Left Field Debates over socialism are now shaking up both baseball and politics. By 1993, the revenue and payroll disparities in the MLB had become untenable. The small-market franchises were struggling to keep up with escalating player salaries in larger markets: the Yankees’ television and radio income was $47 million, while the Brewer’s collected only $5.5 million. It wasn’t until the 1990s — following a strike by the Major League Baseball Players Association (MLBPA) — that baseball’s moguls adopted a version of Veeck’s decades-old revenue-sharing plan (although without giving him credit). Yankees owner George Steinbrenner was one of those who fiercely resisted any proposal to redistribute revenue. “I’m a charitable guy,” he said. “But I don’t like the idea of a socialist state. The Yankees shouldn’t be punished for their success.” In January 1994, the owners approved a plan to pool local broadcasting and gate revenues to aid small-market clubs. But they made their plan contingent on the players’ union agreeing to a salary cap. The MLBPA rejected the deal, which it saw as a way to suppress player earnings and restrict the free-agent market. The owners rebuffed the union’s counterproposal, which included a luxury tax on the sixteen highest-payroll teams and putting 25 percent of all gate receipts into a pool that would be shared with the lowest-payroll teams. The impasse led to a 232-day strike that canceled the 1994 World Series. It only ended in 1995 when federal judge Sonia Sotomayor (now a US Supreme Court justice) issued an injunction against the owners. The collective bargaining agreement, finalized in 1997, severed revenue sharing from the idea of a salary cap. Under that landmark agreement, wealthy, large-market clubs transferred a portion of their local revenues to small-market teams, and a luxury tax was placed on high-spending teams to fund the revenue-sharing pool meant to level the playing field. Despite these reforms, there’s still a wide gap in revenue and payroll between the thirty teams. Both the NFL and NBA have much more progressive formulas for leveling the playing field. MLB rules don’t require teams to spend their additional revenue on payroll. On average, teams spend about half their total revenue on salaries, but the range is dramatic. The Mets allocate about 90 percent of their revenue on player salaries, for instance, while the Marlins spend only 26 percent of their $317 million revenue on payroll. Fans in some small markets have accused their teams of treating revenue-sharing as a profit center, not as a way to create a competitive balance. The Brewers are the most generous spenders among small-market teams, investing 41 percent of revenue into player salaries. But the small-market teams have a hard time retaining their best players because they can’t match the salary offers of the wealthier teams. The most expensive players are not going to play in Milwaukee, Pittsburgh, Miami, or Tampa Bay. Baseball’s owners and the union are currently in the middle of negotiations over the next collective bargaining agreement. Under current Commissioner Rob Manfred, the owners are more adamant than ever that the union must agree to a salary cap. Close observers believe that it could lead the owners to initiate a lockout. Many fans view baseball’s labor-management battles as a conflict between billionaires and millionaires. Twenty-seven of baseball’s thirty owners are billionaires. Only Colorado Rockies owner Dick Monfort (worth a mere $700 million), Miami Marlins owner Bruce Sherman ($500 million), and Cincinnati Reds owner Bob Castellini ($400 million) fall below the threshold. Meanwhile, thanks to the players’ union and the end of the reserve clause in the 1970s, players’ salaries and working conditions have improved dramatically. The average player salary is $5.34 million, but this is heavily skewed by a small number of star players with enormous contracts. The median salary — $1.4 million — provides a more realistic picture. But even that figure is misleading, because the typical major league career is only five years. Most players are only short-term millionaires. Attendance at major league games this season reached 71.7 million, close to the highest on record. The thirty teams generated $12 billion in revenue this year, the second highest amount in the sport’s history. The typical cost for a family of four to attend a major league game ranges from $159 for the Miami Marlins to $413 at Dodger Stadium in Los Angeles. In most major league ballparks, the workers who clean the stadiums, sell the food and concessions, and work in the parking lots are unionized with UNITE HERE or SEIU, but these are seasonal jobs and many of the employees have a hard time making ends meet. Baseball’s owners can afford to reduce the price of tickets, parking, and concessions for fans, and improve wages for stadium employees, without placing a cap on player salaries. The cost of attending baseball games is part of the nation’s affordability crisis, itself a symptom of the widening gaps in wealth and income. It is one reason why socialism is gaining traction. Polls show growing support for socialism over capitalism, while the number of socialists elected to office is skyrocketing. Membership in DSA reached over 120,000 this year, a huge increase in just a decade. These trends are reflected in Milwaukee. The local DSA chapter has grown from 350 members in 2025 to about one thousand today. (Madison, the state capital and home to the University of Wisconsin, has a similar number of members.) The dramatic spurt in members across the state made it possible for Francesca Hong , one of four DSAers in the state legislature, to come close to winning the Democratic nomination for governor this year. Alex Brower, a thirty-nine-year old DSA member and former union organizer, was elected to Milwaukee’s city council in 2025. He grew up in Wisconsin and is a stalwart Brewers fan. He is thrilled that his team will be in the current World Series. But he also views Major League Baseball as a big business that cares more about its profits than the lives of its customers (the fans) or its working-class employees. The Milwaukee DSA chapter has been waging campaigns to municipalize the city’s private utility, restrict the repressive forces of Immigration and Customs Enforcement, protect tenants from unscrupulous landlords, and support workers’ union drives. Brower would eventually like to see DSA and its allies wage a campaign to bring the Brewers under public or community control, similar to the arrangement that governs the nearby Green Bay Packers . Even though a government agency owns American Family Field, “the Brewers get all the benefits and the community gets nothing,” said Brower. He noted that zip code 53206, which is just a few miles from the stadium, is one of the poorest areas in the state. “The Brewer’s merch is flying off the shelves,” he said, while some of the poorest Milwaukeeans are paying a sales tax to finance and fix up the ballpark. “The rest of us have to pick up the tab, including paying for the cops who direct traffic on game days, and the wear and tear on the streets.” “Milwaukee’s brewery workers were socialists,” Brower explained. “So shouldn’t the Milwaukee Brewers reflect that legacy?” Daniel Hoan couldn’t have said it better. How American History Celebrates Enslavers and Erases Their Victims

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Sharon Kyle ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-23T16:58:18+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. Recently, a friend asked whether I had seen the documentary Natchez . Knowing of my involvement in Coming to the Table , he sent a link, encouraged me to watch, and asked whether I would write about it afterward. Coming to the Table brings together descendants of enslaved people and enslavers, along with others committed to racial justice, to uncover history, build relationships, pursue healing, and take action. Through that work, I have come to know Tom DeWolf, a founding member and longtime leader. His ancestors, the DeWolfs of Bristol, Rhode Island, were the largest slave-trading family in U.S. history, responsible for trafficking approximately 10,000 Africans into slavery. Tom joined nine relatives on a journey confronting that history, documented in Traces of the Trade: A Story from the Deep North and his book, Inheriting the Trade . His work asks whose suffering supplied a family’s wealth and what responsibilities follow from knowing that truth. Those questions shaped my viewing of Natchez . Watching it left me gutted. As a Black woman descended from enslaved people, I cannot separate this history from my ancestors’ lives. The persistent disregard for their pain hurts. Watching that disregard persist among people alive today adds another injury. Natchez exposes competing accounts of the plantation South. Black guides and preservationists challenge the mythology. The film’s willingness to expose it matters. What gutted me was the continuing appetite for the fantasy it documents. In 2026, there is still a market for plantation grandeur that keeps the people whose captivity sustained it at an emotional distance. Houses become treasures. Enslavers maintain the veneer of accomplished gentlemen. The people they brutalized are little more than background. A documentary needs an audience to be made and distributed. Audiences can seek confrontation with history or reassurance about it. Natchez brings those competing desires into view. My indictment is of the continuing acceptance and appetite for the antebellum myth: the desire to enjoy the beauty without fully facing its brutality and the human cost. I have encountered that desire intimately. Years ago, I dated a man who told me slavery was wrong but insisted we could not judge the Founders by today’s standards. I ended the relationship that day. His defense asked me to accommodate his admiration for the founders while accepting his emotional distance from hundreds of people they harmed and the generations descended from them. I could not build intimacy with someone whose admiration mattered more to him than my ancestors humanity. Enslaved people did not need today’s standards to understand the wrong being done to them. Their resistance, escapes, and testimony bore witness to their suffering at the hands of tormentors we continue to deify—and to their rejection of a system whose brutality admirers of enslavers still find ways to excuse. My ancestors’ suffering was historical. His unwillingness to take it seriously was happening then, to me, the Black woman standing before him. Consider how differently we recognize captivity elsewhere. In 1991, Phillip Garrido and his wife, Nancy, abducted eleven-year-old Jaycee Dugard. They held her captive for eighteen years. Beginning when Jaycee was 11 years old, Phillip repeatedly raped her; she gave birth at fourteen and seventeen. In Austria, Josef Fritzl imprisoned his eighteen-year-old daughter, Elisabeth, in a cellar for twenty-four years, repeatedly raping her. She bore seven children; one died in infancy. Both men were convicted and imprisoned. Garrido received 431 years to life; Fritzl received life imprisonment. Their crimes were recognized as horrific violations of human beings. Before their arrests, Garrido and Fritzl had careers. Garrido operated his own printing business. Fritzl was an electrical engineer and rental-property owner. Now imagine documentaries inviting us to admire Garrido’s entrepreneurship or Fritzl’s engineering accomplishments. Imagine mentioning their victims briefly before returning to the men’s achievements. Who would buy a ticket to see that? - The producers would be hard pressed to find a market that could stomach that distortion of the facts. Yet change the setting to a plantation, and the arrangement becomes familiar. The brutality required to hold hundreds of people captive recedes from the story. Admirers come for the gowns, the table settings, the grandeur. Imagine presenting Hitler’s home that way: inviting visitors to admire his taste while relegating his victims to the background. We would recognize the obscenity. Why does plantation nostalgia still find a willing audience in the United States? Harriet Jacobs, who escaped slavery and became an abolitionist, described her enslaver’s relentless sexual pursuit in her 1861 autobiography, Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl . She testified to the terror of girlhood under another person’s claimed ownership. Under hereditary slavery, children inherited their mother’s enslaved status. An enslaver could rape a woman and claim the resulting child—even his own child—as property to work, inherit, or sell. The resemblance to modern captivity is therefore substantive: control over bodies, labor, movement, and children. Legal cover enlarged the perpetrator’s power and wealth. It did nothing to diminish the victim’s suffering. Black men lived under that power, too. In his 1845 autobiography, Frederick Douglass described the violence and relentless labor imposed by Edward Covey: “I was broken in body, soul, and spirit.” Bloodied after a beating, he appealed to Thomas Auld, the man who claimed ownership of him. Auld sent him back, insisting Covey “was a good man.” That judgment echoes through our commemorations. How much cruelty can a celebrated man’s reputation absorb before admiration becomes indefensible? At Choctaw Hall, one of the mansions toured in the film, guide and co-owner David Garner recounts defending Southern gentlemen against an accusation of arrogance: “We’re just proud of what we accomplished.” He credits seven generations of hard work for the surroundings visitors see. Whose work? Who built, cooked, cleaned, cultivated, and served? Whose captivity generated wealth? Where are their names when family pride takes center stage? The market for this selective memory was cultivated. After the Civil War, the Lost Cause movement promoted honorable Confederates, benevolent enslavers, and faithful enslaved people. Monuments, entertainment, and commemorations carried those images into national life. Confederate defeat opened the possibility for another future. During Reconstruction, Black Americans voted, held office, and built institutions. But white supremacists successfully fought those gains through terror and political resistance. As a result, federal protection receded. The former Confederate insurrectionists regained power, established Jim Crow and the racialized terror of lynching. That outcome was made possible by political choices in both the North and the South. Popular culture helped sustain it by romanticizing the defeated Confederacy while marginalizing the people it fought to keep enslaved. The blockbuster success of Gone with the Wind reveals how eagerly audiences embraced that mythology. Germany’s reckoning with Nazism was neither immediate nor complete. Nevertheless, its eventual official culture of remembrance made acknowledgment of Nazi crimes a national responsibility. Not so in the United States which has continued to uphold admiration for enslavers by distancing them from the implications of historical truth. That protection has contemporary consequences. When exploitation disappears from the history of wealth, inherited advantage looks entirely earned. When imposed disadvantage disappears, racial inequality looks like Black failure. Demands for repair then look like requests for undeserved favors. The injury also reaches beyond money. We learn whose pain requires an explanation before it deserves concern. I believe that habit connects the minimization of enslaved people’s suffering to the excuses made for mistreating Black people today, including by law enforcement. Authority receives the benefit of the doubt. The injured person must establish innocence, compliance, or respectability before receiving empathy. What did he do? Why didn’t he comply? Was the officer afraid? These questions can shift attention away from whether the person exercising power acted justly. Historical defenses can perform a similar function: explaining the powerful while distancing us from those they harmed. No single cause explains unequal policing. But the danger is documented. A 2019 study found that Black men and women faced greater lifetime risks of being killed by police than their white counterparts. Research also shows that Black pain can be discounted. A 2016 study found that white medical students and residents who endorsed false beliefs about biological racial differences made less accurate assessments of Black patients’ pain and less accurate treatment recommendations. That study concerns medicine. It nevertheless demonstrates that assumptions about Black bodies can affect whether suffering receives an adequate response. The stories we circulate can challenge those assumptions or leave them comfortable and undisturbed. This is a public safety issue. Black people are part of the public. Our safety cannot be expendable in the pursuit of someone else’s sense of order. After George Floyd’s murder, Coming to the Table explicitly connected police violence with the responsibility to transform institutions sustaining white supremacy. That connection explains why I belong at the table. Truth-telling must lead to changes in conduct, policy, and accountability. Healing cannot mean asking injured people to make peace with everyone else’s comfort. When someone says slavery happened too long ago to matter, I wonder why that reasoning applies to the debt but never to the inheritance. The mansions of Natchez embody wealth created through uncompensated labor. Why is it too late to compensate the descendants of the people whose labor created that wealth, but never too late for others to inherit its benefits? And why, in 2026, does an audience still want a version of that history organized around the enslaver’s pride? That appetite is the indictment. Every generation decides what it will celebrate and whose suffering it will accommodate. Today’s indifference is another injury, inflicted by people who can choose differently. The friend who sent me Natchez asked what I thought. I’m left asking: Whose history are we preserving when the enslaver’s reputation receives more protection than his victims ever did—and whose safety are we compromising when we carry that same hierarchy of concern into the present? Vancouver Is in the Middle of a Socialist Surge

Vancouver’s contrasts are visible on its streets. Luxury cars cruise past some of North America’s worst homeless encampments. Last Saturday, October 17, the city produced a political contrast to match: after four years of a center-right city hall, its municipal council has transformed into one of the most left-wing in North America. The breakthrough comes alongside strong socialist showings in municipal elections around the world, from Zohran Mamdani’s win in New York to Die Linke’s recent state election victory in Berlin. The Coalition of Progressive Electors (COPE), a democratic socialist party, elected all twelve candidates it ran: four to council, four to school board, and four to park board. COPE’s Sean Orr topped the council ballot in raw votes. Together with the four Green councilors, OneCity’s sole council seat, and Green Mayor Pete Fry , COPE’s four council wins give the progressive bloc nine of ten councilor seats and the mayor’s office — ten of city hall’s eleven elected seats. As right-leaning candidates made gains in other British Columbia municipalities, and as the social democratic New Democratic Party fought to retain power in the October 24 provincial election, COPE’s result testified to the party’s hard work and organizing efforts. It vindicated a party that was presumed dead after its near wipeout four years ago. The Breach reported in depth on COPE’s lengthy effort to rebuild its membership and organizing capacity, give members a meaningful role in shaping the party, and embrace its socialist identity rather than blur it into generic progressivism. That work positioned COPE to capitalize on the growing working-class discontent and the material realities of everyday life in Vancouver. A City in Crisis Vancouver’s housing crisis formed the backdrop to COPE’s victory. More than half of the city’s households rent. In 2021, approximately 39 percent of renter households spent at least 30 percent of their income on shelter, while roughly one-quarter were in core housing need: their homes were unaffordable, unsuitable, or inadequate, and they could not afford acceptable alternatives. The problem extends beyond the poorest residents. Even relatively well-paid workers can struggle to find housing near their jobs, while homeownership is increasingly out of reach for younger people. Economic insecurity has become a defining feature of everyday life in the city. ABC Vancouver came to power promising to address the problem by speeding up approvals and building more housing. Its administration can point to real increases in housing approvals and rental development. But more construction hasn’t made Vancouver affordable for many of the people who need homes. In September 2026, a report found that more than five thousand completed condominiums across Metro Vancouver remained unsold, with an estimated value of $4.45 billion. The chasm between new housing and what people can afford was stark. Mayor Ken Sim’s city council also shuttered the Vancouver Renters Office, which provided resources and information on tenant rights and how to navigate the legal system. Homelessness also increased in Vancouver under ABC. In contrast, COPE’s platform called for the city to acquire existing apartment buildings and convert them into permanently affordable public housing and co-ops. It also proposed a tax on properties worth more than $5 million to fund efforts to end homelessness, tripling the Empty Homes Tax, as well as stronger tenant protections. It put housing and care at the center of its strategy to respond to the city’s interlocking problems. That emphasis was particularly significant in the Downtown Eastside, where COPE has historically maintained a strong base. The neighborhood has been at the center of Vancouver’s overlapping problems of poverty, housing insecurity, and drug poisoning. Its residents have endured these conditions through successive governments that have promised to restore order without providing the necessary housing and care. The Campaign On Election Day, volunteers reportedly knocked on more than ten thousand doors. The campaign gave voters a positive vision of the city, championing socialized housing, public grocers, and expanded public services like Wi-Fi, libraries, and washrooms. Its “Fight for Vancouver” platform set out a high-level road map. The slogan, “Evict Ken Sim,” aimed at the outgoing right-wing mayor, became a meme, appearing in art, on buttons dotting the city, and on campaign merch like T-shirts. COPE’s younger candidates, including Solomon Yi-Kieran , Vancouver’s youngest-ever city councilor, and Chloe Leslie, brought passion and vitality to the party’s communications. Rather than imitating Zohran Mamdani’s style, they drew on Vancouver’s own culture, making high-energy, often playful social media videos. The new council has an opportunity to reverse some of ABC’s policies and pursue shared progressive priorities. But a progressive majority is not the same thing as a socialist government, and the parties will not necessarily agree on how to use their power. COPE’s platform went much further than the Greens’ on important questions of public ownership and the role of municipal government. Its proposals for public acquisition of housing and taxes on high-value properties went beyond simply making private development easier. The question is how much of this agenda COPE can persuade its coalition partners to adopt. Will the new council commit to a more ambitious public housing program? Will it restore tenant protections and supportive-housing investments? Will it redirect resources toward public services? Just as important is how COPE will maintain its independent political identity while participating in a governing coalition. Building a movement from outside city hall can be easier than sustaining it once its representatives become responsible for budgets, compromises, and the day-to-day operations of government. Moving Forward COPE’s members now have to decide how to use their electoral mandate. They can negotiate for improvements inside the council while continuing to organize tenants, workers, and neighborhoods to demand more from the new administration. The two approaches can complement and strengthen one another: COPE’s ability to mobilize people outside city hall may determine how much it can achieve inside. The challenge will be to keep the routines of municipal government from displacing the organizing that made the victory possible. Sean Orr, first elected in a 2025 by-election, has demonstrated that he can be a serious legislator as well as an organizer; COPE’s new batch of councilors can draw on his experience as they orient themselves to the workings of city hall. The coalition also faces a profound public safety challenge. Ken Sim’s council majority put two nonbinding plebiscites on the ballot: one asked whether the city should toughen laws against open-air hard drug use in public spaces; the other asked whether the city should push the province to create a secure medical facility with voluntary as well as one-hundred-plus involuntary beds. Voters backed both questions by roughly 68 percent. COPE and the Greens opposed putting the question to a vote, but the results largely refute any claim that the new progressive majority has a clear mandate to counter these proposals. It is very likely that some of the same people who voted for COPE voted yes to the plebiscites. Vancouver has been at the center of Canada’s overdose crisis, and the incoming council will be responsible for addressing conflicting expectations about the best way to respond to visible street-level poverty, public drug use, and complex mental health and addiction challenges. COPE will have to demonstrate that an approach rooted in housing, harm reduction, and public services can address residents’ concerns without abandoning its commitments to the people most affected by the crisis. The election does not resolve these questions. It makes them more urgent. One part of COPE’s rebuild was reconnecting members with the party’s history. Organizers made a slideshow of its victories since its founding in the 1960s, giving members a sense of that history and purpose. From declaring Vancouver a nuclear-weapon-free zone to Sean Orr establishing an arts and culture land trust, COPE exists in a long line of struggles for better housing, care, and socialism in the city. On October 17, the members gathered in a packed auditorium had good reason to pop champagne bottles, sing, and celebrate. They had done something many thought impossible. She worries about falling behind, being evicted and losing her entire investment if she’s unable to move her home in time. Both Muz and Pedrotte name housing affordability as a top political issue for them — and the common ground between the two neighbors may be something of a national microcosm, according to Steven Kull, director of the University of Maryland’s Program for Public Consultation, which surveys the public on policy issues where there appears to be substantial common ground, despite gridlock in government. In July, Kull’s team published results from polling on housing affordability administered across 11 electorally competitive states and 28 competitive congressional districts, including the one encompassing Saginaw. The survey found strong support for key elements of the new federal housing law — including limiting corporate home-buying and stimulating private investment through tax credits — but also support for ​ “ policies that go substantially further,” Kull notes. For example, 86 % of Democrats and 64 % of Republicans said that they would back billions in direct investment to build housing affordable for low- and very low-income households. The takeaway, Kull says, is that dissatisfaction with the cost of living is driving ​ “ a remarkable level of support … for a much more activist government role.” Will an affordability agenda deliver on this demand for bolder action on housing? It depends on whose agenda. Democrats’ near unanimous embrace of the term followed the June 2025 primary victory of then mayoral candidate Zohran Mamdani, who ran on freezing rents for more than 2 million rent-stabilized tenants. A year later, you’d be hard-pressed to find a politician or caucus that hasn’t released an affordability agenda, which might refer alternately to tax breaks, permitting reform, or sweeping new social programs funded by taxes on billionaires. Housing is central to the push and pull over the politics of affordability, as a new crop of left-wing congressional candidates run on freezing rents and protecting renters, building off the momentum of years of resurgent tenant organizing. From Pedrotte’s vantage point, it makes little sense to talk about affordability as an abstraction. ​ “ I think the conversation about affordable housing and the conversation about corporate greed have to go hand-inhand,” she says. ​ “ Because truly that is who is pricing us out of our homes.” In a July 2024 speech before the NAACP’s annual convention, then President Joe Biden struck an unexpectedly populist note on housing. Speaking in Las Vegas, where rents had increased more than twice as fast as wages over the preceding four-year period, Biden pitched a new plan to withhold federal tax write-offs from large landlords that raise rents by more than 5 % annually — a form of national rent stabilization, more or less, that drew cheers from progressives and backlash from the real estate industry. The last-ditch July 2024 rent cap announcement was part of a bid to shore up support from progressive standard-bearers for Biden’s re-election campaign as questions about his mental fitness mounted. (Squinting at the teleprompter in Las Vegas, Biden actually garbled the plan’s details and told the crowd that corporate landlords wouldn’t be able to raise rents by more than $ 55 a month.) Tenant organizers saw a presidential candidate talking about rent caps as a welcome rhetorical opening. But the actual policy intervention was ​ “ too little and way too late” to register with voters, says Tara Raghuveer, director of the Tenant Union Federation, a national organization that spent the waning months of the Biden administration pushing for stronger federal action to protect tenants, including by attaching rent caps to federally backed apartment loans. But this lifeline was blunted by broader dynamics in the rental market, as corporate landlords routinely flouted state and federal eviction moratoria while helping drive double-digit rent increases through alleged price-fixing using the real estate software RealPage, now the subject of multiple antitrust lawsuits. In 2022 , the share of renters considered cost-burdened rose to nearly 50 %, with a record high of 22 . 4 million households spending more than 30 % of their income on rent. Inaction on landlord profiteering exemplifies the grave political miscalculation that helped pave the way for President Donald Trump’s return to the White House, according to Raghuveer. While the Biden administration focused on selling voters on the idea that ​ “ Bidenomics is working, the stock market is great, jobs numbers are up,” that upbeat message ignored ​ “ the real pain that everyday people were feeling every month when they paid their rent,” she says. Soon after the July 2024 rent cap announcement, of course, Biden would drop out and be replaced by then Vice President Kamala Harris as the party’s new de facto nominee. At her first major campaign rally in Atlanta, Harris delivered a fiery speech pledging to take on corporate landlords and ​ “ cap unfair rent increases,” stoking speculation that she would stay the course charted by Biden’s eleventh-hour populism. But after her nomination became official, Harris pivoted toward middle-of-the-road economic messaging and embarked on an ill-fated quest to reach Republican voters through conservative campaign surrogates like former U.S. Rep. Liz Cheney​.An ad in which Harris looked straight at the camera while pledging to ​ “ crack down on landlords who are charging too much” performed best at persuading voters, according to message testing by the firm Blue Rose Research, but rarely aired. Harris did release and campaign on a plan to lower housing costs by building 3 million units nationwide. The plan focused primarily on supply-side solutions like tax incentives for homebuilding and an expansion of the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC), which has subsidized millions of apartments classified as ​ “ affordable” nationwide. But there’s an open secret about the nation’s go-to affordable housing program: It’s rarely actually affordable. Because allowable rents are pegged to area median incomes, not tenants’ actual incomes, more than 40 % of LIHTC residents are cost-burdened, according to program data. From the program’s early days, analysts warned that tax credits were a fundamentally inefficient way to subsidize housing. A 1992 analysis by the Congressional Budget Office concluded that the program was ​ “ more suited to the needs of investors than poor renters.” Supply-side housing solutions are also a difficult sell politically. ​ “ Telling people about hundreds of thousands of units that are going to be built years from now, that’s not good politics,” Raghuveer says. ​ “ That doesn’t resonate with people who can’t pay their rent today.” That doesn’t mean that those urging stronger renter protections shun supply-side measures. A burgeoning social housing movement seeks to resolve what its proponents see as a key contradiction in the debate over housing supply: Rents are set, in part, to satisfy the returns expected by for-profit housing financiers, so when rent growth begins to slow, financing for new housing may start to dry up. Expanding options for public financing, development and ownership of housing could help fulfill the promise of building our way to greater affordability. Alongside boosting supply, tenant organizers — and a growing number of economists — are continuing to make the case that the federal government must play a role in protecting tenants from landlord price gouging. Neoclassical economists have been nearly unanimous in their rejection of rent control. But the signatories also argued that the debate over rent regulation is reaching the same inflection point as the one surrounding the minimum wage in the 1990 s, when a series of empirical studies concluded that wage hikes were not leading to job losses, as both mainstream economists and business lobbies had predicted they would. In July, Mark Paul, an associate professor at Rutgers University and one of the letter’s signatories, proposed a framework for national rent control that would limit annual increases to 3 % or three-quarters of the local Consumer Price Index, whichever is lower — and apply those limits to all rental housing nationwide, including single-family homes and manufactured housing. Paul also proposed establishing regional boards to enforce the policy and assess whether additional increases are needed to maintain housing quality. The standard economic objection to capping rents is that it distorts markets, ultimately harming renters by discouraging new construction. Anisha Steephen, a former Biden Treasury Department adviser and current fellow at the Roosevelt Institute, contends that something closer to the inverse is true: Housing markets are already distorted by the kind of rampant speculation that helped drive runaway rent increases during the pandemic. Rent regulation doesn’t just protect tenants; it can also act as a form of financial regulation important for broader economic stability. That’s because rents aren’t set according to what prospective tenants can afford; they’re determined by the cash flow landlords need to pay off their often-risky mortgage loans. Similar to ability-to-repay rules for home mortgage lending enacted in the wake of the 2008 financial crisis, ​ “ rent regulation exposes where lending has overpromised on the ability of landlords to increase rents in perpetuity,” Steephen says. Absent financial regulation, Steephen says the apartment market could be at risk of the same kind of calamity that occurred in owner-occupied housing after 2008 . Take the 2023 collapse of Signature Bank, the fourth-largest bank failure in U.S. history. The bank had become an aggressive lender for some of New York City’s most notorious landlords, with many of the loans ​ “ predicated on plans for displacing rent-regulated tenants,” according to then New York City Comptroller Brad Lander, who reviewed the bank’s portfolio after it collapsed and the city intervened. Beyond staving off disaster, rent regulation can also open up opportunities for ownership by the public sector, cooperatives or nonprofits otherwise unable to compete in an environment of aggressive speculation. When you have a limit on speculation, Steephen says, ​ “ you allow a broader universe of actors that may meet more mission-aligned goals to participate in the housing market.” In early August, a billboard unveiled in New York City’s Times Square pronounced Mayor Zohran Mamdani the ​ “ Florida Economic Developer of the Year.” The ad winked at a favorite narrative of the business interests that paid for it: that Mamdani’s economic agenda — first and foremost, a recently approved two-year rent freeze — would send taxpayers running toward the more predictable politics of the Sunshine State. But just a week later, supporters waving ​ “ Freeze the Rent” signs broke into cheers as Angie Nixon, a member of Florida’s House of Representatives, notched a stunning victory in the state’s primary to become its Democratic nominee for U.S. Senate. Outspent 13 to one — a jaw-dropping margin even in a record-shatteringly expensive election cycle — Nixon ran a campaign laser-focused on affordability, calling for a national rent freeze, Medicare for All and universal childcare. That message found fertile ground in the state’s lowest-income and least college-educated counties, according to a Washington Post analysis, defying the trends that have often beleaguered progressive candidates. Nixon’s full-throated endorsements of national rent stabilization may have been an especially easy sell in a state whose renters are the most cost-burdened in the nation, thanks to a combination of rising rents and flat wages. She ​ “ speaks about affordability in a real way, the way that our members have been asking about for such a long time,” says Cynthia Laurent, a housing justice campaigner with the nonprofit Florida Rising. Ron DeSantis signed a law preempting local passage of rent stabilization and most other tenant protections. Florida Rising’s 501 (c)( 4 ) arm has endorsed Nixon. Nixon is now among a handful of congressional candidates testing the strength of their affordability message against Republican opponents in November, after making federal rent caps and tenant protections key tenets of their primary campaigns. In Tennessee, after Republicans redrew electoral maps to carve up the state’s last blue district covering Memphis, State Rep. Justin Pearson is pitching the white, rural voters he must now appeal to on his vision for affordability — including a data center moratorium, federal minimum wage increase, rent caps and massive federal investment in affordable housing. And in Michigan, tenant organizer Will Lawrence is running in a district where Trump won by just one point in 2024 , making it a top pickup prospect for Democrats. A co-founder of the youth climate group Sunrise Movement, Lawrence also spent the last several years helping to build tenant unions and win new renter protections through the statewide coalition Michigan Rent Is Too Damn High. Under the state’s Democratic trifecta from 2023 to 2024 , the coalition hoped to pass a suite of new housing affordability measures, including removal of the state’s ban on rent control and a $ 4 billion allocation for social housing as part of a state push to build tens of thousands of new homes. While the coalition did win some new tenant protections and funding for affordable housing, Lawrence says it was a ​ “ drop in the bucket” compared to the scope of the crisis. If elected to Congress, Lawrence says he would push for a federal tenant bill of rights and ​ “ a new generation of public housing for the 21 st century,” modeled after the success of publicly financed, mixed-income developments in Montgomery County, Md. Lawrence’s approach to running on affordability is to get specific. ​ “ No voter says, ​ ‘ I have an affordability problem,’” he notes. ​ “ They say, ​ ‘ the rent is too damn high.’ Or ​ ‘ this energy bill is outlandish.’ Or ​ ‘ I just paid $ 17 for three chicken breasts at the grocery store.’” That framing echoes the takeaways of an August poll that found that while 70 % of 2026 Democratic candidates are deploying some form of populist rhetoric, certain messages work better than others. In a survey of more than 6 , 000 adults, conducted by the Center for Working-Class Politics and the Rural Urban Bridge Initiative, kitchen-table populist messages that led with concrete costs, not abstractions about a fair economy, were far more persuasive. A Sweeping Reelection Victory for Shawn Fain and His Slate at UAW

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: In These Times ／ 著者: 不明 ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-23T15:45:56+00:00

United Auto Workers President Shawn Fain is poised to win a second four-year term, with an overwhelming lead in member balloting for the union’s top officers. The election was a referendum on the new fighting direction Fain has taken the union by leading 2023 ’s Stand-Up Strike against the Big 3 automakers, other bold contract fights and new organizing campaigns. It marks the second time members have voted for their top officers in the union’s 91 years. UAW members voted to switch to a one-member-one-vote system in December 2021 . Fain took more than two-thirds of the vote, against five opponents. His nearest competitor, incumbent vice president Rich Boyer, had around 13 %. Fain is running as part of the United UAW slate. His slate-mate for secretary-treasurer, Brandon Campbell, has also garnered two-thirds, against two rivals including incumbent Margaret Mock. The slate’s candidates for the three vice president spots appeared on track to be elected with 19 - 25 % each in a field of 11 candidates. (VP candidates need 16 . 7 % to win.) The slate has also won all the regional director races counted thus far that it contested. If the slate sweeps, as appears likely, it will hold all the seats on the union’s International Executive Board except for one regional director. Fain has won a majority of the vote in most locals, from assembly plants to legal services, and a plurality in almost all the rest. Boyer, who heads the union’s Stellantis division, has won just two of the union’s dozens of big locals: Local 140 , Warren Truck, his home local, and Local 1700 at Sterling Heights Assembly Plant, both near Detroit. Turnout was low and similar to the 2022 election, with roughly 10 % of the union’s 400 , 000 active members and 600 , 000 retirees returning their mail ballots. (Retirees also vote on top officers in the UAW.) While some expected Fain’s strongest support to come from the higher education and legal aid locals on the East Coast, turnout there was down by more than half from 2022 , with fewer than 2 , 000 ballots counted. Turnout was up in Local 4811 at the University of California, which went 75 % for Fain, but still only 2 , 500 of 60 , 000 members there voted. As in the last election, it was members in the big manufacturing locals that were key to Fain’s victory. Perennially online candidate Brian Keller had 7 % of the presidential vote. As the ballots were counted he took to Facebook Live to swear at members for 30 minutes and call them ​ “ morons” for not voting for him. Motivators In phone interviews with a dozen members, auto workers cited pensions and post-retirement health care as their key motivators for backing Fain. They also raised concerns about tackling automation and job security. But the biggest issue was the better contracts the UAW has won since Fain took office. Infighting between Fain and his opponents on the outgoing International Executive Board — Rich Boyer, Margaret Mock and Mike Booth — came up, but people drew widely different conclusions, with many repeating the phrase Fain ​ “ isn’t perfect.” Jeep worker Megan Fagan in Toledo said some members have checked out due to the internal drama, reinforcing the cynical view that not much has changed. For most, though, disagreements at headquarters were not of interest or on their radar. They looked at their pay stubs and voted accordingly. Boyer, Mock, and Booth came together to form the Integrity Slate but failed to offer a counter to Fain’s focus on fighting the companies and launching new organizing drives. Boyer’s main pitches in his campaign statement in the UAW magazine were to lower union dues and reduce the staff at union headquarters. “ Fain and his slate are the only ones that have a real program for the future of the union,” said historian Nelson Lichtenstein, author of Walter Reuther: The Most Dangerous Man in Detroit . ​ “ You’d think the other people would have come up with something aside from they want to reduce dues, which means basically cripple the new organizing.” “ The members chose Fain, despite the Monitor’s best efforts to create turbulence, because we’re ready to fight for retirement health care and protection from A.I. layoffs in 2028 ,” said Gary Chynoweth, vice chair of the reform network UAW Member Action , in a statement. ​ “ Our work now is to keep helping members step up as leaders in a union that’s ready to fight.” Keep on track UAW Local 551 member Candyla Brown is one of 5 , 000 workers at Ford’s assembly plant in Chicago. She said she backed the United UAW slate to keep the union on track in 2028 , to win pensions, post-retirement health care, and pay bumps that keep up with inflation. To win these demands, ​ “ we need strong leadership,” she said. ​ “ We had a good experience with the 2023 strike, and I’ve been watching the new organizing efforts,” she said. ​ “ I think the UAW has come a long way since Fain got into office.” She pointed to her own local’s successful organizing of nurses at her plant and a narrowly lost election at Julian Electric, a parts plant that supplies Ford, as examples of how the union is devoting resources to organize, even if some campaigns have come up short. She said organizing in the South is another bright spot. Volkswagen workers in Tennessee voted to join the UAW in 2024 and won a first contract earlier this year, becoming the first unionized foreign-owned auto assembly plant in the South. Jessica Acosta of Ford Local 249 outside of Kansas City said she voted for Fain and his slate because she’s seen transparency at the top of the union and wants to see the same in her local, where there was a contested election recently. ​ “ I want to know what’s going on,” she said. ​ “ I don’t want to be left in the dark.” Her top priority is winning pensions, though she recognizes that’s a tough demand to win. At Daimler Truck in North Carolina, Jon Greene, president of Local 3520 , said workers are building about 80 trucks a day, after winning a new record contract in 2024 with 25 percent wage bumps and job security. Their contract expires in March 2028 . In the next one, Local 3520 wants to win more product for the plant to ensure job security, especially after Daimler shut down a Freightliner truck plant in Oregon. But the company is slated to open another plant on the East Coast in 2029 . ​ “ So it’s important that we put the right people in place, or keep them in place, who know what they’re doing to get us to 2028 ,” he said. He said Daimler has staffed up its labor relations personnel in preparation for another showdown, and corporate HR has been closely following the election. ​ “ We tripped them up with the last contract,” said Greene. ​ “ It wasn’t any more of that friendly bargaining that had been happening over the years with International, so we caught them off guard.” Temp workers converted Kirk Hoddinott started as a temp line worker in 2020 at Toledo Jeep in Ohio, building Jeep Wranglers and Gladiators. Toledo Jeep was one of the plants that launched the Stand-Up Strike, which forced Stellantis to convert all temporary workers to permanent roles after nine months on the job. Hoddinott became one of 1 , 957 temps at Stellantis who were converted within three months of ratification. In spite of the 2023 contract’s big gains, he said, the lingering effects of past corruption scandals contribute to cynicism among Local 12 members who blame reps for poor communication in grievance handling. ​ “ People still think that the union is in bed with the company because they feel that they’re not being represented as strongly as they want to be,” said Hoddinott. “ I would like to see more communication through our union from top to bottom, and back from bottom to top,” he said, ​ “ because I think that the company is talking to each other, and they’re making a game plan. I think we should be doing the same thing.” Nonetheless, Hoddinott said he believes Fain has made ​ “ good progress” and ​ “ I want to see another term and see how much more progress that he can really make.” Hoddinott’s top priorities are post-retirement health insurance and job security around automation. ​ “ Every person that works there for more than 10 years is going to have wear and tear in their body that you’re going to have permanent health problems from,” he said. Before the massive concessions of 2007 , Big 3 retirees had company-paid health insurance until they were eligible for Medicare. Those hired since then no longer do. He worries when he reads news stories about humanoid robots at BMW plants. At the Jeep complex, ​ “ they have a robot that can go up and down the racks and look at inventory to try to find parts,” he said, ​ “ and they are telling me that so far it hasn’t taken any jobs.” Renewed mandate With a renewed mandate, the pressure is on Fain’s team to organize members to step up when contracts expire at the Big 3 automakers on May 1 , 2028 . In 2023 , the companies were caught off guard, accustomed to a sedate strike, if any, and modest union demands. When the union launched a spreading strike against all three companies at once, with more plants walking out week by week, company bargainers were blindsided. They gave up more in that one contract than they had in the previous four contracts combined. They will likely be better prepared for May Day 2028 . Unions may face another obstacle in 2028 — in an election year, Democratic politicians traditionally bring heavy pressure not to rock the boat, fearing blame from voters. In 2023 , though, a poll found 75 % of Americans supported the UAW’s strike. Some expensive union demands were left on the table last time: pensions and retiree health insurance for workers hired after the big concessions of 2007 - 2009 , and protections against mandatory overtime and automation. Flush with victory in fall 2023 , Fain urged other unions to line up their contracts with the Big 3 ’s, for a united assault on corporate coffers. Dominick Moss, a member of Local 1853 at General Motors’ Spring Hill, Tennessee, assembly plant, said he backed United UAW because it built a broad coalition and set a goal of preparing for a potential May Day 2028 general strike. “ I think that we have a solid ground laid in terms of how we’ve been connecting with other unions,” Moss said. ​ “ If enough of us go on strike, we could get more attention on universal health care. I would like to see Medicare for All and the PRO Act passed. I would like to see consequences for corporations doing rampant automation.” (The PRO Act would make it easier for workers to organize and secure contracts.) Moss said his plant’s chassis department in general assembly lost 38 jobs as a direct result of automation over the last couple of years. ​ “ Cameras replaced quality gates,” he said. ​ “ All of the seat loading is done by robots now, and my team was previously loading seats.” The Spring Hill assembly plant builds electric vehicles like the Cadillac Lyriq, and they aren’t selling as well after Trump and congressional Republicans eliminated a $ 7 , 500 tax credit. ​ “ When the price of those electric cars went up about $ 8 , 000 overnight, we started selling less of them,” he said. Ditching the tax credit also had an impact at the Chattanooga Volkswagen plant, where Zach Costello, who backed Fain, said ​ “ there have been massive cutbacks.” Volkswagen stopped producing the company’s ID. 4 SUV in April, instead ramping up production of the gas-powered Atlas SUV. Government hands off UAW The election results are a sharp rebuke to the government-appointed monitor , Neil Barofsky, whose original mandate was to oversee clean-up of the endemic corruption that sent 13 union officials to jail. Barofsky developed a strong antagonism to Fain when the UAW Executive Board passed a resolution calling for a ceasefire in Gaza in late 2023 , which the monitor said could be seen as ​ “ antisemitic.” Fain resisted Barofsky’s attempts to reverse that stance. Ever since, Barofsky has issued a series of reports criticizing Fain and siding with his opponents on the Board. Some UAW members who followed the drama drew cynical conclusions, but apparently most voters found Fain’s actions spoke louder than the monitor’s words. Barofsky’s mandate is set to end in early 2027 , but the consent decree keeps open the possibility of extending the monitorship. If Barofsky’s previous behavior is any indication, he may push to stay in place longer. Fain’s opponents made much of the monitor’s reports, which accused Fain of misdeeds including retaliating against Boyer and Mock, after he relieved them of some of their major responsibilities. Opponents spent most of their energy attacking him personally and detailing their own grievances, rather than talking about the direction of the union. Their campaigns did not have much reach. Fain, on the other hand, reminded members of the union’s achievements and tried to keep the disagreements from looking personal. Despite encouraging leadership at the top, the UAW has a long way to go to become a member-driven union that consistently takes on the boss. While Fain has charted a new direction, much of the union bears the scars of decades of concessions, or is still stuck in the old, company-friendly ways of operating. That includes seeing the union structure as a career ladder, a way to get off the factory floor. Years of one-party rule means that the bench of members able and willing to push the union in a new direction is still shallow. That has the potential to change in locals where the International has brought big resources for contract campaigns and strikes. The UAW Member Action network is slowly working, through education and trainings, to build up the bench. After this election, it looks like the UAW will continue to be back in the fight. From the Green Line to the Yellow Line: A Story of Colonization in Palestine

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: In These Times ／ 著者: 不明 ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-23T15:45:56+00:00

In October 2025 , a ceasefire agreement between Israel and the Palestinian factions was supposed to bring calm to a Gaza devastated by genocide. Instead, Israel has continued to violate the ceasefire, murdering more than a thousand Palestinians since the agreement began and usurping more land via the ​ “ yellow line” — an arbitrary line splitting the Gaza Strip into eastern and western halves. A year since the ceasefire, Israel has continued to push the yellow line inwards and now controls nearly 70 percent of the Gaza Strip and continues to kill Palestinians on a daily basis simply for being near the arbitrary line. Ahmad, a father of two who lives near the yellow line in Al-Tuffah in Gaza City, talked about how difficult life is for Palestinians living near the yellow line. “ It’s a very painful feeling to be living in your own home, with only moments separating you from death,” he said. ​ “ We’re constantly hit by gunfire, missile shrapnel, and explosions. It’s a very difficult feeling when you’re in your home and never feel safe for even a minute.” According to the ceasefire agreement, the yellow line was intended to only demarcate the first stage of Israeli troop withdrawal, but it’s become the de facto border of Gaza and now Israel has constructed gates, sandy embankments and military posts along the yellow line suggesting preparations for a more permanent militarized frontier. For Palestinians, the yellow line is only the most recent chapter in colonial violence. Growing up in Gaza, the ​ “ green line” circumscribed our lives and separated Palestinians from one another and from the world. In 1949 , Israel agreed on new borders for Palestine with Egypt, Jordan, Syria, and Lebanon. These borders, like the state of Israel itself, were created just a year prior on the ruins of 530 Palestinian towns and villages. The line, drawn with the help of U.S. diplomat and mediator Ralph Bunche , defined Israel’s new borders and became known as the green line for one simple reason: Bunche used a green marker to draw it. The green line also marked the boundary of the West Bank. Following Israel’s occupation of the Gaza Strip and the West Bank in 1967 , the entire territory came under Israeli control. As time passed, Israel pushed and blurred the boundaries of the green line with bypass roads and settlements built on top of and displacing Palestinian towns and communities. Over the years, Israel’s green line swallowed approximately 200 square kilometers of Gaza, more than a third of Gaza’s territory under the 1949 agreement. In reality, the green line had no color. My mother once traveled from Gaza to the West Bank, and my uncle asked her to meet him at the green line on the West Bank side. She kept searching the area for something green but found nothing. After wandering around for nearly an hour, she finally found my uncle and learned that the green line did not actually have a color at all. The ​ “ line” was simply a fiction created by Israeli colonizers, made real by brutal violence and internalized by colonized people as an act of survival. A green line drawn in marker on a map has profoundly shaped the lives of Palestinians. In 2018 , hundreds of thousands of Palestinians in Gaza marched every week to that arbitrary line demarcating the Israeli border, calling on the world to end Israel’s blockade of Gaza and recognize their rights as refugees in mass marches called the Great March of Return. These nonviolent protests were organized and attended by a cross section of Palestinian civil society. Israel responded with lethal force, killing more than 200 marchers and injuring more than 20 , 000 for getting near that line. For the last three years, Palestinians in Gaza have been subjected to a new and terrifying onslaught of lines, zones, and borders that became a core feature in Israel’s genocide. Israel divided the Gaza Strip into blocs, assigning each area a number to make it easier for the Israeli military to issue evacuation orders. Israel dropped millions of leaflets on Gaza marking blocs for immediate evacuation and demolitions, sending waves of displacement and fear among Palestinians. The blocs were marked with red; Palestinians were given only a few hours to leave. At the same time, Israel split Gaza into two main sections, the north and the south, ordering Palestinians to leave the northern part of the Strip. This division was enforced through a military corridor stretching from the Mediterranean coast in western Gaza to its eastern border, passing through the site of the former Israeli settlement of Netzarim, which existed until Israel’s 2005 disengagement. The corridor consequently became known as the Netzarim Corridor. My colleagues and family members in Gaza were forcibly displaced dozens of times, traveling dangerous roads with thousands of others with no guarantee their destination would be safer than the neighborhoods being pummeled by airstrikes behind them. In 2025 , the Israeli military deployed yellow concrete blocks to mark the yellow line. These blocks have become a nightmare for people in Gaza because they keep moving. In many cases, Palestinians living near the yellow line have woken up to find that soldiers have moved the blocks further into their neighborhoods, placing their homes inside the newly expanded zone. Overnight, their homes were now in an area under direct Israeli military control. And the moving line has continued to consume more and more of Gaza’s fertile agricultural land, contributing to the starvation of Palestinians. Arwa, an educator from Gaza who lives near the yellow line in Deir Al-Balah, said the constantly moving nature of the line has made it nearly impossible for Palestinians to rebuild the essential aspects of their daily lives. “ We’d be outside trying to enjoy fresh air in the middle of the blazing summer and suddenly we’d hear tanks shooting out of the blue. Shortly, quadcopters would follow and start shooting,” she said, ​ “ Other than the safety of my friends and colleagues, the displacement of so many of our school teachers is making me worried about the school shutting down, one of the last few spaces still providing education for around 600 elementary students and around 800 preparatory students.” The yellow line is Gaza’s newest nightmare. It echoes the violent imposition of the green line in 1949 , yet it’s more expansive, more fluid, and more frightening. Unlike the green line, which marked an armistice boundary, the yellow line continues to move, steadily shrinking the cage that Israel has created for Palestinians in Gaza. Rather than allowing new militarized borders to further confine Palestinians, the world must draw a line against continued violations of international law and the ongoing colonization of Palestinian land. The yellow line should be abolished. It has no place in a just and peaceful future, for Palestinians or for anyone else.

Wall Street Is Getting Very Nervous About AI

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Branko Marcetic ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-23T14:15:08+00:00

For an industry that we’re told is central to our collective future, the artificial intelligence (AI) industry increasingly seems to be built on a combination of artificially inflated hopes and bad accounting. The industry’s critics have been raising concerns about its finances for a while: the fact that most of its flagship firms are grossly unprofitable , that they’ve had to jack up their prices to try break even (leading their business customers to pump the brakes on using their products), or that many of the eye-popping investment announcements that are meant to show demand for AI is strong have actually been circular financing — meaning different AI firms cycling money to and from each other. But none of it has seemed to slow the industry’s boom or the relentless hype machine behind it. Until now, anyway. It used to be that you mostly heard concerns about the industry’s underlying economics in newspapers, blogs, social media posts, and podcasts, and that those concerns went completely unreflected in the markets. But these concerns seem now to have finally bled over into investors’ thinking. Money Walks The price of bonds issued for the borrowing that undergirds the build-out of data centers has sharply dropped in recent months, as has the bond price for “neocloud companies,” middleman firms that provide the computing power for AI firms who may not have their own data centers. The largest of these neoclouds, CoreWeave, recently saw its bonds enter distressed territory. What does that mean? In short, that investors are suddenly abandoning the AI-related debt they’d happily financed until just a few months ago when it seemed like a great investment, because they’re starting to suspect these firms are not actually going to end up being able to pay it off. Nearly half of the bonds raised this year to finance data center projects by some of the biggest tech firms are now rated “junk.” At the same time, the market for credit default swaps (CDSs) for AI is booming , with the value traded of CDSs linked to major AI firms having more than quadrupled between July and September. Made famous by their role in the 2008 financial crisis, CDSs are basically insurance against future debt defaults, another sign of investor doubt that AI industry firms are actually good for the money they’ve borrowed to fund their build-out. To put it plainly: the people who move money around for a living are starting to have serious doubts about how sustainable the AI boom actually is, and we know they are not because they’re just saying so, but because they’re making financial decisions as if they are. What’s changed to bring this shift about? A number of things, including the fact that, some voices in the financial world now are saying so. The AI industry’s current unprofitability was acceptable as long as it seemed plausible that, at some unspecified future date, this technology would actually start making money. But a spate of recent news and analysis has underscored the fantastical math behind AI firms’ accounting. According to one highly cited Brookings Institution paper, the colossal amounts of money already being plowed into the AI build-out mean that US consumers and businessses will need to spend enough on the technology by 2032 to generate $3.5 trillion worth of revenue, or just shy of 9 percent of the country’s GDP, to make good on it. There is quite literally no precedent for this in American history — the railroad boom involved capital spending more than an entire percentage point of US GDP smaller than the AI build-out. According to the Wall Street Journal , that would mean the nation spending around the same amount every year on AI as it currently does for food, or seven times the amount it spends on the internet, phones, and other communications combined. Needless to say, this looks unlikely. Last month, the chief economist of asset management firm Apollo pointed out the wide gulf between the huge future operating cash flow that analysts are expecting for the tech sector specifically, and the much smaller future operating cash flow analysts are expecting for companies in other sectors. In other words, the businesses that are in theory meant to be the tech world’s customers are not even seriously expected to have the money on hand that tech firms are boasting they will extract from them. Someone’s math here is off, and it’s reasonable to assume that it’s the AI firms’. Years of overoptimistic revenue projections and consistent money-losing from the sector were dramatically illustrated earlier this month when one prominent firm, Character.ai, filed for bankruptcy. In its filing, the company revealed that it had been losing around $8–9 million per month over the course of this year — which it said was an improvement from previous years — and that its subscription revenue had only ever been a fraction of the amount it was spending on the computing needed to run its AI. Risks Abound These fears were heightened by some bad news around several high-profile AI firm initial public offerings (IPOs), when a privately held company starts selling its shares to the general public for the first time. First, OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, delayed its IPO over internal worries that it might end up a flop, because retail investors — basically, you and I if we got into the stock market — may not actually end up as eager to buy its shares as they hoped. Among the reasons why were general market uncertainty caused by the Iran war, growing skepticism toward AI, a spate of damaging headlines about the technology’s safety issues, and a stagnated user base for ChatGPT. The company has now had to pare back its valuation for its latest round of funding due to a palpable drop in enthusiasm from investors. Meanwhile, Claude’s maker Anthropic has made headlines for a prospectus — the document put out by a firm going public that explains the business and lays out its financial reality for possible investors — that has seemed to prove true every worry just laid out about AI’s business model. The latest version of the document, obtained by the Wall Street Journal this week, showed the firm posted a more than $80 billion loss this year, while its spending on computing and infrastructure is exponentially ballooning, its demand for computing capacity outstrips what is available, and its list of possible risk factors are longer than the company description. One of those risk factors has become a major general concern for AI investors: the growing public hatred of data centers and the push to restrict or even outright stop their continuing construction. Popular distaste for data centers cuts across party and ideological lines and has become a central issue in the upcoming midterm elections, making it more likely a government clampdown on them intensifies. Just in the first half of this year, environmental activists and local residents have blocked more than a hundred data centers worth roughly $198 billion in capital. That included a major project of Oracle’s in New Mexico that pushed the company into declaring force majeure — meaning, tear up a contract because of an unforeseen act of God — after having already spent millions . This is yet another, and an especially big, reason for investors in AI to worry they’re not going to get their money back. For AI companies to have any hope of meeting their already unrealistic revenue goals, data centers have to be built somewhere; but voters across the country are vehemently opposed to having them built in their neighborhoods, because they find them noisy, intrusive, harmful to the environment, and have a habit of driving up their electricity bills. So they’re organizing to have them restricted or outright banned, which is what they have done in more than five hundred counties and municipalities. Something will have to give here. That’s not to mention investor fears that they could be left holding the bag on data center debt. The Brookings study mentioned earlier estimates the AI data center build-out will cost a total of $10.3 trillion by 2032; market research firm Gartner estimates that AI data center owners will have to generate a staggering $8.5 trillion in revenue through 2030 to cover the build-out while making a decent return. These are enormous sums behind something that is being financed largely by massive and rapidly growing levels of borrowing. On top of this is the fact that the chips AI firms rely on, and which they spend billions of dollars on, are becoming obsolete quicker and quicker — much more rapidly than the lifespan many of these companies initially budgeted for. It’s another case of wishful accounting by the industry, and another reason to worry for investors, since borrowing by AI firms is often secured on the back of chips that will lose their value before the lifetime of the loan is up. So it’s no wonder that investors are now souring on AI and hedging their bets in anticipation of a bubble bursting. That could come sooner than we think: Oracle, considered a “bellwether” for the wider AI credit market, has seen its bonds downgraded to just above junk status. One economist believes that if its bonds ever do hit that nadir, it would be a “sign of contagion” for the sector more widely. Beyond AI The stakes here are high. By some estimates , AI is now a leading pillar of the economy, making a bigger contribution to GDP growth than the dot-com bubble did in 2000 before it burst, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of St Louis. So any AI slowdown would likely affect the wider economy, particularly the construction industry, where it’s driving a blue-collar job boom . But maybe more important are the ripple effects it could have on the financial system. The Bank of England and the International Monetary Fund have both singled out the shoddy economics behind the AI boom as a potential threat to global financial stability. Jason Thomas, the head of investment firm Carlyle Group’s global research and investment strategy, has warned repeatedly that he sees similarities between AI project financing and the subprime mortgage crisis. Lenders right now are not giving out money based on the economics of a project itself, he said, but on the ability of the firm sponsoring the project to swallow the liabilities if need be — similar to what they were doing in the years leading up to the 2008 crash. He warned that, given this, investors have far too much exposure to the industry than they should to breathe easy. What’s notable about this is that Thomas isn’t just an analyst, but has skin in the game: the firm he works for, Carlyle, is heavily invested in the AI sector. Tied up in this risk is the fact that many AI firms are building data centers without adequate insurance , since the projects are simply too expensive, complex, and risky for many insurers to take on, leaving the companies building data centers on the hook for what could be billions of dollars of losses if something derails their construction. Also tied up in it is the $3 trillion private credit industry, or what used to be called “shadow banking”: the private equity firms, asset managers, and other nonbank entities whose systemic risks have long worried financial analysts, since they act like banks but without any of the accompanying regulation, oversight, and government backstops. Do Not Forget This doesn’t mean that a 2008-style crisis is necessarily in the cards. There is an ongoing debate about the level of risk AI poses to wider financial stability, and there are some experts who believe AI’s contribution to the US economy is overstated. We won’t know exactly what the end of the AI boom will look like or how badly it will affect the rest of us until such a thing happens. But it does seem implausible at this point to believe it won’t mean at least some significant amount of pain for US workers and households more generally. What we can safely say is that the math behind both AI firms’ business model and their future fiscal projections simply don’t add up, and that we can be sure of this because the people who have actual money on the line — investors, bond traders, insurers — are acting like it. Despite the supposed rationality of markets and the private sector, the AI boom is looking more and more like yet another historical episode where herd thinking and hype is driving the high-risk decisions of private capital. Beyond everything else, it’s a colossal waste of money. The gargantuan sums we’re talking about are comparable to what we should have started spending years ago on the renewable energy transition in order to prevent a climate catastrophe — a catastrophe that the AI build-out, perversely, is making worse . Instead, because this money is concentrated in private hands, these resources are being plowed into an industry that doesn’t actually make money and seems to be built on wishful thinking. We see a similar thing happening in China, where, because these decisions are made similarly undemocratically, vast sums are also thrown into one big bet on AI while the rest of the economy stagnates. If this all does fall apart and the ripple effects of that collapse are as bad as some predict, it’s more than likely that taxpayers will be asked to foot the bill for another bailout. If and when that time comes, remember that there were umpteen warning signs and that it all could have been avoided. Class Struggle in the Major Leagues

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Reform & Revolution ／ 著者: 不明 ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-23T13:47:58+00:00

On December 1st, 2026, at 11:59 PM, the collective bargaining agreement between Major League Baseball (MLB) and the Major League Baseball Player’s Association (MLBPA) will expire. Within a minute of the expiration, MLB owners will institute a lock-out of players , even though it is the off-season. Their likenesses will be scrubbed from all public-facing MLB media; teams will no longer be able to communicate with players and vice-versa. This will lead to a stalemate: neither side will make concessions, each waiting until the start of “spring training”, the pre-season for MLB. It is at this point that the struggle begins in earnest, as delays threaten the loss of paychecks and game revenue. Public pressure will begin to mount on both sides. In other sports, the risk of missed paychecks usually causes the union to buckle long before any concessions are extracted from ownership. In effect, the work stoppage never transitions from a lockout to a strike. In recent decades, notably from the end of the 90s until the recent 2022 collective bargaining agreement, the MLBPA was no different in its reluctance to press the issue. However, prior to those years the MLBPA was the most militant and strongest union in American sports; since the 2022 CBA it has reclaimed that mantle, willing and able to use the threat of missing games to achieve its goals. The 2026-2027 CBA lockout is likely to be the biggest labor struggle in major American sports in decades. As the most visible labor struggle in American public view prior to the 2028 May Day strikes, it will be critical for DSA to intervene in support of labor to show our role in the fights and demonstrate the power of organized labor. To do so, DSA should draw on the history of organized labor in baseball. A brief sketch of Baseball labor history The long run of baseball’s history in the United States has been equally matched by a history of labor struggles, essentially beginning when baseball’s rules reached near-modern form in the 1880s. Early baseball players were overworked and highly exploited. Whereas baseball pitchers today work either once every five games or in more frequent short bursts, early pitchers were expected to throw hundreds of complete games every year, destroying their shoulders and elbows in the process. Careers were not long, yet even in a player’s prime they could expect only a pittance, despite bringing all the value to the sport. This was due to a monopoly suppressing labor costs: players were owned by teams for their life. In 1890, the players recognized that they were the source of value, and almost all of them walked off the job—not to strike, but to form a league of their own: the player’s league. It was a raucous hit, but they were engaged in a game of chicken with the owners, who formed a scab league. The investors backing the player’s league swerved first, and the owners ruthlessly crushed labor when players slunk back to the sport. Baseball’s labor movement went into a 75 year coma. It is little coincidence that this coma coincided with most of baseball’s time as a segregated sport. Just as racism served to divide and conquer the working class elsewhere, racist appeals certainly played their part in baseball’s labor history. When the league finally integrated, organized labor struggles came back with it. A decade after Jackie Robinson retired, in the middle of the sixties, players voted to form the Major League Baseball Player’s Association. From the beginning, the MLBPA was a fighting union, and a black player would lead one of baseball’s most important labor fights. In the 1960s – just as in the 1890s – there was no competition for players: once a player was signed to a team, the team owned his rights in perpetuity. This was known as the reserve clause, and in 1969, a player named Curt Flood decided to challenge this system. He had been traded to Philadelphia, a city he despised for its racist fans, and he decided he was going to refuse to sign a contract with the team. Backed by the MLBPA, he threw away his career because he knew it would open the door for other players. He sued the team and the league, taking them all the way to the Supreme Court, where he lost. Afterwards, he was blackballed by every team in the league, but he proved that the reserve clause system was unsustainable. With continuous pressure, the union forced the league to adopt free agency—a development which preceded free agency in sports like football and hockey by decades. Why do MLB owners lock-out the players, instead of just negotiating with the old CBA in place? In 1994, when owners and players kept working under the terms of the old CBA, they continued negotiations into the regular season. At the mid-point of the season, when playoff races really heated up and owners began to cash in, the players launched a strike. They had received half of their wages, but owners had not received most of their profit. They had more leverage at that moment than at any other point, and they used it well. The owners dug their heels in, and the 1994 season, and its playoffs, were lost forever. The players won, and the owners caved on their salary cap demand. Owners vowed never to let a strike like this happen again; now, they begin the lockout during the off-season, when they have the most leverage and their revenue is farthest away from the threat. Why would players lose a season to prevent a salary cap? In every North American sports league except baseball, the implementation of a salary cap crippled the sport’s union, making them no longer a serious threat to ownership. The cap pits labor against itself and ties labor’s salaries to league baseball revenue, which can be manipulated by the league through unrepresentative accounting. By the union’s own accounting, baseball players’ revenue split is as good or better than any other sport with a capped league. And they get better benefits as well. What kinds of benefits have the players won? One big advantage MLB players have over the most popular sport in the US, football, is that their contracts are fully guaranteed. In football, an injury results in the immediate loss of millions for the player. In baseball the player will be paid the full value of his contract, no matter what happens to him. Players also get very good healthcare benefits, which become accessible for life after four years in the Major Leagues. With just 43 days in the big leagues, they are also entitled to a lifelong pension. This pension increases in size with continued playing time, maxing out at 10 years of service. Players are also offered sizable draft bonuses and granted the ability to refuse assignments and trades depending on their service time. There’s also no max compensation for players, nor any system of forcing players to stay with their team when they finish their initial contract. In sports like football and hockey, players can be essentially forced to sign under-market deals in these situations via methods which penalize other teams if they try to make counter-offers; in some cases, management simply bars the player from negotiating with other teams at all. What will this CBA negotiation center on? As they did in the 1990s, the owners are attempting to force a salary cap on the players. Player demands center around increasing the money going to younger players, including increases in the minimum salary, and around forcing low-payroll teams to spend more. Both sides are appealing to public opinion by utilizing a “competitive balance” framing, though the players are open about seeking the best deal for players’ rights. Competitive balance is the idea that teams should have an equal opportunity to acquire talent and win games: owners suggest that the payroll disparities between teams are unfair. Players would riposte that every owner can afford to spend on their team: they simply choose not to. Further complicating matters is that a number of teams consistently succeed without high payrolls—including the Rays and the Brewers, the two teams playing in the 2026 World Series. How does the public respond to baseball labor struggles? Historically, media coverage and reactionary attitudes have led the public to be firmly against players. The media has rarely portrayed the union sympathetically, and framings like “they get paid to play a kids game” have proliferated, neglecting the fact that many players, especially Minor League lifers, only ever receive a pittance. Further, there is a huge asymmetry in transparency: every baseball player’s salary is public, but until very recently (the Atlanta Braves are currently a publicly traded asset), no baseball team’s revenue is public. This asymmetry makes it difficult for the public to have a real sense of the disparity between the wealth of owners and the wealth of players. However, public opinion has rapidly shifted in recent years, especially as baseball’s audience has trended nerdier, younger, and more progressive, and now players are viewed much more sympathetically. The post-94 practice of owner lockouts has further hurt the owners’ ability to control the narrative. Now, the players aren’t stopping you from watching the game: the owners are. Why has the union regained its militancy? There are three reasons: first, the shift in public opinion in its favor, as we noted; second, the experience of watching every other major sports union be crushed in negotiations over the past two decades; and third, the primary answer, Bruce Meyer’s hiring before the COVID year. Three years after a CBA negotiation in 2016 that was broadly regarded as an ownership victory, the MLBPA hired Bruce Meyer as one of their lead negotiators, likely expecting his first big fight to be in the 2022 CBA. Instead, his fight came during the COVID year, where the players managed to force a desperate league to agree to pro-rated, guaranteed salaries in a 60 game season, a far better deal than any other sports player’s union achieved! The players managed to turn public opinion against the league, refusing to budge on any CBA modifications and successfully portraying the league as the villain. Galvanized by this victory, they fought the league in the 2022 negotiations to the point of the season being at threat, and they managed to secure a few victories at the bargaining table. During all this, the union also quietly began an effort to unionize players in the Minor Leagues, the developmental system employed by Major League Baseball. In September 2022, with almost no warning, the union announced that Minor Leaguers had agreed to unionize under the MLBPA and presented it as a fait accompli, forcing the league to acquiesce immediately, without even challenging the MLBPA with a card check. Before the next season, the two sides settled a collective bargaining agreement in which Minor League player salaries doubled. Before this unionization effort, the MLBPA had often been accused of throwing Minor Leaguers under the bus in favor of superstars: this undercut a major line of attack against the union. If the union is going to be successful in winning concessions against ownership, it must continue fighting for Minor Leaguers, a task which will ensure a steady pipeline of union members ready to struggle against ownership. Are the players ‘woke,’ though? Broadly, no. Baseball players tend to be the second-most conservative athletes in major North American sports, behind NHL players. However, the vast majority follow a “stick to sports” attitude. Further, the team union reps tend to be more progressive (as far as can be gleaned from social media, comments, and actions during 2020), and we should also note the league’s inclusion of many hispanic players from both the US and from the Caribbean, though foreign-born players are even more reluctant to comment on politics. What role will Trump’s National Labor Relations Board play in the fight? And for that matter, what will Trump do? Just like in labor negotiations in other sectors, the NLRB acts to mediate class conflict and produce compromise. It does so by ensuring that each side is negotiating in “good faith”, which usually means making concessions every time a side comes to the negotiating table. What counts as a sufficiently real concession is determined by the NLRB. Under the Trump administration, we can already expect increasing bias against workers in this mediation, even without direct marching orders from Trump. However, Trump has already publicly picked a side in this fight, saying that MLB should implement a salary cap, in fact arguing that MLB should have broken the union in the 90s during the first cap fight. As the fight begins to seriously threaten the season, we should expect Trump to take an oppositional stance and pressure the union. Why and how could DSA intervene? Labor battles in baseball are demonstrations of how our state and economy operates, a high-profile snapshot of class conflict. They provide an opportunity to show the strength of organized labor, the role of the state in class conflict, and the need for unified action. Socialists have the opportunity to highlight this and push the union to keep fighting. Chris Wickham Revolutionized Our View of the Middle Ages

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Paolo Tedesco ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-23T13:09:52+00:00

With the passing of Chris Wickham earlier this month, we have lost the greatest medieval historian since Marc Bloch, and one of the most original Marxist historians ever. In his career, which spanned half a century, Wickham wrote more than twenty books and two hundred articles. In these works, he showed how the social and economic structures of Europe and the Mediterranean transformed in the period from 500 to 1500. Key factors in his analysis were the rise and fall of the state, the political and economic role of the landowning elites, and the protagonism of the peasants. His massive intellectual production revolutionized not only our idea of the Middle Ages but above all our understanding of how societies function and change across time. Formative Years Chris Wickham was born on May 18, 1950, into a middle-class family in the Welsh village of Rossett. He attended a private school in Somerset, England, before beginning his studies at the University of Oxford in 1968, from which he graduated in 1971. During these years, he frequently traveled to Italy, becoming familiar with its early medieval landscape. When he decided to undertake postgraduate research at Oxford, Wickham departed from the themes that were predominant at that time in British medieval studies, such as Anglo-Saxon England and Carolingian France. He focused instead on early medieval Italy with a thesis titled “Economy and Society in Eighth-Century Northern Tuscany,” which he completed in 1975. After his PhD, he continued to teach at Oxford, traveling from time to time to Italy, where he started to participate in excavations with eminent archaeologists such as as Graeme Barker, Riccardo Francovich, and Richard Hodges. Fieldwork in Italy was certainly one of the experiences that shaped his approach to history for the rest of his life. A second turning point in his career was his move to the University of Birmingham in 1977. Although this university no longer reflected the working-class lifeblood of the pre–World War II town, Birmingham still had a far greater number of students from middle-class and working-class backgrounds than Oxford and Cambridge did in the 1970s and ’80s. Rodney Hilton, the most renowned historian of the medieval English peasantry, also taught there. Hilton belonged to the group of British Marxist historians that included such figures as E. P. Thompson, Christopher Hill, and Eric Hobsbawm. Their work shaped the emergence of history from below from the 1950s onward. Hilton had an enormous intellectual influence on Wickham as well as on other young scholars, like the Byzantine historian John Haldon and the social scientist Gregor McLennan. They were all involved in politics, and Haldon and Wickham both joined the Communist Party of Great Britain (CPGB). Hilton had also been a party member before he resigned in 1956 after the Soviet invasion of Hungary. By the 1970s, the CPGB was rather more critical of the USSR and had condemned the invasion of Czechoslovakia in 1968. Given his ties to Italy, Wickham also became a member of the much larger Italian Communist Party, which he saw as a model because of its history and ideas. Unfortunately, the effort of this small group of historians and militants to push the CPGB toward a Eurocommunist trajectory failed, and they all left the party before the end of the 1980s. In our numerous conversations, Haldon very fondly recalled the meetings at Hilton’s house where the junior participants presented their Marxist interpretations of history to their severe but open-minded host. Hilton’s circle was a true laboratory of ideas in the 1980s. The Making of a Marxist Historian In this cultural milieu, Wickham began to revise the dogmatic Marxist approach to medieval history that was still predominant at that time. In two seminal essays published in 1984 (“The Other Transition”) and in 1985 (“The Uniqueness of the East”), Wickham rejected the idea that a contrast between slavery and serfdom marked the transition from the ancient to the medieval world. Instead, he argued that these two historical periods had different political and economic organizations that he called “tributary” and “feudal” modes of production. In the tributary regime, power was concentrated strongly in the hands of a ruling elite at the top of the political system. Tributary political structures ultimately depended on their capacity to extract sufficient revenues from the agricultural population to fund their centralized machinery of power, such as a court, a bureaucracy, and a salaried army. In feudal societies, in contrast, the means of coercion were in the hands of local landholding elites, with a fragile structure of rule at the top. The crucial factor for exercising power in such political formations was the direct ownership and control of the land. Wickham also argued that when landlords or the state were not able to appropriate the surplus from the peasantry in a systematic way, an economic organization that he called the “peasant mode of production” became predominant. We can find several examples of such peasant economies in Europe and the Western Mediterranean from the sixth to the eighth centuries. He labeled the period between the fall of Rome and the rise of the Carolingian Empire as a sort of “golden age of the peasantry.” In the following years, Wickham demonstrated his ability as an unorthodox and open-minded Marxist historian by refining his interpretation on the basis of theoretical dialogue with other scholars as well as the results of his fieldwork. We can find evidence of his intellectual achievements in two monumental volumes written eighteen years apart: Framing the Early Middle Ages: Europe and the Mediterranean, 400 – 800 (2005) and The Donkey and the Boat: Reinterpreting the Mediterranean Economy, 950 – 1180 (2023). In these works, Wickham rewrote the economic and social history of the Middle Ages, overturning some interpretative paradigms of past historiography, including his own original conception of “modes of production.” He made clear that his reinterpretation of the transitions from one historical period to another was deeply influenced by the theoretical reflections of two members of the Birmingham historical materialism group: John Haldon and the Turkish historian Halil Berktay . In his new formulation, Wickham used the concept of modes of production exclusively to mark the passage from a preindustrial economic logic to that of capitalism. From this perspective , categories like tributary, feudal, or peasant no longer identified a given mode of production in the traditional Marxist sense. Instead, they referred to specific configurations of the societies predating the advent of capitalism. Wickham’s Long Vision of History Expanding on this new interpretation, Wickham argued that three major cycles — ancient, medieval, and modern — have framed the economic configuration of Western Eurasia and Africa in the last two millennia. Beginning from a unified Mediterranean in the second century BCE under Roman expansion, the ancient trade cycle peaked in the imperial period (first to fourth centuries CE). From that point, it began its decline in Western Europe around 500: at the same time, the Eastern Mediterranean kept flourishing for at least two centuries, until the collapse of the Eastern Roman Empire led to the fragmentation of the Mediterranean economic space. The second Mediterranean trade cycle emerged around 900 in southern and central Mediterranean regions such as Egypt, North Africa, and Sicily. The localization of Mediterranean economic life led to slow but steady growth over the following two centuries, expanding to encompass northern and central Italy in the twelfth century. The differences between the ancient and medieval periods lie in the varying interventions of the state and landowning elites in the peasant economy: on the one hand, tax-raising by states; on the other, rent-taking by landlords. Their points of convergence “lie in the fact that, in both types of society, the peasant majority controlled the basic production process .” The state and the elites created infrastructures of exchange, but the access of peasants to larger surpluses and, consequently, the market for food and handcrafted goods made it possible for production and commerce to significantly increase. This means that the scale of production, circulation, and consumption of bulk commodities generated by peasant demand was the most obvious proxy for the level of economic sophistication that ancient and medieval structures attained. Luxury goods historically entered the market only in response to the purchasing power of quantitatively limited elites. In this framework, “states and landlords could affect the production process by demanding different types of taxes or rents, thus curtailing the levels of commercialization of the peasant surpluses. However, they did not have a structural role in production, nor did they exercise forms of direct control over it.” The start of the third economic cycle on the eve of the modern era saw the economy reorienting itself toward the Atlantic — a factor that historians have typically interpreted as the end of the medieval economy. The trade ties between Europe and the rest of the world greatly expanded in this new economic cycle, bringing with them new types of exploitation. According to Wickham, this final cycle, which gave rise to capitalism, deviated from the economic rationale of the other eras. It was only at this historical juncture that peasants ultimately lost control of production and were forced to relocate to cities or factory towns, where they struggled to survive as proletarians completely exposed to the fluctuations of the market: “Under capitalism, in fact, capitalists dominate the labor process directly, and the exploitation involved is hidden by the apparently free nature of the wage-labor contract .” From Peasants to the World Economy As a historian of the Middle Ages, Wickham was capable of moving from the minute study of local societies to the macroeconomic factors that shaped the world economy in premodern times. After his dissertation on Tuscan rural societies, he continued his research on Italian rural communities, from the countryside surrounding Lucca to the remote areas of Valva and the Biferno Valley in the central Apennines ( Studi sulla società degli Appennini nell’Alto Medioevo , 1982; Il problema dell’incastellamento nell’Italia centrale , 1985; The Mountains and the City , 1988). In these studies, he examined every aspect of people’s lives: How did peasants behave toward local authority figures, from the bishop or the aristocracy to the landlords? What strategies of negotiation or resistance did they undertake in court or on the ground to resist their powerful counterparts? Where did they live, and what did they produce and eat? What did they exchange, and under which conditions did this trade occur? In essence, the key question Wickham asked in his research was why the society he was investigating worked in the way that it did. To answer this question, he moved to explore comparable communities in the Mediterranean and Europe, from the villages of Aphrodito and Jeme in central and Upper Egypt to those of central Anatolia, and from the interior of the Iberian Peninsula to the large plateau of the Île-de-France. In Framing , he also invented a village, which he called Malling, to describe the archetypal rural community of Anglo-Saxon England. In a 2017 article, Wickham expanded his range of case studies enormously, even comparing Northern Italy and the Jiangnan region of China from the tenth to thirteenth centuries. As in his previous studies, Wickham concentrates on the structures of production and consumption of these two “highly complex economies by the standards of the period.” As he explains, no matter how global we want to be, “most of the economic interaction lay at local and regional levels.” The recurrent theme through all these studies was the question of how a small minority of people loosely defined as elite managed to appropriate and channel the wealth produced by the rest of the population — the 90 percent addressed in the work of Kim Bowes — while transforming (or failing to transform) the peasant economy from a local to a global-oriented structure. Legacy In a fascinating interview from 2022 with historians Charles West and Maria Elena Cortese, Wickham revealed that during his formative period at Oxford, he never had someone whom he considered his mentor. He built his scholarship through a massive and at times disordered reading of diverse literature and by exchanging ideas with his peers. His intellectual formation was the result of a lateral collaboration between students of history rather than a vertical transfer of knowledge from a senior to a junior scholar. Wickham pursued this strategy even in the later stages of his career by participating in collaborative projects in which everybody was welcome to offer their contribution. For this reason, perhaps, Wickham never created a proper school in the traditional academic sense: a group of scholars who can be recognized as his students. That said, he had an enormous impact on the way many of us write about history. A small group — including myself — embraced his Marxist approach to history, even though, in keeping with the leftist tradition, we never agreed on a single Marxist interpretation (Chris was glad of this variety). The majority appreciated his rigorous methodology, his ability to combine history, archaeology, and anthropology, and his deep familiarity with the scholarly tradition of each country he interacted with, through his research and through his personal and intellectual relations. But the human factor made the true difference. Since he could not change the world the way he wanted, he introduced a sort of intellectual socialism in the academic world. We did not all have (or want to have) a master to follow, and we could not all go to Oxford to learn history, but we all had the chance to share our ideas with Chris Wickham: he was always there offering advice, comments, or criticisms. Chris has left us an enormous legacy, a gift I like to call, paraphrasing the historian Giovanni Levi, “immaterial inheritance”: a rich and polyhedric constellation of methods, questions, and ideas that have fostered — and will indubitably keep fostering — deeply meaningful academic connections. If you live in California, you've probably watched mushrooms pop up overnight in your yard after the first good rain. Maybe you spotted something bright orange on a trail, or a perfect white cap growing near an oak tree and thought — what is that thing? California's wet winters (roughly November through March) create ideal conditions for fungi. Whether you're in San Diego, the Bay Area, or somewhere in between, these are 20 species you're most likely to run into. If you want a second opinion in the field, try MushDex mushroom identifier — a free mushroom identification tool you can use online or through its mobile app. One thing before we dive in: never eat a wild mushroom based solely on an online article. Always confirm your ID with an experienced forager or your local mycological society. The Dangerous Ones and Common Lawn Mushrooms 1. Death Cap ( Amanita phalloides ) This is the one that keeps toxicologists up at night. Responsible for most fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide, it's established across California — especially under coast live oaks from the Bay Area northward. The cap ranges from olive-green to yellowish, and there's a sac-like volva at the base of the stem. If you remember only one mushroom on this list, make it this one. 2. Western Destroying Angel ( Amanita ocreata ) Southern California's most dangerous mushroom. It's all white, elegant, and fruits under oaks from late winter into spring. People mistake it for edible species constantly. If you find a pure white mushroom growing near oaks anywhere from San Diego to Santa Barbara — just walk away. 3. Fly Agaric ( Amanita muscaria ) The classic fairy-tale mushroom: bright red cap dotted with white warts. You'll see it under pines and in landscaped areas statewide. Toxic, yes, but so distinctive you're unlikely to confuse it with anything edible. Still makes a great photo. 4. Green-spored Parasol ( Chlorophyllum molybdites ) The number-one cause of mushroom poisoning in North America, and it loves Southern California lawns. Large, white, and often growing in fairy rings on grass. The giveaway? Its spore print comes out green. You'll see these pop up in San Diego and LA backyards after rain. 5. Yellow Stainer ( Agaricus xanthodermus ) This one looks a lot like the button mushrooms at the grocery store. The trick: scratch the cap or cut the base, and it turns bright chrome yellow and smells like ink. Very common in SoCal gardens and parks, and a frequent cause of nausea. 6. Common Fieldcap ( Agrocybe pediades ) Those small, tan, button-shaped mushrooms dotting your lawn after rain? Probably these. They're one of the most common lawn mushrooms in California. Not edible, and despite what you might read online — they're not psychedelic. Just a plain little lawn mushroom doing its thing. 7. Mower's Mushroom ( Panaeolina foenisecii ) Another tiny brown lawn mushroom you'll see everywhere. It gets confused with psychedelic species because of its size and color, but it isn't one. You'll mow right over hundreds of these every season. 8. Fairy Ring Mushroom ( Marasmius oreades ) Ever noticed dark green circles in a lawn with small tan mushrooms growing along the ring? That's a fairy ring, and these are actually edible — they're popular in European cooking. The catch: they grow alongside toxic look-alikes like certain Clitocybe species, so this isn't a beginner pick. 9. Yellow Houseplant Mushroom ( Leucocoprinus birnbaumii ) If a bright lemon-yellow mushroom appeared in your potted plant, this is probably it. Common in nursery soil and indoor pots year-round. Mildly toxic, so keep curious kids and pets away, but it's harmless to the plant itself — it's just feeding on organic matter in the potting mix. 10. Shaggy Mane ( Coprinus comatus ) Tall, white, and shaggy, these pop up along roadsides, in backyards, and on disturbed soil across the state. The cool part? Within hours of maturing, the cap dissolves into black ink. Edible when young and firm, but you have to cook them fast before they self-digest. One of the easiest mushrooms in California to recognize. Wood-Lovers, Edibles, and the Rest of the Crew If you're just getting started with foraging and want to practice identifying species before heading out, mushroom-identification.com is a free online tool that can help you build confidence. 11. Common Ink Cap ( Coprinopsis atramentaria ) Similar to the Shaggy Mane — grayish, and it dissolves into ink too. Here's the important part: if you eat this mushroom and drink alcohol within a couple of days, you'll get violently sick. It contains a compound that blocks alcohol metabolism. Technically edible, but most people skip it for obvious reasons. 12. Dyeball ( Pisolithus arhizus ) That weird, dark, lumpy blob at the base of your eucalyptus tree? Probably a Dyeball. Super common in Southern California, especially in sandy or poor soil. Not edible, but it's actually a beneficial fungus helping your tree absorb nutrients underground. 13. Western Jack-o'-Lantern ( Omphalotus olivascens ) A gorgeous orange mushroom growing in clusters at the base of oaks. It actually glows faintly in the dark. Unfortunately, it's toxic and gets mistaken for chanterelles. The key difference: Jack-o'-Lanterns have true gills and grow on wood, while chanterelles have blunt ridges and grow from soil. 14. Chicken of the Woods ( Laetiporus gilbertsonii ) Bright orange-and-yellow shelves growing on tree trunks — you can't miss it. One of California's most popular edible mushrooms, and beginners love it because nothing else really looks like it. One caveat: specimens growing on eucalyptus can cause stomach trouble for some people. 15. California Golden Chanterelle ( Cantharellus californicus ) The crown jewel of California foraging. Golden-yellow, funnel-shaped, and fruiting under oaks from December through March. These can get surprisingly large — some weigh over two pounds. Found across the state, including parts of Southern California. Just make sure you know the Jack-o'-Lantern comparison (#13) before picking. 16. Black Trumpet ( Craterellus calicornucopioides ) Dark gray to black, thin, and trumpet-shaped. Almost invisible against the forest floor — foragers call them "the horn of plenty" because once you spot one, you realize you've been standing on dozens. Mostly found in Northern California under oaks and tanoaks. Delicious, with a deep, smoky flavor. 17. King Bolete ( Boletus edulis var. grandedulis ) California's version of the famous porcini. Big brown cap, thick stem, spongy pores underneath instead of gills. Look for it in Northern California coastal pine forests in fall. If you find one in good shape before the bugs do, you've hit the foraging jackpot. 18. Candy Cap ( Lactarius rubidus ) A small, orange-brown mushroom that smells exactly like maple syrup when dried. People bake them into cookies, churn them into ice cream, and fold them into pancake batter. Found under oaks and conifers in Northern California during winter. One of the most unique edible mushrooms you'll find anywhere. 19. Morel ( Morchella spp.) That honeycomb-looking mushroom selling for $40 a pound at the farmers market? That's a morel. In California, your best bet is burned areas in the Sierra Nevada the spring after a wildfire. Unmistakable once you've seen one, but watch out for the false morel ( Gyromitra ), which has a wrinkled, brain-like cap instead of clean pits. 20. Turkey Tail ( Trametes versicolor ) Thin, leathery, fan-shaped brackets with beautiful concentric color bands in brown, tan, and white. You'll find them on dead logs and stumps everywhere in California, year-round. Not a culinary mushroom, but one of the most heavily researched fungi in the world — and one of the prettiest things you'll spot on a fallen log. Keep Going These 20 barely scratch the surface of what's out there. If you want to go deeper, connecting with your local mycological society — like the San Diego Mycological Society or the Bay Area Mycological Society — is the single best move you can make. One afternoon on a group foray will teach you more than months of reading. Happy mushrooming — and when in doubt, don't eat it.

Prepare Your E-Bike

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Sejal Desai ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-22T23:52:30+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. Winter can bring cold temperatures, snow, rain, ice, and shorter days. These conditions can affect your electric bike's battery, tires, brakes, and other important parts. Preparing your e-bike before winter arrives can help you ride more safely, maintain reliable performance, and protect your bike from unnecessary wear. Whether you use your e-bike for daily commuting, weekend adventures, or outdoor activities, a little preparation can make a big difference. You do not need expensive equipment or advanced mechanical skills to get started. Simple steps, such as checking your battery, cleaning your bike, inspecting your brakes, and choosing suitable tires, can help you enjoy more comfortable winter rides. Here is how to prepare your e-bike for the winter season. 1. Check Your E-Bike Battery Your battery is one of the most important parts of your e-bike, and cold weather can affect its performance. Most electric bikes use lithium-ion batteries, which work less efficiently at low temperatures. As a result, you may notice that your battery runs out faster in winter than it does during warmer months. Before winter begins, inspect your battery for visible damage, loose connections, or signs of moisture. Make sure it locks securely into the frame and that the electrical contacts are clean and dry. If your e-bike has a removable battery, take it indoors when you park outside or leave your bike in an unheated garage. Store it in a dry place at a moderate temperature, away from direct sunlight, heaters, and other sources of extreme heat. It is also important to avoid charging a very cold battery. Bring it indoors and allow it to warm naturally before connecting the charger. Many lithium-ion batteries should not be charged below 32°F (0°C), but you should always follow the temperature limits in your manufacturer's instructions. If you plan to store your e-bike for several weeks or months, keep the battery partially charged rather than completely full or empty. A charge level of around 50% to 70% is a common guideline, although your manufacturer's recommendations should take priority. Check the charge level periodically during storage. For more guidance, see Shimano's advice on looking after an e-bike battery in winter. 2. Understand How Cold Weather Affects Battery Range Even when your battery is in good condition, winter temperatures can reduce the distance you can travel on a single charge. Cold weather slows the chemical reactions inside lithium-ion cells, which can reduce the energy available during a ride. The amount of range you lose depends on the temperature, battery condition, riding speed, terrain, tire pressure, and level of motor assistance you use. To prepare for this change, avoid planning winter trips based only on your summer riding range. Leave extra battery capacity for unexpected delays, cold winds, steep hills, and longer routes. You can also improve your winter range by following a few simple habits: Keep your battery indoors until you are ready to ride. Use a lower pedal-assist level when conditions allow. Accelerate smoothly instead of using maximum power immediately. Avoid unnecessary stops and detours. Charge your battery before longer rides, following the manufacturer's charging instructions. A battery cover designed for your particular battery may provide some insulation against cold air. However, it cannot completely prevent range loss, and it must not interfere with ventilation, electrical connections, or safe battery operation. Planning ahead helps you avoid running out of power far from home. 3. Inspect Your Tires and Tire Pressure Your tires are especially important during winter because they are the main point of contact between your e-bike and the road. Wet leaves, snow, mud, ice, and loose gravel can all reduce traction. Start by checking the tire tread. Look for worn areas, cracks, cuts, and embedded objects. If your tires are badly worn or damaged, replace them before winter riding becomes more difficult. Next, check the tire pressure. Cold temperatures can affect pressure, and tires that are too soft or too hard may not perform as expected. Follow the pressure range printed on your tire or recommended by your e-bike manufacturer. On wet or loose surfaces, a pressure adjustment within the permitted range may help improve grip. However, do not reduce pressure too much, as this can increase the risk of rim damage, tire problems, and poor handling. If you regularly ride on snow or ice, consider tires designed for those conditions. Winter tires may use a different rubber compound or tread pattern, while studded tires can provide additional grip on suitable icy surfaces. Remember that even wide tires cannot guarantee traction on ice. Ride slowly, avoid sudden turns, and leave plenty of space when braking. 4. Test Your Brakes Before Winter Arrives Reliable brakes are essential throughout the year, but they become even more important when roads are wet, muddy, or slippery. Before your first winter ride, inspect both the front and rear brakes. Check the brake pads for wear and make sure the brake levers feel normal when you squeeze them. For disc brakes, look for excessive rotor wear, visible damage, or contamination. If you notice unusual noises, weak braking, or a lever that feels soft or spongy, have the system checked before riding. Hydraulic brakes may require professional attention if they leak or lose pressure. Mechanical brakes may need cable adjustments as parts wear. Test your brakes at low speed in a safe area before riding in traffic. Make sure the bike slows down smoothly and predictably. Winter roads can increase stopping distances, even when your brakes are working properly. Give yourself more space between vehicles, pedestrians, and other cyclists. Apply the brakes gradually instead of squeezing them suddenly, particularly on snow, ice, or wet surfaces. If your brakes do not perform as expected, do not continue riding until the problem has been corrected. 5. Clean Your E-Bike and Protect It From Road Salt Winter dirt and road salt can damage your e-bike if they remain on the frame and components for long periods. Salt can encourage corrosion on metal parts, including the chain, gears, bolts, and electrical contacts. Before winter starts, give your bike a thorough but gentle cleaning. Remove mud, dust, and old lubricant from the frame and drivetrain. Use a damp cloth, a soft brush, and a bike-friendly cleaning product when necessary. Avoid directing high-pressure water at the motor, battery compartment, display, bearings, or electrical connections. High-pressure water can force moisture into areas that should remain protected. After cleaning, dry the bike with a clean cloth. Pay attention to the chain, brake components, battery contacts, and areas where water can collect. Apply an appropriate bicycle chain lubricant after the drivetrain is clean and dry. A wet-weather lubricant may be useful for winter riding because it is designed to resist water better than many dry lubricants. Follow the product instructions and wipe away excess lubricant to reduce dirt buildup. After riding on salted roads, wipe down your bike as soon as practical. Regular cleaning is easier than removing layers of dried salt and grime later. Keeping your e-bike clean helps protect its components and makes it easier to spot damage before it becomes a bigger problem. 6. Check the Lights and Electrical Connections Winter often brings darker mornings, earlier sunsets, fog, and poor visibility. Working lights are therefore essential for safer riding. Check your front light, rear light, and any reflectors fitted to your e-bike. Make sure the lights are secure, bright enough for your riding conditions, and positioned correctly. If your lights use rechargeable batteries, charge them before your rides. If they are powered by the main e-bike battery, confirm that the lighting system works properly and does not display warning messages. Inspect the visible wiring around the handlebars, frame, motor, and battery. Look for damaged insulation, loose connectors, or cables that could become caught in moving parts. Do not attempt to repair damaged electrical wiring without the necessary knowledge. Contact a qualified technician or the manufacturer if you find a serious problem. You should also check the display and control buttons. Confirm that the pedal-assist system, speed display, and other basic functions work as expected. Good lighting helps other road users see you, but it does not replace reflective clothing, safe riding habits, or careful route selection. 7. Lubricate the Chain and Inspect Moving Parts Cold weather, moisture, and road dirt can increase wear on your e-bike's moving parts. A properly maintained drivetrain helps your bike shift smoothly and operate more efficiently. Inspect the chain for rust, stiff links, and excessive wear. If the chain looks dry or makes unusual noises while pedaling, clean it and apply suitable lubricant. Check the gears and derailleur to make sure shifting remains smooth. If the chain skips, the gears shift poorly, or the drivetrain makes persistent grinding noises, arrange a repair before riding in difficult conditions. You should also inspect the pedals, crank arms, handlebars, and other parts that experience regular movement or pressure. Make sure important bolts are secure according to the manufacturer's instructions. If your e-bike has suspension, check for visible damage, leaks, or unusual movement. Suspension components can help improve comfort on uneven roads and trails, but they still need regular maintenance. For complicated repairs, a professional bike shop can check the drivetrain, bearings, suspension, and other components before the winter season begins. 8. Prepare Your E-Bike for Wet and Slippery Roads Preparing your bike is only part of winter safety. You should also adjust your riding habits to match the conditions. Wet roads, snow, and ice can reduce traction and make it harder to control your e-bike. Choose routes with better surfaces whenever possible, and avoid steep hills or poorly maintained paths when conditions are dangerous. Use a lower pedal-assist setting on slippery surfaces. Sudden motor power can cause the rear wheel to spin, particularly when starting on snow, mud, or wet leaves. Accelerate gradually and keep your movements smooth. Avoid sharp turns, sudden braking, and aggressive changes in direction. If you encounter ice, slow down before reaching it and avoid abrupt steering or braking. When the surface is too slippery to ride safely, get off the bike and walk it if doing so is safe. Fenders can also be useful during winter because they help reduce the amount of water, mud, and road dirt sprayed onto you and your bike. After a wet ride, inspect the tires, brakes, chain, and electrical connections. Clean and dry the bike before putting it away. 9. Wear Suitable Clothing and Winter Accessories Comfortable clothing can make a major difference during winter rides. Cold hands, wet feet, and strong winds can quickly turn a pleasant trip into an uncomfortable experience. Wear several layers so you can adjust your clothing as your body temperature changes. A wind-resistant outer layer can help protect you from cold air, while moisture-wicking clothing helps manage sweat. Gloves designed for cycling can improve comfort and help you maintain control of the handlebars and brake levers. Choose footwear that provides warmth and a secure grip on the pedals. A properly fitted helmet remains important in winter. Consider a thin thermal layer under your helmet if it fits correctly and does not interfere with the helmet's position. You may also want to use clear or high-visibility eyewear to protect your eyes from cold wind, rain, and road spray. Reflective clothing can improve visibility in low-light conditions. If you commute regularly, waterproof bags and suitable fenders can help protect your belongings from rain and splashes. Choose accessories that work with your bike and do not interfere with the brakes, wheels, lights, or other safety equipment. 10. Consider an E-Bike Designed for Comfort and Versatility If you are planning to ride throughout the year, your choice of e-bike can influence how comfortable and practical your winter trips feel. A model with suitable tires, reliable brakes, a comfortable riding position, and suspension can be useful on uneven roads and mixed surfaces. However, no e-bike is automatically safe on snow or ice, so tire choice, maintenance, and riding technique remain important. It features a full-suspension design, 20-inch wheels, a 750W motor, and a 48V 15Ah battery. Its compact wheel size and suspension can make it appealing to riders who value maneuverability and comfort on uneven surfaces. Before using the D5 2.0 during winter, check the tire condition and pressure, inspect the brakes, and confirm that the battery and electrical components are working correctly. If you regularly encounter snow or ice, check whether the installed tires are suitable for those conditions and consider appropriate winter tires if needed. As with any electric bike, cold weather can reduce battery range. Keep the battery warm before riding, allow it to return to a safe charging temperature after cold trips, and plan your route with enough battery capacity. A versatile e-bike can help you maintain your riding routine through the colder months, provided you prepare it properly and ride within safe conditions. 11. Store Your E-Bike Properly During Winter If you do not plan to ride regularly during winter, proper storage can help protect your e-bike and extend the life of its components. Choose a dry, sheltered location away from rain, snow, and extreme temperature changes. An indoor storage area is generally preferable to leaving the bike exposed outdoors for long periods. If possible, remove the battery and store it indoors according to the manufacturer's instructions. Keep it partially charged, check its charge level periodically, and avoid leaving it completely empty for an extended period. Clean the bike before storage and dry it thoroughly. Lubricate the chain, inspect the tires, and make sure the brakes and other components are in good condition. Inflate the tires within the recommended pressure range. If the bike will remain unused for several months, consider placing it on a suitable stand or checking the tires periodically to prevent problems from prolonged storage. Do not leave the battery connected to a charger continuously unless the manufacturer specifically recommends that arrangement. Keep the charger and battery away from water, direct heat, and combustible materials, and follow the manufacturer's battery safety instructions. When spring arrives, inspect the bike again before your first ride. Check the battery, tires, brakes, lights, chain, and electrical connections. Take a short test ride in a safe area to confirm that everything works properly. 12. Create a Simple Winter Maintenance Routine You do not have to complete every maintenance task after every ride. A simple routine can help you keep your e-bike in good condition without spending too much time on maintenance. Before each ride, check the tire pressure, brakes, lights, and battery charge. Look around the bike for obvious damage or loose parts. After riding in rain, snow, or on salted roads, wipe away dirt and moisture. Pay particular attention to the chain, frame, and areas around electrical connections. Once a week, depending on how often you ride, inspect the tires, drivetrain, brake pads, and visible cables. Clean and lubricate the chain when necessary. During periods of cold weather, pay closer attention to battery performance. If the range drops noticeably, allow the battery to warm naturally and review your riding conditions before assuming the battery is damaged. For longer storage periods, follow the manufacturer's recommended battery charge level and storage temperature. Check the battery periodically rather than leaving it unattended for the entire winter. If you notice unusual motor noises, repeated electrical errors, weak brakes, or battery damage, have the bike inspected before using it again.
