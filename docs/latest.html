No, Seriously, Democratic Socialists Want to Abolish the Senate

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Rochester Red Star: News from Rochester DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-06T18:34:34+00:00

by Gregory Lebens-Higgins On July 14, 2026, the national body of the Democratic Socialists of America released Workers Deserve More, a program outlining the organization’s goals and values. Workers Deserve More was drafted by a committee consisting of members of the National Political Committee, leaders of other national committees, and rank and file members following a process outlined by a resolution passed at DSA’s 2025 National Convention . Upon its release, Workers Deserve More received significant attention in mainstream media. The coverage has provided free advertising for DSA’s popular demands such as universal healthcare, improved working conditions, and replacing the two-party system with a multi-party democracy. Appearing on Fox News , DSA Co-Chair Megan Romer was pressed on the earnestness of these demands. Host Shannon Bream projected DSA’s proposals on screen and asked a series of true/false questions – Replace the presidency and Supreme Court as we now know them? “Yep,” replied Romer. Abolish ICE? “Absolutely.” Among these demands, the call to abolish the Senate has been met with a particularly strong reaction. On newspaper opinion pages, in exchanges with the public at chapter tabling events, and across the family dinner table, it is greeted with a tone of disbelief: “No, seriously, democratic socialists want to abolish the Senate,” exclaims USA Today . As Red Star contributor Jean Allen recently noted , “It is easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of America’s undemocratic institutions.” Our indoctrinated attachment to American institutions and the mystification of their undemocratic nature limits our possibilities for creating something better. James Madison, architect of the Constitution, wrote clearly of his antidemocratic intent. In the tenth of The Federalist Papers , a series of arguments published to support the ratification of the Constitution, Madison considers the problem of “control[ling] the violence of faction.” It is not mere difference of taste or opinion to which Madison is referring, but opposing class interests. “The most common and durable source of factions,” he writes, “has been the various and unequal distribution of property.” Madison continues, “Those who hold and those who are without property have ever formed distinct interests in society. Those who are creditors, and those who are debtors, fall under a like discrimination. A landed interest, a manufacturing interest, a mercantile interest, a moneyed interest, with many lesser interests, grow up of necessity in civilized nations, and divide them into different classes, actuated by different sentiments and views.” The “principal task” of the Constitution is to suppress the struggle between these opposing interests, or at least to ensure the property-holding faction is never on the losing side. The task is achieved by dissipating the popular will and by impeding the ability to reform entrenched power structures. The Senate carries out both of these functions. The United States Senate website ( Senate.Gov ) explains the “Idea of the Senate” by adopting the remarks of British Liberal politician and later ambassador to the United States James Bryce, from his 1888 work The American Commonwealth . Bryce explains the purpose of the Senate as fivefold: (1) to reconcile the independent spirit of the states by giving them equal representation; (2) to create a council to advise and check the President; (3) to “restrain the impetuosity and fickleness of the popular House, and so guard against the effects of gusts of passion or sudden changes of opinion in the people”; (4) to create a body of experienced men as an element of stability; and (5) to establish a court of impeachment for abuses of power by the executive. The Senate has failed on each of these counts. The equal representation of states in the Senate was reached as a compromise between large and small states at the Constitutional Convention. This arrangement leads to wildly undemocratic results, with most senators representing more empty land than people. A majority of senators (fifty-two) from the smallest twenty-six states represent only 21% of the United States population. Though California holds sixty-six times the population of Wyoming, both are entitled to only two senators. The subversion of democratic will to states rights should have been decided by the Union defeat of the Confederacy, yet it remains enshrined in Article I of the Constitution. Bryce’s “body of men [with] greater experience [and a] longer term of membership” has failed to guarantee stability for the country, but has cemented itself as a ruling class. With six-year terms and no recall process, senators are rarely held accountable for selling out their constituents. By amassing wealth and political connections, incumbents in the Senate are reelected at a rate above 80%, and about one-third of senators are currently above seventy years old. As for meritocratic qualification, it is impossible to view Senate press briefings and sustain the belief that America serves up the best and brightest leaders. The Senate is an instrument of obstruction. Its most important tool is the filibuster, requiring sixty votes to end debate and bring most legislation to a vote. Thus, policies with majority support are unable to become law. Since the end of Reconstruction, the procedure has frequently been used to kill Civil Rights legislation and anti-lynching laws. More recently , the Senate filibuster has stifled bills on election integrity, LGBTQ protections, workers rights, immigration reform, climate change, healthcare affordability, and more. Constitutional checks and balances more often subdue challenges to power than restrain the powerful. The inability of the Senate to pass legislation has further empowered the Presidency, which now enacts change rapidly through a flurry of executive orders. The Senate has ceded its role in holding the President accountable. Party loyalty transcends inter-branch responsibilities, and the high bar for impeachment and removal (two-thirds) makes it a toothless and undemocratic remedy. The Supreme Court has also taken on the task of legislating, issuing sweeping rulings on matters that Congress has failed to resolve. Abolishing the Senate is not something that will happen in a vacuum. It is a maximalist demand for achieving a working-class democracy. To reach this horizon, other significant measures must be implemented. These include getting money out of politics by breaking the lobbying power, overturning Citizens United, and publicly funding elections. It includes creating a more representative Congress by implementing proportional representation and ranked-choice voting, and ending gerrymandering to create fair districts. It also includes expanding democracy by protecting voting rights and ending voter suppression. Each of these demands is contained in Workers Deserve More. These changes will help transform Congress into a deliberative body that is more responsive to the democratic will. In this context, the concept of an upper house responsible for checking democratic decisions would seem even more ridiculous. The failures of American institutions arise not from too much democracy, but too little. It must be the mission of all democracy-minded activists to throw off the vestiges of upper-class rule embodied in the Senate. India’s Gen Z Protests Are Breaking the Grip of Fear

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Ashok Kumbamu ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-06T13:46:05+00:00

“I swear, I can die in peace now. I can die smiling.” Coming from the renowned writer and activist Arundhati Roy , these words are startling. For more than three decades, Roy has stood alongside people fighting to protect their land, their homes, and their rights from destructive development projects, state repression, and policies imposed in the name of national growth. She has seen movements rise and victories won, but she has also seen resistance criminalized, promises broken, dreams crushed, and hopes worn down. And yet watching India’s Gen Z take to the streets, Roy seemed to glimpse something she had almost stopped expecting: hope. Challenging Power The immediate trigger for the protests appears to have been the paper leak involving the National Eligibility cum Entrance Test (NEET), the entrance examination for undergraduate medical education programs. But the anger went well beyond that. When the paper was leaked, students lost not only their faith in the system’s capacity to conduct a fair examination but also their faith in its promise that education and hard work would be fairly rewarded. Around the same time, another controversy gave this anger a new political expression. On May 15, 2026, during a Supreme Court hearing, Chief Justice Surya Kant made a deeply prejudiced remark, describing unemployed young Indians as “lazy” and calling them “ cockroaches .” Young people began reclaiming the insult: first through mockery, then with pride. They called themselves “cockroaches” and created the Cockroach Janata Party on Instagram. What began online did not remain there. The anger moved from memes and Instagram posts to protests on the streets of New Delhi. The movement didn’t stop at the paper leak. It brought other major issues to the surface: unemployment, corruption, inequality, and systemic oppression. None of these grievances is new. So what makes this moment different? After decades of watching movements rise and fall, what does Roy see in this generation that gives her hope? Narendra Modi is still the prime minister. The Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) remains the country’s dominant political force. The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS), the ideological parent of the BJP, and its vast organizational networks have not disappeared. Institutions that were strangled by Hindutva forces have not started breathing again. Caste remains deeply rooted in most social interactions. Inequality is staggering. And yet something appears to have shifted: not necessarily in the structure of power but in the way power is perceived and confronted. Young Indians who have grown up under Modi are doing something unusually simple: refusing to treat power as sacred. They not only question it but also ridicule it. In doing so, they are puncturing the aura of invincibility that Hindutva forces have strategically constructed over the years. This really matters because, in a culture of fear and compliance, people begin policing themselves. Journalists reconsider what they write; students hesitate before speaking up or joining a protest; activists weigh what a slogan, photograph, or social media post might cost them. When power goes unchallenged, over time, fear becomes routine, and routine produces compliance. In this way, power creates people who are conditioned to move aside. Learning to Fear I knew this kind of power long before. Growing up in a remote village in Telangana in the 1980s, I learned that power had a face. We called him dora — the dominant-caste landlord who ruled our village as though ruling it were his birthright. No constitution gave him authority; no government appointed him. He inherited it by being born into a landowning Reddy family, one of the dominant castes in the region. Yet hardly anyone questioned how he had acquired his land or his authority or why he should possess either. His power had been reproduced for so long that it no longer appeared as power. It appeared natural, even functional. As children, no one told us to fear him. We learned it by watching the people around us. Adults stood when he passed in the street. Older children stopped playing; conversations suddenly stopped; voices grew quieter; people moved aside. No one had to tell us to keep our distance from him, let alone question him. By the time I knew him, he was already in his early seventies. He struggled to complete a sentence and rarely engaged anyone in conversation. He did not converse with others; he simply gave orders. Looking back, what strikes me most is how little his authority had to do with who he was as a person. He was neither physically imposing nor intellectually remarkable. He was a dora because he was born into the caste, class, and family that had historically produced doras. He did not have to earn or prove his authority. He inherited it, and people recognized and reinforced it through the everyday routines of village life. Over time, it became difficult even to imagine the village organized in any other way. Common Sense Years later, reading Antonio Gramsci gave me a language for understanding what I had witnessed. Domination is most durable when it becomes part of common sense — when hierarchies created by history begin to seem natural, inevitable, or even necessary. Coercion remains available to those in power, but it need not be used constantly once people have learned the boundaries within which they are expected to behave. In the Indian context, B. R. Ambedkar saw inherited social hierarchies, particularly those of caste and class, as sources of power, and he showed how they created deep contradictions within Indian society and democracy. For him, political democracy and constitutional morality have little meaning when everyday life remains deeply entrenched in these social hierarchies. Yet decades after the Constitution came into force, constitutional morality had barely entered the social relations of my village. The dora effectively presided over a feudal constitution of his own. The village itself functioned as a school of subordination and self-subjection. It had its own hidden curriculum, grounded in feudal and Brahmanic domination, that taught each generation to see hierarchy as natural and reinforced it through everyday customs, rituals, symbols, and social practices. Its syllabus was simple: know whom to fear, whom to obey, and whom never to question. It was into this world that the Naxalites — the communist revolutionaries — entered our village. In Telangana and Andhra Pradesh, they were popularly known as annalu (“brothers”), a term they would later disown for its patriarchal and paternalistic overtones. They quickly understood where power lay. But they also understood that eliminating the individual who embodied that power would not necessarily transform the social relations that sustained it. They figured out that something else had to be confronted first: the fear of the dora. Post-Fear One morning, the annalu mobilized a small group from the Dalit wada , the segregated settlement on the outskirts of the village where Dalit (the so-called untouchable) families lived. Together they walked to the dora’s house, brought him outside with minimal force, tied a towel around his neck, and marched him through the streets. As they walked, people came out of their homes and joined the procession. The annalu addressed the crowd. They explained that the problem was not just the dora as an individual but the feudal system he represented. Then they turned to him and said that he could continue to live in the village, but he could not exercise his authority as the dora. That was how we witnessed the fall of the last dora in our village. But the fact is that nothing materially changed that day. Land was still unequally distributed. Caste did not disappear, bonded labor continued, and the power structure that had sustained the dora was still in place. Yet something had changed all the same. People had seen the dora brought out of his house. They had watched him walk through the same streets where they had once moved aside for him. An authority that had appeared natural had been publicly challenged. After that, fear could no longer reproduce itself in quite the same way. I call this moment the post-fear phenomenon. Post-fear does not mean that fear disappears or that coercive power has collapsed. It describes a political condition in which fear remains but begins to lose its capacity to organize everyday behavior — to produce silence, self-policing, obedience, and deference. People may still be afraid, but they begin to act despite their fear. Post-fear, then, is neither liberation nor revolution. It is a transitory and uncertain political condition. But it makes one thing possible: tomorrow may not look like yesterday. False Narrative Forty years later, I found myself thinking again about the moment when the dora’s authority began to collapse in my village. Young Indians are questioning, ridiculing, and confronting a government that has spent more than a decade cultivating an image of strength and invincibility. Things that once appeared beyond challenge are being challenged, and political possibilities that seemed closed are opening up again. It feels as though India, too, may be entering a post-fear phase. But post-fear explains only why people may become more willing to confront power. It does not explain the anger that brings them into the streets. The BJP came to power promising to curb corruption, expand welfare, create jobs, accelerate economic growth, and make India “shine” brighter in the global economy. Eventually, however, the narrative of growth and prosperity encounters a simple test: Can people recognize the experience of their own lives in it? They hear that India is rising, but secure employment remains difficult to find. They hear that rapid growth is creating enormous wealth, and they begin to wonder who owns it. They were told to study hard and compete on merit, but they repeatedly encounter examination leaks and recruitment failures. And while the ruling class and much of the media celebrate India’s economic success, around 800 million people continue to receive free food grains from the government. According to the International Labour Organization–Institute for Human Development India Employment Report 2024 , young people aged fifteen to twenty-nine accounted for nearly 83 percent of India’s unemployed population in 2022. Among unemployed youth, the percentage who had completed secondary education or higher increased from 54.2 percent in 2000 to 65.7 percent in 2022. This systemic crisis has intensified as the public sector has shrunk and the privatization of production and services has grown. Altogether employment opportunities for educated young people have been declining. India’s problem now is not that we have too many young people without education but rather that too many qualified young people are unable to find appropriate employment. Government statistics show that India is getting richer every year, but they do not tell us the whole story. Much of this wealth is created at the expense of the impoverished and is distributed in highly unequal ways. According to the World Inequality Lab , in 2022–23, the richest 1 percent captured 23.3 percent of national income and controlled 40.1 percent of national wealth. By contrast, the bottom 50 percent received only 15 percent of national income and owned only 6.4 percent of national wealth. The researchers describe this concentration at the top as the rise of a “Billionaire Raj.” Weapons of the Weak For young people, it is increasingly difficult to see these contradictions as unrelated problems. The question is no longer simply whether India is growing but who is growing with it and at whose expense. The current movement did not emerge from a sociopolitical vacuum. This generation grew up watching — and, in many cases, experiencing — how the state responds to organized movements and challenges to its ideological domination. They saw students and farmers beaten and arrested. They watched struggles over forests, mining, and displacement unfold in the name of national development. They also witnessed the intensification of counterrevolutionary operations in Adivasi (indigenous) regions, most recently through Operation Kagaar. The BJP government carried out this operation with a declared goal and a deadline: to eliminate the Maoist movement by March 31, 2026. They saw activists, writers, poets, academics, and artists subjected to surveillance, arrest, and criminal prosecution, including on allegations of conspiracy or links to banned organizations. This history of struggle and repression formed part of the political world in which this movement emerged. More important, young people have turned to humor , ridicule, memes, videos, and other everyday forms of defiance. James C. Scott’s ideas of the hidden transcript and the weapons of the weak, developed through his study of peasant resistance in Southeast Asia, are useful here. For Scott, the silence of the weak should not be mistaken for consent. People may appear compliant in the presence of power but express anger, criticism, and resentment in its absence. Scott calls this the “hidden transcript.” In such a situation, people observe, remember, talk among themselves, make jokes, and ridicule those in power, even when they are unwilling or unable to confront them openly. Intellectual Naxals However, there is an important difference between Scott’s peasants and Indian youth today. For young Indians, the hidden transcript is no longer entirely hidden. Their “weapons of the weak” — jokes, memes, ridicule, and mockery — now circulate openly on social media. Moreover, the protests were not confined to Scott’s “weapons of the weak.” At Jantar Mantar, young people also showed remarkable steadfastness, collective resistance, and militancy in confronting the state directly. Their struggle eventually forced Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan to resign . On August 15, 2026, speaking from the Red Fort, Modi declared that the government had succeeded in eliminating armed Naxalites but warned that the country still faced another threat: dimagi Naxals — literally, “intellectual Naxals” — a term he used to describe those he accused of ideologically supporting or encouraging unrest. He said they needed to be “identified and isolated.” This new term joins earlier forms of name-calling, such as “urban Naxal” and “anti-national,” that Modi and his allies have used to intimidate activists, intellectuals, lawyers, academics, and others critical of the government. Yet in what I describe as a post-fear phase, people responded within twenty-four hours by mockingly forming an informal political party on Instagram called the Dimagi Naxal Janata Party. This response shows how the post-fear phenomenon operates: by appropriating labels intended to intimidate and turning them into a form of discursive resistance. Here a historical irony is difficult to miss. Former Union Home Minister P. Chidambaram responded by declaring that he was “ proud to be a dimagi Naxal .” As union home minister from 2008 to 2012, Chidambaram presided over a major escalation of the state’s counter-Maoist campaign. The offensive became widely known as Operation Green Hunt. Unorganized Unity The rising movement does not fit neatly into classical Marxist categories of class struggle or conventional forms of political organization. The people involved do not necessarily share the same ideology, party, leadership, or program. They come from different classes, castes, genders, regions, and political traditions. I describe this possibility as an unorganized united front. I use this paradoxical term deliberately because they are united in struggles, but they are not yet organized. The history of antifascist movements tells us that such a political formation is fragile. Shared anger does not automatically become a coherent and sustainable political movement. Post-fear opens political possibilities, but it does not determine what follows. To give these possibilities political direction, however, the movement must eventually confront harder questions that resistance alone cannot answer: What is it fighting for? Whose interests will it represent? What kind of society does it seek to build? The Gen Z movement also poses a challenge to the Left and Ambedkarite political parties and organizations, many of which have been targets of Hindutva politics. Gen Z has opened a political space that the organized Left, despite years of resistance and sacrifices, has struggled to create over the past twelve years. The question now is whether these political traditions can move beyond their polemical and ideological divisions and engage with this emerging movement. Academics and other public intellectuals, too, can borrow the freedom that younger generations have created and use that space to defend broader freedoms. Perhaps this is where Roy draws her hope from the movement. Her hope need not rest on the belief that the movement has already defeated the forces it confronts or even that it knows where it is going. Perhaps, then, her hope lies somewhere more modest but no less consequential: a generation that dares to look power in the eye and say, “Enough is enough. This time, you move aside.”

Trump Family Prediction Market Conflicts Are a Good Bet

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: David Moscrop ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-06T13:46:05+00:00

There’s nothing to see here, folks. Don’t believe your lying eyes. The eldest son of the president of the United States and his investment firm, 1789 Capital, are raising money in a funding round for Polymarket, the gambling website that styles itself a “prediction market” and which DonaldTrump Jr’s firm values at $21 billion. But it’s a nothingburger. Really. Except, if the relationship seems suspect on its face, that’s because it is. Trump Jr and his company stress that there’s a thick, heavy wall between his political connections — for instance, his father, the president — and his business activities. Never mind that, as the New York Times reports , the Trump family is invested in the “prediction market” business, and Trump Jr is an adviser for Polymarket and competitor Kalshi. Never mind that Trump Jr lobbied Republican state attorneys general to leave these companies alone as they sought regulatory capacity to rein in these gambling giants. So much for the wall between business and politics. The New Threat on the Block You can bet on just about anything on definitely-not-gambling sites like Polymarket and Kalshi. On an overcast Saturday morning, September 5, at 10:21 a.m. Eastern Standard Time, the “politics” tab on Polymarket’s site is offering odds on whether the Digital Asset Market Clarity (or CLARITY) Act will be signed into law (15 percent chance), the Federal Reserve rate decision in September (50 percent chance of a 25 basis points increase), and a Russia-Ukraine ceasefire by June 30, 2027 (57 percent). The CLARITY Act, introduced in Congress by a Republican lawmaker, and backed by Donald Trump, would regulate the cryptocurrency market. Last week, President Trump threatened to stop trading with some US partners unless the Fed cut interest rates. On the weekend, Trump’s son-in-law Jared Kushner and US special envoy Steve Witkoff arrived in Moscow ahead of talks with Russia and Ukraine in an effort to end the war. A thick, heavy wall indeed. Scroll down any politics or economic market page on Polymarket, Kalshi, or any of the newfangled gambling platforms, and you’ll find no shortage of wagers that could be affected by the activities of someone in or close to the Trump family. The connection between the Trumps and the gambling markets is a prima facie conflict of interest — one that should matter in a sane, stable, democracy, but doesn’t seem to in the United States today. It’s a risk that won’t abate. In April, an American soldier was charged with using classified information to gamble on Polymarket, allegedly making more than $400,000 by misusing privileged information. The Anti-Corruption Data Collective has found evidence of more than 150 wallets on the platform that might be up to similar activities. In 2024, in the final hours of the Biden administration, someone made $300,000 betting on the outgoing president’s last-minute pardons. The profits were then sent to a crypto wallet. These stories are, predictably, a dime a dozen. Las Vegas 2.0 in the Palm of Your Hand Platforms like Kalshi and Polymarket stylize themselves as prediction markets, but they’re not. They’re gambling sites that you can hold in the palm of your hand and on which you can bet beyond sports to just about anything — emphasis on the bet . Don’t buy into the industry propaganda that suggests these are prediction markets in which traders exchange contracts and forecast the future. Bettors on these sites place bets on events that may or may not unfold in one way or another, and those people will win or lose money based on the outcome of said events. That’s gambling, plain and simple. Labeling these platforms and actions as anything else is marketing at best. In August, a federal appeals court ruled that these sites were indeed gambling platforms. The Trump-appointed judges decided the states could regulate such “exchanges” as gaming, rejecting the claim that participants were traders exchanging contracts and arguing that “the substance of the sports event contracts offered on Kalshi’s (exchange) is sports gambling, regardless of whether Kalshi calls them swaps.” Kalshi is, of course, appealing, and the fight is far from over, especially given that the Trump administration favors a light regulatory touch combined with political boosterism of the industry. A Simple Solution to a Serious Problem Neither the president nor anyone in his immediate family should be involved in gambling platforms as an investor, adviser, or gambler. To be involved in these sites is to invite corruption or the appearance of corruption, which for the purposes of public trust — or distrust — in government and democratic institutions is the same thing. Moreover, no one in the administration, the executive or legislature, organs of state, or anyone in the immediate family of someone in such a position should be involved in these platforms in any way, and for the same reasons. This would rule a lot of people out of the market, but why shouldn’t it? There’s a better solution still. Ban these monstrosities altogether. The risks that accompany real-world-events gambling platforms are serious and go well beyond sowing institutional distrust and enabling corruption. Nizan Geslevich Packin and Sharon Rabinovitz argue that so-called prediction markets are a public health threat that foster addiction. With the risk of addiction comes all the usual deleterious outcomes, from financial ruin to crime. And it gets worse. The risk extends beyond individuals, though, to shaping the very events that bettors are gambling on in the short, medium, and long term, alongside attitudes toward their underlying issues. As these platforms style themselves as aggregators of public opinion and expectation — even as forecasters — there is a serious threat of deliberate or accidental manipulation of reality and producing bad outcomes. In the Guardian , economic historian Jamie Pietruska references this risk for climate change and bets on the future of global temperatures: “If I go online and see the perception that the world will pass 2°C (3.6°F) warming by 2050, it creates a sense of a predetermined future, even though this is an aggregation of public opinion. It’s not a truth machine.” Platforms like Polymarket and Kalshi provide no tangible good for society, but plenty of tangible bad. They have no reason to exist beyond further enriching a handful of powerful, wealthy people and leaving everyone else rolling the dice to make mortgage or rent payments in a market where, like casinos, the house always wins. In such a world, the second-best option is not to play; the best is to make sure no one can.

Tyson Shuttered a Plant and Laid Off 2,500 Workers Overnight

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Caitlyn Clark ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-06T13:46:05+00:00

Ouano Tante worked the afternoon shift at the Tyson beef plant in Joslin, Illinois, for five years. But when she and hundreds of her coworkers arrived at work on August 14, they found the plant closed. They speculated that someone on the morning shift might have gotten injured. Then two mid-level supervisors came outside in tears. Plant supervisors told workers the plant was closing permanently, effective immediately. Inside, the early-morning shift had just ended when the workers were informed it would be their last. The cafeteria erupted into chaos . Plant supervisors stood guard by the doors, workers said, while everyone received their closure paperwork. “People were crying, shaking,” Tante said. “When we were leaving the parking lot, there were so many car accidents. People were so upset.” “They gave no reason, nothing,” said Ahoro Asselissime. “People have no job. No money to pay our bills. For eleven years, I made money for them. And now they’re not doing anything for me.” Disaster for Community Joslin is home to just fifty-six residents. But 2,500 workers from Africa, Latin America, and Burma toiled at the meatpacking plant there, butchering cattle for Tyson, one of the world’s largest meatpacking companies. The workers were represented by Food and Commercial Workers (UFCW) Local 1546. Most of the workers live in the Quad Cities, clustered around the Illinois-Iowa border 160 miles west of Chicago. Everyone there knows someone who works at Tyson, the area’s ninth-largest employer. The closure announcement sent the community into a tailspin. Tante, originally from Togo, had the job of determining the age of cattle by checking the size of their teeth. An overhead conveyor would move carcasses, suspended by their ankles, along the chain. Tante checked the teeth, sprinted up a set of stairs to mark the cattle’s ankles with ink, then sprinted back down to catch the next carcass. Simultaneously, other workers drained blood from the animal by slicing the neck with knives. If Tante didn’t work fast enough or was off-pace with the other workers, she said, she could get cut by their knives. Paltry Severance The laid-off workers will be paid for thirty-six hours a week until October, but that’s far less than they had been making with overtime. “I got $500 this week,” said Asselissime. “My rent is $925. I have to put gas in my car, buy food for my kids. Everything is so expensive now.” Prior to the closure, Asselissime could expect around $900 a week. “I’ve been sounding the alarm about how Tyson treats their workers for years,” said Nana Ouro-Agoro, founder of Akwaaba, a nonprofit organization that provides support to the Quad Cities’ immigrant population. Originally from Togo, Ouro-Agoro came to the Quad Cities after winning the immigration lottery in 2008. She learned that many of her neighbors worked at Tyson, where they were frequently injured on the job and afraid to speak out. Workers told her they were being treated “worse than the cattle.” “It was hard for me to sleep at night, hearing my fellow immigrants were working hard, getting injured, and nothing was being done,” she said. Akwaaba helped Tyson workers with translation services and resources to help them assert their rights, including when Tyson abruptly fired dozens of Togolese workers as alleged retaliation for reporting injuries and sexual harassment. Now, about fiver hundred of their clients — roughly one in four — are out of work. And because so much of the local economy depends on income from Tyson workers, “we’re going to see a crisis in every sector,” Ouro-Agoro said. One Monopoly After Another It’s been a profitable few years for Tyson. So why shutter the plant? Tyson cites the nationwide cattle shortage. Cattle stock in the United States is at a seventy-five-year low. The costs of raising cattle have gone up significantly for ranchers, in part due to drought and higher prices for fertilizer and diesel, exacerbated by the war on Iran. But the crisis facing Tyson workers and cattle ranchers alike is driven by one major factor: corporate consolidation. The “Big Four” — Tyson, Cargill, JBS, and National Beef — own over 80 percent of the US beef industry. The Big Four have , according to multiple court decisions , colluded to fix prices for just about everything, from the price customers pay at the grocery store to the price cattle ranchers receive for their livestock. Yet despite the strain placed on cattle herds, US beef production in real terms has remained relatively steady , due to the heavier weight of today’s cattle compared to decades ago. Tyson isn’t the only one closing plants. JBS, the world’s largest meatpacker, closed a facility in Memphis in June, laying off two hundred. JBS also announced plans to close a 1,500-worker processing plant in Souderton, Pennsylvania, but efforts from union and political leaders pushed JBS to convert the plant to a smaller facility, saving four hundred jobs. With nearly four thousand meatpacking jobs cut just this summer, is the cattle shortage really to blame? As of the most recent fiscal quarter , Tyson’s operating income is up 8 percent, or $547 million, over last year. Tyson attributes the increase to growth in its chicken and prepared foods segments. Tyson CEO Donnie King took home nearly $35 million in total compensation last year, 781 times more than the median Tyson worker and double the average CEO-to-worker pay ratio among S&P 500 companies. While cattle shortages could be cutting into beef profits (Tyson reported a third-quarter beef operating loss of $138 million), overall the company is making money hand over fist. And as the meatpackers squeeze cattle producers, the shortage itself is a problem of the Big Four’s own making. But meatpacker consolidation is only one piece of the puzzle. The meatpackers may claim that they are also the victims of collusion — by the grocery giants. Walmart, Amazon, Kroger, and Albertsons can collude to lower prices paid to meatpackers too. Estimates place Walmart at between 20 and 25 percent ownership of the grocery industry, while Amazon (thanks to its acquisition of Whole Foods) is now the second-largest grocery retailer in the United States. Grocers are pushing down prices paid to packers, while packers are slashing prices paid to ranchers. In 1980, cattle producers took home $0.63 of every consumer beef dollar, and the packers and retailers took home a combined $0.37. As of 2021, that number is exactly flipped, according to More Perfect Union . All the while, workers and consumers get the shortest end of the stick; price collusion lines the pockets of meatpacking and grocery store executives, while depressing worker wages and inflating consumer prices. Union Pens Agreement After the Joslin plant closure, the union released the following statement : “UFCW Local 1546 has a long-standing and proud union partnership with Tyson Foods, and we were extremely disappointed in not receiving advance notice of this news. If given the opportunity, we could have sat down and found an economic solution to keep this plant operating, but sadly our voice was silenced.” Four days after the company’s closure announcement, Local 1546 President Robert O’Toole signed an agreement with Tyson, which includes severance pay of one to four weeks, depending on years of service, on top of the two months of payments. The UFCW agrees to “not to take any steps or action to. . . . interfere in any way with the orderly closing of the Plant, and the Union agrees not to support any such steps or action by others.” Many workers feel their union leadership has left them hung out to dry. “The union doesn’t care about us, Tyson doesn’t care about us,” said Asselissime. “A lot of immigrants work for this company, from Mexico, from Africa. That’s why they don’t care.” Some workers were already mobilizing independently. The day after Tyson announced the closure, laid-off workers rallied outside the plant gates with community supporters and political groups. The workers demanded Tyson make a plan to keep the plant open; if that wasn’t possible, they wanted six months of severance pay, not two. At various points in its history, the UFCW has taken up key fights against the packers. In 2007, it spearheaded a lawsuit against the Department of Homeland Security after Immigration and Customs Enforcement raids detained twelve thousand workers at six meatpacking plants. And in 2008, after a sixteen-year battle, it organized the world’s largest pork processing plant, the Smithfield plant in Tar Heel, North Carolina. But in recent years, the International union seems to have changed its tune, tying itself to the meat cartels in exchange for short-term, modest gains. Now Tyson workers say that labor-management “partnership” is swinging back at the members, with little response from union leadership. “The union hasn’t said anything, aside from telling us to go apply for unemployment,” said Tante. “I thought the union should defend us, so I was surprised they didn’t do anything. The union could have fought for us to get more severance, but they didn’t.” Protests Continue Several hundred maintenance workers who remain in the plant are reportedly dismantling equipment and preparing the plant for demolition. Meatpackers are notorious for refusing to sell plants to other packers, wanting to stifle competition. In 2018, for example, when Tyson closed a plant in Cherokee, Iowa, it refused to sell until the next owners agreed to limit the number of cattle they would slaughter there. While some workers might move and get jobs at other plants, Tyson has not outlined formal transfer rights or relocation stipends (in previous plant closures, relocation bonuses have been up to $15,000). JBS has held mass hiring events in the Quad Cities for those willing to relocate to Nebraska or Utah. Veronica Ceballos, originally from Mexico, worked at Tyson for nineteen years, commuting on rural backroads through harsh Iowa winters. Even after she injured her back working on the line, she transferred to a different position and continued working. Since her children are now adults, she says, she has the flexibility to relocate if necessary. But she worries for coworkers who don’t have that option. Several hundred Tyson workers and their families have continued organizing protests and rallies, calling on Illinois governor J. B. Pritzker to invoke eminent domain to bring the plant under state ownership and reopen its doors, and on Tyson to increase severance pay to six months. On September 3 , workers and community supporters will caravan to Springfield to march on the state capitol building at noon. “There are people who’ve worked at Tyson for twenty years, thirty years. People with health issues, people who are sick, people who can no longer work elsewhere,” Asselissime said in a speech at a rally of about one hundred workers on August 27. “We need fair severance pay, so we can have a future.”

Guinea-Bissau approves new constitution as opposition calls referendum a fraud

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Nicholas Mwangi ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-06T03:27:09+00:00

Guinea-Bissau has approved a new constitution that expands presidential powers, but the result has triggered a political dispute after opposition parties and groups rejected the referendum as illegitimate and accused the National Electoral Commission (CNE) of fabricating turnout figures. The constitutional referendum was held on August 30, less than a year after the military seized power following disputed presidential and legislative elections. According to provisional results announced by the CNE on September 1 , 70% of valid votes supported the new constitution, while 30% opposed it. Turnout was about 60%. The opposition, however, has strongly disputed those figures. The PAI-Terra Ranka and API-Cabas Garandi coalitions said the referendum was widely boycotted, claiming participation was below 10% across the country. In a statement issued after the CNE announced the results, the two opposition platforms described the referendum as a “fraud” and accused the electoral commission of presenting figures that could not withstand scrutiny. A referendum held under military rule The military seized power on November 26, 2025, in what is considered by the PAIGC a fake coup to prevent the opposition coming to power, after the result of the presidential elections were being contested. The takeover interrupted the electoral process and subsequently established a transitional administration and pledged to oversee a return to civilian rule. Opposition parties argued that the constitutional process was conducted without adequate political participation, amid restrictions on political activity and civil liberties. The PAI-Terra Ranka and API-Cabas Garandi said political parties and independent civil-society organizations were excluded from effective oversight and that independent monitoring of the vote was not possible. International reporting before the vote similarly documented opposition claims that supporters of a “No” vote were unable to campaign freely, while the military-backed authorities promoted the new constitution. Central to the referendum is a major redistribution of political power. Under Guinea-Bissau’s existing semi-presidential system, the prime minister is drawn from the parliamentary majority and plays a substantial role in running the government. The proposed constitution strengthens the president at the expense of the prime minister and parliament. The new framework allows the president to appoint and dismiss the prime minister and cabinet ministers, chair the Council of Ministers and reorganize government ministries. It also provides circumstances under which the president can dissolve parliament. Opposition sees the changes differently. They argue that concentrating more authority in the presidency risks weakening parliament, political parties and other democratic checks and balances. The concern is particularly concerning given that the constitution is being changed by authorities who themselves came to power through a military intervention. The constitutional changes also substantially alter the legislature. Parliament will be reduced from 102 seats to 65, while the number of electoral constituencies will fall from 29 to 12. The proposed constitution also introduces stricter requirements for presidential candidates, including a five-year continuous residency requirement. Such provisions would have implications for political leaders living outside the country. Opposition challenges the vote For PAI-Terra Ranka and API-Cabas Garandi, they question whether the referendum itself had sufficient democratic legitimacy. In their September 2 statement, the opposition compared the CNE’s referendum figures with those from the November 2025 elections. They noted that the CNE reported 611,583 voters in the 2025 election but claimed 572,714 people voted in the referendum, a difference of only 38,869 voters. The opposition argues that this is inconsistent with what it says was the visibly low participation at polling stations on August 30. The opposition also alleges that the referendum law was altered after its initial publication to remove a requirement that more than half of registered voters participate for the constitution to be approved. According to their statement, the original version required both a majority of “Yes” votes and participation exceeding 50% of registered voters, while a subsequent version required only a simple majority of affirmative votes. The referendum also comes only months before elections scheduled for December 6, 2026, which the transitional authorities say will mark a return to civilian rule. That timetable gives the constitutional changes immediate political importance; the rules approved in the referendum could shape the institutions and balance of power under the government that emerges from those elections. The disagreement over turnout and the credibility of the CNE’s figures is therefore likely to remain central to the political debate. The opposition have called on the international community, including ECOWAS, the African Union, the Community of Portuguese Language Countries, and the United Nations, not to validate the referendum.
