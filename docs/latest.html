The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. LA has a new Mayor. But if you now expect major changes from her, let me set you straight. The new mayor, Nithya Raman, may have a different personality, age, and separate supporters from the defeated mayor, Karen Bass, but they have much more in common than their differences. And if you think that things in Los Angeles will be substantially different under the new mayor, it is time for you to reconsider. The County is still losing 54,000 people per year and the City of Los Angeles, 5,000 to 10,000 residents per year. The missing people left the Los Angeles region for nearby cheaper areas or for other states, like Nevada or Texas. They are not coming back. Nevertheless, we are told by the “experts” that we still suffer from a housing shortage. The thousands of vacant units are supposedly not suitable for those in desperate need of housing, the homeless and overcrowded. In fact, the Los Angeles area suffers from both vacant housing and people who live in overcrowded conditions because they can’t afford a vacant apartment or house to move in to. As for new housing, the homeless and overcrowded are both priced out. Related: Over-crowded Homes in LA County and How to Help Them Why is the LA region stuck in this rut? The basic answer is that so many people are low income that they cannot afford existing, vacant housing. Landlords choose to keep their units empty rather than lowering the rent, so the homeless and overcrowded cannot afford them. It is easy to understand why the units still sit empty, but harder to fathom why so many working people cannot afford a permanent and appropriate place to live in Los Angeles, the country’s second largest city. The main explanation for why there are so many homeless and overcrowded people in the Los Angeles area, and why they cannot afford to live in an apartment or house is financial . They can’t afford to buy, and they can’t afford to rent, even though many of them hold a fulltime job. They sleep in their cars or in nonresidential structures, such as garages. They pray they don’t get caught or have any unexcepted expenses. Related: Living in Cars: LA's Hidden Homelessness Crisis Life on the margin. We don’t know how many people fall into these categories, but we know that there are over 1,000,000 people living in overcrowded conditions in LA County, as shown on this map. The bulk of them are in the South Central area, but also in Burbank, Long Beach, and Alhambra. Overcrowded housing in Los Angeles County. If LA’s new mayor, Nithia Raman, can truly tackle homelessness and overcrowded, she will have sufficiently separated herself from her predecessor, defeated Mayor Karen Bass, to warrant serious praise. Until then she will be remembered for her similarities, not differences, with her defeated opponent. So far Nithia Raman’s reputation is based on her successful campaign to become LA’s next Mayor, not for solving LA’s closely related problems of homelessness and overcrowding. If her administration can somehow solve these two citywide housing problems, then she will have something to boast about. If not, as I predict, then she can only brag about her successful campaign to unseat LA’s outgoing mayor, Karen Bass.

Nicaragua Threatens US Security

On November 3, President Trump extended for another year the national emergency concerning Nicaragua, first declared in 2018, citing what the White House continues to describe as an “unusual and extraordinary threat” to US national security and foreign policy. The scale of the “threat” Here are some key figures by which to judge the scale of the “threat” posed by Nicaragua. First, some basics. The US population is nearly 50 times Nicaragua’s and its economy is 1,400 times bigger, with Nicaragua’s national income (GDP) only 0.07% that of the US. Second, a military comparison. Nicaragua has one of the smallest defense budgets in the Americas, devoting just 0.6% of its GDP ($116 million) to its military. The US, of course, has the world’s biggest “defense” budget at $954 billion or more than 3% of its GDP (see chart), and Trump wants to make it even bigger. Nicaragua deploys around 12,000 military personnel, under 1% of its northern neighbor’s 1.3 million. Third, its military has little or no offensive capability. Its principal duties include preventing drug trafficking, protecting borders, and conducting maritime patrols. The military also responds to emergencies such as hurricanes and earthquakes and protects the nation’s vital coffee harvest, of which the US is the biggest consumer. So, the notion that Nicaragua – 1,200 miles from the south Texas border – threatens US security is clearly absurd. What, then, is the basis of the so-called “emergency” and what has Washington done to stop it in the eight years since it began? Related: Colombia Threatens Its Caribbean Neighbor: Nicaragua US campaign against Nicaragua In Trump’s first term, he signed the original executive order after the US-inspired coup attempt against Nicaragua’s then recently re-elected Sandinista government in 2018. The anticipated collapse of the government in the face of a violent uprising failed to materialize. Trump’s executive order was an integral part of a political and media campaign to ostracize Nicaragua internationally. Bodies such as the UN’s Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR) and the Organization of American States (OAS) were instrumentalized to attack Nicaragua’s human rights record, even though they had sent delegations to the country in 2018 that received evidence of assassinations and torture perpetrated by the coup-mongers. Washington started to put the squeeze on Nicaragua’s economy, cutting off access to loans from bodies such as the World Bank and the IMF, worth $500 million annually. Government officials were sanctioned, often on the basis of little or no evidence . Vital trade arrangements were threatened, including its membership in the regional free trade agreement that improves access to the US market. Media campaigns attempted to target Nicaraguan goods, such as its beef exports . Under Biden, the US Trade Representative launched a spurious investigation into Nicaragua’s allegedly unfair trade practices, such as use of child labor. Biden also launched a damaging “humanitarian parole” scheme designed to lure away skilled Nicaraguans, one reason for an increase in their migration northwards. Trump’s second administration imposed worldwide tariffs that singled out Nicaragua for higher penalties than its neighbors. Washington even attempted to damage Nicaragua’s important tourism sector by raising its travel advisory in 2018 to the same level as those for Guatemala and Honduras, where violent crime is much more prevalent. Nicaragua was refused help during the Covid pandemic. Consequently, its tourist trade recovered more slowly than in countries like Costa Rica. Visits by cruise liners, an important outlet for Nicaragua’s many artisan crafts, have never returned to previous numbers. Related: The Nicaragua Deportation Story U.S. Media Failed to Investigate Renewed executive order Despite these hostile measures, Nicaragua’s economy made a robust recovery after the damaging attempted coup of 2018 and the subsequent Covid pandemic. In 2025, Nicaragua recorded the region’s fastest GDP growth, along with low public-sector debt and high foreign reserves. Its 2027 budget forecasts higher spending but a lower deficit. S&P Global Ratings has recently upgraded Nicaragua’s credit rating. Precisely because Nicaragua successfully fended off Washington’s attacks and rebounded, the hegemon was compelled to renew its proclamation of an “extraordinary threat.” According to Trump’s executive order, Nicaragua poses three main “threats” to US security and foreign policy. First was the “violent response by the Nicaraguan government to the protests beginning on 18 April 2018.” These supposedly peaceful protests led to well over 200 deaths, including those of 22 police officers, and caused at least $1 billion of direct economic damage. Arguably, Nicaragua’s defensive actions were much more measured than Washington’s would have been if confronted with similar violence. Second, Trump’s order hypocritically cites the “dismantling and undermining of democratic institutions and the rule of law” together with “indiscriminate violence and repressive tactics against civilians.” In fact, the measures taken by the Nicaraguan government to defend ordinary people and their constitutional rights have largely responded to US-funded criminal attacks and political interference. Third, Trump calls out alleged “government corruption” contributing to “economic destabilization.” Again, this grossly hypocritical charge hardly squares with Nicaragua’s economic achievements or its massive success in improving life for ordinary Nicaraguans. They have seen poverty reduced to under 14%, along with major improvements in public health services, free education, subsidized housing and infrastructure such as water supply, electricity, highways and public transport. Related: Is Nicaragua Next? Rubio, the OAS and Washington’s Regime-Change Pressure The real threat The real strategic threat to the United States is Nicaragua’s ability to improve the lives of its citizens while maintaining a strong economy, despite Washington’s best efforts to prevent it. Trump’s renewal of the executive order signals that these threats will continue. His newly appointed chief of staff, Stephen Miller, identified Nicaragua as an exception to the realignment of countries behind Trump’s agenda for the hemisphere. He euphemistically suggested that the US could secure “a better future for the people of Nicaragua” via its regional alliance, the “Shield of the Americas.” All three of Nicaragua’s immediate neighbors are part of Trump’s “Shield.” Secretary of State Rubio has used the OAS to push Nicaragua into compliance. Although the country is no longer an OAS member, ten Latin American nations have been persuaded to participate. This arrangement could further threaten Nicaragua’s trade links, its diplomatic status and even regional air connections. The OAS is convening again on December 1 to decide how to ramp up this pressure. The ultimate hypocrisy in Trump’s executive order is, of course, that it is the US that poses an ever-present strategic threat to Nicaragua, not the other way around.

The different generations who lived through the three weeks between the first and second rounds will never forget their significance. It may have been the greatest mass educational process that Brazilian society has ever experienced. There were hundreds of demonstrations across Brazil; millions of people took to the streets. Some organized in their neighborhoods, while others wrote flyers and distributed them door-to-door. Flyers were handed out at subway stations, bus stops, and bus terminals. Young people with signs took to the streets, ready to listen to people’s grievances and discuss what was at stake. Carpooling was organized to ensure everyone could vote. Young people drove their grandmothers to the polls on Election Day. There was no shortage of creativity in this decentralized movement. Its members shared a clear objective: elect Lula and stop the return of fascism—along with everything a Flávio Bolsonaro victory would bring. History—and the people—proved generous to us. And perhaps this was our last chance in this new cycle to take a radically different course and prevent the near future from ending in tragedy. What Ballot Measures Told Us About America

The blue tidal wave that swept congressional and local elections around the country is leaving a new political landscape in its wake. The generational scope of Democratic wins and unprecedented success of democratic socialist candidates notwithstanding, the president’s party almost always loses midterm elections, often by wide margins. How much do the election results reflect a shift in society’s politics, and how much do they reflect an extreme version of the standard two-party pendulum swing? And more importantly, where are the opportunities to push organizing to new horizons in this new terrain? The results of ballot initiative votes are especially useful in analyzing the political realities on the ground. Ballot measures present a simple yes-or-no choice on a piece of legislation, making them excellent barometers of public sentiment. Between state and local ballots, Americans vote on hundreds of initiatives every cycle. Coming out of Election Day 2026, three ballot measures demand particular attention. In California, a pioneering attempt to tax billionaires’ wealth to fund the hollowed-out health care system was drowned in almost $400 million in billionaire spending. Missouri saw the first Republican attempt to overturn abortion rights that were previously won at the ballot by packaging the rollback with transphobia; it was resoundingly defeated. And a whopping three-quarters of voters in Providence approved a measure to divest from countries and companies committing severe human rights abuses, making Rhode Island’s capital the largest US city to approve divestment from Israel. Not Yet Taxing Billionaires in California The California Billionaire Tax was as novel a proposition as it was consequential. Trump’s 2025 “One Big Beautiful Bill” made major cuts to Medicaid, with major coverage restrictions scheduled to take effect after the midterms, in January 2027. The cuts are expected to leave millions without insurance and put health care facilities at risk of closure. Billionaires’ fortunes have skyrocketed by hundreds of billions of dollars more since the bill’s passage. In California, a union of hospital and health care workers put two and two together. In collaboration with leading progressive economists, the Service Employees International Union–United Healthcare Workers West (SEIU-UHW) proposed a first-of-its-kind, one-time 5 percent wealth tax on the two hundred–odd billionaires living in California. The union projected that it would raise roughly $100 billion . Ninety percent of the revenue was earmarked for health care, with the remainder for education, food assistance, and administration. Proposition 40, as it was designated once it qualified for the ballot, was written to be applicable to billionaires residing in the state on January 1, 2026 , anticipating the capital flight argument that billionaires would simply move out. With the mind-boggling amounts of money billionaires have been making in recent years, the 5 percent wealth tax would still have left California’s billionaires vastly wealthier than they were just a year ago, while the state’s health care system would avoid disaster. Seems like common sense — unless you’re a billionaire who thinks taxation is oppression. Yet the billionaires were not the only ones opposed to Proposition 40. Labor was mostly aligned behind UHW in the push, including the California Labor Federation, Teamsters California, and California Nurses Association. But it wasn’t unanimous: the California Teachers Association and the State Building and Construction Trades Council of California opposed. SEIU California remained neutral, despite Proposition 40 being proposed by one of its largest locals. Some prominent progressive advocacy groups like Planned Parenthood Affiliates of California and the NAACP California/Hawaii State Conference joined unlikely forces with the likes of Peter Thiel against the billionaire tax. The apparent split in left forces, particularly the elements of labor that opposed the billionaire tax, was prominently featured in ads from Building a Better California , a billionaire-backed organization opposing Proposition 40. Many of Building a Better California’s attacks were focused on UHW president Dave Regan , who was on the receiving end of a litany of allegations , including resurfaced claims he used intimidation tactics in political negotiations seventeen years ago — all of which Regan frames as a transparent smear campaign aimed at distracting from the issue at hand. Still, if the billionaire lobby’s goal was to sow mistrust around Proposition 40, the intra-left divisions and air of scandal around UHW certainly helped. To add insult to injury, UHW’s staff union declared a strike in the days leading up to Election Day, obstructing Proposition 40’s final get-out-the-vote push. Ultimately, neither the measure’s necessity — even now, nobody seems to have an alternative plan for when the Medicaid cuts kick in — nor its meticulous construction ended up matching the unprecedented amounts of money the billionaire lobby dumped into this election. Opponents raised at least $378 million , overwhelming the pro-tax campaign and helping swing the popular vote away from Proposition 40. The billionaire lobby also financed two poison pill measures, Propositions 41 and 42, which could have blocked Proposition 40 even if it passed, provided they received more “yes” votes. Proposition 40’s chaotic loss should not distract from its importance. The front line often doesn’t survive the battle, but in this case it opened an important new front. By organizing to put the issue directly on the ballot, UHW forced a public conversation about wealth taxes that put the idea on the political menu. Not only did Bernie Sanders and Ro Khanna wholeheartedly endorse the measure, they had already introduced legislation for an annual federal billionaire wealth tax. California governor Gavin Newsom, in his haste to kill the California billionaire tax, was forced to admit that America needs a national minimum tax on the ultrawealthy (as long as it didn’t start in his state). Proposition 40’s hard-fought loss is at once an illustration of how hard many billionaires will fight being taxed and how important it will be to tax them anyway. In September, Elon Musk reportedly authorized more than $100 million in midterm spending by his PAC, which launched transphobic ads, including in Texas, Tesla’s headquarters. That strategy failed, as targeted Democrats swept in the midterms. However, transphobic attacks also made it directly onto the ballot in multiple states, where they were largely successful. Majorities in Arizona, Colorado, Nebraska, and Washington approved restrictions on transgender students’ participation on school sports teams aligned with their gender identity. Another Colorado measure, banning gender-affirming surgeries for minors, also passed. Niche an issue as it may seem to many, the Right has found a foothold in youth sports. Nevertheless, the biggest test of how far conservatives can leverage transphobia failed in Missouri, where Republican legislators tried using it as a lever to overturn abortion rights. The victory against this tactic is instructive for how the Left can defeat transphobic scapegoating at the ballot moving forward. In 2024, Missouri residents voted to enshrine abortion rights in the state constitution. That vote, titled Amendment 3, had been proposed as a citizen initiative and won narrowly but decisively with 52 percent of the vote. It didn’t take long for Republicans in the state legislature to hatch a scheme to countermand the popular vote. The result was Amendment 3, part two. This year’s Amendment 3 was a legislatively referred repeal of the citizen-initiated Amendment 3 from 2024. It combined a rollback of abortion rights with a constitutional ban on gender-transition procedures for minors, including surgery, puberty blockers, and hormone treatment, subject to specified medical exceptions. Rather than trying to change majority opinion on abortion rights, the antiabortion campaign focused its talking points on transphobia. Voters also had to wade through the confusion. Not only had they just voted on abortion two years ago, but when a majority of Missouri voters checked “yes” on Amendment 3, they were voting to support abortion rights. Now, a “yes” vote on Amendment 3 meant the opposite. It’s not the first time Republican legislators have attempted this kind of ballot title shell game , to little effect. It didn’t work in Missouri either. On Election Day, Missouri voters decisively rejected the new Amendment 3 with 55 percent of the vote — an even higher percentage than voted for abortion rights in 2024. To cement their rebuke, voters also passed a citizen-initiated constitutional amendment to protect the ballot initiative process from legislative tampering. A contingent of the campaign headed by PROMO, a Missouri LGBTQ organization, leaned into the opportunity to move the needle on trans rights for the long term. They committed to an intensive deep canvassing program aimed at changing hearts and minds — an approach that has worked on gay marriage and trans antidiscrimination votes over the past decade and a half. Therein lies the lesson. The political Right has a fast-evaporating pool of persuasive appeals to the public. Following the Right’s trouncing in the midterm elections, conservatives can be expected to double down on anti-trans arguments as a foot in the door to a popular audience. Missouri shows that the best defense against transphobia is a good offense, both pointing out that scapegoating is designed to distract and also engaging in proactive public education to stand in unflinching solidarity against all forms of bigotry. Explicitly pro-Palestine candidates won across the country, most prominently Abdul El-Sayed, who won a US Senate seat in Michigan despite what he described as $42 million in AIPAC spending against him . These wins proved beyond a doubt that questioning US support for Israel — a nearly unthinkable stance in mainstream politics only a few years ago — calling the genocide in Gaza a genocide, and advocating for an arms embargo against Israel are no longer liabilities for elected leaders. Still, it is difficult to distinguish those who voted for El-Sayed because of his stance on Israel from those who supported him in spite of that stance. Such is the nature of two-party elections, when voters must shoehorn or project a variety of wants, needs, and hopes onto a single personality and often hold their noses about the rest. But devil’s advocates will find no solace this year, because there was one vote last Tuesday that was about Palestine and nothing else, and the results were not ambiguous. Providence, Rhode Island, is the first US city to enact a binding divestment law by popular vote, targeting investments in countries and companies engaged in severe human rights abuses like genocide, ethnic cleansing, and apartheid — a campaign explicitly focused on divesting from Israel. It won by a landslide, with 75 percent of the popular vote. The effort was led by a local coalition that included Jewish Voice for Peace; Democratic Socialists of America; Reclaim Rhode Island, a housing justice and generally progressive organization; and American Federation of Teachers Local 6516, representing graduate workers at Brown University. (You can read more about the initiative in this Jacobin piece by two organizers from the campaign.) Following the initiative win, Providence becomes the largest US city yet to approve divestment from Israel. It will join at least six other cities around the country in passing some form of divestment legislation — Hayward and Richmond in California; Dearborn, Michigan; Medford, Massachusetts; Belfast, Maine; and most recently Cambridge, Massachusetts. Providence does not have a proportionately large Muslim or Arab population, but it does have a sizable Jewish population. The overwhelming victory for divestment at the ballot cements beyond a doubt the swing happening in the American public not only away from Israel but toward decisive action against its genocidal policies. It is also worth noting, with the California Billionaire Tax in mind, that while Providence marks a triumphant success for the Palestine solidarity movement in a watershed election, it was not the first attempt to divest from Israel by direct democracy. The first two attempts , both in Pittsburgh, lost court challenges and never made it to voters. The third, in Somerville, Massachusetts, won majority support a year ago, but was a nonbinding initiative. Somerville’s City Council has committed to pursuing a binding ordinance , and an ethical procurement proposal remains before it. Sometimes it takes time for game-changing policies with deep-pocketed adversaries to succeed at the ballot. Alongside Question 7, Providence elected a mayor who endorsed the divestment initiative, twenty-eight-year-old democratic socialist David Morales, as well as a slate of allied city council members. Should Question 7 survive the court challenges that have already been threatened, it will be implemented by the new city government. But even if it is somehow beaten back in the courts, the Providence vote will echo across the country and well beyond, showing that after three grinding years of misery in Gaza and across Palestine, firm policy against genocide is not only a viable position but an asset for candidates and elected officials. American politics is changing fast. To make the most of it, it is helpful to understand how majorities vote when they get a chance to legislate key issues directly. Our moment requires taking big swings in redistribution and economic justice. At the same time, the closer those moves get to billionaires’ money, the more severe the reaction will become. Going up against those kinds of resources, setbacks are to be expected, but the worst thing we could do would be to interpret losses as undermining the necessity or viability of taxing billionaires’ wealth. As the Right’s bid for legitimacy falters, it will lean harder into scapegoating and demonizing marginalized groups. Contra antipopulist liberal fantasies, American society is not hopelessly bigoted. People can be won over to both economic and social justice, but only if movements commit to the protracted work on both fronts as part of the same political project. And policy positions that appear to be third rails, like divesting from Israel used to be, can be transformed through mass organizing and popular pressure. Two years ago, then Assemblymember Zohran Mamdani launched his campaign for mayor with a series of on-the-street interviews of Donald Trump voters on Fordham Road in the Bronx. In the course of the following year, the cost-of-living crisis solidified as the uniting theme of his mayoral campaign, anchored by a clear agenda to bring costs down and build a city where New Yorkers can “afford to dream.” Last week, a big tent of Democratic politicians used this same message to rout the Right across the country — potentially opening the door to a new kind of politics that embeds climate action within economic populist agendas. New York City is already providing a governing framework for legislators across the country seeking to translate campaign slogans into material improvements for working-class people. Through initiatives like discounted groceries at municipal grocery store pilots and a public stake in New York University’s Quantum Institute , the Mamdani administration has begun to demonstrate that the public sector can and must play a critical role in tackling the affordability crisis and meeting people’s everyday needs. In this approach, Mayor Mamdani is drawing on a rich history of public ownership in the United States catalyzed by the New Deal. But in the modern era, we can only ensure that all New Yorkers can “afford to dream” if we tackle the climate crisis — a core driver of economic instability, high costs, and the foreclosure of a stable future. A new report from Climate and Community Institute , Public Renewables Project , and Public Power NY , PowerNYC Development Corporation : The Case for a Public Renewable Developer in New York City , lays out a clear path for New York City to lead on climate action. Drawing on a New Deal–inspired approach to the energy sector, authors Jason Kowalski and Johanna Bozuwa make the case for New York City to take charge of its own energy future via a city-owned development corporation called PowerNYC. By rapidly deploying solar, battery storage, and thermal energy networks (TENs) across the five boroughs, PowerNYC would accelerate decarbonization to lower utility bills, ensure more reliable service, and phase out the fossil fuels driving the combined affordability and climate crisis. Since the New Deal era, public development has built systems to service common needs from international airports to water and sewage systems across the country. Many of these systems still stand as durable remnants of a more ambitious era of governance. PowerNYC Development Corporation applies this formula to twenty-first-century needs: it would leverage the public sector’s ability to coordinate and plan at scale to create a glide path for renewable energy projects that might otherwise languish in the limbo of private markets. Such an institution would fill a clear gap in New York City’s renewable energy landscape, delivering climate and economic benefits in the form of lowered emissions and utility savings. Overlapping Energy Crises New York City currently faces multiple overlapping energy system crises that are on track to get much worse in the coming years. First, utility rates are becoming unaffordable for New Yorkers, who are now paying 11 percent more for their electricity bills than they did just last year, largely due to the costs of fossil fuel infrastructure and volatile natural gas prices. Second, the city’s supply of energy is constrained by an outdated grid, power plants reaching the end of their lifespan, and lack of needed transmission lines to bring renewable power from upstate New York, leading to blackouts in the summer and even higher risk of service interruptions in the future. Third, the cascading effects of fossil fuel generation threaten the well-being of New Yorkers through dangerous air pollution and increasingly catastrophic weather events. To address these crises requires tackling the root cause of continued reliance on volatile fossil fuel markets and transitioning to green energy sources. To date, the state has relied chiefly on market incentives to spur renewable energy development, but this strategy has fallen well short of meeting the state’s climate targets. And New York City has fallen way behind the rest of the state, with 90 percent of downstate electricity still coming from fossil fuels rather than renewables. The technology to tackle such challenges exists, and renewable energy provides a cheap and clean alternative to polluting fossil fuels. But bringing projects themselves into fruition faces technical, bureaucratic, and cost barriers. Thousands of viable sites for clean energy projects scattered across public schools, hospitals, affordable housing, and other sites are currently underutilized due to the significant amount of time, expertise, and up-front resources required to turn a promising site into an operating clean energy project. Clean energy development is rarely a core competency of institutions or organizations with other primary purposes. While some of this market is served by private developers, navigating transaction costs, small project sizes, complicated sites, and other requirements and economic considerations often means such projects are deprioritized in the short term. The burden of such delays falls on ratepayers and the public. This summer, heat waves overwhelmed the grid and left thousands of New Yorkers without power during the hottest days of the year. With winter temperatures around the corner, heating oil costs have risen nearly 70 percent since last year. Too often, working-class households are forced to choose between keeping themselves safe or paying their utility bills — even as the climate collapses around them. We are now facing a stark choice: continue business as usual and suffer the consequences, or embrace a new approach that mobilizes the economic strength of the public sector to ensure the job gets done. As timelines for implementation of Local Law 97 (which mandated strict caps on emissions on large buildings in New York City), broader climate mandates, and escalating climate chaos loom, the city needs a public developer like PowerNYC to rapidly expand the deployment of green technology to electrify existing systems. Introducing PowerNYC Development Corporation PowerNYC would use its position as a public developer to create a financially self-sustaining engine of decarbonization for New York City. While many of the component parts and projects exist across agencies and other institutional actors, PowerNYC would consolidate the support available at various levels and provide a means to ensure renewable energy projects move from the page to implementation. One major benefit of PowerNYC would be its ability to recycle capital to create a financially self-sustaining engine of decarbonization. After initial capitalization, its ownership of revenue-generating assets would allow it to back funding for further rounds of development. Applying this model to thermal energy networks and other green technologies would leverage the power of aggregated planning to ensure the public sector can tap into the same low-cost revenue sources from which private utilities already benefit. For example, through PowerNYC, deploying battery storage across the five boroughs would allow the city to package a fleet of batteries’ worth of power to sell back to the electricity grid at peak demand times to displace the fossil power on which the city currently relies. The contract for such a deal could then be leveraged to secure further financing for ongoing development of renewable energy assets. By accessing long-term, low-cost, stable financing, public developers like PowerNYC can unlock the potential for large-scale infrastructure projects while ensuring benefits are democratically distributed. Instead of taking a project-by-project approach, it would maximize the government’s ability to coordinate at scale with the goals of multiplying affordability benefits for ratepayers and expediting urgent climate action. Research shows that deploying an initial 200 megawatts of distributed batteries alone via PowerNYC could generate New York City’s Con Edison ratepayers at least $170 million of savings annually by lowering capacity market costs and distribution costs. And publicly developed thermal energy networks, an alternative to gas pipe replacement, could create savings for National Grid customers in Brooklyn, Staten Island, and Queens who are currently paying for unnecessary gas infrastructure investments. Over time, its development mandate could expand to other clean energy and grid infrastructure needed to support decarbonization and affordability. A Green Era of Public Institution-Building No single city or even country can take on the planetary scale of the climate crisis alone, but the establishment of PowerNYC is a major opportunity for the largest city in the country to chart a new era of public institution-building to rapidly scale up climate ambitions. Cities and states across the country have already embraced the potential of green public development. Nearly a decade ago, the Connecticut Green Bank established a similar, successful model for solar that has driven the state to the top of the national list for its percentage of public schools with on-site solar projects. Countries from Costa Rica to Denmark are using strong networks of public institutions to accelerate the planning and development of public power. Public development is a proven method for bringing public goods to fruition. PowerNYC would use it to accelerate the deployment of green energy systems and begin to fix our toxic, cost-burdening energy system. Capitalism Is More Than a Legal Regime

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: E. Ahmet Tonak ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-11T14:24:10+00:00

“Capitalism is a legal regime, not just an economic system as conventional wisdom has it.” With this opening sentence, Katharina Pistor takes the argument of her previous book , The Code of Capital , a step further. There she showed how lawyers turn assets into capital through property, contract, collateral, trust, corporate, and bankruptcy law. Her new book extends the argument to capitalism as a whole. If law so thoroughly constitutes capitalism, she asks, why is it “so resistant to legal governance”? And how might we transform it? Pistor has much to say about the legal powers of capital, and her account deserves serious attention. Yet I find two fundamental problems with her argument. Although she insists that capitalism is legal to the core, she has remarkably little to say about the wage relation and the extraction of surplus labor in the production process. Without these, we can understand neither capitalist production nor its capacity to reproduce itself. This omission carries over into her proposals for transforming capitalism. Her project of democratizing private law leaves the basic relations of production intact. The result is a reform program that may constrain some of capital’s powers but offers little prospect of overcoming capitalist exploitation. The second, related problem concerns the economic foundations of her argument. Pistor describes her approach as “imbued with a strong dose of political economy.” Yet she makes claims about the redistributive achievements of the welfare state and the sources of capitalist profit without engaging with a substantial body of Marxian theoretical and empirical research that bears directly on both questions. At the heart of Pistor’s account is a definition of capital by its tradability. For her, capital is any asset that law has coded so that it can be “mortgaged or sold for profit.” Without such coding, she writes, ordinary assets “will not produce wealth on the scale that legally coded capital assets can.” Three things follow. Capital becomes an asset that law codes for trade at a profit. Capitalists become “traders in any asset that promises financial returns,” and capitalist ownership becomes a matter of “maximizing pecuniary gain from an asset.” Capitalism thus becomes the legal regime that creates such assets and gives their holders privileged access to the coercive power of the state through private law. Conspicuously absent from this triad is the capitalist as employer of wage labor, a point to which I return below. Drawing on Evgeny Pashukanis, Pistor locates a defining feature of capitalist law in the separation of private and public law. Each rest on a different normative foundation. Public law concerns constitutions, democratic self-government, and limits on state power; private law, by contrast, revolves around individual autonomy and freedom of contract. Yet private rights, no less than public ones, depend on the coercive power of the state for their enforcement. This arrangement enables capital to exercise considerable power through private law while escaping many of the constraints that public law imposes. Pistor develops this argument through three mechanisms. The first is legal empowerment: property, contract, and the corporate form confer powers on private actors while limiting their accountability. The second is access to legal coercion, through which private parties can call on the state to enforce their claims. The third is legal arbitrage: capital can move between jurisdictions and choose favorable legal arrangements, whereas workers and other individuals face the restrictions that immigration and nationality laws impose. In Pistor’s account, these mechanisms also help explain why earlier reforms proved so vulnerable. The New Deal introduced substantial changes in finance, housing, labor relations, and social security, but “did not alter private law and its normative foundations.” Capital subsequently found ways around these reforms, including the outsourcing of labor and the creation of new financial instruments. Pistor therefore argues that regulation alone cannot do the job; we must rewire private law itself and place it on a different normative foundation. Production and Exploitation My principal difficulty with the book concerns its treatment of production, or rather its failure to examine it. In the opening paragraph, Pistor lists several phenomena that Karl Marx and Joseph Schumpeter regarded as economic: trade, labor relations, private ownership of the means of production, corporations, banking, and money. She then declares that “these matters are legal to the core.” Given this claim, one might reasonably expect an examination of the legal relations that govern capitalist production, particularly the employment contract. Surprisingly, there is almost none of it. The terms “surplus value,” “surplus labor,” and “labor power” do not appear in the book, and the index contains no entry for “exploitation.” Production enters the discussion mainly through the ownership of the means of production, with little attention to what happens when workers put those means to use in the production process. Pistor does refer to labor exploitation on three occasions, but none of them leads to a sustained discussion. She notes that recently some Marxist scholars have questioned “the exploitation of labor as the source of surplus that capital extracts.” But she reports this disagreement without a reference, without saying who these scholars are, and without stating her own position. In a related passage, she appears to endorse the view that, “as Marx and [Friedrich] Engels argued, owning the means of production allows capitalists to extract surplus from labor.” That idea, she adds, “economic models have more recently confirmed.” These “economic models,” however, turn out to be a single model , that of Oliver Hart and John Moore, which is a far cry from confirming Marx’s theory of exploitation. For these authors, “surplus” refers to the gains from trade that relationship-specific investment generates, which the parties then divide through bargaining; it has nothing to do with Marx’s surplus value. Pistor also observes that one-sided contracts have long characterized labor relations, and that this “inspired Marx’s analysis of the exploitation of labor through capital.” She recognizes, moreover, that ownership of the means of production gives capital “the power to set the terms for the contractual relation with labor.” This could have been the starting point for an analysis of capitalist exploitation, but she does not pursue the subject further. The discussion turns instead to unionization, the National Labor Relations Act, and gig workers, largely in terms of bargaining power, before moving on to social media contracts. Finally, she remarks that “labor exploitation has never been recognized as grounds for asylum.” Here “exploitation” refers to abusive working conditions rather than to the specific social relation that Marx analyzed. This neglect of exploitation matters for Pistor’s own argument, because the wage contract is surely among the most consequential legal relations in capitalist society. Workers and capitalists enter into it as formally free and equal parties, yet the contract gives the capitalist command over the use of labor power for a specified period. The worker’s separation from the means of production is what makes this arrangement possible and, for most wage earners, necessary. Marx’s analysis of the sale and purchase of labor power is particularly relevant here. In the sphere of circulation, the exchange happens between equals. Exploitation takes place in production, where the use of a unique commodity, labor power, creates more value than its own reproduction requires. The wage contract provides the legal form of this exploitative relation. Pistor’s engagement with Marx follows a consistent pattern. She either sets aside central elements of his materialist analysis or reworks his categories into new ones. The first move shows in the weight she gives to power, intangible property, and data ownership. These largely take the place of the material constraints that, for Marx, the economic structure imposes on social and legal relations. The second move is clearest in her claim that law is “a productive force in its own right, because it is the means that allows holders of assets to extract their pecuniary value.” Pistor knows that she is departing from historical materialism here, and she says so against G. A. Cohen and Jeremy Kessler . The difficulty is her criterion. In Marx’s framework, the capacity to extract value does not make something a productive force. That capacity belongs to the relations of production, the social relations that organize production and determine who appropriates its results. She calls law a productive force, but extracting and appropriating value is precisely what relations of production do. Law adds nothing to a society’s capacity to produce; it organizes who controls production and who claims its results. By her own criterion, then, law belongs among the relations of production, not the forces. Profit on Alienation and the Legal Appropriation of Wealth One Marxian concept could help clarify much of what Pistor describes: profit on alienation, or profit upon alienation, a term Marx took over from James Steuart. It refers to profit that arises from transfers and unequal exchange rather than from the production of surplus value. Anwar Shaikh discussed profit on alienation as a secondary but significant source of profit. Shaikh and I subsequently used the category in our book Measuring the Wealth of Nations , and Shaikh reiterated its significance in his t reatment of the transformation problem. Costas Lapavitsas drew on a related conception in Profiting Without Producing to analyze the profits banks extract from workers’ income. In a recent study , Alper Duman and I examined profit on alienation in Turkey between 2010 and 2019, focusing on real estate and finance. The first case involved the privatization of public land. When the state transfers land to a private developer at less than its market value, the developer gains the difference as profit, although the transaction creates no new wealth; public wealth simply passes into private hands. The second case concerned bank lending to working-class households, which pay the interest out of wages rather than out of surplus value from production. We estimated profit on alienation over the decade at $9.31 billion in real estate and $57.23 billion in finance. These examples are closely related to the processes Pistor investigates. The enclosure of land, data, and knowledge; the privatization of public assets; and the socialization of financial losses all involve legal arrangements that enable private actors to appropriate existing wealth. The law is an indispensable participant in these processes, and Pistor is right to insist on its importance. An account of how assets acquire legal protection and become sources of private profit does not, however, by itself tell us where that profit comes from. It may come from the production of new value or from the reappropriation of existing wealth through unequal exchange. We can understand much of what Pistor calls the legal coding of capital as the legal organization and enforcement of profit on alienation. This is especially apparent in privatization and in financial claims on household income. The distinction between surplus value and profit on alienation would allow Pistor to address a question that remains unresolved in her account. So would the related distinction between productive and unproductive activities, which Shaikh and I developed, and which I have extended to the digital sector . Under what circumstances does the legal coding of assets facilitate the production of new wealth, and when does it merely enable the transfer or reappropriation of wealth that already exists? This question is especially relevant to The Code of Capital , whose subtitle promises to explain “how the law creates wealth and inequality.” Pistor provides a compelling account of the legal construction of claims to wealth. But the distinction between the production and the redistribution of value is essential if we are to understand the economic significance of those claims. This also brings us back to the absence of the labor process. If the law helps constitute capitalist social relations, the employment contract and the property relations underlying it ought to occupy a central place in the analysis. Without them, we learn a great deal about how capital acquires, exercises, and protects its legal powers, but very little about the legal conditions under which workers produce surplus value. The Welfare State and the Net Social Wage Pistor’s case for rewiring private law also rests on a particular understanding of the welfare state. Countries have “experimented with social welfarism,” she writes, but the success of this reform agenda “has often been only temporary.” The New Deal, she argues, “aimed to restore the balance between the working class and the upper class.” Her explanation for the eventual erosion of the welfare state’s achievements emphasizes capital’s capacity to circumvent public regulation through private law. This account takes for granted something that deserves closer examination: How much did the welfare state actually redistribute from capital to labor? For several decades, Marxian researchers have investigated this question using the concept of the net tax and, later, that of the net social wage . They define the latter as the government expenditures on social services that workers receive minus the taxes they pay. The results are much more nuanced than the conventional picture of the welfare state as a sustained transfer of income from capital to labor. Shaikh and I studied the United States and found that, between 1952 and 1997, the net social wage seldom moved outside a range of ±4 percent of employee compensation. It averaged only 0.6 percent, effectively zero. During the postwar expansion of 1952–1969, it was negative: workers paid more in taxes than they received in social expenditures. The Ronald Reagan–George H. W. Bush period, which we described as a “counterattack by capital and the state,” pushed it down again. Our conclusion was that, by and large, “it is the taxes of the working population that essentially pay for the corresponding state expenditures.” Shaikh’s subsequent multicountry study reached a similar conclusion for countries in the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD): “In effect, workers paid for their own social benefits.” Katherine Moos extended the US estimates through 2012 using two methods. On the narrower measure, the net social wage averaged 1.3 percent of GDP; on the more comprehensive measure, which attributes a larger share of taxation to labor, it averaged -3.8 percent. She also identified a shift after 2001, when the net social wage turned positive for more than a decade. Moos did not, however, interpret this development as evidence of renewed redistribution from capital to labor. She linked it instead to mass unemployment and underemployment, rising health care costs, and the expansion of wage subsidies such as the Earned Income Tax Credit. As she argues, “subsidies to labor are also subsidies to capital.” Public expenditures, which governments often finance through deficits, increasingly make up for wages and benefits that employers no longer provide. In this context, a positive net social wage can be “evidence of the decline in conditions for the working class, not improvement.” Yakup Karabacak and I found a different pattern in Turkey from 1980 to 2019: the net social wage was negative in all but five years and averaged -1.13 percent of GDP. Turkish workers, in other words, paid more in taxes than they received in social benefits. These results bear directly on Pistor’s explanation of the welfare state’s limitations. Her argument implies that public law reforms initially restrained capital, but that capital later undermined them because the foundations of private law remained unchanged. Taxation and social security are themselves legal institutions, and public law largely constitutes them. The net social wage studies suggest that these institutions have often redistributed income within the working population rather than producing a substantial net transfer from capital to labor. More recently, some forms of social expenditure have effectively subsidized employers by compensating for inadequate wages and benefits. This does not mean that welfare state programs were unimportant to workers or that they brought no improvement in living standards. Public health care, education, pensions, and unemployment protection can have enormous social value; however, governments finance them. But we should not confuse their existence with a net redistribution from capital to labor. Pistor’s account of the welfare state would be considerably stronger if it engaged with this evidence. Public law was not necessarily the counterweight to capital that her argument seems to assume; it has also helped reproduce capitalist relations, including through the taxation of wages and the public subsidization of labor costs. Capital’s ability to escape into private law therefore cannot by itself explain the failure of welfare state reform. For a book whose central concern is the difficulty of governing capitalism through law, engagement with this literature would have provided a direct test of its central claim. Reform and the Question of Political Agency The problem with Pistor’s program is not simply that her proposals are modest. Reforms can matter, and there is no reason to dismiss them because they fall short of abolishing capitalism. What is difficult to understand is why Pistor expects reforms of this kind to transform a system whose underlying relations of production would remain unchanged. Consider the place of workers in her program. They appear as possible beneficiaries of greater participatory rights and as weaker parties who might gain access to public courts if restrictive arbitration clauses no longer stood in the way. Both changes could be worthwhile, but they leave the employment relation itself largely untouched. Nor does Pistor propose changing the ownership of the means of production, which, as she herself acknowledges, gives capital the power to set the terms of labor contracts. Martha Nussbaum’s “capability of being able to work as a human,” which Pistor draws on, comes closest to addressing the conditions of work, but it does not confront the social relation through which capital appropriates surplus labor. There is a further difficulty. Pistor says remarkably little about the social and political forces that might bring about the changes she advocates. Pistor recognizes the rise of “populist” discontent, but she treats it primarily as a danger to channel before it becomes “difficult to control.” She does not seriously consider whether the conflicts that give rise to such discontent might also provide a basis for collective political action. One might object that a book about law need not offer a comprehensive theory of political organization. But once Pistor moves from explaining capitalism’s legal structure to proposing its transformation, the question of agency becomes unavoidable. Through what organizations and struggles could anyone achieve and defend the proposed changes? Treating law as constitutive of capitalist relations is not the problem (what is objectionable, as I argued above, is treating it as a productive force); indeed, Marx’s analysis of the wage contract provides ample grounds for doing so. But accepting the material significance of law does not settle the question of which social relations to transform. Pistor’s stated aim is to “chart a path beyond capitalism,” yet the economy she envisages is a “genuine market economy” without capital’s legal privileges. If the objective is to move beyond capitalism, a serious program cannot leave out the legal relations governing wage labor and the ownership of the means of production. Transformation Without Transformation The neglect of production and of collective agency also shapes Pistor’s strategy for transforming capitalism. She explicitly rejects revolution: “Traditionally, Marxists have called for revolution, a violent overthrowing of the ruling class. This has never happened in advanced capitalist countries, seems unlikely, and is also undesirable.” Her preferred approach draws on Nicos Poulantzas’s image of “walking on a tightrope,” a gradual process of change within existing institutions. She mentions Andre Gunder Frank’s call for a “revolutionary Marxist strategy” only in passing, observing that “Obviously, this has not happened.” For Pistor, the existing legal system “cannot simply be undone”; instead, “law needs to be rewired from within.” She returns to this position in the epilogue: “The transformation of the existing system will not happen in a single leap. Rather, transformation happens as the cumulative effect of a myriad of smaller steps.” This preference for incremental “rewiring” rests partly on the claim that revolution has never occurred in advanced capitalist countries. Pistor does not analyze revolutions elsewhere — in Russia, China, Cuba, and Vietnam, and in the anti-colonial struggles — as revolutions; they appear only as “disappointing” outcomes or as instances of convergence on capitalism. Moreover, her own examples of law’s capacity for transformation, from feudalism to republicanism and from colonialism to a new international order, include several revolutionary ruptures. This sits uneasily with her claim that history has never moved “in a single leap.” Pistor’s proposal to “democratize private law” rests on a theory of the state that is more sophisticated than the strategy she builds on it. She rejects both the liberal picture of the state as a neutral arbiter and the classical Marxist picture of it as an instrument of the ruling class. Following Poulantzas, she treats the state as a “condensation of the relations of forces,” a terrain of struggle that private law has caught “in its own trap.” She concludes that “calling for the state to free society from the excesses of capitalism is therefore misguided.” However, her proposed transition still runs almost entirely through the state’s institutions. A binding “Just Private Law” code would require action by legislatures or a revision of the EU treaties. The precautionary principle, an expanded duty of care, and a broader doctrine of veil-piercing would depend on judges willing to apply them. Ending the banks’ money franchise, she admits, “rests in public hands”: legislatures, central banks, and finance ministries, the very bodies her own analysis shows to be most exposed to the influence of finance. Capital has shaped each of these tools, and it has long influenced the processes that staff them. Her own evidence shows these institutions reworking or reversing reforms, from the New Deal to the civil procedure reforms of 1938. It is therefore unclear why a democratized private law would escape the same fate. Pistor’s selective use of Poulantzas makes the problem worse. She takes over his relational conception of the state and, as we have seen, his tightrope image of gradual transformation. But she leaves aside what, in Poulantzas’s own framework, made any change from within the state conceivable at all: an organized social force outside it, capable of shifting the balance of forces that the state condenses. One need not accept Poulantzas’s strategic conclusions to see the inconsistency. If the state is a condensation of class forces, then a reform program that names no force able to change that condensation has no road to realization, even on its own terms. Pistor offers no such force. No class, party, trade union, or movement appears as the agent that would push a reformed private law through captured legislatures and courts and defend it afterward. Instead, she relies on deliberation, on crises as moments of decision, on judges, legislatures, and citizen tribunals, and on the hope that small steps will accumulate. When she anticipates the charge of naïveté, she answers that law is itself part of the raw material of capital, so normative change can act as a material lever. This shows that law can be a lever, but not one that anyone could pull in a state whose topography capital has shaped. Without a theory of collective agency, her proposal risks being less realistic than the socialist alternatives she sets aside, not more. If we go beyond the limits Poulantzas imposed on his own approach, the main way out lies in a struggle by that collective agent to abolish the capitalist (or bourgeois) form of the state. That form of the state allows the private law system to function as a source of profit when capitalism finds itself in great crises. In its place, the struggle would establish a public law order that takes care of the collective interest of the whole society: socialism. An Analysis Without a Strategy Pistor has written an important book about the legal organization of capitalist power. Her insistence that law is constitutive of capitalism challenges the tendency of mainstream economics to treat legal institutions as an external framework within which economic activity takes place. Her account of the dismantling of New Deal reforms persuasively shows how private law arrangements enabled capital to circumvent public regulation. The net social wage evidence suggests, however, that we need to assess the redistributive achievements of those reforms more critically. Her discussion of central banks and the socialization of financial costs since 2008 is also valuable. Her observation that contracts are “only rarely expressions of bargains” and more often “instruments of control” is especially striking. This insight could provide the basis for a much richer examination of the employment relation. My disagreements concern the economic foundations of Pistor’s analysis (production, profit on alienation, and the net social wage) and the political conclusions she draws from them. These gaps matter for her proposed reforms. The democratization of private law may restrain corporate power and strengthen the hand of weaker parties, and these are worthwhile objectives. But wage labor and capitalist ownership of the means of production would remain in place, and Pistor barely discusses the political forces capable of bringing about even the reforms she proposes. I therefore find The Law of Capitalism more convincing as an analysis of the legal powers of capital than as an account of how we might transform capitalism. Pistor has considerably enriched our understanding of the former. A theory of the latter would need to address the production and appropriation of surplus value, the role of the state in reproducing class relations, and the social struggles through which working people might change those relations. Brian Karem has spent much of his professional life asking questions that powerful people would rather not answer. He's questioned presidents, challenged government officials, gone to jail to protect a confidential source, and taken Donald Trump to court over his White House press credentials. But when I recently sat down with Karem for what was supposed to be an informal conversation, I was interested in something beyond his reputation as a combative journalist. I wanted to know what made him that way. More specifically, I wanted to understand whether his experiences had shaped his views about race, power, and the institutions that govern American life. At LA Progressive, we have long maintained that America's racial hierarchy is not some peripheral problem that can be addressed after we solve our economic and political challenges. It is central to understanding why American democracy has never fully delivered on its promises. Karem's personal history offered an unexpected starting point for that discussion. Growing Up Different in the American South Karem was born in Louisville, Kentucky, into a family with Lebanese roots on his father's side. Growing up in the South, he experienced the hostility directed at people who didn't fit neatly into America's racial categories. He recalled being subjected to anti-Arab slurs and learning early that prejudice was not an abstraction. But his family's response to racial injustice may have been even more influential than the discrimination he experienced. His grandfather, a Kentucky judge, knew Muhammad Ali and had a personal relationship with the legendary boxer. Karem told me that his family helped Ali navigate conflicts with the people controlling his early boxing career. He also recalled a revealing confrontation between his grandfather and a neighbor who insisted on calling Ali by his former name, Cassius Clay. His grandfather refused to tolerate it. As Karem remembered the exchange, his grandfather insisted that every person had the right to determine what they would be called. Anyone unwilling to respect Ali's chosen name was no longer welcome in his home. It was a small but consequential lesson about dignity, self-determination, and the importance of refusing to participate in another person's degradation. Karem also described meeting Ali as a nine-year-old participating in a Louisville boxing program. Ali visited the young boxers, and Karem had an opportunity to shadowbox with the heavyweight champion. The encounter left a lasting impression. Karem grew up in the same city that produced both Ali and journalist Hunter S. Thompson. Looking back, he sees those two figures, along with his parents, as important influences on his willingness to challenge authority. Ali demonstrated that one could stand up to powerful institutions without surrendering one's identity. Thompson showed that journalism could be irreverent, confrontational, and unwilling to accept official narratives. Those influences helped shape Karem's understanding of his profession. The Journalist's Job Is Not to Make Presidents Comfortable Karem's first experience questioning a president came during George H. W. Bush's administration. He told me that asking that question cost him his job. His confrontations with Donald Trump produced a different outcome: litigation over his press credentials. Karem takes considerable satisfaction in having prevailed in court. He describes his record against Trump as three victories and no defeats. But he rejects the notion that challenging a president constitutes rudeness. He considers it the fundamental responsibility of a journalist. Early in his White House career, Karem encountered two legendary reporters, Helen Thomas and Sam Donaldson. Thomas impressed upon him the importance of asking questions even when officials refuse to provide meaningful answers. Simply putting an issue before the president establishes that the question has been raised. Donaldson encouraged him to learn from the experience of the veteran reporters surrounding him. Together, they helped shape a philosophy Karem has carried throughout his career. The White House press secretary's responsibility, he explained, is to present the administration in the most favorable light possible. The journalist's responsibility is entirely different. It is to hold government accountable to the people. Karem also offered a definition of journalism that deserves serious consideration: good journalism, he said, is the application of the scientific method to mass communication. Journalists will make mistakes. They will sometimes get facts wrong. But the obligation is to investigate, correct errors, and continue pursuing verifiable information. For Karem, public officials are not our rulers. They are our employees. And employees must answer to the people who pay their salaries. Race, Class, and the Politics of Division Our conversation eventually returned to the subject that had prompted much of my interest in interviewing him. Race. Karem readily acknowledged the destructive influence of racism and misogyny in American political life. But he situated both within a larger economic struggle. His argument was that the wealthy benefit when working people are divided against one another. Poor white Americans, he observed, have far more material interests in common with poor Black Americans than with the wealthy people who dominate the economy. Yet racial hostility and other divisions can prevent people from recognizing those shared interests. It was a familiar argument, but one that raised an important question for me. Does economic inequality explain racial hierarchy, or has racial hierarchy been one of the principal mechanisms through which economic inequality has been created and sustained? The distinction matters. America's history of slavery, segregation, exclusion from property ownership, discriminatory lending, and unequal access to political power cannot be understood simply as unfortunate byproducts of class inequality. Race has helped determine who could accumulate wealth, whose labor could be exploited, whose rights could be violated, and whose suffering could be disregarded. Karem and I did not fully resolve that question. But his recognition that racial and economic divisions are deeply intertwined provided common ground for a broader discussion about democracy. Protest Is Not Enough Karem was particularly animated when discussing political participation. He has covered demonstrations involving thousands of people, including the No Kings protests. He described people traveling long distances, making signs, and devoting entire days to expressing their opposition to government policies. Yet he has encountered protesters who do not vote. That contradiction frustrates him. He believes demonstrations matter, but they must be accompanied by sustained political participation. Voting, organizing, helping others reach polling places, and even running for office are among the tools Americans have available to exercise political power. I agreed with the larger point. LA Progressive exists in part because we believe political outrage must be connected to understanding, organization, and action. It is not enough to identify what is wrong with the system. We must also develop alternatives and work toward implementing them. Karem emphasized that Americans too often forget the degree of power they possess collectively. He worries that political alienation has become so pronounced that some people are willing to entertain authoritarian alternatives when democratic institutions fail to deliver. His answer is not to abandon democracy, but to make it more responsive and accessible. He also believes schools must restore serious attention to civics and the social sciences. That brought us naturally to the institution that has occupied most of his professional life. Journalism. The Billionaire Problem in American Media When I asked Karem about his book, Free the Press: The Death of American Journalism and How to Revive It , his answer was immediate. Break up the media monopolies. Karem believes concentrated corporate ownership is among the principal reasons American journalism has deteriorated. He recalled entering a profession in which experienced reporters learned their craft over many years, often beginning in local newsrooms before moving into national reporting. Today, he argued, major media companies frequently prioritize lower labor costs, audience metrics, and access to powerful officials over the experience and independence required for serious reporting. He was especially critical of journalists who view proximity to the president as a professional achievement rather than a potential threat to their independence. He described some White House reporters as functioning like stenographers or public relations representatives rather than independent investigators. Karem also challenged the obsession with viral moments and performative confrontation. A provocative question designed to generate attention is not necessarily a question designed to produce useful information. His criticism extended beyond traditional news organizations to the technology companies that control digital distribution. Independent publishers may own their publications, but they remain dependent on a relatively small number of platforms to reach audiences. That concentration of power creates another form of vulnerability. Karem proposed several reforms: stronger limits on media ownership, tax incentives for independent publishers, low-interest financing, support for hiring local reporters, and renewed public-interest obligations for broadcasters. He also advocated restoring the Fairness Doctrine, which once imposed certain obligations on broadcasters to address controversial public issues. These proposals would require substantial policy debate. But his central argument was unmistakable. Journalism cannot become genuinely independent if its economic survival remains controlled by a handful of powerful corporations. For those of us operating independent publications, that argument is more than theoretical. It describes the environment in which we work every day. Facts, Truth, and the Responsibility of Journalism One of Karem's most interesting observations concerned the distinction between facts and truth. He does not believe journalists should appoint themselves arbiters of everyone's ultimate truth. Instead, he believes their responsibility is to provide carefully verified factual information from which people can form their own conclusions. That distinction struck me as particularly relevant to the present media environment. Political debate increasingly takes place among people who do not share a common factual foundation. Competing interpretations of events are inevitable in a democracy. But democratic deliberation becomes nearly impossible when citizens cannot agree on what actually happened. Karem sees propaganda, concentrated media ownership, and the erosion of professional standards as major contributors to that problem. He also connects the current media environment to political and regulatory decisions made over several decades, particularly during the Reagan era. Our conversation touched on the Powell memorandum, the conservative political realignment of the late twentieth century, and the dismantling of regulatory structures that once placed greater limits on media concentration. Karem sees these developments as interconnected. His larger concern is that the public has lost access to the independent reporting necessary to understand who exercises power and how that power is used. A Documentary and a Warning About Democracy Karem is also promoting a documentary, The First 100 Days , examining the early period of Trump's second administration and the political response that followed. He told me the film explores the administration's actions, their consequences for civil liberties, and the public dissatisfaction that contributed to the 2026 midterm election results. He was particularly concerned about the need for congressional oversight. But he also warned against treating the election as a permanent political realignment that requires no further work. Voters may have delivered a rebuke to the administration, he argued, but elected officials must respond to the underlying grievances that motivated them. Otherwise, the same frustration could eventually turn against the politicians who benefited from it. He repeatedly returned to the need to distinguish accountability from political retaliation. As he put it, "The divisiveness has to stop. The call for justice should never end." That distinction deserves attention. Accountability should not depend on which political party holds power. It should depend on whether public officials have fulfilled their obligations and respected the rights of the people they serve. What Muhammad Ali, Helen Thomas, and Brian Karem Have in Common As our conversation drew to a close, I found myself thinking about the people Karem had identified as influences on his life. Muhammad Ali refused to surrender his identity to satisfy the expectations of others. Helen Thomas insisted that journalists ask questions even when the powerful would prefer silence. Sam Donaldson demonstrated that reporters must maintain their independence from the officials they cover. Karem has attempted to carry those lessons into his own work. But perhaps the most revealing part of our conversation was his insistence that journalism belongs to the public. It is not the private possession of corporate executives, wealthy investors, or government officials. Its democratic purpose is to provide the information people need to govern themselves. That mission is particularly important for communities whose experiences have historically been excluded, minimized, or distorted by dominant institutions. If journalism is to fulfill its promise, it must do more than challenge presidents. It must examine the racial and economic hierarchies that determine whose voices are heard, whose experiences are believed, and whose interests are protected. Karem's career demonstrates one form of resistance to institutional power. The challenge for independent journalism is to turn that resistance into something durable: a media system capable of informing the public, exposing abuses, and helping communities exercise meaningful democratic power. Near the end of our conversation, Karem expressed his support for independent publications like LA Progressive. He urged audiences to support local journalists and community-based news organizations, describing community journalism as the backbone of the profession. His message was simple. If we want journalism to survive, we must support the people and institutions doing the work. And if we want democracy to survive, we must ensure that journalism remains capable of holding power accountable. That is a mission LA Progressive can recognize. Yet another catastrophic winter in Gaza

For the fourth consecutive year, and more than a year after the US-brokered ceasefire deal was reached, Palestinian people in Gaza continue to suffer from a severe humanitarian crisis caused by extreme weather conditions amid ongoing Israeli assaults and restrictions. On Sunday, November 8, a steep sand dune collapsed onto tents sheltering displaced people in Deir al-Balah in the central Gaza Strip, killing five people , including four children and a woman. A number of other injured people were rescued from the site, according to media reports. A report issued by the United Nations International Children’s Emergency Fund (UNICEF) on Monday, November 9, revealed that the catastrophic weather conditions have affected over 106,000 people in the besieged enclave. Seasonal storms have flooded at least 70,000 tents, while 22,000 others collapsed completely, as Israel continues to restrict and delay the entry of essential shelter supplies, heating equipment, repair machinery, and maintenance parts to the war-ravaged territory. Heavy rainfall also overwhelmed Gaza’s already damaged water and sewage infrastructure, resulting in widespread flooding of raw sewage across displacement camps. Moreover, the sudden drop in overnight temperatures below 10°C (50°F) triggers life-threatening risk, particularly for children and sick people, especially with the shortage of winter clothes, blankets, and heating fuel. International organizations hold Israel accountable for exacerbating the impact of severe weather conditions The Euro-Med Human Rights Monitor accused the Israeli government of subjecting “Palestinians’ right to shelter and recovery to Israeli electoral calculations, particularly amid reports that measures to reconstruct Gaza have been postponed until after the elections.” “Electoral interests and political or military conditions cannot justify suspending civilian protection or depriving people of life’s necessities,” the Geneva-based organization noted. It also blamed international measures for falling short “to stop these practices and hold those responsible accountable,” which the organization said “has allowed Israel to continue using such deprivation as leverage, without facing a deterrent cost.” Meanwhile, Regional Director for the Middle East, North Africa and Eastern Europe at Save the Children International, Ahmad Alhendawi, criticized the US-brokered ceasefire for failing “to protect Palestinian children.” “This is not simply the result of bad weather. Tents, shelter kits, and fuel are waiting at Gaza’s borders while children sleep in floodwater, because the Israeli authorities are blocking their entry. This is a choice, and children are paying the price. More than a year into the so-called ceasefire, it has failed to protect Palestinian children,” Alhendawi stated.

La crisis financiera y política en Paraguay: una muestra más del fracaso del modelo económico liberal

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Thania Saucedo ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-11T01:39:13+00:00

Read in English here Paraguay atraviesa una de las crisis políticas y financieras más importantes de su historia reciente. La intervención de Ueno Bank por parte del Banco Central del Paraguay, luego de detectarse graves irregularidades en sus operaciones y problemas de solvencia, desató una crisis que rápidamente dejó de ser solamente bancaria. Hoy tiene consecuencias directas sobre el Gobierno de Santiago Peña y abrió un proceso de juicio político que pone en cuestión la continuidad del propio presidente de la República. Para entender lo que está pasando es necesario explicar algunos elementos. Ueno Bank experimentó un crecimiento exponencial en los últimos años, coincidiendo con el inicio del Gobierno de Santiago Peña. Llegó a tener cerca de tres millones de clientes, más del 50% de la población adulta del país, y recibió alrededor de 800 millones de dólares en depósitos del sector público. Entre esos recursos se encuentran fondos vinculados al Instituto de Previsión Social (IPS), que administra los aportes jubilatorios y de salud de miles de trabajadoras y trabajadores. Uno de los problemas es que el propio presidente de la República mantuvo vínculos empresariales con el grupo relacionado con la entidad bancaria hasta marzo de 2025. A esto se suma que Carlos Carvallo, quien había presidido Financiera Ueno, pasó posteriormente a dirigir el Banco Central, organismo responsable de supervisar y controlar al sistema financiero. Durante este Gobierno también se modificaron las disposiciones que buscaban impedir este tipo de conflictos de intereses, conocidas como normativa de puerta giratoria. La intervención del banco, la inmovilización de los depósitos y las investigaciones sobre créditos concedidos a empresas vinculadas a sus propietarios terminaron exponiendo una trama de relaciones entre intereses privados y las instituciones públicas. Sin embargo, además, detrás de las cifras y de las operaciones financieras, hay miles de personas que hoy no pueden disponer de sus ahorros; trabajadores que enfrentan incertidumbre sobre sus salarios y jubilados cuyos recursos están comprometidos. La inclusión financiera, que se presentó como uno de los grandes logros del modelo económico, terminó convirtiéndose en una enorme estafa para la población. Los vínculos de Santiago Peña con el grupo empresarial, las decisiones adoptadas desde el Ejecutivo y las responsabilidades de los organismos de control forman parte de los cuestionamientos que dieron origen al pedido de juicio político. La oposición impulsó el proceso en el Congreso y el 5 de noviembre la Cámara de Diputados aprobó la acusación contra el mandatario. Peña presentó su renuncia, pero ambas cámaras rechazaron esa salida y el Senado continuó con el proceso. Al momento de escribir estas líneas, el presidente debe ejercer su defensa ante el juicio político. Sin embargo, para entender esta crisis hay que mirar también hacia la Asociación Nacional Republicana (ANR), el Partido Colorado, – profundamente ligada a la estructura del Estado paraguayo y a los 35 años de dictadura de Alfredo Stroessner – y particularmente hacia Horacio Cartes (HC). Paraguay tiene una particularidad política que resulta fundamental para comprender lo que sucede. El Partido Colorado gobierna prácticamente de manera ininterrumpida desde 1947, la única interrupción de ese largo ciclo fue el gobierno de Fernando Lugo (2008-2012), que llegó al poder encabezando una alianza que logró derrotar electoralmente al coloradismo después de más de seis décadas. Sin embargo, su Gobierno terminó abruptamente en junio de 2012, mediante un golpe parlamentario que en apenas 48 horas lo destituyó a través de un cuestionado juicio político. Para los movimientos sociales y sectores del campo popular, ese hecho no fue un episodio más en la historia sino una operación política articulada por sectores de la derecha paraguaya, con Horacio Cartes como uno de sus principales actores y con presuntas complicidades y respaldo de intereses externos, entre ellos los de la Embajada de los Estados Unidos. En la actualidad, el poder dentro de la ANR está fuertemente disputado por sus movimientos internos, pero el cartismo (HC), se convirtió en el principal espacio de poder del oficialismo. Cartes no solamente fue presidente de la República entre 2013 y 2018, sino que posteriormente asumió la presidencia del propio Partido Colorado y mantuvo una enorme influencia sobre las decisiones del Gobierno de Santiago Peña. Peña llegó a la Presidencia impulsado políticamente por Cartes. Su candidatura y posterior gobierno se construyeron bajo el liderazgo del movimiento Honor Colorado. Por eso, lo que estamos viendo hoy tiene una contradicción política muy importante; el mismo sector que construyó y sostuvo este gobierno ahora decide acompañar el juicio político contra el presidente. La posición de Cartes fue cambiando a medida que avanzaba la crisis. Inicialmente, desde su entorno se rechazaba la posibilidad de un juicio político y se buscaba concentrar la responsabilidad en las autoridades del Banco Central. Sin embargo, el 5 de noviembre, Cartes anunció públicamente que la ANR acompañaría el enjuiciamiento de Peña. Este movimiento no puede leerse simplemente como una decisión institucional del Partido Colorado, también expresa una disputa por el control político, por la continuidad del proyecto de gobierno y por la preservación de un esquema de poder que el propio movimiento contribuyó a construir. A partir de este escenario, desde los movimientos sociales y el campo popular, la denuncia no se limita a exigir responsabilidades individuales y rechazar cualquier intento de impunidad, también advierte que esta crisis es una muestra más del fracaso del modelo económico liberal que, durante años repitió que el mercado podía regularse por sí mismo, que el Estado debía reducir su intervención y que la privatización y la desregulación eran el camino para alcanzar el desarrollo. Prometieron que, si dejábamos funcionar libremente al capital, la riqueza terminaría llegando a toda la población y que el crecimiento económico se traduciría en bienestar y que todas y todos se beneficiarían de ese supuesto derrame. En Paraguay, hoy las consecuencias están sobre la mesa de cada trabajador, producto del narco capitalismo financiero. Un modelo que administra de manera inescrupulosa los recursos públicos, concentra riquezas y establece relaciones entre el poder político y los intereses privados. Un sistema en el que las ganancias quedan en manos de determinados sectores, mientras las consecuencias de sus operaciones terminan recayendo sobre la población. Por tanto, lo que ocurre en este lado del sur de nuestra América, no está ajeno a lo que sucede en otras partes del mundo, es parte de una dinámica en la que los grandes capitales condicionan las decisiones políticas, se apropian de los bienes comunes y convierten derechos y necesidades básicas en oportunidades de negocio. Cambian los escenarios, cambian los actores, pero la lógica sigue siendo la misma. Sin embargo, el evidente fracaso de la política económica del capitalismo financiero también abre una oportunidad para el campo popular organizado: disputar otro horizonte y construir una alternativa política y económica que rompa con esta lógica. De abrir camino hacia un mundo en el que la economía esté al servicio de la vida y no de la acumulación de unos pocos y en el que los pueblos puedan decidir sobre sus recursos, su presente y su destino. La India en el Mediterráneo

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Vijay Prashad ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-11T01:39:13+00:00

Read in English here El 26 de octubre de 2026, el ministro de Relaciones Exteriores de Grecia, George Gerapetritis, se presentó en la sede de las Naciones Unidas y rechazó el acuerdo marítimo entre Turquía y Libia. Gerapetritis habló en nombre de Grecia, por supuesto, pero también de Bahrein, Chipre, Egipto, la Unión Europea y los Emiratos Árabes Unidos. Cinco días después, la fragata india INS Trishul ingresó a la bahía de Souda, en Creta, como parte de actividades con la instalación de la Armada de los Estados Unidos (Actividad de Apoyo Naval) y con el Centro de Entrenamiento Operativo de Interdicción Marítima de la OTAN. Esta secuencia pone de manifiesto una geografía estratégica en desarrollo: el alcance naval de la India, su confrontación con el nuevo socio turco de Pakistán y su ambición de conectar el capital indio con los mercados europeos convergen cada vez más en el Mediterráneo oriental. Campos energéticos El 20 de octubre, el Gobierno de Acuerdo Nacional de Libia, con sede en Bengasi, aprobó un memorando marítimo firmado con Turquía en 2019. Grecia, que mantiene una relación hostil con Turquía, argumenta que la delimitación establecida en el memorando ignora los derechos marítimos de las islas que Grecia reclama, como Creta; la Unión Europea (UE), coincidiendo con Grecia, argumenta que este memorando infringe los derechos de terceros Estados. Por supuesto, Turquía acogió con satisfacción la decisión de Bengasi y rechazó la declaración de la UE. Estos argumentos se ven agravados por el petróleo que yace bajo las olas. En febrero, Chevron y Helleniq Energy firmaron un acuerdo para explorar cuatro bloques petroleros marítimos griegos, incluidos dos al sur de Creta. Chevron tiene una participación operativa del 70% en este proyecto, que prevé la exploración sísmica de yacimientos potenciales. Las concesiones demuestran por qué son importantes las fronteras marítimas, ya que los mapas en conflicto pueden tener un impacto enorme en futuros descubrimientos de petróleo. La odisea de la India En agosto de 2023, el primer ministro de la India, Narendra Modi, viajó a Atenas y se reunió con el primer ministro griego, Kyriakos Mitsotakis. Firmaron un acuerdo que reforzó la Asociación de Conectividad entre la India y la UE de mayo de 2021, la cual, en apariencia, abarca una amplia gama de temas, pero que, en el fondo, se centra principalmente en acuerdos para grandes corporaciones relacionados con puertos, cables submarinos y redes satelitales. En febrero de 2024, Modi y Mitsotakis vincularon explícitamente la cooperación en el Mediterráneo oriental con los objetivos bélicos de los Estados Unidos en Asia, al referirse – en la versión griega – a “un mar Mediterráneo y un Indo-Pacífico libres, abiertos y basados en normas” (Modi le dijo a Mitsotakis que “es motivo de alegría que Grecia haya decidido sumarse a la Iniciativa de los Océanos Indo-Pacíficos”, una plataforma impulsada por la India para la cooperación científica, pero principalmente para la seguridad marítima). Desde entonces, la relación institucional se ha profundizado con una Declaración Conjunta de Intenciones de febrero de 2026 sobre cooperación industrial en materia de defensa y, posteriormente, con un Plan de Cooperación Militar Bilateral. El secretario de Defensa de la India, Rajesh Kumar Singh, visitó Chipre y Grecia a mediados de octubre para dar seguimiento a la Asociación de Seguridad y Defensa entre la India y la UE . A medida que la India y Grecia cerraban estos acuerdos diplomáticos y militares, los buques de la Armada india llegaron uno tras otro a Grecia. Antes del INS Trishul , el INS Tamal hizo escala en la Bahía de Souda en agosto de 2025, mientras que el INS Trikand llegó a Salamina en septiembre de 2025 y participó en el primer ejercicio naval entre la India y Grecia. ¿Por qué la India de Modi está tan interesada en este acuerdo con Grecia? Además de lo que dice el texto, cabe mencionar otras dos cuestiones contextuales: en primer lugar, los acuerdos comerciales impulsados por el principal patrocinador corporativo de Modi, Gautam Adani, y, en segundo lugar, los acuerdos emergentes en materia de seguridad en Asia Occidental desarrollados por Pakistán, Turquía y Arabia Saudita. Los puertos de Adani En 2022, Adani Ports, de Gautam Adani, formó una empresa conjunta con la firma israelí Gadot para adquirir el puerto de Haifa en Israel, en la que Adani tiene una participación del 70%. Esta adquisición le permitió a Adani establecer una posición sólida en una puerta de entrada al Mediterráneo. Menos de un año después, en septiembre de 2023, en la cumbre del G20 celebrada en Nueva Delhi, varios países, como la India y los Estados Unidos, se unieron a la Unión Europea para anunciar la creación de un Corredor Económico India-Oriente Medio-Europa ( IMEC ). El proceso del IMEC forma parte del esfuerzo respaldado por los Estados Unidos para ofrecer una alternativa a la Iniciativa de la Franja y la Ruta liderada por China. La ruta es sencilla: comienza en Gujarat y Maharashtra, se dirige al Golfo por barco, se conecta con las redes ferroviarias a través de Arabia Saudita, Jordania e Israel, y luego, desde Israel, se dirige a Grecia y Francia. Lo que llama la atención es que a lo largo de todo el recorrido del IMEC se encuentran propiedades que pertenecen a diversas corporaciones de Adani: desde los puertos de Kandla y Mundra (en Gujarat, India) hasta Haifa (Israel) y luego a Marsella (con el continuo intento de adquirir participaciones en puertos griegos). En cierto sentido, el IMEC es menos una red de naciones y más una cadena global de puertos propiedad de Adani. El itinerario propuesto por el IMEC elude deliberadamente a Turquía, dando prioridad a una conexión entre el Golfo, Israel y Europa que sirve a la agenda de integración regional de Washington, al tiempo que limita el papel de Ankara como potencia de tránsito. El presidente de los EE. UU., Joe Biden, lo presentó explícitamente como una contribución a un Oriente Medio económicamente integrado que convirtiera a Israel en el punto de tránsito del Mediterráneo en lugar de a Turquía. La lógica de esta ruta debe entenderse a la luz de los Acuerdos de Abraham , diseñados por los Estados Unidos para impulsar a diversos Estados árabes a normalizar formalmente sus relaciones con Israel. El IMEC, al instar a Arabia Saudita a permitir que las rutas comerciales atravesaran Israel, habría terminado siendo, de facto, un ejercicio de normalización. La necesidad de evitar Turquía y pasar por Israel no tenía que ver con el comercio, sino que, para los Estados Unidos, se trataba exclusivamente de la política de normalización a favor de Israel. El Acuerdo de La Meca El ataque estadounidense-israelí contra Irán cambió por completo el paradigma de seguridad en Asia Occidental. La represalia de Irán contra las bases militares estadounidenses en los Estados árabes del Golfo transmitió el mensaje de que los Estados Unidos no puede ser un escudo de defensa adecuado para estas regiones. La opinión generalizada es ahora que los países de Asia Occidental deben unirse para buscar su seguridad a través de negociaciones. El 7 de agosto de 2026, Pakistán, Arabia Saudita y Turquía firmaron un acuerdo de defensa conjunta que consideraba un ataque armado contra uno de ellos como un ataque contra los tres. Antes de ese anuncio, Asim Munir, de Pakistán, viajó a Teherán para informar a los líderes iraníes de que el acuerdo de La Meca no estaba diseñado en contra de Irán, sino para crear una plataforma que no incluyera a los Estados Unidos (y, para octubre, los ministros de Defensa de los tres países viajaron a Irán para calmar cualquier tensión). El 11 de agosto, funcionarios de la India declararon que estaban monitoreando la situación, y más adelante ese mismo mes, cuando Bangladés indicó que estaba considerando unirse al acuerdo, la India reiteró una vez más que mantenía un ojo en el acuerdo. De todo esto, se ha escrito poco sobre la creciente relación de Pakistán con Turquía. En febrero de 2025, Ankara e Islamabad firmaron acuerdos que abarcaban la cooperación en guerra electrónica, los intercambios de personal militar y la capacitación en salud militar. Tres meses después, en mayo, la India y Pakistán libraron una guerra aérea en la que Pakistán utilizó drones YIHA-III y Asisguard Songar de origen turco (con una controversia adicional, negada por Ankara, de que seis aeronaves militares turcas aterrizaron en Pakistán a finales de abril). El 22 de septiembre de 2026, el presidente Recep Tayyip Erdoğan planteó el tema de Cachemira ante la Asamblea General de la ONU, lo cual la India rechazó una semana después. El acercamiento de la India a Grecia, que tiene sus propios conflictos con Turquía respecto a Chipre, le ofrece a Delhi una influencia potencial en la región vecina de Turquía. El equilibrio de Arabia Saudita entre su cercanía con los Estados Unidos y su deseo de ser un actor importante en su región vecina significa que fue participante original del IMEC en 2023 y signataria original del Acuerdo de La Meca en 2026. Funcionarios cercanos a la casa real saudí – lo que ha sido reafirmado por Reuters – afirman que Mohammed Bin Salman, el líder titular del país, ha estado evaluando de cerca los cambios en el equilibrio de poder mundial que se alejan de los Estados Unidos y está ansioso por unirse o crear plataformas (como el BRICS+ ) que acerquen más a Arabia Saudita al sistema multilateral. El compromiso de la India con el Mediterráneo combina la expansión comercial con el cálculo estratégico. Pero, ¿la participación de la India en un conflicto entre Grecia y Turquía, así como en la ocupación israelí y el genocidio de los palestinos, beneficia al pueblo indio o simplemente sirve a los intereses de la política exterior de los Estados Unidos y la OTAN? Extender el conflicto entre la India y Pakistán al Mediterráneo introduce una nueva serie de tensiones en unas aguas que ya tienen suficientes problemas.
