On top sits a photographic image layer covered by a hard, clear resin coating. That resin layer is what gives laminate its excellent scratch resistance. Cold weather can change how an electric dirt bike performs, especially when temperatures drop below freezing. Why Cold Weather Reduces Electric Dirt Bike Range and Power Cold weather affects electric dirt bikes in several ways. Battery chemistry, power delivery, and the extra energy required to ride on winter terrain can all contribute to a noticeable change in performance. Low Temperatures Reduce Available Battery Energy Lithium-ion batteries operate through chemical reactions that become less efficient at low temperatures. When a battery is cold, its internal resistance increases, making it harder to deliver energy efficiently. As a result, riders may have less usable energy available during a winter ride than they would under moderate conditions. This does not automatically mean the battery has suffered permanent damage. Performance often improves as the battery returns to an appropriate operating temperature. However, repeated exposure to unsuitable temperatures, particularly during charging, can affect battery health over time. Battery Voltage Can Drop Under Heavy Loads Electric dirt bikes draw substantial current during hard acceleration, steep climbs, and demanding off-road sections. In cold conditions, increased battery resistance can cause a greater voltage drop under these loads. Riders may experience weaker acceleration, reduced climbing performance, or a power limitation triggered by the battery management system. These effects can be more noticeable when the battery is already low on charge. A higher peak motor-power rating does not eliminate this issue. Actual performance depends on the battery's temperature, state of charge, current limits, and the bike's overall electrical system. Winter Terrain Requires More Energy Cold temperatures are only part of the problem. Mud, wet ground, loose gravel, snow, and frozen ruts can increase rolling resistance or require more frequent acceleration and corrections. Short daylight hours and strong headwinds may also influence riding conditions. If a route requires more energy per mile than usual, the battery will run down faster even when its underlying condition has not changed. It makes a significant contribution to the growing body of legal literature that is documenting in real time the significant role the Roberts Court is playing in creating an imperial presidency. Vladeck sees a Supreme Court that has supplanted district courts, “no longer sitting in review . . . so much as in their place .” [2] He argues that the Court “has done significant damage to the formal, practical, and moral authority of federal district courts to hold the federal government to account—while arrogating some of those same courts’ powers to itself.” [3] Related: The Crackdown on Pro-Palestinian Speech: How Universities Are Silencing Dissent Vladeck divides his argument into three parts, the first of which focuses on “challenges district courts have encountered” in litigating the current administration, followed by a section in which Vladeck argues that the Court is going beyond “undermining” the lower courts and is now behaving “as if it is a court of first impression.” [4] The third and final part of Vladeck’s argument is perhaps his most substantive. Inside Colombia’s FARC, as Victory Gave Way to Survival

In the early hours of September 23, 2010, Tanja Nijmeijer was awakened by a bomb in a FARC guerrilla camp in the Colombian jungle. The strike landed on her commander’s bunker, followed by screams from her comrades. Moments later, dozens of aircraft roared above and launched another wave of bombs. Nijmeijer grabbed her rifle and dived into her foxhole. As the ground shook around her, she closed her eyes, tensed her body and thought: Aquí fue — this is it. Seven years earlier, when the Dutch internationalist had first entered the jungle to join the Fuerzas Armadas Revolucionarias de Colombia (FARC), the war looked very different. She remembers it as “the calm before the storm,” with abundant supplies, comparatively large and settled camps, and a degree of security. Nijmeijer’s autobiography, Into the Jungle , recently published by Pluto Press , traces her trajectory from guerrilla warfare to the negotiations that produced the 2016 peace agreement and the troubled transition that followed. Yet what makes the memoir especially politically revealing is that Nijmeijer joined the FARC at almost exactly the moment when the war started to change so decisively against it. Stark Disparities Initially traveling to Colombia in order to teach English to the children of the rich, Nijmeijer quickly encountered the extreme inequalities dividing Colombia. She witnessed gated communities, stark disparities separating the northern part of capital Bogotá from the south, and the concentration of wealth and power among an elite while poorer neighborhoods suffered neglect and repression. Nijmeijer came into contact with FARC militants through her work and began to question the dominant image of the guerrillas as little more than “narco-terrorists.” Instead she encountered people prepared to sacrifice their lives in pursuit of a revolutionary project. Gradually developing a socialist outlook, she began to recognize that the ruling class was blocking any prospects of meaningful, peaceful change. She increasingly regarded armed struggle as not only legitimate but necessary and was convinced that the guerrillas could “take power and plant the seed for a new world.” Although it was not apparent then, when Nijmeijer went into the jungle, the FARC’s decline was already underway. Right-winger Álvaro Uribe won the presidential election in 2002 on the back of mounting hostility to the FARC following the collapse of the 1999–2002 peace process. Harnessing hegemonic narratives that portrayed the FARC as having acted in bad faith and merely exploited the peace process as an opportunity to expand, Uribe promised a “military solution,” relying on massive US support through Plan Colombia to modernize the armed forces. By late 2003, seven months after Nijeimer went into the jungle, the counterinsurgency offensive organized through Plan Patriota had reached Sumapaz, where military operations targeted guerrilla fronts in a region central to the FARC’s strategic plan for surrounding Bogotá. Areas over which the FARC had historically enjoyed complete control were now being contested. The guerrillas later discovered evidence of around sixty helicopter landing sites, army surveillance of guerrilla roads and infrastructure, and reconnaissance teams operating inside jungle territories that the insurgents had assumed were impenetrable. The state’s expanding war chest enabled significant increases in military personnel, specialized mountain battalions, and airpower. This was supported by satellite imagery and geospatial intelligence , communications interception , and other technologies challenging the guerrillas’ traditional reliance on rugged terrain as a form of defensive protection. The military’s expanding reach is already evident earlier in Nijmeijer’s account, when she describes the penetration of her units by infiltrators who pulled various stunts as part of a tactic to undermine morale — unlocking weapons, defecating in huts, and cutting up the guerrillas’ rubber boots. The result was that “nobody trusted anybody else.” Whereas in the past, counterinsurgency campaigns were temporary, with troops eventually withdrawing due to budget cuts and other constraints, now the Colombian army established a permanent offensive presence and pursued the guerrillas into rearguard zones and jungles, closing in on commanders once thought invulnerable. The shifting conditions led the FARC’s leading military commander, Mono Jojoy, to declare before a crowd of five hundred guerrillas, “Thanks to President Álvaro Uribe Vélez, we are going to learn how to live as guerrilla fighters again! We must go back to basics.” Improvements in airpower gave the state forces a decisive advantage. Vehicles disappeared, supplies and comforts were reduced, marches replaced a comparatively settled life, and concentration gave way to greater mobility and dispersion. “Our days of easy living,” as Nijmeijer puts it, “were over.” For the FARC, going back to basics meant adopting an increasingly defensive posture: land mines, often described as a “ weapon of the weak ,” became an especially useful tool for slowing the encroachments of army patrols. However, they also caused civilian casualties and left the FARC exposed to criticism for disregarding human rights. To reduce the risk of interception when communicating with urban militants, the guerrillas began sending encrypted messages via satellite internet. But this wasn’t a comprehensive solution. Air surveillance, increased extensively under Plan Colombia, could still intercept such communications. They had to switch off the equipment whenever an approaching plane was suspected, but the introduction of small, largely undetectable drones enabling real-time surveillance in 2006 made it harder to know when they were being watched. The transition to a more defensive mode of operating affected daily life. Camps were built with deep foxholes; smoking and flashlights were prohibited after dark; vines were strung along paths so fighters could navigate without light; fireflies were used as improvised illumination. The nightly hour of cultural activities, essential for political education and boosting morale, became a thing of the past in Nijmeijer’s units. Meanwhile, massed forces of guerrillas were restricted — a setback from the FARC’s war of movement strategy at the end of the 1990s, in which large concentrations of troops overwhelmed military bases. Isolation True, this was a form of adaptation. Yet adaptation increasingly meant preserving the guerrilla organization under adverse conditions rather than recreating the offensive possibilities of the previous decade. Nijmeijer recounts, “The war changed, and it changed in the army’s favor.” The psychological impact is described in a way that military histories and government documents rarely capture. Nijmeijer recalls the “pure terror” as bombs drop around her, with nowhere to run for safety, and no visible opponent to shoot at, only the hope that the jungle canopy might offer some protection. Having to be constantly on the move, often in treacherous terrain, had its physical costs. Long marches left the guerrillas hungry and low on calories, and illness and fatigue, inevitable in such conditions, spread and grew more difficult to overcome. The army had repeatedly blocked Mono Jojoy’s attempts to meet up with Manuel Marulanda, the FARC’s founder and long-standing leader. Nijmeijer describes the resulting two-month march through particularly arduous territory on inadequate rations, which left her body aching so badly that she could barely manage to endure the night watch. The psychological and physical impact had broader organizational repercussions. Demoralization deepened, and desertion rates increased , which the counterinsurgents exploited to the full. As my own interviews with various former FARC leaders confirm, from around 2007, the counterinsurgents took the FARC by surprise, systematically hiding tiny GPS trackers in items that the guerrillas relied upon to survive. They identified stores in guerrilla areas and forced merchants to cooperate and even pressured family members and the children of guerrillas, as revealed in an interview with a retired strategic intelligence analyst in the Colombian navy. The trackers guided precision strikes on guerrilla camps, such as in the case of Mono Jojoy, whose boot concealed a GPS tracker. Nijmeijer reflects that “if I had to pinpoint a moment when I began to see we wouldn’t take power by military means, it would be some time before Mono’s death in 2010.” Yet her growing recognition that the FARC could no longer take power militarily was also linked to the organization’s broader political isolation. As many former FARC commanders have admitted, kidnapping and extortion, justified by the organization as “revolutionary taxation,” inflicted enormous political costs and enabled the ruling classes to paint it as a group of terrorists and bandits, especially in urban areas where most people were based. The FARC’s communications strategy — deemed a complete failure by Nijmeijer — also left it politically vulnerable. The state had dedicated task forces whose entire purpose was to undermine the FARC’s claim to political legitimacy. According to Nijmeijer, FARC leader Manuel Marulanda had decided “there was no good reason to respond to falsehoods the state made up.” Given that the FARC was overwhelmingly rooted in the countryside, and the country was increasingly organized around the big cities, political communication was fundamental. Yet Nijmeijer recounts how long the FARC ignored this issue. Nijmeijer concludes that while the conflict had no straightforward military victor, the FARC “certainly lost the ideological battle.” Negotiations The FARC’s strategy had always theoretically left open the possibility of negotiations alongside the more ambitious goal of taking power through a popular insurrection. But as the FARC faced military blows and narrowing strategic possibilities, the idea of a negotiated settlement gained prominence. In November 2011, the FARC’s supreme leader — Alfonso Cano — was assassinated by the counterinsurgents, the first time this had occurred in the organization’s half-century history, a moment Nijmeijer remembers as a “heavy blow” that left even the more radical commanders Iván Márquez and Jesús Santrich sitting in stunned silence, repeating, “We have to go on, anyhow.” While the FARC was capable of sustaining the war over many more years, its influence had been significantly weakened. Key figures, including Nijmeijer, no longer realistically envisaged taking power through a popular insurrection and the point of war, they saw, was to win it, not simply endure it. Unable to foresee victory or a realistic possibility of retaking the strategic initiative nationally, the FARC turned to negotiations from a position of relative weakness. The state only agreed to talks on the condition that changes to its fundamental economic model were off the cards — thus leaving intact the exploitative social relations that sustained the conflict. Ultimately, from the perspective of a former guerrilla fighter who experienced firsthand the FARC’s military reversal, the book reveals that political power does not necessarily grow from the barrel of a gun. To be sure, the FARC became one of Latin America’s most powerful communist organizations, with many thousands of guerrillas, control over territory, and access to an enormous war chest. And yet, this capacity to sustain an insurgency did not translate into nationwide appeal or a credible strategy for taking power. Keytruda was built on public science. Why does Merck own the rewards?

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: David Franco ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-13T17:36:37+00:00

Keytruda is often presented as the ultimate success story of modern pharmaceutical innovation. And in one sense, that is true. Few medicines have transformed oncology as profoundly as pembrolizumab. It has extended survival in melanoma, lung cancer, and many other malignancies, and it has helped turn immune checkpoint inhibition from a scientific hypothesis into a clinical reality. For many patients, it has meant years of life that previously had few options at all. But that is only one part of the story. The other part is far less comfortable. Keytruda is not just a breakthrough drug. It is also a striking example of how the contemporary pharmaceutical system works: publicly funded science lays the foundation, private firms take control of the resulting assets, monopoly rights determine pricing, and health systems are then forced to pay extraordinary sums to access medicines they helped make possible in the first place. This is what makes Keytruda so politically important. It is not merely a story about scientific success. It is a story about ownership, public subsidy, corporate strategy, and the growing gap between biomedical progress and equitable access. In that sense, Keytruda is not an exception. It is one of the clearest illustrations of the current system’s structural logic. The science was public before the profits were private The foundations of Keytruda were laid long before Merck turned it into a blockbuster. The crucial scientific breakthrough came with the discovery of PD-1 by Tasuku Honjo and colleagues at Kyoto University in the early 1990s. Over time , this work revealed that PD-1 was part of the immune checkpoint machinery that restrains T-cell activity. That insight fundamentally changed cancer immunology. It suggested that instead of attacking tumors directly, medicine could work by releasing the brakes on the immune system. Read more: Against the normalization of limited access to CAR-T cell therapies This was not the product of isolated corporate brilliance. It resulted from decades of publicly funded research. Government support in Japan sustained Honjo’s work. In the United States , public funding helped establish the wider domain of immune checkpoint research. University laboratories, academic medical centers, and publicly funded research infrastructures made the field possible long before pharmaceutical firms recognized its full commercial value. That matters because the myth of drug innovation frequently begins too late in the story. It starts at the clinical trial, the patent filing, or the approval announcement, rather than at where innovation usually begins: in publicly supported science, collective knowledge production, and institutions built for the public good. Keytruda emerged from that ecosystem. Without it, there would have been nothing for the industry to commercialize. The market did not automatically recognize Keytruda’s value Another revealing aspect of the Keytruda story is that the drug was nearly lost not because the science failed, but because corporate priorities shifted. The antibody that became pembrolizumab originated at Organon, initially in a program not even designed for oncology. Through mergers and acquisitions, it passed to Schering-Plough and then to Merck. During these periods, the program was deprioritized, sidelined, and reportedly nearly discarded altogether. This should give pause to anyone who assumes that private ownership naturally distributes resources to the most socially valuable innovations. Under a system driven by portfolio logic, mergers and stockholder expectations, even a future life-saving therapy can be neglected if it doesn’t fit immediate strategic calculations. Big Pharma greed keeps lifesaving drugs out of reach for millions in the Global South. Source: ITPC/Facebook Pembrolizumab survived not because the market perfectly understood its promise, but because a handful of committed scientists persisted and external developments forced management to reconsider. Once competing checkpoint therapies began to show promise, Merck moved rapidly. What had been treated as marginal suddenly became central. This is often celebrated as corporate agility. But it can just as easily be read as evidence of structural irrationality. A therapy that would go on to become the world’s top-selling drug was almost abandoned because the system is not designed to maximize public health. It is designed to manage financial risk and competitive advantage. From breakthrough to blockbuster Once Merck committed to pembrolizumab, it did so aggressively. The company rapidly expanded the clinical program , scaled up trials , employed biomarker strategies with considerable success, and leveraged regulatory mechanisms to accelerate approval. Scientifically and operationally, this phase proved impressive. It helped bring a major therapeutic advance to patients faster than would otherwise have been possible. That achievement should not be dismissed. It shows that large-scale clinical development, regulatory coordination, and manufacturing capacity remain essential parts of turning promising science into real treatments. The question is not whether these capacities are needed. The question is who controls them, whose interests they serve, and who reaps the rewards. Because once Keytruda succeeded, the rewards were immense. Read more: Patents, profits and squabbles for share in the pie – Moderna’s pandemic story By 2024, it had become the world’s top-selling medicine, generating nearly 29.5 billion USD in annual sales. That figure is astonishing not only because of its scale, but because it reveals how enormous the extractive potential of pharmaceutical monopolies can be when attached to a clinically transformative product. And yet, even this commercial success was built on a landscape formed by public input . The scientific foundations were public. Clinical trial ecosystems depended heavily on academic hospitals and publicly subsidized research environments. Regulatory acceleration came through public institutions. The risks were socialized at crucial stages, but the financial gains were overwhelmingly privatized. A medicine can be revolutionary and still be exclusionary The great paradox of Keytruda is that a drug capable of redefining cancer care can simultaneously remain not accessible to many of the people who need it. In the United States, treatment costs have exceeded 150,000 USD per patient per year. In Europe , health systems have had to negotiate rebates , confidential agreements , and special reimbursement arrangements to contain budgetary impact. Even wealthy countries struggle to absorb the costs of widespread access. For lower-income countries, the situation is far worse. There, access is often minimal, delayed, or effectively nonexistent. This is not a side issue. It is central to how we should evaluate medical innovation. Read more: Not Omicron but greed of capital threatens the world A therapy cannot be judged only by its efficacy in clinical trials or its scientific sophistication. We must also judge it by whether patients can actually receive it. A drug that exists in principle but remains unreachable in practice is not a complete public health success. Keytruda’s inclusion on the WHO Essential Medicines List should have been a powerful recognition of its importance . But that recognition also exposed a deeper contradiction. What does it mean for a medicine to be considered essential if it remains priced beyond the reach of most of the world? At that point, the designation risks becoming symbolic. A medicine can be clinically essential and commercially exclusionary at the same time. Under the present model, that contradiction is not accidental. It is built into the system. The real question is not whether industry matters, but on what terms Defenders of the status quo often respond with a familiar argument: without patents, high prices, and private investment, drugs like Keytruda would never have reached patients. The claim that large-scale development requires resources, coordination, and risk-bearing has some truth. But that does not settle the matter. It merely restates the rules of the current system as if they were laws of nature. They are not. Read more: When Brazilian health workers discovered Soviet healthcare The issue is not whether clinical development and manufacturing require serious investment. Of course they do. The issue is whether public institutions must always play a subordinate role: finance early discovery, de-risk innovation, build research infrastructure, and then step aside once commercialization begins. Why should taxpayers fund the science, support the research ecosystem, and absorb part of the development risk, only to lose all control over pricing, licensing, and access at the decisive moment? Why should a medicine built on public knowledge become a private asset governed by monopoly logic? And why should governments then pay again, at inflated prices, to buy back access for their own populations? These are not radical questions. They are the basic questions that any serious public-interest pharmaceutical policy must confront. Public Pharma is not a fantasy but a necessary direction This is where the idea of Public Pharma becomes more than a slogan. It becomes a practical and moral challenge to the current order . A Public Pharma approach would begin from a simple premise: if public institutions are central to the creation of breakthrough medicines, they should not relinquish stewardship when those medicines become commercially valuable. Public actors should have the capacity not only to fund discovery, but also to shape development, retain ownership stakes, set access conditions, and, where appropriate, support manufacturing and distribution. That would not mean excluding scientific collaboration or denying the complexity of drug development. It would mean changing the terms of engagement. Under such a model, the benefits of a drug like Keytruda could be organized differently. Public investment could come with enforceable conditions on affordability and licensing. Intellectual property could be structured to prevent extreme monopoly control over entire therapeutic pathways. Revenues could be reinvested into further research rather than extracted as shareholder value. Health systems could negotiate from a position of public stewardship rather than defensive purchasing. Most importantly, access would no longer be treated as an afterthought. A breakthrough for whom? Keytruda demonstrates what biomedical science can achieve at its best. It shows that diseases once regarded as almost uniformly fatal can be confronted in entirely new ways. That should be acknowledged without hesitation. But Keytruda also shows the limits of celebrating innovation while ignoring political economy. A medicine can be scientifically brilliant and socially unjust at the same time. It can save lives while still comprising a system that rations access through price. It can stand as proof of human inventiveness while also revealing how poorly that ingenuity is governed. That is why Keytruda should be remembered not only as a triumph of immuno-oncology. It should also be understood as a case study in the contradictions of pharmaceutical capitalism. The lesson is not that we need less innovation. The lesson is that we need a different system for governing it. The real measure of a medical breakthrough is not only whether it can be invented, patented, and sold at scale. It is whether we can make it available, sustainably and fairly, to all who need it.
