History Becomes Expendable

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Jason Steinhauer ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-28T18:08:23+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. What do Alaska, Wyoming, South Dakota and Vermont have in common? If you answered that none of these states offers a Ph.D. in History at any of its colleges or universities, you would be correct. I came across this fact while reading an article about the next state that might join the list: Oregon. According to a recent article in the Eugene-Springfield Lookout , the University of Oregon’s History Ph.D. program—which has existed since 1899—is being considered for elimination as the university seeks to bridge a $65 million budget deficit. While the University of Oregon athletics department generated more than $119 million last year, the school’s Education & General Fund projected a multi-million-dollar deficit. The university president explained in a recent email that tuition revenues accounted for 80 percent of that fund, most of it being out-of-state tuition (i.e., students and families who pay at- or close-to full price). As those enrollments have plummeted, the budget deficit has gotten wider. This necessitated a “different path,” to quote the president, elevating programs that “meet the needs” of industry and turning University of Oregon into a “destination of choice” for more students. It is likely that a History Ph.D. program does not satisfy either requirement: industry is not demanding more History Ph.D.’s, and a stellar doctorate program does not attract students as much as a winning College Football team. The University of Oregon is not unique; what is happening in Eugene, OR, is unfolding across the United States. Syracuse University in upstate New York is also facing a budget deficit after missing its enrollment targets. The Syracuse chancellor said in response that the school must “commit to differentiated academic excellence,” as it eliminated 93 academic programs . History was not one of them, but its Bachelor degrees in Citizenship and Civic Engagement, Classical Civilization, Digital Humanities, and History of Architecture were. Savannah State University, a Historically-Black College in Georgia, responded to declining enrollments and an $11 million budget gap by eliminating its History program. In 2023, Simmons College in Massachusetts cut several liberal arts majors in response to budget pressures, including History—and then in 2026, eliminated its Humanities major in the face of declining student revenue, outstanding loans and a downgrade of its credit rating. In Indiana, a bill passed last year to eliminate or consolidate over 400 programs at state universities, including in History, American Studies, Africana Studies and Political Science. And History has been on the chopping block at Montclair State University (New Jersey), University of Pittsburgh (Pennsylvania) and Missouri Western State University. Related: What Happened with Europe's Elections? Several converging trends are contributing. Fewer students have been enrolling in American colleges and universities over the past 15 years—though there has been a slight uptick since the Covid-19 pandemic. Smaller private universities have felt the decline the most, so much so that an estimated 150 private, nonprofit colleges have closed in the U.S. With fewer students now in high school due to declining birth rates—and more of those students signaling an interest in trade schools, community colleges or opting-out of higher education altogether—experts expect a continued decline in student tuition dollars through the 2030s, which will lead to more program cuts and campus closures. American colleges and universities have also experienced a decline in international students. International students dropped during the Covid-19 pandemic, rose in subsequent years, then took a steep downturn since 2025. Since international students often pay full price tuition (as opposed to American students who receive financial aid and scholarships), the losses have an outsized influence. Amid the decline in students overall, there have also been cultural forces altering student priorities and interests. The past 20 years have seen a heavy emphasis on science, technology, engineering and math (S.T.E.M.) in the private, public and nonprofit sectors, which has drawn student and parents towards S.T.E.M. and A.I. degrees and away from Liberal Arts and Humanities degrees. In recent years, a nationwide shortage of nurses and doctors have also led to boons for medical and nursing schools, with baccalaureate and higher degree nursing programs seeing increased enrollments across the board . The same cannot be said for History programs. According to the most recent data , between 2012 and 2025, the number of History degrees declined more than 40 percent in the U.S. Dissertations in the field declined by nearly 50 percent. In the humanities more broadly—to include subjects such as Religion, Philosophy, English and others—degrees awarded at American universities were the lowest since 1991. The ratio of humanities degrees to overall degrees in the U.S. is now at the lowest level since comprehensive data became available. Robert Townsend—who I knew when he worked at the American Historical Association and I worked at the Library of Congress—has been monitoring these trends for years in his current role at the American Academy of Arts & Sciences. When the most recent figures were released, he was quoted in several media outlets about the root causes. In his view, critical factors included students’ declining comfort with long-form reading, and their difficulty envisioning what they would do with a humanities degree after college. In other words, because young people read far less than they once did, and reading scores among high school seniors are at their lowest levels in 30 years , the hefty history books required by college-level courses feel like an insurmountable burden for incoming undergraduates. While engaging with such books might answer student questions about the past, it does not help them answer their questions about the future: their future careers, their future job prospects or their future contributions to society. To put it another way, student anxieties about the future are inhibiting them from learning about the past. Townsend also added that the cost of college compels students (and parents) to choose majors with a clearer career path and an attractive starting salary. Different sources have varying statistics, but on the aggregate the cost of higher education in the U.S. has significantly outpaced the rate of inflation, with tuition increasing nearly 15 percent over the past 20 years when adjusted for inflation, and nearly 84 percent when not. Despite scholarships, financial assistance, and new efforts to reduce costs, a college education in the U.S. is still exorbitantly expensive, more so for private colleges than public universities or community colleges. At such prices, students and parents opt for majors they perceive to have more certain career trajectories. Finally, as I wrote in my book History, Disrupted , it is also the case that billions of pieces of “ e -history ” now exist online for free. From Wikipedia to YouTube, podcasts to TikTok videos, one could consume historical content on the social Web from now until the end of time and never reach the end. Without re-litigating the argument on whether such content is “good” or “bad” ( read the book to answer that question), it is unmistakably the case that students do not need to pay $37,000 in tuition each year to get exposed to information about the past. The over-abundance of “free” history online complicates the argument for why students and parents should pay for formal history instruction in school, especially if they do not plan to pursue a career in the field. Regardless of which factors are most prevailing, History is declining within American higher ed—and, indeed, even in high schools and middle schools. And while it may be tempting to assign responsibility to political factors, the reality is that the decline has unfolded over a 20-year period during both parties’ leadership at the federal, state and local levels. Elected officials and school administrators have been unable to do much to alter these trajectories, which show no signs of abating. What could realistically be done? Should something be done? As a reader of this newsletter, you might agree the answer to the second question is ‘yes.’ But not everyone feels similarly. Perhaps this is merely the “market” at work, some people have argued. If there is no demand for History degrees at the collegiate level, then supply diminishes. If there are no students enrolling in History courses, it becomes difficult to justify funding them. If there are no jobs for History majors, then students cannot be faulted for pursuing other options. The answer, it seems, would be to either stimulate more demand among employers, students and parents for History degrees, likely by making History courses more attractive to young people, less predicated on reading and writing, and connecting them directly to other areas of the workforce (e.g., History of Nursing or History of A.I.); or insist that History education is so essential that it be required regardless of student interest, with the requisite funding coming from governments, donations or endowments. In my final chapter of History, Disrupted , titled “ Does History Have a Future ?” I did proffer one potential solution: remove History from the undergraduate curriculum under the stipulation that students must return later in life to complete the coursework. My thinking was that until college becomes significantly less expensive, students will continue to pursue degrees with higher earning capacity in the shortest amount of time. During those anxious early adult years, History courses may not be the best investment of their time and money. But as older adults, hopefully with steady income and a career underway, as well as being more attuned to the realities of contributing to a healthy democracy, History courses will be more relevant to them. Such an approach may not be viable in practice, but a wicked problem such as this probably will require some outside-the-box thinking to solve. In closing, though, I will deviate from solutions to a wider view on what this reality portends for us. Our young people already know so little about history; this surely means they will know less. Our young people already read so little and so infrequently; this means they will do so less. They are already susceptible to historical propaganda and disinformation, and this means they will be more so. And they will enter the wider world with little connection to the world that was made before they arrived in it, with no deep understanding of the fields, professions and forces that are shaping their lives. It is not the case that a decline in History education solely dictates these realities; there are many factors. But the decline in History education contributes to them, and in many ways is emblematic of the broader challenges we face. We are increasingly illiterate, increasingly fixated on “costs” as opposed to “value,” and increasingly privileging our proverbial left-brain at the expense of our right. With an election coming up, it is worth pausing to consider how historically illiterate our electorate is becoming, and what that means for our democracy. Reading and thinking about the past does, indeed, affect the future. History may not move the bottom line as much as TV sports contracts or grants from tech companies. But if colleges and universities are solely worried about the “bottom” line, then we may as well stop calling them “higher” education. Cutting and cutting until we reach the “bottom” may tidy up the spreadsheet, but it does little to enrich the lives, minds and “higher” faculties of young people. That mission remains worthy of our investment, whatever the costs.

historical roots of white Christian nationalism

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Dick and Sharon ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-28T17:42:08+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. Christianity began with teachings about love, compassion, humility, and concern for the poor. Yet throughout history, rulers invoking the name of Christ have justified conquest, slavery, torture, and the destruction of entire communities. How did a religion rooted in the teachings of a man executed by an empire become one of history's most effective instruments of imperial power? That question animated a wide-ranging conversation between LA Progressive co-founders Sharon Kyle and Dick Price and Rev. Peter Laarman, a retired minister and longtime progressive Christian activist, about his recent article, “Christian Fingerprints on Europe's New World Crimes.” Laarman, who formerly led Progressive Christians Uniting in Los Angeles, is a member of the National Council of Elders and serves on its King and Breaking Silence team. The conversation began in 15th-century Spain but quickly expanded to encompass the origins of racial hierarchy, the enslavement of Africans, contemporary Christian nationalism, immigration enforcement, and the possibilities for transforming American political culture. When Christianity Became an Instrument of Empire Laarman explained that Christianity's entanglement with imperial power began long before Christopher Columbus sailed across the Atlantic. He traced a pivotal transformation to the fourth century, when Roman Emperor Constantine embraced Christianity and helped set the religion on a path toward becoming an instrument of state authority. Early Christians, Laarman observed, had represented something fundamentally different: a community that challenged domination and emphasized solidarity with the powerless. But as Christianity became associated with imperial authority, rulers discovered its usefulness in legitimizing their ambitions. That relationship reached particularly destructive heights under Spain's Ferdinand and Isabella. In their campaign to consolidate political power, the monarchs combined military conquest, religious persecution, and lavish public displays of Christian devotion. The Spanish Inquisition, led by figures such as Tomás de Torquemada, subjected suspected religious dissenters to torture and execution. Religious conformity became inseparable from political obedience. Laarman described the Inquisition's enforcers, including Dominican friars who proudly embraced the designation “Dogs of God,” as agents of an emerging authoritarian state. Sharon Kyle drew a comparison between Torquemada's relentless enforcement machinery and contemporary American immigration policies, particularly the role of Stephen Miller and ICE. The parallels, she suggested, were difficult to ignore: authorities identifying supposedly dangerous outsiders, normalizing harsh treatment, and invoking a higher political or moral purpose to justify their actions. For Laarman, the significance lies not in claiming that these historical episodes are identical, but in recognizing recurring patterns of domination. Related: Beyond Plantation Capitalism: Rev. Peter Laarman on the Fight for Economic Justice Columbus, Christianity, and the Origins of Racial Hierarchy One of the conversation's revelations concerned Christopher Columbus. Although Columbus is commonly portrayed as an ambitious navigator seeking a western route to Asia, Laarman emphasized his religious zeal. Columbus understood his voyage partly through the lens of Christian conquest. His ships carried crosses associated with the Crusades, and his ambitions were intertwined with dreams of expanding Christianity's reach. The same religious worldview that helped justify Spain's campaign against Muslims and Jews accompanied Europeans into the Americas. Laarman argued that the conquest of Indigenous peoples and the subsequent enslavement of Africans cannot be understood apart from the religious ideologies that legitimized European expansion. Sharon brought her own genealogical research into the discussion. Tracing ancestors through the Caribbean, she had encountered the Moravian Church and other missionary movements that brought Christianity to enslaved Africans. She wondered whether conversion had once offered enslaved people a potential route toward freedom. Laarman explained that Christian authorities and plantation owners found ways to separate spiritual equality from legal freedom. Enslaved people could be baptized without becoming free. He connected this contradiction to the development of racial categories that enabled Europeans to preserve systems of enslavement while maintaining Christian identities. The Spanish obsession with limpieza de sangre , or purity of blood, also helped establish precedents for later racial hierarchies. These developments, Laarman argued, demonstrate how religious distinctions could evolve into supposedly permanent distinctions based on ancestry. The discussion also explored how Portugal and Spain divided overseas territories, how plantation agriculture transformed the Caribbean, and how sugar became a powerful economic engine of the Atlantic slave trade. Christianity was not merely present during these developments. Religious institutions, interpretations, and authorities helped shape the systems through which conquest and exploitation operated. Related: Curbing Cancerous Capitalism: An Organizing Challenge for the Ages Is Violence Human Nature—or Something We Have Learned? The conversation eventually turned from historical atrocities to a more fundamental question. Are human beings naturally predisposed toward domination and violence? Sharon invoked the biblical story of Cain and Abel, asking whether humanity might be hardwired for conflict. Laarman resisted that conclusion. He suggested that even within the Bible, ethical understandings evolve—from images of a punitive, dominating God toward prophetic traditions emphasizing justice, solidarity, and compassion. Jesus, in Laarman's interpretation, continued that movement away from domination. Christianity's original ethical vision, he argued, was not about enforcing submission to earthly rulers. “We started with an anti-imperial, anti-domination proposition,” he said. He described an alternative tradition in which everyone belongs, everyone possesses dignity, and the poorest and weakest receive priority. Laarman also rejected the assumption that human societies must organize themselves through coercion and superior force. Although domination has become deeply normalized, he maintained that other ways of living and organizing society remain possible. The culture of violence, he suggested, is neither inevitable nor divinely ordained. Related: Marlon James Uncovers Depths of Depravity in Evangelical Christianity Beyond Trump: The Need for Transformational Politics Dick Price brought the conversation into the approaching American elections. Could the country reject not only Donald Trump but also the cruelty, resentment, and authoritarianism associated with his political movement? And would electing Democrats be enough? Laarman cautioned against confusing electoral victories with fundamental change. He distinguished between transactional politics—changing officeholders while preserving existing institutions—and transformational politics, which seeks to alter the underlying structures of power. Simply electing what Dick called another collection of politicians in blue suits would not necessarily address the country's deeper problems. Laarman recalled the excitement surrounding Barack Obama's promise of change, followed by disappointment when established financial and political interests retained substantial influence. Real transformation, he argued, requires more than campaign rhetoric. It requires changing governing institutions, challenging concentrated wealth, reforming congressional procedures, and organizing sustained public pressure. Sharon raised another concern drawn from her experience as a publisher: people often become politically engaged when a crisis directly threatens them, only to disengage once that immediate danger passes. She argued that offering a compelling vision of a better society is not enough. People also need to see what that society would actually look like and how it would work. Laarman enthusiastically agreed. The discussion returned to the New Deal, when organized labor and progressive movements helped push Franklin Roosevelt toward far-reaching reforms. Political leaders, the participants agreed, rarely produce transformational change without organized movements demanding it. Related: Recalling the Torments of a Radical Truthteller Reclaiming Christianity's Radical Promise Near the conversation's conclusion, Laarman was asked what single misconception about Christianity's role in creating the modern Western world he would most like to correct. His answer returned to Columbus and the European conquest of the Americas. Christianity was not simply the incidental religion of the explorers, he argued. A militant conception of Christianity as a conquering faith profoundly shaped their ambitions. That history matters because contemporary white Christian nationalism did not emerge from nowhere. It draws upon a centuries-old tradition in which religious identity becomes intertwined with national power, racial hierarchy, and the suppression of people deemed outsiders. But Laarman refused to concede that domination represents Christianity's essential character. The alternative remains available in the teachings of Jesus, the prophetic tradition, and the commitment to solidarity with society's most vulnerable. The conversation ultimately offered more than a critique of historical Christianity. It posed a challenge to the present. If religious institutions and political movements helped construct systems of domination, people can also organize to dismantle them. And if the culture of violence is learned rather than inevitable, another political and moral culture can be built in its place. Laarman described the possibility in terms that brought the conversation full circle: a politics of revolutionary love . Not merely a change of parties or presidents, but a transformation in how people understand their obligations to one another. That, perhaps, is the most important lesson to draw from Christianity's troubled history: the past need not determine the future, but understanding it is indispensable to changing what comes next. China Miéville’s The Rouse Is a Haunting Magnum Opus

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Jon Repetti ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-28T17:10:39+00:00

China Miéville’s The Rouse is a book that should not exist, and yet here it is. It should be read cover to cover, and then immediately read a second time. For twenty-one years, Miéville wrote and rewrote it while publishing shorter works at an impressive pace: four novels; two novellas; a history of the Russian Revolution; a book-length essay on The Communist Manifesto ; an out-of-left-field collaboration with Keanu Reeves; a children’s book with Zak Smith; countless articles and reviews. Each of these now seems like a stepping stone on Miéville’s path toward his great project. Into The Rouse ’s 1,200-plus pages have gone a lifetime of writing as well as a lifetime of reading — Miéville’s influences range from pulp horror to high modernism, from Marxist theory to esoterica. From the bones of this personal canon, he has developed the most ambitious and successful synthesis of genre fiction, formal experimentation, leftist political commitment, and metaphysical inquiry produced in English since Thomas Pynchon’s Gravity’s Rainbow . The novel weaves together two main threads, both written in an extraordinarily close third person. One begins in 1970s London and concerns Maureen Towns, a downwardly mobile single mother to a reserved, awkward, and increasingly violent son named Ewan. Through Maur’s eyes, we see Ewan grow fascinated with guns in his adolescence, join the military when barely out of his teens, report “see[ing] stuff” during a deployment to Sarajevo, become involved with shadowy acronymous divisions, and then finally turn up dead in the woods outside his barracks. When his death is ruled a suicide despite the two bullet holes in his temple, Maur begins to investigate and soon joins forces with other parents of soldiers who perished under mysterious circumstances. The other thread opens in the 1950s and covers half a century in the life of Steven Allardyce, operative of R Unit, a secret task force charged with investigating supernatural events at the far-flung outposts of the British Empire. With Allardyce, we come to understand the R Unit’s history, mandate, and methods through glimpses and innuendos; Miéville does not hold our hands, and much is left up to the reader to reconstruct. To give the gist: for centuries, from John Dee’s Elizabethan alchemy to Arthur Conan Doyle’s Victorian séances, the Brits have demonstrated a unique talent for all things paranormal. In the twentieth century, those scattered traditions are brought together under one concrete-reinforced roof — R Unit’s base of operations — paid for by tax dollars and pursued with scientific rigor. R Unit’s research centers on “revenants” (ghostlike beings that emerge when a person dies) and “apports” (powerful objects that give their holders the ability to interact with and influence revenants). It is difficult to fathom how quickly the British Empire fell. Though the process stretched from the 1947 Indian Independence Act to the handover of Hong Kong in 1997, the collapse was all but complete by the time Rhodesia became independent in 1965. Allardyce and his comrades deploy to most of the key conflict zones, moving from site to site so quickly that readers and characters tend to lose track of where they are on the map. In Malaya, the Suez Canal Zone, the Congo, Kenya, and elsewhere, the unit’s job is to take consolation prizes: local knowledges and practices, apports to add to their collection, and revenants they enlist through a ritual known as “the Rouse.” All this constitutes what one character calls “a new era of exploration” — a journey into another “dark continent” amid the loss of Britain’s military and economic hegemony. The Cold War being the Cold War, the Russians and Chinese soon assemble their own supersecret paranormal units, setting off an arms race complete with clandestine skirmishes fought with apports. As the 1960s roll into the ’70s, however, R Unit’s most dangerous adversary proves not to be another state actor, but a left-leaning postcolonial terror cell of powerful mediums known as the KH. Whereas Allardyce and company view their Russian counterparts with begrudging respect and acknowledge that the commies at least have comprehensible political goals, they can only conceive of the KH as a death cult intent on summoning an ancient being (called, among countless other names, “Old Pal”) to usher in the actual, literal apocalypse. Stakes that high justify ever more brutal acts in the field and increasingly dangerous experiments in the lab. What these stakes do not justify, at least in the eyes of the army brass and their accountants, is more funding for supernatural research. In the lean years around the oil crisis and the rise of Thatcherism, R Unit is sidelined and all but shuttered; nobody in the government wants to hear about impending global doom when there’s a budget to balance. Allardyce is one of many who quit in frustration and go “freelance,” working as mercenaries for various military and private enterprises while continuing to pursue esoteric knowledge, apports, and the KH through the fall of the Berlin Wall and into the present day. Like the masterworks from which Miéville has clearly taken inspiration — Pynchon’s Gravity’s Rainbow , Don DeLillo’s Libra , Dennis Johnson’s Tree of Smoke — The Rouse flirts with but ultimately resists making history into a conspiracy. One can easily imagine a less serious (or a less Marxist) writer casting all the great atrocities of the later twentieth century, from the Malayan Emergency to the Bosnian Genocide, as mere cover for the real war beneath the surface: a battle between cadres of warring spiritualists in which the rest of us are mere pawns. Though his characters might slip into such narratives to justify themselves, Miéville refuses to let us off so easily. Despite its self-regard, R Unit remains a small branch of a (relatively) minor military power, subject to the frustrations of bureaucracy and politics — which are in turn downstream of properly global economic trends, neoliberalization chief among them. These forces have as little to do with ghosts as they do with personal ideologies. The Specter of Anti-Communism Around page 800, Miéville writes that “talking about politics without talking about haunting meant not telling the truth.” This may be as close as we get to the novel’s thesis statement. He leaves it to us to fill in the dialectical inversion: that talking about haunting without talking about politics (or economics, or history) is equally false. Old Pal may be a timeless entity, but we are never allowed to forget that its summoning is the result of processes triggered by twentieth-century capitalism and warfare — the accumulation of thousands of apports in centralized hordes, the scientific rationalization of their study and usage, and so on. Old Pal is less like George R. R. Martin’s White Walkers than it is like the atomic bomb or climate change, which are almost never mentioned in The Rouse but hang over its events like the shadow of a shadow: possibilities woven into the fabric of reality, but only actualized once capital made the technological advancements necessary to discover them. To be clear, The Rouse is emphatically not an allegory for atomic power, climate change, or any other particular threat, nor is it propaganda for nuclear disarmament, degrowth communism, or any other particular program. Such reductive readings are always tempting when dealing with works of such unmanageable scale. Indeed, the members of R Unit are faced with a similar problem of linguistic excess — the enormous catalogue of myths, rituals, and terms they have pilfered from history books and indigenous tribes — and are also driven to impose abstract order on the field. Like pop structuralists, they want to boil down all competing stories into a coherent monomyth, to read each as the expression of a single phenomenon experienced across times and cultures, which would be the key to all mythologies. Likewise, they seek to “reduce” the apports themselves, filing or melting them down into increasingly dense secretions with the goal of wielding the primitive core of “apport-ness” as a weapon. Miéville is often described as a writer of “weird fiction,” a subgenre that has long been preoccupied with the hubris of such quests for totalizing knowledge. Think of all the H. P. Lovecraft stories about a scientist who pursues his investigations until an encounter with some indescribable monster destroys his mind. Think of Victor Frankenstein. Or, if you’d prefer to go further back, think of Gilgamesh’s quest for immortality, an allusion that comes up throughout The Rouse . This is a book in which “everyone who knows something knows less than they think, and none of them can be trusted,” as Mark Bould puts it in Boston Review . In a world where haunting and history are inextricable, the only admissible stance is rigorous epistemological humility. This requires us to reject not only naive positivism but also deterministic Marxism — to embrace a long historical durée without guarantees and a world that even the most precise description will never exhaust. Still, Miéville insists, we cannot cease to act and speak. “It’s all metaphor” is Allardyce’s mentor’s rule of thumb for speaking about the revenants. But the metaphors we use to discuss them help determine what facet of their spooky being we encounter, without canceling out any of the others. The middle third of the novel includes hundreds of pages in which characters debate their preferred metaphors and explore their consequences; each character’s fate is inseparable from the set of metaphors they favor. In the lab, the revenants appear through the language of mathematics, as “functions” or “vectors”; on the battlefield they are “roused,” a metaphorical invocation of their status as conscripted soldiers. In different local contexts they are “lespries,” “haints,” “hantus,” “gallas,” or “fantasmi,” each name indexing an entire culture and mode of relating to them. And what is a culture, The Rouse seems to ask, but a system of practices for relating to the dead? Experiences of meeting the dead always exceed attempts to contain their meaning, however. When Maur grows adept at detecting spiritual presences, a narratorial voice that is both hers and not-hers reminds us that “there can be no verb for her new sensing action any more than there can be a noun for what it apprehends.” Across the novel’s scenes of visitation, similes pile up inside other similes. Descriptors accumulate, standing in contradiction without overwriting one another. Sensations become abstract and abstractions are felt in our bones. Consider this bravado passage in which Allardyce witnesses a helicopter navigate the thick Malayan jungle with help from a revenant: The Sycamore turned as if to regard them. It ascended. They stood in the pillar of its turbulence, a storm not formless but of infinite configuration, a helix of dead matter and those rotor words. At the limits of the trees the chopper veered, and while the others applauded as it pulled away for the city, Bowler and Allardyce looked into the whirlwind, the gusting dead centre of the hole in the forest, at debris still turning. On Bowler’s face, a look almost of recognition. What gyred was darkness like a standing rock or old animal skin, like something emptied and refilled, an outline, cyclone visitant, eyeless, watching. Miéville provides us some guideposts for dealing with this deafening swirl of words and images: not to reduce but to multiply; not to elide differences, but to live within the gaps between discourses; not to defer choosing one metaphor over another, but to choose without grounds for choice. This is the task left to Maur, Allardyce, and all the others who are sensitive to the revenants. A Uniteer puts the matter like this: “If you ask a question and you don’t hear an answer, and I don’t mean you you, I mean anyone, you have to work out if nothing said anything or if something didn’t say anything or if something said nothing . And if it’s the last, how to make sense of it.” How to read the signs without the cipher key, without even knowing if a sign is present or absent, or if this absence is its own kind of sign? How to maintain a state of absolute expectancy without concrete expectations? This is the dilemma of the spiritualist in a scientific age, but it is also the predicament of the revolutionary subject in a nonrevolutionary time. Like the protagonists of The Rouse , we have no choice but to wait for some tremors from beyond our circumscribed present — for some cracks to peer through, if not to crawl out of. Dockworkers Worldwide Are About to Strike for Palestine

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Peter Cole ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-28T16:05:37+00:00

In 1984, while the global struggle against apartheid burned hot, union dockworkers in San Francisco refused to unload South African cargo to demonstrate their hostility to a white supremacist, fascist regime. In so doing they reaffirmed what Harry Bridges, the first president of the International Longshore and Warehouse Union (ILWU), asserted when asked about what role organized workers should play in world affairs: “Interfere with the foreign policy of the country? Sure as hell! That’s our job, that’s our privilege, that’s our right, that’s our duty.” Few workers believe that they have sufficient power to express political opinions at work, let alone insert themselves into global affairs by downing their tools. And yet, as revolutionary unionist “Big” Bill Haywood reminded, “If the workers are organized, all they have to do is to put their hands in their pockets and they have got the capitalist class whipped.” Starting in 2023, the Palestinian General Federation of Trade Unions (PGFTU) called on workers and unions to “Stop Arming Israel.” In 2025, the PGFTU asked international workers to “organize days of rage and global solidarity in factories and workshops, in ports and airports, in the streets and public squares, in support of Palestine and its brave people.” The Palestinian Boycott, Divestment, and Sanctions movement clearly builds upon the struggle against apartheid South Africa, arguably the most effective global social justice movement of the late twentieth century. As early as 1959, African National Congress President Chief Albert Luthuli , later awarded the Nobel Peace Prize, stated: “Economic boycott is one way in which the world at large can bring home to the South African authorities that they must either mend their ways or suffer for them.” This Friday, October 30, dockworkers in around one hundred ports in nearly twenty countries will engage in perhaps the largest coordinated work stoppage in world history. The epicenter is the Mediterranean — where dockworkers in dozens of ports in Italy, Greece, France, Cyprus, the Basque Country, and Morocco — will refuse to work for either part of the day or the entire day. They will be joined by dockworkers and other workers who will rally in Spain, Turkey, Germany, Ireland, Portugal, Denmark, Sweden, Australia, Veracruz, Mexico, and Oakland, California as well as many ports in India. They’re not demanding higher wages or shorter hours. In the words of two of the many posters circulating, “Dockworkers do not work for war” and “No Harbour for Genocide.” Dockworkers are saying they refuse to be complicit in waging war, let alone genocide. The Ship Must Sail on Time The history of strikes is inextricably linked to shipping. Tellingly, the English term for workers’ most powerful weapon, “strike,” has nautical origins. In their book Many-Headed Hydra , Peter Linebaugh and Marcus Rediker described how, in 1768, sailors in London “struck [took down] the sails of their vessels, crippling the commerce of the empire’s leading city and adding the strike to the armory of resistance.” Since then, the term for work stoppage has been strike . Shipping is central to capitalism: even today, upward of 90 percent of all goods consumed (either raw materials or finished products) are moved by ships. For at least a century, dockworkers, seafarers, and other maritime workers have occasionally refused to load or unload cargo to protest imperialism, fascism, and war. In 1935–36, dockworkers in the southern African ports of Durban, Cape Town, and Lüderitz Bay, as well as in San Francisco, refused to load cargo aboard Italian ships to protest that fascist empire’s invasion of Ethiopia. In the late 1930s, dockworkers in Local 10, the ILWU’s San Francisco Bay Area branch, partnered with the Chinese community to refuse to load cargo for imperial Japan to protest its invasion of China. The most widespread example of global dockworker activism was against apartheid South Africa. In San Francisco, Brooklyn, Sydney, Port of Spain (Trinidad), Gothenburg (Sweden), Copenhagen, and many other ports, union workers refused to handle cargo. Anti-apartheid activism among workers was not limited to shipping. Irish supermarket workers led a multiyear campaign by refusing to check out customers who bought South African products, as dramatically described by Irish author Sally Rooney . Perhaps the most relevant example was ILWU Local 10’s campaign against fascist Chile. In 1978, San Francisco dockworkers and clerks of ILWU Local 34 discovered the US military was secretly shipping weapons to Chile. The ILWU had staunchly supported the previous regime, led by socialist Salvador Allende, overthrown in 1973 in a military coup led by Augusto Pinochet, with the active support of the United States. Local 10 activists including Herb Mills, who later wrote a fictionalized account called Presente , partnered with Chilean exiles, Bay Area Latinos, the Catholic Church, and others to press the US government to stop any sending of military supplies. In tandem with sympathetic congressional members, the ILWU helped convince the Carter administration to reverse US policy and stop sending military equipment to Chile. Dockworker Solidarity With Palestine The PGFTU’s 2025 “Cry Before Death” stated: “Our union comrades, we await your role in delivering the cry of the children and workers of Gaza to decision-makers and to the streets.” In solidarity with the Palestinian labor movement, the second International Day of Action of Dockworkers seeks to disrupt the global supply chain first and foremost in Mediterranean ports, which is the “front line” in the struggle against Israel’s war on Palestine — as well as its wars on Lebanon, Turkey, Syria, and Iran — all supplied by US and European military equipment and ammunition. Since 2019, when they refused to load weapons for Saudi Arabia’s war in Yemen, dockworkers in Genoa — Italy’s busiest port — have emerged as a leading force. Last year, in solidarity with the Global Sumud Flotilla that sought to break Israel’s illegal blockade of Gaza, one Genovese dockworker promised, “If we lose contact with our boats, with our comrades, even for just twenty minutes, we will block all of Europe.” Last fall, among the flotilla participants was the Genovese docker activist José Nivoi and the Amazon union activist Chris Smalls. The Genovese dockers have strong support from a well-organized civil society, student groups, and supportive local government. This fall, Genoa also is hosting a sound installation by artist Hajra Waheed, Hum III : From Port to Port, A Sonic Convening . Waheed’s exhibition explores the historical tradition of radical labor organizing, antifascism, and internationalism in Casablanca, Chattogram, Durban, Genoa, Sydney, Jaffa, and other ports. Waheed intentionally connects histories of direct action to dockworkers engaged in opposing apartheid Israel, including on October 30. The World Federation of Trade Unions ( WFTU ), an Athens-based organization with affiliated unions representing 105 million workers, is cosponsoring this action: The mobilization is taking place at a critical time, as military conflicts are spreading and war fronts are dangerously expanding and becoming interconnected, from Ukraine to the Middle East. The murderous state of Israel, with the support of the USA, the European Union and NATO, continues its crime against the Palestinian people and escalates its military aggression against Iran and Lebanon, increasing the danger of an even broader conflict. The WFTU highlights that workers “in the metal industry and shipyards, railways, energy, telecommunications, transport and logistics. . . . ports, industrial zones and military infrastructure. . . . [also] can play a decisive role in the common struggle against imperialist war and exploitation.” Charmaine Chua, a scholar of supply chain disruptions and an organizer with Workers in Palestine, provides more specifics about strategic nodes sustaining Israel’s genocide: from Turkey supplying crude oil through the port of Ceyhan, to coal exports from South Africa, to shipments of components to Elbit Systems from Genoa, Ravenna, Livorno, and more, to Oakland’s role in supplying US military operations across the world. To trace the role of these ports in genocide is to see the tentacles of fuel, weapons systems, and renewable energy feeding the Israeli and US war machines. But if ports have become an astonishing crystallization of our states’ callous complicity in the war machine, the immense solidarity of dockworkers and civil society reveals the mass opposition forming in response to these ongoing catastrophes. One must also understand that, since the supply chain — which makes “modern” war possible — is global, shutting down one port, or even the ports in one country, is insufficient. That is why Katy Fox-Hodess wrote that no strategic choke point is a “magic bullet” and why she, along with coauthor Rafeef Ziadah, began studying “ Labour Internationalism in a Time of Genocide .” Chua goes further: “Meaningful labor solidarity with Palestine will require all of us to find where we have other points of leverage in our own workplaces, to sustain organizing on our own shop floors, and to build a unionism committed to organizing everywhere, rather than only at strategic sites.” While dockworkers soon will take the stage, some could not wait. Last week in Livorno, dockworkers disrupted the movement of US military cargo from the joint Italian Air Base and US Naval Air Station Sigonella , nicknamed the “Hub of the Med.” This Friday, dockworkers across much of the Mediterranean and beyond will stop work and rally against war and genocide. In today’s world, workers seeking to shape global politics must organize across industries and nations. While this Friday’s action should be huge, how workers respond next week and those to come will be even more important. When U.S. immigration officials sent a Salvadoran man to Mexico, he relived the trauma he’d experienced when he’d fled his own country — trauma that had won him protection from deportation there. Juan Antonio Pineda Vaquerano fled El Salvador in 2022 after police there tortured him, he said, and in 2024, a U.S. immigration judge believed his evidence that he would be tortured again if returned to his homeland. But, in 2025, after President Donald Trump returned to office, Immigration and Customs Enforcement officers arrested him at his scheduled check-in appointment and sent him to Mexico. In the process, he said, they broke his hand and placed him in a restraint that has been called a torture device by human rights workers. Pineda Vaquerano is among more than 300 people that the Trump administration has sent to countries where they have no ties after immigration judges granted them protection from deportation to their home countries because of legitimate fear of persecution or torture — people who fit the international definition of refugees — according to a report from Human Rights First. “This is just a complete subversion of the global protection system,” said Savi Arvey, director of policy, refugee and immigrant rights at Human Rights First. “At the core it’s the most perverse part of their very deliberate attempt to send people to places where they have no connections or ties and they might and in many cases have faced immense harm. It’s really unprecedented.” ICE did not respond to a request for comment from Capital & Main. A photo of Juan Antonio Pineda Vaquerano taken while he was working at a senior living community in Maryland. Photo courtesy Pineda Vaquerano. According to the report, the Trump administration has systematically targeted people who were granted protection from deportation as part of its third country removals program, breaking from decades of policies that allowed people with these protections to remain in the U.S. Many of those people had protection as members of the LGBTQ+ community from countries that do not allow those identities to exist freely. U.S. immigration law offers three kinds of protection to people fleeing their countries. Asylum, the most well known, offers a path to citizenship, but it is discretionary, meaning that judges can choose not to award it. Historically, that typically happened because the person seeking protection had some kind of serious criminal history, but under both the first Trump administration and the Biden administration, the U.S. government added requirements that disqualified most people arriving at the border from obtaining asylum, meaning they had to turn to other forms of protection to stay in the country. The other two kinds of protection are not discretionary — if someone qualifies, the judge has to grant them refuge. The first, withholding of removal, means that the person met the U.N. definition of a refugee and is more likely than not to be persecuted if returned to their home country. The second, protection under the Convention Against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment, offers refuge to people who can prove they will likely be tortured if returned. Under both withholding and protection under the Convention Against Torture, the immigration judge technically issues a removal order to deport the person but then blocks their own order by granting one of these protections. Before the second Trump administration, people with this status had to check in periodically with the U.S. government and renew their work permits, but as long as they stayed on U.S. soil, they generally would not be forced to leave. In 2019, the Trump administration restricted anyone who had passed through another country after leaving their own country and before entering the U.S. from getting asylum. Related: Trump Deports Immigrants to Countries the U.S. Says Are Unsafe In 2023, the Biden administration reintroduced this restriction for anyone who did not use the CBP One mobile application to make an appointment to request protection at a port of entry. Many asylum seekers struggled to get appointments through the app, and many found themselves in danger in Mexico. Instead of waiting in danger, they chose to cross between ports of entry, blocking them from receiving asylum. A federal judge has since blocked the restriction, but Human Rights First researchers identified at least 41 people who received one of the other forms of protection instead of asylum because of that restriction whom the Trump administration has now sent to third countries. “It had nothing to do with the merits of their claim, and as we know, the wait times for CBP One were super long,” Arvey said. “There were people who didn’t know about the app, who couldn’t access it in their language, who were afraid to wait in Mexico. We just in particular have been so horrified to hear about those cases.” Pineda Vaquerano said he crossed the U.S.-Mexico border in the summer of 2022 in Texas. A Texas judge initially denied his case, but he was one of about 6,000 people affected by an ICE data breach , and he was given a second chance at his case, this time from a Georgia detention center with notoriously low grant rates for protection. Even without an attorney representing him, he managed to convince an immigration judge based on all of the evidence he brought with him from El Salvador, including a broken clavicle suffered at the hands of police there, that he would be tortured if returned to his country, and she granted him protection under the Convention Against Torture. “It was a relief,” he said in Spanish. “I felt more at peace because they weren’t going to send me to my country.” ICE released him from custody in January 2024, he said, after he won his case. He moved to Maryland where his cousin lived. His wife and five of his children were still in El Salvador, so he said he began working two jobs — one as a cook at a restaurant and a second overnight cleaning kitchens for restaurants and hotels — to send money back to help them. He knew that because he hadn’t gotten asylum, he wouldn’t be able to sponsor them, but he hoped to save money to somehow reunite with them. The social unrest currently gripping France is in many ways a national phenomenon. Frustration with a stretched education system boiled over in the form of campus blockages and marches beginning late last month. By mid-October, students were joined by broader swaths of the population fed up with high gasoline prices, sticky inflation, and declining real wages. There’s also little new in the violent police response that has shocked protesters and observers alike, both in France and abroad. Emmanuel Macron’s allies above all want to protect the president’s agenda of pro-business tax cuts, an often-elided factor in the country’s fiscal crisis. France’s state deficit is expected to reach 5.4 percent of GDP in 2026, the highest level in the eurozone, sending tremors in bond markets and leading to calls for drastic cuts to public services. On October 26, the government made its first overtures toward the students, hinting at the possible replacement of the reviled university selection platform Parcoursup, caps on classroom sizes, and state support for building renovations. But shared European realities are also driving the anger. Spain’s recent demonstrations over the cost of housing pushed Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez to call snap elections for late November. Meanwhile, youth protests have disrupted cities in Italy, Belgium, and Greece, part of what some commentators are calling a “Gen Z” revolt against economic insecurity, the uncertainty surrounding the rise of artificial intelligence, and the withering political response to climate change. As far as Europe is concerned, these issues collide in a grim political economy of diminishing expectations. Flying to Gulf states to negotiate stopgap energy purchases last week, Macron pleaded that declining purchasing power is the result of external shocks hitting the EU. In France, diesel prices have hovered at around €2.36 per liter in October, or around $10 per gallon — a direct effect of the turbulence roiling global energy markets since the United States and Israel launched their latest war on Iran. This has only added to the residual inflation from Europe’s efforts to wean itself off of Russian oil and gas since Moscow’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine. In November 2018, at the onset of the so-called Yellow Vest revolt that shook Macron’s first term, gasoline prices were at €1.50 per liter. But energy is one among the many stress points for European capitalism. The trade globalization that underpinned Europe’s accumulation engine between the 1990s and the 2010s is breaking down in the face of Donald Trump’s tariffs and China’s success in moving up the value chain to now threaten core EU industries. Looking ahead, the continent lags increasingly behind the US and Chinese corporations carving up the high technology and artificial intelligence sectors. Merz to Europe: Drop Dead Protesters on Europe’s streets this fall aren’t the only ones calling out an untenable economic situation. In recent years, factions of the European Union’s technocratic elite have issued increasingly dire warnings over the bloc’s bind and the policy inertia aggravating it all. In September 2024, former Italian Premier Mario Draghi authored an influential report on the EU economy, warning of the continent’s “slow agony” as investment droughts in infrastructure, green technology and public services combine with low wages to feed into a protracted economic malaise. That prognosis is playing out in part in the anger erupting this autumn, with the eurozone’s GDP growth expected to level out between 0.8 and 1 percent in 2026, according to the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development. Since 2019, according to Le Monde , real wages in France have in fact declined , with 21.7 percent in cumulative inflation more than gobbling up the 18.7 average increase in salaries. Even in the face of entwined crises like these, EU policymaking has a remarkable capacity to trudge on as if by inertia. Little seems able to fundamentally shake the course of low investments and minimal economic interventionism from Brussels. Hopes for flexibility were again doused in the mid-October meeting of the European Council, bringing together heads of state and government from the twenty-seven-nation body. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz and his alliance of fiscally conservative Northern European states are demanding “several hundred billion euros” worth of cuts for the bloc’s next budget, set to cover the 2028–34 period. The goal is to reach a deal before the cycle of elections next year in important European member states like France, Italy, and Poland. This is not a new position. For decades, EU economic policy has been a tug-of-war between advocates of greater economic federalism and fiscal hawks arguing for conservative retrenchment, a dispute largely carried by the latter. Hovering at around 1 percent of the European Union’s annual GDP, the minimal budget afforded to Brussels is already a victory for the bloc’s dominant conservative members. Facing his own domestic pressure amid the rise of the far-right Alternative für Deutschland (AfD) party, Merz wants to scale down appropriations for EU agricultural subsidies and cohesion grants, clearing up resources for things like rearmament. Germany’s stonewalling, despite mounting evidence of European stagnation, is shocking even some liberals. “It’s difficult to insist on the need for more Europe when it comes to defense and innovation and then push to cut an already woefully low and insufficient budget,” said Sandro Gozi, a MEP and leader of Macron’s Renew Group in the European Parliament. “Merz fashions himself an EU leader but is playing a very dangerous game in the current situation,” Gozi wrote to Jacobin . “An Image of Our Future” No doubt, a lot separates these budgetary wranglings in Brussels from what’s playing out on the streets in European cities. But it’s not hard to deduce from Germany’s position how it would react to a runaway fiscal crisis in a member state like France, a scenario that has come to preoccupy policymakers in EU institutions. For that matter, France’s protests are indeed a salvo in the escalating confrontation over budgeting. The cost of serving French debt this year is estimated to exceed the education ministry’s operating budget. French government bond rates hit 5 percent in the week following the October 17 marches, which brought large crowds out to the streets in cities and devolved into heated rioting in some cases. “The situation in France is an image of our future,” said one adviser close to Enrico Letta, a former Italian premier who like Draghi also issued an influential 2024 report on Europe’s economy. “Fiscal politics are becoming extremely difficult to navigate, weak majorities struggle to pass budgets, investment needs are very high, people are unhappy, and markets are pricing it all in by raising interest rates.” Meanwhile, French Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu is struggling to stitch together a majority for a cost-cutting 2027 budget, seeking tens of billions of euros worth of savings in a bid to steady the deficit at around 5 percent of GDP next year. But the ongoing demonstrations are increasing pressure on the majority-opposition parties in parliament to not extend the government a hand. Many in France are arguing that the solution to the fiscal crisis requires a new economic deal in Europe. La France Insoumise, the left-wing party led by four-time presidential candidate Jean-Luc Mélenchon, has argued for freezing state debt owned by the Bank of France, a subsidiary of the European Central Bank (ECB). There’s not an off chance that France’s next president will have been elected on a program of confronting the existing EU setup. For the April 18 first round of the presidential elections, Mélenchon is currently polling a distant second to Marine Le Pen, candidate of the Rassemblement National. The leader of the far-right party recently told the Financial Times that she was prepared for a “trial of strength” with the EU on France’s contribution to the EU budget, an appropriation that La France Insoumise has also threatened to cut by 10 percent. Though Le Pen has come out in favor of installing a constitutional “debt brake,” in addition to pledging steep spending cuts if elected, she has also called on the ECB to intervene to ease interest rates. Whoever wins, the lines are being drawn for a significant showdown on France’s fiscal situation. To judge by the tenor of the last few weeks, EU leaders should be wary of where the situation could turn if the French are pushed too far. Eugene V. Debs Eugene V. Debs (1855 – 1926) was a union leader and socialist politician. As presidential candidate for the Socialist Party of America, Debs received 6% of the popular vote in 1912. In June of 1918, Debs was arrested for a speech that urged resistance to the military draft in violation of the Espionage Act. Harding commuted his sentence on December 23, 1921. I said then, and I say now, that while there is a lower class, I am in it, and while there is a criminal element I am of it, and while there is a soul in prison, I am not free. I listened to all that was said in this court in support and justification of this prosecution, but my mind remains unchanged. I look upon the Espionage Law as a despotic enactment in flagrant conflict with democratic principles and with the spirit of free institutions. . . . DSA Is Eyeing a 2028 AOC Presidential Run

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Eric Blanc ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-28T13:15:36+00:00

The Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) is entering a new phase. After decades on the political margins, DSA has helped elect a socialist mayor of New York City, won a string of congressional primaries, and grown to over 130,000 members. Now, as chapters across the country call for Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez to run for president in 2028, the organization faces a crucial test of how to turn its growing popularity into national political power. There are encouraging signs that DSA is rising to the occasion. From Los Angeles and Chicago to smaller communities like Ithaca and Wichita, chapters have passed resolutions urging AOC to run. Last weekend, Bread and Roses — a caucus I am a member of and that within DSA’s national leadership often holds the balance of power between the organization’s different political wings — voted overwhelmingly to join the effort to draft her. DSA’s national leadership has also approved a process that could allow an endorsement early in 2027, rather than waiting until its summer convention. None of this means the organization is united, or that its remaining disagreements are trivial. But the trend is clear: more socialists are coming around to the possibility of a national campaign capable of reaching millions of working people. The Growing Coalition for AOC At its 2025 national convention, held before Zohran Mamdani’s mayoral victory reenergized the organization, DSA committed to helping unite labor and the Left behind a socialist presidential candidate in 2028. That decision did not settle whom to support. But as members have organized for an AOC candidacy this year, the case for rallying around her has gained force. Over recent months, with support from caucuses on what’s sometimes referred to as the right wing of DSA’s political spectrum such as Socialist Majority and Groundwork, chapters from Los Angeles, Chicago, and Boston to smaller chapters like Ithaca, Wichita, and Sonoma County have passed resolutions encouraging AOC to run for president. These resolutions argue that an AOC campaign could upend American politics. Atlanta DSA’s resolution highlights her support for Medicare for All, the Green New Deal, and an arms embargo on Israel. Unlike establishment Democrats such as Gavin Newsom and Kamala Harris, AOC could offer working-class Americans a clear alternative to the status quo, championing an agenda that puts “people over profits.” Rather than simply defending existing institutions against the far right, the resolution argues, her campaign could rally millions around an ambitious vision for improving their lives. The precedent of Bernie Sanders’s presidential campaigns looms large. His runs helped turn democratic socialism into a recognizable political force and gave millions of Americans a language for challenging billionaire power. In supporters’ view, an AOC campaign could build on that legacy, bringing demands for economic redistribution, universal public services, and a more democratic political system to an even wider audience. And unlike Sanders in 2016, she would begin with a much larger socialist organizational base from which to build. The resolutions also reflect an urgency about the country’s political direction. In California, Sonoma County DSA warned that both major parties have been moving rightward for decades and declared that “it is time to stop that slide.” Its September statement argues that a socialist presidential campaign could rally voters around alternatives to growing inequality, authoritarianism, and war, while advancing struggles for health care, housing, immigrant rights, and Palestinian freedom. Similar sentiments have emerged in smaller communities. Announcing its overwhelming vote to support AOC, Mid-Hudson Valley DSA declared that “we cannot cede ground to a politics of yesterday.” The growing support for AOC also reflects the influence of Zohran Mamdani’s breakthrough in New York. His victory demonstrated the appeal of campaigning on concrete improvements to working people’s lives, including rent freezes, universal childcare, and taxing the rich. Just as importantly, it showed that socialists could reach far beyond their existing base by combining popular demands with ambitious grassroots organizing. Supporters of an AOC candidacy see the possibility of taking that approach nationwide. For these chapters, a presidential campaign also presents an opportunity to strengthen socialist organizing well beyond Washington. As one Atlanta DSA member put it , “We need a DSA of millions, one that’s strong enough to contest state power, fuse with a strong labor movement, and energetic enough to get bystanders to come out to support us.” Chicago DSA offers perhaps the clearest case for how an AOC campaign could strengthen socialist organizing beyond the presidential race. Noting that presidential elections are “how most Americans engage with politics,” its recently adopted resolution proposes pairing an AOC campaign with a slate of candidates for the Illinois legislature and Congress, who would run on demands including rent control, fully funded public schools, immigrant protections, and higher wages. Just as importantly, the chapter lays out a plan to turn electoral enthusiasm into lasting organization: building relationships with unions, recruiting and training socialist candidates, and converting campaign volunteers into DSA members. For its part, Los Angeles DSA has made “Run, AOC, Run!” one of its three main priorities for the coming year. The breadth of these resolutions suggests that DSA’s electoral victories and influx of new members are encouraging greater ambitions. Bread and Roses’ decision to join the draft effort is especially notable, suggesting that the emerging coalition is extending well beyond the Socialist Majority and Groundwork caucuses. Increasingly, socialists with different strategic traditions are seeing an opportunity to translate diffuse popular support into organized power. Why Some Socialists Remain Skeptical Not everyone is on board. Over the summer, several caucuses from the organization’s far left, who generally espouse some version of revolutionary socialism, raised objections to an AOC campaign, though the intensity of their opposition has varied, and they have been less vocal about the issue over the past two months. Red Star, whose member Megan Romer is one of DSA’s two national cochairs, has expressed “serious doubts” about throwing its weight behind her, citing a lack of “political and practical alignment with DSA.” Marxist Unity Group and Springs of Revolution have questioned moves toward an early endorsement and stressed the importance of holding socialist elected officials accountable. Liberation, a much smaller Maoist caucus, has rejected the idea of making her the standard-bearer of the socialist movement. In 2024, disagreements over Palestine led national DSA to withdraw its national endorsement of AOC, even as NYC-DSA continued to back her. Critics have pointed to her statements at the Democratic National Convention about Kamala Harris “working tirelessly” for a ceasefire, her past views on Israel’s Iron Dome, and her sometimes conciliatory approach to Democratic Party leaders. Although she has pledged to oppose military funding to Israel, some DSAers worry that such commitments might not hold under the pressures of a national campaign or once in office. There is a deeper strategic concern, too. Over the past century, popular left-wing politicians around the world have frequently won office only to be hemmed in by party leaders, wealthy donors, and the institutions of the state. Socialists are right to avoid subordinating their independent organization to the fortunes of any single politician, and they’re right to worry about the potentially demobilizing effects of holding executive office. But those concerns are ultimately an argument for shaping an AOC campaign, not standing aside from it. And their specific criticisms are often overstated. Whatever DSA members might think of some of her past rhetoric on Palestine, AOC has never voted for military aid to Israel. And without justifying all of her tactical approaches, much of what those on the DSA left see as AOC’s excessively accommodationist approach to Democratic leaders reflects a sober and appropriate understanding of the need for some degree of unity in action to stop Trumpism. AOC combines a national audience, an identification with democratic socialism, and a plausible path to mobilizing working people on a scale few others could match. Refusing to engage out of the perception that she falls short on certain questions would not, by itself, make DSA and the broader movement more independent or more powerful. The event marks Israel’s first general election since the October 7 attacks, which Netanyahu’s government expanded into a multi-front regional war. Who is competing in the elections? For many, these elections serve as a direct referendum on Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and the far-right bloc he heads. The Pro-Netanyahu Bloc This bloc comprises the National Liberal Party ( Likud ) led by Netanyahu, the Religious Zionist Party ( HaTzionut HaDatit ) led by current Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, and Jewish Power Party ( Otzma Yehudit ) led by National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir. The coalition advocates for the consolidation of Israeli rule throughout the occupied Palestinian territories, explicitly rejects the possibility of Palestinian statehood, promotes the expansion of settlements and the implementation of hardline security measures against Palestinians. It also calls for disavowing the Oslo Accords, and the total disarmament of Hamas. The anti-Netanyahu bloc The primary competitor to Netanyahu and his coalition is the bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot , a former military chief of the Israeli forces. It represents a center-right to center-left opposition when it comes to Israeli internal affairs, but of course not to Palestinians and their destiny. Eisenkot is the architect of the “Dahiya Doctrine,” an Israeli military strategy that calls for applying massive, disproportionate firepower and the widespread destruction of civilian infrastructure in order to create long-term deterrence, and discourage the local population from supporting any force resisting Israel. He was also a member of Netanyahu’s war cabinet for the first eight months of Israel’s genocide in Gaza. Eisenkot’s bloc includes the Yashar Party, led by Eisenkot, and the Together alliance ( Beyachad ), led by former prime minister Naftali Bennett. It also includes The Democrats (HaDemokratim) – a merger of the Labor and Meretz parties – led by Yair Golan , a retired IDF Major General. Israel Is Our Home Party ( Yisrael Beiteinu ) led by Avigdor Liberman, is also part of Eisenkot’s coalition. Liberman, who formerly held ministerial portfolios in the Israeli government as minister of finance, defense, foreign affairs, strategic affairs, transportation, and national infrastructure alongside being a deputy prime minister, is known for his hawkish stances towards Palestinians. Defending the use of live ammunition by Israeli forces against Palestinian demonstrators during the 2018 Gaza border protests (known as the Great March of Return), Liberman stated : “There are no innocents in Gaza.” Although the components of this coalition are united by their goal to oust Netanyahu, they remain deeply aligned with their political rival’s traditional right-wing stance regarding the Palestinian cause. The coalition opposes the creation of an independent Palestinian state, while supporting the ongoing military operations in the Gaza Strip, the West Bank and other parts of West Asia, and maintaining existing settlement frameworks. Some of the coalition’s affiliated parties further encourage separating Israeli and Palestinian populations, and retaining complete Israeli security control over strategic areas such as the Jordan Valley. They also demand the full demilitarization of any future Palestinian entity. Others call for the dismantling of the Palestinian Authority, the annexation of Area C in the West Bank, and the entrenchment of discriminatory policies against Arab citizens in Israel. The Arab-led lists The Arab-led lists represent the minority of Palestinian citizens in Israel (also known as Arab-Israelis), a community that makes up roughly 21% of Israel’s total population, estimated at 10.3 million. Even though both lists fight for the same minority group’s rights, they hold opposing views on the goals, values, and strategy needed to represent them effectively. While representing a minority, these lists hold a critical and often polarizing role in Israeli elections, as they frequently act as a critical balance of power that strongly determines whether a governing coalition can be formed. These lists include the Joint List and the United Arab List. The Joint List The Joint List consists of: The Democratic Front for Peace and Equality ( Hadash ) led by Yousef Jabareen , a prominent Palestinian legal scholar, academic, and politician. The Arab Movement for Renewal ( Ta’al ) led by Dr. Ahmad Tibi , a prominent Palestinian politician, who has served as a member of Knesset since 1999. The National Democratic Alliance ( Balad ) led by a prominent Palestinian politician and historian Sami Abu Shehadeh. The coalition advocates for Palestinian self-determination and civil rights, for an end to the Israeli occupation, and for the establishment of an independent Palestinian state along the 1967 borders with Jerusalem as its capital. It also demands full legal equality and state recognition for Palestinian Arab citizens as an indigenous national minority, and calls for immediate government and law enforcement intervention to curb the increase in organized crime in Arab municipalities. Structural reforms to secure building rights, expand municipal boundaries, and stop punitive home demolitions are top priorities on the Joint List’s agenda. The United Arab List The United Arab List ( Ra’am ) is an Islamist and conservative political party led by Mansour Abbas, a prominent Palestinian politician, whose party was the first independent Arab-Muslim faction to join an Israeli government. Unlike traditional Arab political lists, Ra’am demonstrates a willingness to ally with Jewish political parties in government. It uses a pragmatic, integration-focused strategy to gain political influence and enhance the socioeconomic wellbeing of Arab-Israeli citizens. Ra’am prioritizes the immediate domestic needs of Arab communities inside Israel, such as fighting local organized crime, improving public infrastructure, securing budgets, and resolving housing issues. He also claimed that left-wing women escorted Arab women to the polls. It is expected that the final tally and initial results will be published between Thursday afternoon, October 29, and Friday morning, October 30, while the official results will be announced on November 4, 2026. I sit down on a thick wooden fence-post under a native coastal live oak at the mouth of the hidden oasis that is a section of San Pascual Park. Joining me are Amada Segura and Josh Kashapata, two stellar LA resident volunteers with the Save San Pascual Park group. For Amada and Josh, their Save San Pascual Park journey started with spotting yard signs in their neighborhood. Soon after, they attended a land defense meeting at the Coyotl + Macehualli Lending Library community space in El Sereno. There they met one of the main organizers in the defense of this park: a proud long-time Angeleno and land defender hailing from East LA, Clara Solis. Since then, Amanda and Josh have joined the park defense, offering their talents to aid in the battle against corrupt city governments, golf course management, and major media outlets. Just as a single spark can start a prairie fire, a single neighbor—Steve Crouch—raised the alarm to others upon receiving mail about the Arroyo Seco Water Reuse Project. Among those was his childhood friend Clara Solis. This collaborative project by the cities of Pasadena and South Pasadena—both cities boasting significant affluent areas—requires the destruction of a natural space containing 136 trees, a biologically active waterway, and a safe haven for species including the majestic great blue heron. The parcel of lush land would be replaced with artificial pools and water-purifying systems to filter out bacterial contamination in the Arroyo Seco channel. Also included will be enough carefully landscaped native foliage to suitably cover the project in green paint and call it environmentally aligned. The project claims to be a community-oriented and nature-based solution….However, the city’s efforts, which have refused to educate and include the public in the process, reveal a different pattern. The project claims to be a community-oriented, nature-based solution and receives funding from the Safe Clean Water Program, Measure W. However, the city’s own efforts, which have refused to educate and include the public, in the process reveal a different pattern. Los Angeles is almost 50% Hispanic or Latinx, but the outreach was only in English until people started calling them out. The pamphlets themselves contain so much misinformation; Save San Pascual Park went line by line on their social media and debunked them using the city’s own sources. One of these sources includes an environmental impact report completed by other cities, which only happened because they were sued by Clara Solis. “There’s no ecological basis for any of their projects,” says Josh. “It’s kind of like a lot of them ignoring the root causes of issues but using those issues as justifications for projects. So like, runoff from the golf course or Rose Bowl is still going to be coming through here. This [project] is a band-aid for that; it’s not treating the problem at the source, which is obviously the ideal.” The environmental impact report itself points to two golf courses as the sources of pollution for the waterways, but the cities have targeted this specific area instead. “They admit themselves, that they cannot conduct a nature-based system yet,” Amada adds. Meanwhile, groups like the Stewards of the Arroyo Seco, who are committed to natural restoration, have not been approached by either city. Importantly, the Kizh Nation has been involved since the beginning, working through the AB 52 CEQUA process. AB 52 requires tribal consultation for projects such as the Arroyo Seco Water Reuse Project. “They say they’ve had the consultation,” Amada states. “Chief Salas can tell you, and he has stated multiple times that all the consultation was [the tribe] receiving the initial project plans and the Kizh Nation saying ‘we’re not ok with this’ and Pasadena basically saying ‘Ok. The risk of continental spread of Ebola looms as Nigeria becomes the fourth African country to report a confirmed case of the deadly virus since the outbreak was declared in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) in mid-May. Caused by the rare Bundibugyo strain of the virus, it has since become the world’s second-deadliest Ebola outbreak ever and the fastest-growing on record. It has killed 4,803 patients , nearly half of all the 9,876 confirmed cases in the eight provinces of DRC’s eastern region as of October 23​ The real scale of deaths is likely much higher, since community transmission of the virus had already begun in DRC’s Ituri province by mid-February , three months before it was detected and officially declared an outbreak on May 15.​ Neighboring Uganda, which is backing armed groups in Ituri, undermining the already delayed and severely underfunded health response, also reported its first case on May 15. However, after two deaths and 20 confirmed infections, the health ministry declared Uganda Ebola-free in late July. The WHO also certified this in late August, after no new cases were confirmed for 42 days.​ However, its Ebola-free status has now come under question with the first case in Nigeria. The 42-year-old patient is a Ugandan national and Swiss citizen. She is the wife of a Swiss diplomat at its embassy in Nigeria’s capital, Abuja, where she resides.​ After a two-and-a-half-week-long vacation in Uganda, she and her husband returned to Abuja on October 3. Four days later, she fell ill. Initially suspected of Malaria, she was treated at two private hospitals before being tested positive for Ebola and isolated on October 20.​ Uganda makes a false claim Soon after she was tested positive in Nigeria, the Ugandan health ministry claimed that the patient had traveled to the DRC. However, the “information supplied to WHO did not include [her] travel” to any country other than Uganda, WHO representative in Nigeria Pavel Ursu said on October 22. “So far, there ​is no validated information that this case traveled to DRC,” added Wessam Mankoula, the Head of Health Emergencies of the continent’s highest health authority, the Africa Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).​ Then on October 23, the Swiss foreign ministry said explicitly that the patient had not traveled to the DRC, directly contradicting Uganda’s claim, indicating that she was infected in Uganda. Uganda, meanwhile, still maintains that it has no confirmed Ebola cases. The WHO and Africa CDC are now examining a hypothesis that she may have contracted the virus at the airport in Uganda’s capital, Kampala, where a Kenyan businessman, infected during his travels in the DRC, was also present while she was boarding her flight to Abuja. The Kenyan businessman had spent seven years in the DRC. While traveling on business, he had already fallen ill in September and was treated in multiple hospitals, but was not diagnosed with Ebola. Early symptoms of Ebola are often similar to other diseases in the region, including Malaria. He was reportedly provided antibiotics and sent home at one of the hospitals.​ An illegal border crossing The man in question traveled by road to Uganda from the conflict-ridden, mineral-rich, Ebola-affected province of North Kivu, parts of which are occupied by the Rwandan army and its proxy, the M23, which Uganda also supports , according to the UN.​ It is unclear how the Kenyan businessman reached Kampala from North Kivu. Soon after the outbreak was declared, Uganda had closed the border with DRC, except for humanitarian trucks. Mankoula said he had crossed the border illegally. In Kampala, he boarded a flight to Nairobi on October 3 from Entebbe Airport, through which the Uganda-born Swiss citizen who later tested positive in Nigeria was also passing that day to catch her flight to Abuja. “Has there been any touch or contact between two cases [him and the case in Nigeria] at the airport at the same time?” This is one of the theories that is currently under investigation,” said Mankoula of the Africa CDC. When the Kenyan businessman landed in Nairobi, he was already ill. The family member who picked him up from the airport drove him straight to the Nairobi Hospital, where he died two days later. Eight others who came into contact were also infected and isolated. They include four health workers and four family members, one of whom died later on October 23. Even as health authorities examine whether he was also the source from whom the Nigerian case contracted the virus, virologist Marion Koopmans, who is also a member of the WHO’s Ebola emergency committee, has cast doubt on this theory. “I think that is very speculative unless there is evidence of sharing the same ​space in a manner that could explain that. Random presence at the same location, a large airport, is not a supported epidemiological link by any standard of what we currently know,” she said . If he was not the source, that could mean Ugandan authorities are either not revealing or are unaware of potential cases in the country. Even if he was the source, questions remain about Uganda’s Ebola-free status, as others he may have come into contact with – either in the airport or on his way to it in Kampala – could also have contracted the virus. Nigeria at risk of community transmission Either way, Nigeria is now at risk of community transmission. The Nigerian case landed on October 3 in Abuja, at one of the busiest airports in West Africa. Only on October 20, 13 days after developing symptoms, was she tested positive for Ebola and isolated.​ Amid fears that the virus may have infected others who may have come into contact with her during this period, especially health workers, Nigerian Health Minister Muhammad Ali Pate said that authorities were tracing all 44 contacts. “We are trying to contain it so it doesn’t go out to the communities. Hopefully, it won’t get to that,” he added.​ “When we identified at-risk countries, Nigeria was not part of this list. It is now. That list would have to be redrawn, and North and Southern Africa could also be at ​risk,” said Jean Kaseya, director-general of the Africa CDC, adding, “We need to consider that no one is safe today.”
