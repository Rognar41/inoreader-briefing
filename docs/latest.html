Review: The Wind Cries Freedom: An Oral History of the Next American Revolution

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Detroit DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-28T04:15:22+00:00

The cover for The Wind Cries Freedom by Michael Albert By Will Froberg Michael Albert takes you into a parallel universe in which the fictional Revolutionary Participatory Society (RPS) mass movement has grasped the levers of power in the United States, and is now in the process of implementing their participatory vision. Through a series of interviews with RPS organizers, Albert explains the means by which the movement eventually prevailed. Their vision includes an alternative to capitalism called a participatory economy , which the author invented alongside Robin Hahnel back in the 1960’s, and which he has spent his life advocating for. One of the RPS organizers reflects on the direction the movement settled on at their first convention. She describes a participatory economy as being “a system of decentralized, cooperative negotiation between workers’ and consumers’ councils,” in which “resources, workplaces, and the very infrastructure of production were not owned by a few but shared by all.” It is a democratic economy with remuneration for need and effort/sacrifice. Another key component is balanced jobs, which are described in the following passage: “But each worker should be in a position to participate — really participate. That meant restructuring work itself. No more corporate hierarchies where a small class gets the empowering, decision-heavy tasks, and everyone else is left with rote obedience. We called the alternative ‘balanced job complexes.’” I find Albert’s vision difficult to dismiss: it’s comprehensive, rigorous, and strikes a great balance between various values that I believe are highly desirable. One of these values is self-management: that people should have a say in decisions in proportion to the degree they are affected. The RPS program extends well beyond economics. Albert makes very clear how important it is to avoid putting one sphere of relations above any other. He does this by having the organizers also discuss vision and strategy for political, community, and kinship relations — as well as environmental and international relations. This book is very different from Albert’s other works because it does not provide a detailed technical breakdown of either his vision, or even critiques of current systems. Although the interview format can be slightly dry at times, it allows Albert to do what he does best: let his originality flow while communicating ideas simply and, in this case more than in his previous works, evocatively. The book’s coverage is incredibly wide. It covers all the essential facets of what a movement like this would entail: the many tactics and strategies RPS used to win, psychological tendencies they had to contend with, and so much more. Ultimately this is a very human exploration of a movement’s possibilities, struggles, failures, and successes. A surprising bonus was that it is a smooth and easy read. Its ideas often leave you nodding along and asking yourself: “Why didn’t I think of that?” Some of the insights feel like common sense, but only after you encounter them, like when one of the RPS organizers says: “We understood a basic principle that is often lost in political projects: that a less-than-perfect agreement achieved with genuine consensus and mutual trust, real participation, can be far superior to a technically ‘better’ proposal imposed through division or maneuvering.” This book often made me wonder why leftist movements have not already been working like RPS. Perhaps it’s time to! Will Froberg is a DSA member and former editor of The Detroit Socialist. He writes about post-capitalist vision and strategy and works as a data architect for the City of Detroit. It’s not a secret that Mike Rogers supports the Iran war. Since Donald Trump launched the war six months ago, the Michigan Republican candidate for Senate called Iran “an imminent threat to our country” that “must not have a nuclear weapon,” said the war was “absolutely right” and “a win-win-win for us,” believes “we are better off today than we were when it started,” and has said he “doesn’t mind” the idea of giving Trump congressional authorization to keep the war going. “I was for all of this when it wasn’t very cool to be for all of this,” he boasted last year, after Trump launched his first attack on the country. Rogers wasn’t exaggerating. A review of the public record shows that Rogers has been pushing for war with Iran for at least two decades, since all the way back when George W. Bush was mulling it. Rogers used his perch on the House Select Committee on Intelligence, which he chaired from 2011 to 2015, to make a host of dubious claims about Iran’s nuclear ambitions and capabilities, hype the threat it posed to the United States, and undermine efforts at diplomacy that could have prevented the current conflict. At one point, when Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu launched a pro-war pressure campaign virtually identical to the one in the lead-up to this year, Rogers chided then-President Barack Obama for his refusal to bend to it, and he later supported the Israeli leader’s attempt to undermine the US president’s successful attempt at diplomacy to resolve the standoff with Iran. At one point, his advocacy for an aggressive approach toward Iran saw him make common cause with a terrorist group responsible for killing Americans. The Rogers campaign has not responded to a request for comment. Rogers’s decades-long hawkish record could prove a liability in his Michigan Senate bid, his second tilt at the seat after falling just short in 2024. The Iran war’s ripple effects have hit the state hard, with the economic impact expected to top $2 billion. West Michigan families are paying an average of $15–$20 a week more for gas, while the state’s farmers, nearly half of whom already can’t afford the fertilizer they need, are looking at paying as much as $22,000 more this season on average. This history has largely escaped notice in the midst of an election season that has been intensely focused on politicians’ past statements and associations, even as officials’ support for the current war has become a major election issue in other races. Mike Rogers Has a Difficult Relationship With Facts While serving in Congress, Rogers was repeatedly criticized for spreading misinformation to the effect that Iran was making or dangerously close to having a nuclear bomb, misinformation that was likened to the Bush administration’s fraudulent case for war with Iraq. In 2006, as chair of the House Subcommittee on Intelligence, Rogers signed off on a staff report charging that “Iran is seeking nuclear weapons,” and that it even “likely” had research capability into chemical weapons and “probably” had a biological weapons program. Painting Iran as a direct threat to the United States, the report stated that Iran would have a nuke “sometime in the beginning to the middle of the next decade,” and cast doubt on the sincerity of Iran’s outreach, made just a day before, for “serious negotiations” with the United States. “It’s beyond a shadow of doubt for me that they are trying to stall for more time to continue their uranium enrichment and the building of their nuclear program,” Rogers said in the wake of the report. In reality, he said, across the intelligence world, “we’re all pretty much coming to the conclusion that they are aggressively pursuing that technology.” With hindsight, we know none of these claims were true. After six months of war with the United States and counting, there’s been no evidence that Iran has deployed chemical or biological weapons. Yet this didn’t stop Rogers from claiming otherwise. Between 2006 and 2026, Rogers publicly referred to Iran’s “nuclear weapons program” or charged that it was pursuing or close to having a nuclear weapon dozens of times, according to Jacobin’ s review of the public record. His four-year tenure as House intelligence chair lent these dubious claims added weight, including after he left Congress in 2015, when he continued to make such claims on networks like CNN. At times, he came under heavy criticism for playing fast and loose with the facts. Rogers was forced to acknowledge to CNN that the claim in the caption was false. Americans are wary of AI data centers, and politicians have taken note (much faster than they usually do). In July, New York Governor Kathy Hochul imposed the country’s first statewide moratorium . Weeks later, Republican Texas Governor Greg Abbott — a longtime data center recruiter — paused new grid connections pending an audit . And just this month, Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro — who celebrated Amazon’s data center investment as recently as last year — signed an executive order marketed as the nation’s strictest guardrails on the industry. But while opposition to data centers grows, the labor movement remains divided, for obvious reasons. The data centers are creating jobs (at least, right now), while AI threatens to eliminate jobs in the future. And those jobs largely belong to different sets of people. The Building Trades So far, the building trades have been supportive. In May, the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers (IBEW) urged its members to lobby Congress against any ban on data center construction. Several weeks ago, Don Slaiman of IBEW Local 26 took to The New York Times to argue that data centers have delivered tens of thousands of well-paid blue-collar careers, and that communities should be welcoming them. It’s not hard to see why. Data centers now account for 2.3 percent of all US construction spending, and spending has more than quadrupled since 2020. Believe it or not, tech companies often use union labor on their projects (despite their best efforts not to; I wrote recently about Meta’s attempts to skirt union labor in data center construction). An Associated General Contractors survey estimates that data center construction is about one-third union, which is three times the construction industry’s overall union rate. And according to reports from the trades (so take it with a grain of salt), this is growing. Union locals report doubling apprentice classes and expanding training centers to keep up with demand, and North America’s Building Trades Unions (NABTU) hit record membership in 2025. In March, NABTU announced partnerships with OpenAI, BlackRock, and Meta; in April, NABTU signed a labor agreement with OpenAI and Oracle to build the multibillion-dollar Stargate campus. Roughly 700 union tradespeople are already working on the site in Michigan, with thousands more expected to join. The leaders themselves aren’t shy about praising the job opportunities proffered by data centers. Robert Wilson of IBEW Local 7 in western Massachusetts described a proposed hyperscale facility as “a generational type project for our geographical area.” “At a time when educators are asking young people to consider the building trades over college,” he told me, jobs like these “could enable many young workers the ability to save for a down payment on their first home.” Rob Bair, president of the Pennsylvania State Building and Construction Trades Council, made the same case: data center work “has allowed members steady employment with overtime work which allows them to live the middle-class lifestyle,” plus union-managed health care and a real pension waiting at the end. And Dan McConnell, business manager of the Minneapolis Building and Construction Trades Council, told me his members “take pride in building the infrastructure that keeps Minneapolis competitive.” Unions Against Data Centers? And still, there is another wing of the labor movement that wants to pump the brakes. National Nurses United (NNU) formally endorsed the Bernie Sanders–Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez AI Data Center Moratorium Act. NNU copresident Jamie Brown called the unchecked growth of data centers “a public health crisis, an environmental crisis, and a workers’ rights crisis.” The American Association of University Professors endorsed the bill too. And the opposition runs deeper than national endorsements. Thomas Meyer — deputy political director of Food & Water Watch, which organizes against data center projects — rattled off local examples: a teachers’ union and UNITE HERE local in Seattle (United Auto Workers 4121 later joined), Communications Workers of America locals in New Jersey, UNITE HERE in Philadelphia. Caught in the middle are some of the labor movement’s most prominent leaders. Association of Flight Attendants–CWA President Sara Nelson stood with Sanders and expressed support for a slowdown , but AFA hasn’t formally endorsed the moratorium bill. American Federation of Teachers President Randi Weingarten stood at the same Sanders press conference and then announced that her union does not officially support the moratorium. Two of the most recognizable leaders in American labor were willing to stand behind Bernie Sanders at a podium, but not behind his bill. Colorado State Senator Cathy Kipp has had a front-row seat to this divide. She recently proposed a bill regulating data center construction. The bill drew opposition from construction unions like the pipefitters and the IBEW; some derided it as the “Wyoming Jobs Act.” As in, the jobs would flee forty miles north to Cheyenne. The state AFL-CIO opposed the bill too — though Kipp says the federation had factions pulling in different directions. Her bill died several months ago. Since then, she told me, the conversation has shifted. At a conference earlier this summer in which local leaders were expressing support for new data center construction, Kipp stood up and cited a poll showing 91 percent of Coloradans want guardrails on data centers. “You guys cannot put me in the position of: Is it you or my constituents?” she recalls telling the room. Several other legislators echoed her. Afterward, several labor leaders came up to thank her. Privately. “Some of them might be more nervous about saying it publicly, but it’s changing. A lot of people understand that their jobs are potentially going to be replaced,” she says. The union leaders who support data center construction are about as wary of AI as the rest of us. But they’re also up against cratering public opinion, combined with some misinformation. To be clear, this has not turned into any kind of inter-labor war. At least, not publicly. Even Meyer — who professionally organizes against data center projects — told me he has “no quarrels with a union or any other organization working on behalf of their members.” McConnell, from the other side, expressed a similar sentiment: he respects the unions that support moratoria. In his words: “This should not be framed as one group of unions against another.” In fairness to McConnell, when I began this research, I wondered if that’s what I might find. But I didn’t. The union leaders who support data center construction are about as wary of AI as the rest of us. But they’re also up against cratering public opinion, combined with some misinformation. Consider the belief that AI data centers consume inordinate amounts of water — an idea so memed it made its way into a Jason Kelce ad. Wilson described a city council meeting in Westfield, Massachusetts, where a city councilor gave a presentation arguing that a proposed facility would destroy the aquifer beneath the city. The facility in question would be cooled by a closed loop of glycol — the same fluid, recirculating indefinitely — and wouldn’t draw water from the aquifer. When the public was invited to comment, “the developer for the proposed data center tried to address the concerns with water but the council president immediately shut him off and said the discussion could only be about the moratorium,” Wilson told me. “As an observer at the meeting, it appeared the one year moratorium was going to be pushed through without allowing any debate.” Wilson is correct: the concerns about water consumption are often overstated, though not negligible. The most widely cited estimate , from Lawrence Berkeley National Lab, puts data centers’ total water consumption at roughly 230 billion gallons in 2023 — a figure that includes the water consumed by the power plants feeding them, which is most of it. This amounts to less than 1 percent of national water consumption. As Robin Gaster of the Information Technology and Innovation Foundation wrote in July: “There is no universal national water problem.” The concern, of course, is that the number is growing, and that some areas are higher risk than others. We certainly cannot write off the concerns about AI’s water usage. Still, given how far the water consumption idea has spread, it’s understandable why a union head in an area that’s not at risk for drought would be frustrated. Wilson also called worries about bright lights and heat islands “nonsense” (data centers, he noted, are windowless warehouses full of servers). McConnell made the same point: many of the impacts people fear — water consumption, noise, higher utility rates — are design choices, not inevitabilities, which is exactly why he wants enforceable standards. In his words: “Strong rules do not block good projects. They help us identify which projects are actually good.” Several leaders also noted that data centers face an unfair critique about their impermanence. Construction work is, by nature, temporary. Nobody faults a hospital because the people who built it were eventually out of a job. As McConnell told me: “Lawyers, consultants and many other professionals build stable careers by moving from project to project; the building trades are no different.” Support for Regulation At the same time, the labor leaders I spoke with were hardly AI cheerleaders. Bair’s working groups in Pennsylvania are negotiating agreements to limit AI in call centers, hospitals, and classrooms. In his words: “We realize there is a time and place for AI but not at the expense of employees. AI is accelerating the displacement, but we’ve seen it coming in many shapes and forms.” McConnell told me his members raise concerns about AI, but that he doesn’t think moratoria are the solution. “Stopping construction by itself does not create an AI policy or protect workers from automation.” Even his members’ support for the build-out has limits, he was careful to add: “Enthusiasm does not mean blind support.” It’s important to mention that their skepticism collides with a lack of options. The federal government has spent decades declining to seriously invest in our country. Joe Biden’s Bipartisan Infrastructure Law was a partial step, but it was nowhere near enough: even after it passed, the American Society of Civil Engineers still projected a $3.7 trillion gap over the next ten years between planned investment and “a state of good repair.” And that assumes current funding levels hold; the law’s authorizations expire in 2026. There is no New Deal on offer to employ the trades for a generation. And so, the trades are stuck in an impossible position, one they’ve been in before. In a sense, none of this is new. Emergent technology often requires a significant upfront labor investment while threatening jobs in the long run. In the words of Todd Vachon, a labor studies professor at Rutgers: “Any legislation that phases out one form of technology in favor of another is going to draw opposition from the union supporting the technology being eliminated, while the union representing workers manufacturing the new technology will be supportive of it.” Anne Lofaso — a law professor at the University of Cincinnati — cited coal as “a very tight analogy.” The well-paid union mining jobs were always partly myth: many weren’t union, many weren’t safe, and the jobs disappeared as the technology improved. “Data centers are similar,” she told me: a promise of good union jobs, hazards for the workers, environmental costs for the communities making the greatest sacrifices. But there’s only so far historical analogies can go. In Vachon’s words: “What feels different with AI is that this friction is pretty broadly felt and breaks labor into two distinct categories of who is and who isn’t threatened by AI, and it mostly falls along the line of blue-collar vs. white-collar workers.” Vachon hit upon something that’s always bothered me about the data center backlash. Technology — almost by definition — has always replaced jobs. And for centuries, the professional class largely filed it under progress. Inevitable, even. But now that the automation is coming for people who went to college, pausing it has become a mainstream idea. If lawyers were asked to give up a decade of work to protect electricians’ jobs, would they? History suggests probably not. That doesn’t mean we don’t need a pause. But construction isn’t the root of the problem. The root of the problem is AI. People don’t like it, don’t trust it, and don’t know what it’s going to do to our society. Data centers have existed for decades; we don’t see widespread protests outside a hospital’s server room (the erroneous conflation of all data centers with AI data centers was cited by multiple union leaders). In the same Annenberg survey, 68 percent of Americans said the government has done “too little” to regulate AI. A July Emerson College poll found 63 percent of voters concerned about AI, versus 14 percent excited. For Meyer, the reason so many workers oppose data centers — including, per one recent poll, a majority of union households in New York — comes down to AI itself. Specifically, who gains and who pays. “The benefits are so concentrated and the costs are so massive,” he told me. “People don’t like Big Tech billionaires trying to bully or bribe to get what they want.” The people he talks to “feel like AI is being forced on all of society” and see fighting data centers locally as “the best way to make an impact and exert some agency.” People are scared of AI, and we’re so far away from even the most basic regulation. In July — just days after OpenAI disclosed that two of its models escaped a testing environment and hacked another AI company (cool!) — Congressmen Ted Lieu (D-CA) and Nathaniel Moran (R-TX) introduced the bipartisan AI Kill Switch Act, which is exactly what it sounds like: a requirement that the most powerful AI systems be built with an off switch. Imagine Ford trying to sell a car without a brake. I generally hate the term “commonsense regulation” (who doesn’t think their own ideas are common sense?), but come on. This is common sense. A month later, the bill has gone nowhere . It’s challenging to even talk about AI without answers to basic questions like “How do you turn it off?” “Is it a bubble?” “Will it tank the economy?” “Will it channel wealth exclusively to the top 1% while everyone else suffers?” “Will it kill us all?” AI regulation is about far more than just the existential threat: it’s also about whether it will perpetuate America’s destructive and expanding economic inequality. There is no mystery about why we haven’t yet regulated the AI companies directly. In what I believe will be remembered as the most iconic image of Trump’s second term, tech CEOs lined the second row of his inauguration, ahead of his own incoming cabinet. They had paid for the view, a million dollars apiece to the inaugural fund, and the corruption has only continued since then. The return on investment has been excellent. Republicans tried twice to attach a ten-year moratorium on state AI laws to must-pass legislation; when Congress wouldn’t cooperate, Donald Trump signed an executive order directing the Department of Justice to challenge state AI laws and threatened to withhold federal broadband money from states that regulate the technology. The federal government is not merely declining to put guardrails on AI; it is actively dismantling the ones states try to build. We are a country understandably scared of AI, with a federal government unwilling to regulate it. And so, we’ve found the only available option: halting the AI data centers. It’s a smart strategy: stopping construction is famously easy in America — just ask Ezra Klein. Unlike regulating Trump’s buddies, it’s working: in the first quarter of this year alone, community opposition blocked or delayed at least seventy-five data center projects worth roughly $130 billion, about as much as all of last year combined. The industry is expanding fast enough that record construction and record obstruction are happening simultaneously. And public opposition keeps climbing, as support for a moratorium grows. But it’s worth getting specific about what a moratorium entails. The Sanders-AOC bill would freeze construction of new large AI data centers until the government passes federal AI guardrails. And I definitely support those guardrails (I would go so far as to call them “common sense”), but we should be honest: realistically, that isn’t happening before January 2029. Under the proposed moratorium, existing facilities would keep running, and the white-collar workers at Anthropic and OpenAI would carry on. The pause lands entirely on the people who build. In short: construction workers are being asked to pay for the sins of Sam Altman, because the government is too corrupt to regulate him directly. McConnell calls the moratorium “a very blunt instrument,” and he’s right. The question is whether anything sharper is available. A Kill Switch I’m torn myself. I understand the building trades’ position, and I understand what a national moratorium would mean for their members. But I’m also very uneasy about AI, and I see the argument for using anything in our toolkit to slow it down, blunt or not. In Lofaso’s words: “As someone who likes to see scientific progress, I would like to see unions negotiate job retraining, social assistance, etc. But AI feels different and potentially more dangerous. I wish we could have a moratorium just to think through the ramifications of AI and how to create safe AI and protect workers and their jobs.” Still, I found cause for hope. The version of the fight that ends well is already underway. The trades are asking for guardrails themselves. Bair’s council backed the principles behind Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro’s order regulating data center construction. Wilson wants rates and water regulated by law, not by promise. McConnell wants project labor agreements, ratepayer safeguards, and enforceable community benefits. The tech companies had a better chance of skirting union labor before their popularity cratered: our AI overlords are now on their back foot, scrambling to get communities and elected officials to support data center construction, which means it’s the ideal time for the labor unions to make demands — about safety, job protection, profit distribution, and more. Vachon suggested something similar: that labor exchange its support for guardrails. In his words, they would say: “We’ll only build these if we can be reasonably assured they won’t kill us.” That sounds good to me; I would prefer AI not kill us all. I don’t want to suggest that communities should stop fighting unwelcome data centers. But union leadership can be an important ally in this battle, even leaders who don’t support a moratorium. This Congress appears unwilling to pass a bill requiring AI to come with an off switch. But the trades control something the industry can’t route around: whether the infrastructure gets built. Right now, that’s the closest thing to a kill switch this country has.

The United Auto Workers Are Voting for Their Top Leadership

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Luis Feliz Leon ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-27T14:32:58+00:00

United Auto Workers (UAW) members are voting by mail ballot for their top officers, for the second time in the union’s ninety-one years. Ballots went out August 21 and are due back by October 5. The contest will decide whether President Shawn Fain, who became famous for leading 2023’s “ stand-up strike ” against the Big Three automakers, will be able to continue the union’s about-face from its previous employer-friendly ways. William Hughes, a member of UAW Local 249 at Ford Kansas City, said the election is about maintaining the union’s momentum in winning gains for the membership. “During my twenty-seven years in the union, I’ve never seen anything even close to the amount of gains that we’ve been getting in all these contracts — not just in the Big Three,” said Hughes. “I built seats before I came to Ford, and under no circumstances that I can recall would the parts sector ever strike these plants and try to really push for bigger contracts.” Fain was first elected in 2023 on a platform of “no corruption, no tiers, no concessions.” He is running on a record of successful contract fights at the Big Three and elsewhere and a pledge to keep organizing parts suppliers and nonunion auto plants in the South. His opponents have downplayed the gains made in the last three years, asserting that any president could have won them. Instead, they have focused on strife at union headquarters and unflattering reports issued by the federal monitor, Neil Barofsky, who was appointed in 2021 to oversee the union after massive corruption was uncovered and a dozen officers went to jail. New Smiles Fain’s strongest base of support is in locals where members have experienced the new willingness to fight management. JoAnna McClenathan is a senior designer at General Dynamics Electric Boat in Connecticut and a steering committee member of the reform network UAW Member Action , which is backing Fain. She credits Fain for opening up bargaining and creating a new Department of Bargaining Strategies that helped her local score a “record contract” with 30 percent raises. Fain set up that department to research employers and involve members in escalating campaigns. “During our contract fight last year, he came down and met with us, and he was a big reason we had the resources we did,” she said: We had essentially been bargaining behind closed doors since the 1980s. For so long, a lot of us in our local felt powerless: this is what they’ve given us, so we’re just going to vote for this. Last year, the rank and file was able to use our voice and become more engaged, and that’s due to the new strategy. Similarly at American Axle, an auto parts supplier in Three Rivers, Michigan, members are happy with the results of a two-week strike in June. They will be making $30 an hour by 2030, up from a top pay of $22. “We’re starting to see a change in the parking lot,” said bargaining committee member Jon Krause. “People are now able to buy cars that can get them to work. Some are looking to buy their first homes. We’ve seen a few people smiling that we haven’t seen smile in years.” Krause predicts Three Rivers members will vote for Fain’s United UAW slate. Legal services worker Brian Sullivan, a member of UAW Local 2325, said workers in his industry have set their sights on bargaining sector-wide. They were inspired by Fain’s industry-wide strategy with the automakers, he said: “We want a UAW leadership that’s going to back us as we take similarly big swings.” Five Challengers Fain’s five challengers spent most of the union’s presidential debate July 30 attacking him. They are Rich Boyer, vice president for the Stellantis Department; Tricia Geiger, an international servicing rep; Stellantis worker Brian Keller; Mack Truck worker Will Lehman; and Greg Mooney, recording secretary from Local 2147. Of the challengers, Boyer and Keller have the most name recognition. Keller won 14 percent of the vote in 2022 and spearheaded failed efforts to recall Fain last year. He finished fourth out of five candidates in his local union’s (Local 1248) delegate election this spring. Boyer and Fain ran on the same slate in the last election, as did Vice President Mike Booth and Secretary-Treasurer Margaret Mock, also now opposing Fain’s slate on their own slate, Integrity. Their differences have been the subject of long reports by the monitor. Fain accused Boyer of failing to enforce the contract at Stellantis and of responsibility for a new attendance program that members hated, as well as trying to secure union jobs for family members — standard practice in the old UAW. Boyer has consistently maintained that it’s the union’s job to enforce company attendance policies. In a video message to members about the 2023 contract ratification, he bemoaned the overuse of medical leave by people who “don’t want to do the work” and implied that plants would close if the union didn’t help fix attendance. The union’s executive board voted to relieve Boyer and Mock (who had also tried to put a family member on the payroll) of some of their assignments. Fain and other board members said Mock was using her office to block projects and retaliate against staffers she disliked. The monitor agreed with Boyer and Mock that their removal was “retaliation,” and they were reinstated. Earlier the monitor had called Fain to lobby against the executive board’s December 2023 call for a ceasefire in Gaza. Fain angrily refused to back down, and he charges the monitor with having it in for him ever since. Barofsky accuses Fain of seeking favors for his fiancée and her sister. “The truth when it comes to Boyer is that I didn’t want him running the Stellantis Department because he wasn’t doing a good job for our members,” Fain told The Nation magazine. “I wouldn’t let him hire family members into UAW positions, and I wouldn’t stand by while he bargained concessions with Stellantis and failed to enforce our contract.” Boyer’s supporters say he is outspoken and a fighter. His main pledges in his campaign statement in the UAW magazine were to lower union dues to two hours a month, reduce the staff and salaries at union headquarters, and hire only from within the membership. Delegates at this summer’s convention voted to keep dues at 2.5 hours to build up the strike fund and provide resources for aggressive organizing and bargaining campaigns. The dueling ugly accusations have fed member cynicism, the prevailing attitude before 2023. Fain’s opponents are largely focused on the “infighting” among the leadership. “I don’t like the way [Fain] treats people,” said Jennifer Miller, recording secretary at Local 1700 near Detroit, who backs Boyer. “I don’t like the arrogance of him. I don’t think he deserves credit for everything he says he’s done.” In the last election, Fain, who was running against then President Ray Curry, benefited from a powerful anti-incumbent sentiment, stemming from years of frustration with concessions, plant closures, and corruption at the union’s top levels. This time, he won’t have that advantage. J. J. Barbosa, a millwright at Stellantis’s Jeep plant in Toledo, has backed Keller, a prolific Facebook presence, in all three of his runs. He sees Keller as tenacious and consistent, and he is “disgusted that there are still tiers.” He says all the incumbents are guilty of “cronyism and nepotism.” Momentum In interviews with members across the union, it was common to hear that they were impressed that Fain showed up at locals to talk with members and supported their contract fights. Carlos Gonzalez-Rios, a twenty-seven-year worker at the General Motors engine plant in Buffalo, New York, said he met Fain twice. He rattled off a few of the gains he’s seen, including a pay increase of 25 percent. “We got COLA back,” he said. “We don’t have endless temporary workers for five or six years.” He also cited the union’s win at Volkswagen in Tennessee, where workers voted to join the UAW and won a first contract earlier this year — becoming the first unionized foreign-owned auto assembly plant in the South. What’s at stake in the election, Gonzalez-Rios argues, is whether this momentum will continue. “I’d rather go with the guy that’s been proven and has the leadership skills that brought us what we have right now,” he said. “And we still have issues that we are working towards — we want health care for all at GM after retirement.” “I’ve held people accountable and that pisses some people off, I understand that,” said Fain during the presidential debate. “But running on a platform of ‘the monitor said this or that,’ that’s not going to deliver for the membership.” Changing Alliances The union’s 400,000 active members and nearly 600,000 retirees will be electing all fourteen positions on the union’s International Executive Board. Fain’s United UAW slate, which is contesting thirteen of the seats, includes candidates who ran against him in the last election as members of the “Administration Caucus” that had run the union since the 1940s and overseen multiple waves of concessions in recent decades. Some have been won over by the impressive contract results that Fain’s bold approach has achieved; others may be inclined to go with the one in power. In early August, three ex–UAW presidents, Ray Curry, Rory Gamble, and Ron Gettelfinger, attempted to resurrect the old ruling caucus by releasing a letter backing Geiger, Mock, and other Fain opponents. Fain shot back, reminding members of Gettelfinger’s “ sellout deal ” in 2007 that cut wages in half for new hires and created the two-tier system. Four regional directors on Fain’s slate are running unopposed: Mark DePaoli in Region 1A, Mike Miller in Region 6, Tim Smith in Region 8, and Brandon Mancilla in Region 9A. The other five director seats and three vice president seats are all contested, as is the secretary-treasurer slot. Candidates need a majority to avoid a runoff, and votes will be tabulated October 6.

Democrats Are Losing the Billionaire Primary

Democrats enter this year’s midterms with a host of political advantages: a deeply unpopular Republican president, a faltering economy , and a fired-up base. But Republicans hold a clear edge in at least one crucial area. With Election Day less than three months out, fundraising data shows the GOP with vastly more cash on hand. That financial advantage stems entirely from well-funded Republican super PACs and party committees, which together hold over a billion dollars — nearly three times the reserves of comparable Democratic groups. The GOP’s enormous war chest has been filled primarily by corporate and billionaire donors, who overwhelmingly favor Republicans this cycle. An analysis by reporters at the Washington Post found that this year’s top fifty donors have already contributed around $1.6 billion, with more than a billion of that going to pro-Republican groups and another quarter billion funding “bipartisan” special interest groups. Another report published in March by the advocacy group Americans for Tax Fairness found that roughly 80 percent of all spending by the fifty biggest billionaire families has gone to Republican candidates and causes. Meanwhile, at the battered Democratic National Committee (DNC), big donors have “gone on strike,” as a recent Wall Street Journal headline put it. The latest numbers from July show the Republican National Committee sitting on $130 million compared to $16 million at the DNC, which is also $18 million in debt. This clear billionaire preference for Republicans continues a trend that became unmistakable two years ago, when some of the country’s wealthiest individuals spent record sums to boost Donald Trump and other Republican candidates. A New York Times analysis found that the three hundred wealthiest contributors in 2024 accounted for one-fifth of all federal donations, spending a princely sum of $3 billion. That dwarfs the pre– Citizens United era, when billionaires accounted for just 0.3 percent of federal election spending in 2008. The explosion in billionaire political spending has coincided with a broader rightward shift among the country’s oligarchs. In 2016, billionaires spent approximately $684 million on federal elections — a record at the time — with only a slight majority of that going to Republicans. Two cycles later, billionaire contributions favored Republicans by a staggering five-to-one margin. These two trends have, if anything, accelerated since Trump’s return to power last year, with oligarchs leaning into their reactionary politics and opening up their wallets for various conservative and anti-progressive causes. Nowhere has this been more apparent than in California, where the state’s top fifteen billionaire donors have already poured hundreds of millions into this year’s state and federal elections, according to the labor and community coalition California Common Good. As of early July, the top four California-based donors alone — Sergey Brin, Marc Andreessen, Ben Horowitz, and Chris Larsen — had given more than $200 million. Those numbers have continued to climb in the months since, with much of the surge fueled by billionaire opposition to Proposition 40, the wealth tax on the state ballot this year. Eight of the top fifteen donors have contributed to the political committee Building a Better California, which exists chiefly to defeat the measure. California’s biggest political spender this year, Google cofounder Sergey Brin, has funneled more than $100 million into the organization, while his fellow oligarchs have contributed an additional $50 million . How the Deal Fell Apart The billionaire backlash in California underscores how the growing force of economic populism inside the Democratic coalition has driven plutocrats to the right. A decade ago, billionaires were far more circumspect in their political pronouncements and spending decisions, often hedging their bets and splitting their contributions between establishment Republicans and Democrats. Though wealthy donors have historically leaned right, neoliberal Democrats like Barack Obama and Hillary Clinton were broadly acceptable to America’s plutocrats, especially if they hailed from a blue state like California. While these Democratic leaders would occasionally call out reactionary billionaires like the Koch brothers, they were positively chummy with liberal-coded billionaires and steered clear of any policies that might actually challenge their power. Venture capitalist Marc Andreessen has often invoked what he has called the “deal” to explain that earlier arrangement. According to the billionaire investor, the deal amounted to an unwritten pact between Silicon Valley’s tech elites and Democratic leaders dating back to the Clinton era: entrepreneurs and investors like himself could grow fabulously wealthy and face little scrutiny, so long as they devoted some of their fortunes to liberal causes and philanthropy. By donating to charity and supporting “fashionable” social issues like same-sex marriage, Andreessen observed, the billionaire could “absolve” himself of all his “sins.” This was “just something everybody understood.” In Andreessen’s telling, the deal’s collapse began about a decade ago, with the millennial generation’s embrace of “radical” politics in light of the financial crisis and the Iraq War. The “kids turned on capitalism in a very fundamental way,” he complained to the New York Times last year, pointing to Occupy Wall Street and the youth vote for Bernie Sanders. Things only grew worse under Joe Biden, whose administration waged what Andreessen described as a “terror campaign” against billionaires and fledgling industries like crypto and AI. Overwrought as it is, Andreessen’s sketch of the past decade still captures a real shift that began with the 2016 Sanders campaign and carried through the Biden years. Though a centrist by nature, Biden appointed genuine economic populists and progressives to leadership roles in his administration. Under figures like Lina Khan, Julie Su, and Jennifer Abruzzo, the administration embraced a far more populist posture on antitrust and labor, reviving long-dormant antitrust enforcement and advancing policies to strengthen unionization efforts. These and other shifts infuriated oligarchs like Elon Musk and Andreessen, but for the latter the administration’s proposed “Billionaire Minimum Income Tax” — which would have taxed unrealized capital gains — was the “ final straw .” Jumping on the MAGA train has paid off for oligarchs like Andreessen and Musk. The Trump administration has more or less reversed the Biden-era policies and enforcement actions they most opposed, and it has openly advanced a billionaire-first agenda that has accelerated the country’s slide into full-blown oligarchy. Big Money or Mass Politics America’s oligarchs have had remarkable success in reshaping the nation’s government and laws over the last twenty months, but their triumph has also awakened many to the breakdown of democracy. If modest efforts to rein in corporate and billionaire power sparked a furious oligarchic backlash in 2024, the oligarchs’ brazen power grab has since stirred a broader populist revolt. One of the clearest lessons of the 2024 election was that Democrats must embrace economic populism if they hope to win back the support of working-class voters, particularly in must-win battleground states. During her brief presidential run, former Vice President Kamala Harris adopted a more conciliatory posture toward corporate America and strongly hinted that her administration would back down from some of the more populist positions taken during the Biden years. That strategy helped Harris raise a great deal of cash and perhaps earned her a few dozen extra votes from “ Cheney Democrats .” But her retreat from economic populism and her refusal to condemn the genocide in Gaza likely cost her millions of votes in an election where a shift of a few hundred thousand could have changed the outcome. In the two years since, the appeal of economic populism has only grown, as voters have witnessed a hostile billionaire takeover of the US government. The Left’s winning streak in this year’s Democratic primaries has revealed a base that is hungry for candidates willing to challenge institutional power and “ fight the oligarchy .” There are other signs of the party’s shift toward economic populism. In a recent analysis of 280,000 fundraising emails sent by Democratic candidates since 2017, Stanford political economist Andrew Hall found that mentions of “billionaire” have soared since the start of Trump’s second term, almost all of them negative. As he observes, “Anti-billionaire fundraising tactics are now a mainstay of Democratic messaging.” What all of this means for the future of the Democratic Party is unclear. Even as populists and leftists continue to rack up victories in Democratic primaries across the country, the party’s leadership remains hostile to its left flank and cool toward economic populism. Where big donors still back Democratic or “bipartisan” candidates and causes, their money has mostly gone toward stopping the ascendant Left or killing populist proposals like the wealth tax. Special interest groups like the American Israel Public Affairs Comittee (AIPAC) and the pro-crypto super PAC Fairshake have already spent hundreds of millions on House and Senate races this cycle, with most of that going to undermine progressives. The good news is that big-donor money has clear limits, especially when it runs up against an increasingly frustrated electorate (this was perhaps most evident in 2024, when Kamala Harris actually outraised and outspent her opponent). Despite special interest groups like AIPAC spending heavily to block progressive candidates this cycle, most of those efforts have fallen short . Individual Democratic candidates have also been routinely outraising their Republican opponents thanks to a surge of smaller donations, narrowing the GOP’s overall financial advantage. Whether these trends will push more Democrats to embrace populism remains to be seen. What’s fairly certain is that billionaires will continue to flee the party as long as progressives and democratic socialists keep gaining ground inside the Democratic coalition. At a certain point, Democratic leaders will have more to lose by angering the Left than by alienating the megadonors who have already left the fold.

Over the last two decades, Caparezza has been one of the most original voices in Italian music. A constantly evolving rapper, he has a vast and loyal audience who have followed him through ever-changing concept albums mixing languages, musical genres, and imagery. His most recent album, Orbit Orbit , released last fall, is accompanied by a comic book written by him. In July, it won the Targa Tenco for best album of the year — the most prestigious award in Italian songwriting, and a rare one for a rapper. Caparezza (real name Michele Salvemini) is one of the few contemporary Italian musicians capable of combining commercial success, artistic experimentation, and political engagement. His songs echo through marches and demonstrations throughout Italy without him ever conforming to the aesthetic and linguistic stereotypes of the “left-wing singer.” While his albums strike mainstream success, he remains somehow in the shadows, far from the obsessive search for visibility. In his home region of Puglia there was some controversy when the album was released, as some politicians contrasted the empty polling stations of the regional elections, held around the same time, with the long queues for Caparezza’s book signings. A few days later, Jacopo Custodi met him in his hometown of Molfetta to talk about freedom, music, politics, and the “pathosphere” — a term the rapper coined to refer to the sphere of empathy and sensitivity, which we increasingly tend to drift away from. In the interview, Caparezza explains how he came to the Left as a young man despite a family background that pointed elsewhere, and the central role played in his political awakening by the local branch of the communist party Rifondazione Comunista, whose members, he says, “were the ones who did the most interesting things, they were the most sincere and honest local politicians.” Nationally Rifondazione has been a small and marginal force for many years, but in Molfetta it has always retained substantial support — to the point that the town has just elected as its new mayor Rifondazione’s candidate Manuel Minervini, at the head of a broad left coalition. When Custodi spoke with Caparezza, not long before Minervini’s election bid, he said he hoped that passionate people would come along and give the Left new energy. That hope has aged well: Molfetta is now one of few Italian towns with a communist mayor. Jacopo Custodi Your new album, Orbit Orbit , was released on October 31 together with a comic book. It is a hybrid work that seems to conclude a trilogy: Prisoner 709 spoke of mental imprisonment, Exuvia of escape, and Orbit Orbit of freedom. What kind of freedom are you talking about — and how achievable is it? Caparezza When we talk about freedom, we immediately open a Pandora’s box, because every idea of freedom also brings its own contradictions. Who is truly free, which society is truly free: Western society or those that oppose it? How do we measure freedom? We immediately head into a quagmire. I have always felt like a “prisoner of Earth” — as we all are — because I have always seen the planet as a beautiful prison from which there is no escape, and gravity as a kind of electronic bracelet. So, I asked myself: What has been a true form of freedom in my life? Because in the end, the album is very personal. The only obvious answer was imagination. For me, imagination is an authentic form of freedom: you can’t forbid someone from imagining something better or worse. The only constraint is that imagination takes reality as its reference point. I’ll take an example from Karl Jaspers, a German philosopher who studied Vincent van Gogh and concluded that his creative act was a reaction to illness. According to Jaspers, the creative act is like a pearl: just as the shell creates the pearl, so the human being creates the artwork. But the shell creates the pearl when a grain of sand or something irritating gets into it: to numb that pain, it covers it with mother-of-pearl and the pearl is born. All pearls have something disturbing inside them. In my case, reality is the disturbing thing, imagination is the mother-of-pearl, and the pearl is the work — in this case, Orbit Orbit — that I bring into the world. So, for me, imagination is a form of reaction to reality: freedom but also resistance. I don’t think it’s “pure” freedom; in my case, it’s really a form of resistance. And Orbit Orbit is, in comics, the onomatopoeia of imagination: that’s why the album is called that. Jacopo Custodi An orbit is a movement but also a repeated course around some center. For a project that talks about freedom, were you more interested in the idea of escaping the system or learning to move within its gravitational fields? Caparezza You have to learn to move within it. It’s the same in comics: the panels are called “cages,” and freedom is exercised within these cages. Escape, for me, was the theme of Exuvia , the previous album, and represented the transition from one state to another. In part it really happened, because at a certain point I became a comic book writer, so this transition was achieved. But I realized that absolute escape is not possible. Imagination must be able to coexist with reality and move within these micro and macro cages. Jacopo Custodi On your latest album, there is a beautiful song in which you talk about how we have lost empathy, how we have left the “pathosphere.” How do you think we can return to it? Caparezza It’d hard to answer, because mine is more of a desire than a recipe. I’ve noticed that as we grow up, everything around us becomes increasingly unsustainable. To save myself, to avoid being constantly hurt, I realized that I too was starting to build barriers around myself, to pretend not to see, to numb myself, to leave the pathosphere. I think it’s a mistake that many people share today, in the age of social media, where you are constantly under attack. And often the people doing the attacking can’t take the blows when they suffer them. So you defend yourself by creating a shell of cynicism. That’s very dangerous, because when you lose faith in humanity, you’re on the brink of losing your own humanity. The real danger is becoming insensitive: no longer recognizing the beautiful things and people around you. But you can only recognize them if you also accept their negative side. I believe this is a great lesson in life, one that I learned as I reached my fifties. So you have to be empathetic, but empathy is a violent act, in the sense that when you really put yourself in someone else’s shoes, it’s not easy to bear the weight and the contradictions. We have to accept the fight with reality and with what hurts us instead of numbing ourselves. I know that there are also people who are a little disappointed by my “shift” on these issues after albums that were considered more political. But for me, politics is not necessarily about being a tribune of the people; feelings are also part of politics. In fact, I would say that the politics of feeling is the most authentic, the strongest, the most engaging. Jacopo Custodi This introspection is something you have in common with other politically committed Italian artists — I’m thinking, for instance, of the comic book artist Zerocalcare — who, like you, often start from their own existence to talk about the world. Do you think it’s a sign of the times? Do we need to start from autobiographical experiences to talk about society at large? Caparezza For me, that happened as I grew up. When I was young, I felt like I was an integral part of a world that was moving in the present. Then, at a certain point, the past began to peek out inside me. I don’t know why, perhaps because there is ever less of a future ahead of us. And somehow the past reminds you that you have been many different things, that you have had many lives. So I started to see myself as a journey and no longer as a young man living in his social context, fighting for his present and for a better collective future. I began to analyze all the stages of my journey, which is not only made up of beautiful places but also of horrible places, like all of our lives. And this is probably a necessity; I see it as an adult necessity. I felt that I no longer wanted to talk, that I no longer wanted to point the finger, but I wanted to turn it toward myself, to understand what had happened in my life. That doesn’t mean that I stopped thinking about community; I simply thought that before getting up on the pulpit, I needed to better understand who I am on the pulpit. Because then what you say from the pulpit becomes more credible. Jacopo Custodi In an interview, you said that the first album you bought was by Run DMC and, years later, once you were well-established, you also collaborated with Darryl McDaniels. What influence has US rap — and more generally US music — had on you? Caparezza US rap was the epiphany of my musical life. As a child, I never wanted to be a singer, because I was very introverted — and I’m still very introverted, even though I’ve worked on myself. As a child I wanted to be a cartoonist, because I imagined myself in a room away from my surroundings, just me and my imagination. Then at some point something happened: I saw Run DMC’s “You Be Illin’” on TV. The image of these three guys in tracksuits rapping, with one of them making sounds by putting his hands on vinyl records — and I had a lot of my father’s vinyl records at home — struck me deeply. It was like using an object for something it wasn’t “born” for, a bit like Frank Zappa when he started playing the wheels of a bicycle on a TV show. What’s more, as a child I was fascinated by the sound of words: I loved tongue twisters. Seeing that rhythmic cadence, that musical way of speaking, was a revelation like the road to Damascus. I didn’t even know it was rap or who those three guys really were. I went to a record store in Molfetta and asked insistently for the Run DMC album that had “You Be Illin’.” “There was no internet, the record came from the United States and wasn’t distributed in Italy, so I went back to that store countless times to see if it had arrived. When it finally did, I remember standing at the cash register, with the owner taking his time doing the receipt and me eager to run home and listen to it all. For me, US rap was the big bang. Of course, talking about “US rap” is saying everything and nothing, because it started out one way and became something else entirely. For example, I was never drawn to gangsta rap: I started to dislike a certain type of attitude. But with Run DMC, and then with groups like Public Enemy and the Beastie Boys, every new album was like opening a box of toys. I started thinking that I could try my hand at rap when I first heard Frankie hi-nrg mc, someone who had found a way to translate that communicative power into Italian. As someone who works on collective imagery and emotions, how do you experience this political shift? What do you think it says about our country? Caparezza I believe that the Italian Communist Party was the most credible and serious party of all. It was a party in which there was also poetry. I don’t know how to put it. . . . Today, however, we live in a country that, in my opinion, is not right-wing but populist, where there is a disaffection with complexity. There is a general rejection of complexity, and not only in politics. Today when there is a complex problem — think of immigration — the simple answer is the one that takes root. The famous politics “of the gut” works better, because looking deeper into things is tiring. But if you refuse to look deeper, you are also impoverishing the cerebral mechanism that leads you to evolve as a person. So you begin to live in a Manichaean society. However, I don’t think this is a problem unique to Italy; we see it in the United States too. It is a widespread problem, perhaps fueled by the internet, where news stories become nothing more than clickbait headlines and algorithms feed polarization. This is impoverishing dialogue and our approach to life. Simplistic thinking is the serious problem of this historical moment which, mind you, is not the same thing as simplicity. Simplicity is a value, especially in popularizing things; simplification is the problem. Jacopo Custodi In recent years, there has been much discussion on the Left about the relationship between culture and politics. We have seen how focusing politics on this can produce problematic culture wars; and yet, in Italy, we have known ever since Antonio Gramsci how important cultural hegemony is for winning new rights. As an artist who does not overlook politics, what do you think of this relationship? Caparezza Let’s start with art: I think that in art, everything is permissible, and I say this categorically. In art, you absolutely cannot set limits of any kind. Artists will then take responsibility for what they say, but art is something other than community or life, so let’s say that I don’t want limits on art and creativity. I am against cancel culture. I am not in favor of taking things from the past and censoring them, because they are a testimony to the past and give strength to what is happening today. Today if you watch an old movie that mocks a certain group and compare it to a movie from today, where people are more careful, you understand that a step forward has been made. However, if you erase the testimony of the past, this evolution is lost. The same goes for statues of historical figures: I’m not in favor of tearing them down but of changing the way we talk about them. Otherwise, it’s like erasing parts of your own life. I’ve made mistakes in my life, but I can’t erase them, otherwise I would exit the pathosphere — to circle back to what we were discussing earlier. They must have had some meaning, even if only to make me a better person. We should look at society in the same way: as a human being that grows and changes. Obviously, in life and in politics, cultural battles are necessary and important. However, personally, I am in favor of conscious cultural revolutions: not that I wake up and decide that from today we are going to do things this way, because that is not how you really change people culturally. I prefer small collective steps forward that, once they become conscious, will at least hopefully become collectively recognized. Caparezza I don’t know, it seems to me that my approach to life derives more from who I am, from my character, than from where I was born. I also have a difficult relationship with the Catholic religion, which is very deeply rooted here. Sometimes, especially during the holidays, faith is experienced with a somewhat painful intensity, with the ostentation of pain. One good thing, however, is that aside from religion, in Molfetta and the surrounding towns people tend to play things down a lot, and this has helped me never to take myself too seriously, not to feel like a star. I have always played down my role, and fortunately I have never felt like “I’m the shit.” I think this is part of the Molfetta way of life. They were the ones who did the most interesting things; they were the most sincere and honest local politicians. I could hardly see myself voting for the center left today [as the Partito Democratico’s camp is widely called]. They seem too centrist to me, too much like the old Christian Democrats. Caparezza My favorite album is always the last one I made, because it’s the freshest, but also because every new album for me is like a death to be celebrated. When I release an album, I free myself from thoughts that I have struggled to put on paper for years, and so for me it’s a death to be celebrated. Maybe it’s my religious and Molfetta upbringing coming back! Jacopo Custodi Zohran Mamdani was also a rapper before he became mayor of New York City. I’m not one for simplistic views: among the people who choose to go into politics, there are always some who have a real fire inside them, and that should be recognized. It’s like in music: there are those who have that fire, but you might not find them in your Spotify playlist — you have to go looking for them. I take politics very seriously, and precisely because I take it seriously, I know I don’t have that fire. Over the years, I have been courted several times to run on some electoral list. I have always refused, also out of respect for politics. I would not have the skills, and above all I don’t have the desire. We need people on the left who are driven by genuine passion. I know some, and I have seen them gravitate toward that sphere. No one is completely incorruptible. Everyone makes mistakes, but if someone has a real fire and does it because they believe in it, then we need to put that person forward.
