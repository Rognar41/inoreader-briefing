The Uprising Wastes a Good Class-Struggle Story

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Jeremy Gong ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-19T17:00:45+00:00

In 1381, more than a hundred thousand poor English people rose up demanding an end to serfdom. During the uprising, they killed a coterie of hated government officials, and, in turn, the fourteen-year-old King Richard II massacred thousands of them. The best-remembered figure of the English Peasants’ Revolt is one of its leaders, John Ball, whose speeches and sermons captured the spirit of what would eventually, centuries later, develop into socialism. “When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then the gentleman?” he famously asked, meaning that in the Garden of Eden, the Bible’s first people labored only for their own sustenance, with no concepts of lords and serfs. As a socialist and fan of medieval period pieces, I was excited to see the new film The Uprising , which dramatizes the English Peasants’ Revolt and stars Andrew Garfield as a fed-up farmer-rebel. The subject matter has the makings of a thrilling class-struggle movie — and indeed, Garfield and director Paul Greengrass ( Captain Phillips , two films in the Bourne trilogy, and Bloody Sunday ) promoted the movie as a tale of resistance to oligarchy. As The Hollywood Reporter writes , “ The Uprising feels strikingly of the moment. Modern-day oligarchs wield outsized influence over elections and self-enriching policies. Working people, meanwhile, continue to struggle with the rising cost of living.” The film’s director and star express hope that The Uprising will be “galvanizing.” “We’ve had enough,” declared Greengrass. “I hope there’s some awakening,” Garfield told a live audience this month. “And I hope that if any billionaires watch it,” he continued, that “they’re scared shitless.” It’s a welcome sign that Hollywood wants to tell these kinds of stories. But far from giving hope that the masses of ordinary people can make social change, The Uprising condemns social movements as self-defeating, promotes a misguided faith in select elites, and ends with a nihilistic moral of individual revenge. Billionaires have nothing to fear from The Uprising . Medieval Class War The fourteenth century was a grim time for the common people of England. Kings threw thousands of lives away in a failed effort to take the French throne, known as the Hundred Years’ War, which bankrupted the state in the process. As much as half of England’s population died in the black plague in the mid 1300s. Many rural poor were serfs. Serfs had their own plots of land to feed themselves, but for that privilege they had to work on their lord’s land for free and pay exorbitant fees. Serfs and their children were bound to their land and their lord forever, not unlike slaves. Other peasants were free but still chafed at the high rents and violent control that their noble or ecclesiastical landlords exerted over them. Despite its modern designation as the Peasants’ Revolt, many of those who would rebel in 1381 were not landholding peasants but unlanded laborers and artisans who worked for a wage — the proto-working class. A common source of rage for peasants and workers alike was that their employers were also their landlords, lawmakers, judges, and jailers. As Marxist historian Rodney Hilton writes in Bond Men Made Free , the rebellion would be “one of the whole people below the ranks of those who exercised lordship.” By the late 1300s, these groups had begun pushing for political rights, lower rents, and higher wages. The plague, while traumatic, increased their leverage: labor demand now far exceeded supply, and depopulated land needed tenants. Thus, peasants and workers benefited from greater bargaining power vis-à-vis their superiors, and they pressed their demands with protests, petitions, and riots. This plebeian agitation threatened the social system and outraged England’s rulers. In response, they passed a series of laws to keep the poor in their place. One law fixed wages at their pre-plague, pre-inflation rates. Another commanded that those who abandoned their employer to seek better pay be branded on the forehead with an F for “falsity.” This ongoing class struggle forms the backdrop for The Uprising ’s opening scenes, where the king’s greedy, gold-encrusted advisers decide to once again tax the common people to pay for the crown’s losing war. The 1381 “poll tax,” a flat tax for every person over the age of fifteen, was the straw that broke the camel’s back. One particularly hated official even conceived of an invasive “puberty test” to check who was of age. The Great Unwashed The story of 1381 should encourage a healthy hatred of the rich and sympathy for the poor, and The Uprising gets off to a strong though lurid start. Tax collectors come to the village of the rustic Ploughman, a fictional farmer turned reluctant leader played by Garfield, and push people around. After the third teen girl is violated by a soldier’s puberty check, Ploughman’s rage at the medieval Epstein class boils over: “That’s enough!” he shouts, before cutting them down with his scythe. Ploughman soon meets the historical leaders of the movement, the outlaw preacher John Ball (Jamie Bell) and the soldier turned revolutionary Wat Tyler (Cosmo Jarvis). We get to see Ball deliver his famous line about Adam and Eve to a cheering crowd — but missing from the speech is Tyler and Ball’s more temporal vision for an egalitarian society. One chronicler reports Ball calling for a society in which “all things shall be held in common, when there shall be neither vassals nor lords, when the lords shall be no more masters than ourselves.” Unfortunately, The Uprising gives us little chance to appreciate the radical and modern vision animating the rebels. In fact, the movie — perhaps unwittingly — seems to hold the mass of commoners who comprise the movement in contempt. Besides the leaders of the movement and a fictional love interest for Ploughman (the fanatical Alyce played by Thomasin McKenzie, hair cropped close like Joan of Arc), the entire crowd of thousands is little more than background scenery. There’s hardly a close-up of even one rank-and-file rebel, and in the background their faces are usually blurred. This illustration shows the priest John Ball on a horse, encouraging Wat Tyler’s rebels of 1381, from a ca. 1470 manuscript of Jean Froissart’s Chronicles in the British Library. This is a far cry from the historical epics Greengrass claimed to be emulating like Gladiator or Braveheart , and further still from the plebeian-centric films of leftist directors like Ken Loach. In one incredible scene from Land and Freedom , Loach’s 1995 movie about the Spanish Civil War, peasants and antifascist militants debate whether they should redistribute their former lords’ land in the context of civil war, imperialism, and revolution. If Loach imbues the poor with full, vibrant, and dignified interiority, Greengrass mostly collapses them into a threatening swarm. In The Uprising , virtually no peasant besides the protagonist even speaks a line that isn’t a background shout or scream. Given the opportunity to exact revenge on their rulers, the mob goes completely wild. In an animalistic frenzy, they not only decapitate their foes but bask in their grisly victory with smiles and songs. Alyce is shown speaking in tongues during one lynching. At the height of their power, the mob invades the Tower of London, where they capture and behead several of the king’s hated advisers. The mob goes on to attack anyone who looks rich and even start scapegoating Flemish immigrants. Their leaders are soon overwhelmed by the bloodthirsty mob in a slow-motion montage of carnage reminiscent of the D-Day landing in Saving Private Ryan — except that, besides Ball, Tyler, and Ploughman, all the rabble are joyfully hacking away. The next morning, an ashamed Ball and Tyler try to make sense of the massacres. Ball, the most extreme of the revolutionary leaders, explains to Ploughman that “If the end be well then all be well.” The film further establishes a parallel between the rebel leaders and the king’s hated council. The base passions on both sides — pride and a lust for power, wealth, and revenge — hasten the tragic massacres. The mob, meanwhile, is easily manipulated, and Richard slaughters them before they can consolidate their victories. For all their talk of striking terror into the heart of billionaires, one senses that the film’s architects are consummate liberals. The Uprising’ s John Ball is certainly how liberals see the revolutionary misleaders of the twentieth century, whose lofty goals were betrayed by cynical ends-justify-means tactics. And the message conveyed by the film’s attitude to the mob is familiar after a decade of centrists’ warnings about “populism,” left or right: radicals and the untamed masses they unleash are as much a problem as the oligarchs they oppose. Woody Norman and Katherine Waterston star as King Richard II and the queen mother, Joan of Kent, in The Uprising . (Blumhouse Productions) Nowhere is the film’s tepid liberalism more evident than in the character of the king’s mother. Throughout the film, the widowed Joan of Kent (Katherine Waterston) is portrayed as reasonable, compassionate, and moral. She objects to the punitive policies of the king’s advisers and supports the commoners. Joan becomes the moral voice of the movie, consonant with but more articulate than Ploughman himself. When the two later meet in London and together decry the mob violence on the streets outside, Joan puts forward a thesis straight out of The West Wing : it is reason and words that uplift, she says, “not the mob. Without words, there is only chaos and fear.” Later, after her son massacres his peasant detractors, Joan frowns her disapproval. In reality, of course, a member of the royal family would have had a vested interest in maintaining the feudal system, the wealth and power of the monarchy, and the church and aristocracy who upheld these. The poll tax that sparked the rebellion was, after all, meant to fund the monarchy’s war to conquer France. But in the gauzy moral world of The Uprising , Joan is Ploughman’s most important ally: if only the two of them could get her son’s ear and use their words, all the violence and suffering could be avoided. The Mangionification of Robin Hood Until the rising fails, Ploughman counsels moderation. When the rebels finally bring their demands to the king, Tyler asks for the end of serfdom, and Ball demands the executions of the royal councilors. Ploughman, more modest and less bloodthirsty (for now), declares with a straight face: “There will be no peace until we are healed.” But the king betrays them, and neither freedom, justice, nor “healing” is forthcoming. Failed by the idiotic mob, unable to persuade the king through his benevolent mother, only one option remains for our hero: individual vigilante violence. The movie jumps one year ahead and we see Ploughman and his band, all sporting green hooded capes, jumping from the woods to ambush the king’s entourage. Ploughman has become Robin Hood! Greengrass has made it clear that he intended The Uprising to be a Robin Hood origin story, and US promoters marketed the film as the “Rise of Robin Hood.” While he admits he is departing from historical accuracy, Greengrass wanted the film to have a happy ending after the uprising gets crushed, and the Robin Hood turn is his solution. So Ploughman returns as Robin Hood, but not to steal from the rich and give to the poor — which is the core of the traditional Robin Hood story and, while not transformative, is at least useful. Instead, this is Robin Hood as a Luigi Mangione figure, terrorizing the rich as payback. After killing the last of the king’s hated advisers in a rote and bloody duel, Ploughman delivers a speech befitting Joaquin Phoenix’s Joker. While Ploughman won’t kill the king (because “there’ll always be another”), he announces that now “we rule ourselves” in the forest, with “no kings.” Ploughman then promises to haunt the king’s dreams for the rest of his days. A knowing look is shared between Ploughman and the king’s mother, who apparently approves of this violence, maybe because it’s carried out by Andrew Garfield rather than the faceless mob. The film fades to a long shot of the king’s residence in the evening. We hear screaming and know the king’s sleep is indeed haunted by Ploughman and by the guilty conscience of everything he’s done. This is the happy ending Greengrass invents after the end of the rising. The horrid, stupid mob and their dishonest leaders are gone. The queen mother has failed to persuade the king to dismantle feudalism. At least our hero can carry out gruesome assassinations and disturb elites’ mental peace. I went into The Uprising hoping for a good class-struggle flick. But I left the theater feeling that this movie, rather than inspiring an “awakening,” will be discouraging for all but would-be vigilante terrorists. But don’t scare the rich too much! Since the film disregards the agency of the poor masses it should be celebrating, we’re left hoping that rich people come to their senses and save the day. Satisfied that The Uprising contributes to this goal, Garfield explains that “billionaires [are] the ones that might be the most soul sick. They’re the ones that might need healing the most.” When the Rabble Won Lucky for us, the real history of the 1381 English Peasants’ Revolt is itself galvanizing. Groups rose up simultaneously in different parts of the country, and then more risings followed as word spread. Peasants and laborers, led by sympathetic lower clergy or literate town leaders, demanded, and in some cases temporarily won, reprieve from feudal strictures and confiscatory fees, as well as local autonomy for villages and towns. The rebels did kill, and some Flemish migrants were slaughtered. (Hilton speculates these were the victims either of their competitors, the London weavers’ guild, or their employees, English journeymen weavers.) On balance, however, the rebels’ destruction was relatively contained and focused for a medieval army of more than one hundred thousand. Top government officials, all ultrawealthy nobles or church leaders themselves, were prime targets, as were their palaces. The most common targets for burning, however, were the documents that recorded the feudal dues and services owed to lords by their subjects. After King Richard II tricked and killed rebel leaders — Tyler was stabbed in the back, Ball drawn and quartered — he unleashed a campaign of bloodshed and terror far larger than that inflicted by the poor against the rich. While estimates vary, the revolt claimed the lives of between ninety and two hundred people; in the three months that followed its defeat, the king killed between 1,500 and 7,000. Surely his guilt-wracked mother frowned for each one. This is certainly depressing, but fortunately it is not, as Greengrass thinks, the end of the story. The revolt was crushed in 1381, but many of the demands of the movement were achieved over the coming years. The flat poll tax that incited the risings was abandoned, de facto and then de jure, as were the laws depressing wages. Some historians contend that serfdom was on its way out by this point anyway due to economic factors. However, it’s hard to believe that such a massive and well-organized show of popular power didn’t also discourage elites from enforcing their rights over their bondmen. As Robert Brenner writes : The revolt of 1381, despite the defeat of the peasant rising, seems to have opened the floodgates. Peasants now streamed away from their manors, and their lords could do nothing to stop them . . . By the second quarter of the fifteenth century, the vast majority of English peasants had won their freedom, and, as a consequence of their much improved bargaining position, were paying, at least for the time being, much lower rents. Through The Uprising , Greengrass and Garfield would warn us to keep aloof from the crowds and put our faith in a few enlightened elites who will right the ship. Failing that, we haunt their dreams. We have yet to see something so spectacular in the twenty-first century as the 1381 English Peasants’ Revolt, or even the labor and civil rights movements of the twentieth century, which also won enormous improvements in social welfare and individual liberty. The powers arrayed against us are enormous, and the conditions not always favorable. But while ordinary people do not choose our conditions, we can, as Karl Marx writes, make our own history. Fourteenth-century peasants couldn’t know that the society they won would become capitalism — more prosperous and free than feudalism but still unequal, chaotic, and violent. As the nineteenth-century socialist William Morris ponders in A Dream of John Ball , “Men fight and lose the battle, and the thing that they fought for comes about in spite of their defeat, and when it comes turns out not to be what they meant, and other men have to fight for what they meant under another name.” Today we’re carrying on the fight for John Ball’s radically egalitarian society. We owe a great deal to our forebears. The least we could do is give them a good class-struggle movie treatment, not The Uprising ’s nihilistic liberalism.

The Prospects for a 2028 May Day Mass Strike

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Luis Feliz Leon ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-19T15:59:38+00:00

Three years ago, the United Auto Workers (UAW), glowing with confidence after a successful strike at the Big Three and pledging ambitious new organizing drives, put out a call for a national strike on May 1, 2028. UAW President Shawn Fain called for unions to align their contracts with the Big Three to build leverage and create a crisis for the billionaire class. It was a new day in the UAW, and a wider labor renewal felt possible. May 2028 is now only a year and a half away. Are unions getting in gear for a season of strikes and labor action around then, as contracts expire in manufacturing, education, logistics, grocery, and the building trades? Some are. Labor Notes has found 830,000 workers with contracts planned or expected to expire from March to July 2028 (see illustration below). These include 150,000 autoworkers at the Big Three; 153,000 members of the American Postal Workers Union (APWU), though they are unlikely to strike; 95,000 grocery workers with the United Food and Commercial Workers Union (UFCW), and 77,000 K-12 educators with the California Teachers Association. “Together, we can turn May Day into a national day of reckoning,” Fain wrote last year, taking stock of the progress towards this goal. “If more union workers stand up together, we will all have more power to win what we’re owed.” Fighting on New Terrain What we are owed has been growing, amid Trump’s extreme attacks on workers and unions. One million federal workers have lost their right to collective bargaining. The National Labor Relations Board and other federal agencies meant to protect workers have been left unable to fulfill their essential roles. Tens of thousands of immigrant workers, including union members, have been stripped of their legal status and rights, subjected to criminalization and detention . Union contract expirations set for 2028. (Labor Notes) May Day 2028 wasn’t initially about fighting Trump’s billionaire power grab. But since Trump took office, the terrain has shifted. Mass protests have become more frequent and more urgently needed to stem the volley of attacks on workers and on democratic elections. Massive weekend protests that drew millions, organized by Indivisible and No Kings, haven’t been able to reverse Trump’s attacks, though they are important training grounds to welcome new activists. (Bring your union banner !) The next big one, “ No Kings: Vote Early ,” is scheduled for October 17. It will include block parties, rallies, and walks to the polls. But the lessons from these mobilizations have started to shift tactics to include large economic disruptions. Today’s resistance movement recognizes that one-off weekend protests aren’t enough. Unions are powerful members of a resistance coalition not only because they are robust voter turnout operations but more importantly because they can channel the structural power of workers in key industries to grind the system to a halt. In January, when Trump sent a swarm of federal agents into the Twin Cities on a rampage, he lit a match to the tinder that unions and community groups had been laying through decades of coalition work. People’s resistance spread into school walkouts, sit-ins, demonstrations, and strikes. “We modeled our ‘ICE Out’ work similarly to how we prepare to go on strike — leaders in each building and regional leads who work together to cover the city,” said St Paul educator Mara Solis. “Each school had parent captains to work with our educator captains.” These escalating actions grew into a mass uprising that nearly shut down a large metro area. Federal agents beat a retreat from the terror spectacles in Twin Cities streets after January 23, when at least one hundred thousand people marched through Downtown Minneapolis and nearly one million across the state skipped work to participate in a massive political strike, including nonunion construction workers and restaurant workers who called out from popular downtown venues and supermarkets. It showed how, when unions act like movement organizations, nonunion workers can join them in common cause, which is necessary in a country where 90 percent of the population working for a private sector employer earns a living in a nonunion workplace. If the Twin Cities offered an example of layer after layer of society erupting into open rebellion, how can that be replicated nationally? We already have a rendezvous date for massive economic disruption in the months leading up to the next presidential election. Durable Organization May 2028 might still seem like a long time from now, given the urgency of the current crises. But as we witnessed with Black Lives Matter protests, it took years for grassroots activism to culminate in a major wave of protests, with effects that went on resonating for years more. With union contracts set to expire in a presidential year, there’s an opening to coordinate bold demands and worker action to defend democracy, as well as to win economic gains. Workers can put pressure on their employers to call for free and open elections. They can build broad alliances to pressure the state and employers for strong pensions , Medicare for All , a shorter workweek , and taxing the rich . In times of crisis, new organizations can spring up like Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) tents in disaster areas and fold away just as quickly until the next crisis. But movement-building isn’t the same thing as crisis management. To win real change and defend it, you have to keep applying pressure over years; that takes durable organizations that become rooted in people’s lives. The May Day Strong coalition of unions and grassroots organizations, formed in March 2025, is organizing under the banner of “workers over billionaires” to concentrate resistance efforts on the tech oligarchs who prop up Trump’s MAGA coalition and to defend the vote ahead of the midterm and presidential elections. This coalition could take the lessons from the Twin Cities and spread them nationwide. If some unions strike together, other union and nonunion workers can join in other ways, like using sickouts and disruptions to shut things down. Workers at airports and other key economic nodes might have to skip work if they find “abnormally dangerous” working conditions. This level of mass organizing will require unions spending down their vast financial reserves to support workers without union strike funds, which could include legal defense funds and mutual aid networks. If your union has bargaining this year or next, it might not be realistic to line up your contract to expire again in 2028. But one possibility is organizing to win sympathy strike language that permits union workers to honor each other’s picket lines. Article 9 of the Teamsters National Master Freight Agreement and other Teamster contracts offer a model to emulate. Standing Up to Trump Meanwhile, Republicans are launching fishing expeditions against unions backing President Trump’s political opponents . The initial targets of Department of Homeland Security spying and subpoenas of financial records were Minnesota unions plus the Communications Workers of America (CWA) and the Service Employees International Union (SEIU). Now, according to to The Guardian , House Republicans are also investigating the American Federation of Teachers, the United Auto Workers, SMART Transportation Division, and two railroad arms of the Teamsters: the Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers and Trainmen, and the Brotherhood of Maintenance of Way Employees Division. And Trump is threatening the integrity of the November elections. The May Day Strong coalition is holding 750 “Solidarity Schools” and actions from late August through mid-September nationwide, bringing together labor organizers and community members to confront the authoritarian threat we face. The trainings are geared toward equipping people with the tools to conduct election defense. Participants learn how to monitor poll sites, and they play out scenarios for how we could respond if federal agents and others attempt to undermine democratic elections through deception, disruption, or suppression of the vote. The scale of action the labor movement will be able to muster on May Day 2028 depends on the organizing we do now.

American Reality Is Beyond Parody, but Satire Isn’t Dead

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Michael G. Vann ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-19T14:59:17+00:00

A specter is haunting contemporary American satire: the specter of reality. This specter poses an existential threat to comedy as social critique. How do you make jokes in this era of hyperabsurdity? Donald Trump’s two administrations sandwiched four years of Joe Biden mumbling incoherently as he stumbling around. Corporations speak in the language of therapy, and airlines charge passengers for micro-comforts while advertising travel as an expression of personal freedom. Billionaires promise to colonize Mars while American cities see their bridges collapse. Streamers make a career out of broadcasting themselves gambling, eating, fighting, shopping, hurling insults, and slowly losing their minds. Kash Patel exists. The late Roman spectacle of a physically repulsive imperial leader planning a massive triumphal arch to honor himself, staging gladiatorial fights for his birthday on his palace lawn, and waging a futile war on Persia. Although Cory Doctorow coined the term “enshittification” to refer to the decay of user experience on online platforms, it now seems applicable to everything. What is the left-leaning comedic artist to do in such conditions? Capitalist Realism Ever in the vanguard, the Chapo Trap House podcast faced this crisis in their first year of existence. From their debut in March 2016, they focused their “dirtbag left” comedic ire on centrist Democrats. During their first live election show in November 2016, they were distraught at Trump’s victory — perhaps not because it harbored a new age of fascism, but because the show’s business model was supposed to be based on dunking on Hiliary Clinton’s neoliberal shit show. Nevertheless, the Chapos persisted and succeeded. With a decade as one of Patreon’s highest-earning podcasts under their belt, they are moving into other media including comic books and now film. Chunks , the new comedy anthology assembled by Chapo Trap House ’s orbit of comedians, podcasters, and filmmakers, offers one compelling answer to 2026’s satire crisis: stop trying to exaggerate America; reproduce its surreal texture and follow its natural absurdities. The result is a hilarious and unsettling ninety-minute journey into the darkness of the American soul. Produced through ColdFeet Films, the production outfit spearheaded by longtime Chapo contributor Amber A’Lee Frost , Chunks consists of six comedy shorts joined by interstitial appearances from Ruby McCollister as a fading Hollywood starlet turned Elvira wannabe public access host out of Bakersfield, California. Its contributors include Sandy Honig, members of Podcast About List and Episode One , Cum Town creator Nick Mullen, Saul Crinier, Nate Fisher, Will Sennett, Michael Cavadias, and Pierce Campion. Chunks moves between animation, live stream aesthetics, public access television, and conventional narrative filmmaking. It’s the jumble of forms appropriate to a country whose citizens increasingly experience reality through competing screens, but also hearkens back to alternative comedy projects such as 1977’s Kentucky Fried Movie and Spike and Mike’s Sick and Twisted Festival of Animation , which toured art house cinemas in the 1990s, as well as Tales from the Crypt and The Twilight Zone . Held together by McCollister’s outstanding performance, Chunks riffs on late night UHF and then cable television from a bygone era, when viewers flipping channels after midnight might stumble upon a deranged local commercial, a public access eccentric, an old movie, an evangelical preacher, or something whose provenance was completely mysterious. The filmmakers invoke that lost world of public access weirdness. Some of the funniest lines are Amber A’Lee Frost’s off-camera banter with the production crew. Those poor interns are probably still editing. Stranger Things But Chunks is not really nostalgic. Its great achievement is recognizing that the disappearance of the old television ecosystem did not make American culture less strange. It made strangeness more centralized, more commercial, and more difficult to escape. The weird guy who once had a half-hour program on channel 63 now has three million followers. The local crank has become a content creator. The public access psychic has become a wellness influencer. The infomercial has conquered the culture so completely that much of ordinary life now possesses the rhythm of an advertisement. Chunks works with this brave new world. Its six segments (“Utopia Air,” “The Eater,” “Office of Tomorrow,” “Poker Night at the Pleasure Trove,” “Charming Bears,” and “You Should Feel Better”) sound like channels encountered while insomnia pushes you deeper into the television dial. Yet their collective subject is recognizably twenty-first-century America: consumption, work, entertainment, technological mediation, therapeutic language, and the bizarre psychological compromises required to survive institutions nobody particularly believes in anymore. The anthology format works so well because contemporary America lacks a coherent narrative. It feels like scrolling. One moment you are watching footage from Gaza. The next you are being shown an advertisement for sports gambling. Then somebody is explaining their trauma. Then a senator is yelling about declining masculinity. Then DoorDash informs you that your burrito is approaching. Then a billionaire explains that artificial intelligence will liberate humanity from work. Then your employer emails to announce a mandatory seminar on resilience. Then you learn that the most powerful military in global history, which has recently murdered over 150 schoolgirls, will be giving the troops testosterone injections to create a “High-T” fighting force. Chunks reproduces this fragmentation without simply imitating the internet. Its world is recognizably downstream from television, advertising, podcasts, animation, live streaming, and algorithmic culture, but the film’s comedy comes from placing these forms next to one another until their shared pathology becomes visible. The Casino Economy Take the very idea of “Poker Night at the Pleasure Trove,” organized around a live streamed poker game, complete with a lively comments section (take the time to pause the video and read these). Gambling may be one of the perfect cultural forms of contemporary America, as the economy increasingly resembles a casino, while the casino increasingly presents itself as an economy. Sports broadcasts are saturated with betting lines, and cryptocurrency transformed financial speculation into mass entertainment. Ordinary people are encouraged to conceive of security not through wages, pensions, public services, or unions but through investments, side hustles, and improbable jackpots. Despite the fact that young men are particularly vulnerable to the dangers of online gambling, the gaming industry buys off cash-starved public universities and turns student athlete uniforms into casino advertisements . Chunks finds morbid humor in how America has transformed risk into leisure because it has already transformed ordinary life into risk. Something similar hangs over the anthology’s take on corporate culture. An animated segment about the private lives of adorable commercial mascots, Nick Mullen’s “Charming Bears,” takes beings created entirely for advertising and asks us to imagine the psychic existence beneath their synthetic smiles. The absurd premise gets at something fundamental about contemporary culture. We inhabit a society crowded with friendly corporate personalities. Insurance companies have mascots, and fast-food chains have personalities. Brands make jokes on social media and undergo carefully scripted moral awakenings. These days, capital doesn’t just want our money; it wants a relationship. Mullen’s bears poke at the nightmare of corporate domination dressed as intimacy. However, his comedic genius takes us deep into the lives of these cartoon bears and finds that they share our existential crises, face all too familiar financial precarity, and ultimately find solace in a quixotic act of defiance that is equally scatological as it is emotionally resonant. Feeling Better Contributor Sandy Honig proves herself to be a triple threat, writing, directing, and starring in “Utopia Air.” While most of this installment does a spectacular job of lampooning the host of indignities that commercial airlines call “service,” she highjacks the narrative and flies us into the forbidden airspace of transcendental knowledge. Despite moments of horror, Honig assures us that the trip is going to be just fine. “The Eater,” the work of Patrick Doran, Cameron Fetter, and Caleb Pitts, is a horror mystery that leaves the viewer with more questions than answers. Its atmosphere and critique of consumerism resonate with the best aspects of Fight Club on commodity fetishism. The final segment’s title, “You Should Feel Better,” could serve as a motto for the current political economic order. America has become extraordinarily proficient at converting social problems into psychological ones. You are not overworked; you need mindfulness. You are not underpaid; you need financial literacy. You are not isolated because public life has been hollowed out; you need to practice self-care. You do not despise your job because work has become degrading and insecure; you need to find your purpose. This is the ultimate neoliberal command: adjust yourself. Chunks belongs to an older tradition of American left-wing comedy, even though it avoids delivering anything resembling a conventional political lecture. Its politics reside in its eye for quotidian humiliations. The America depicted here is ridiculous because its institutions continually demand that people participate sincerely in situations that are manifestly insane. That is also why Chapo Trap House turns out to be a surprisingly natural incubator for a film project. Since its emergence during the 2016 election, Chapo ’s best comedy has depended less on conventional joke construction than on noticing the ideological content embedded in apparently trivial cultural objects: campaign advertisements, bad newspaper columns, superhero movies, conservative Facebook posts, airport bookstores, LinkedIn language, mediocre restaurants, and the endless artifacts produced by an empire trying desperately to entertain itself as it sinks further into decline and decadence. Underground Variety Show Crucially, however, the film does not feel like a filmed podcast. The filmmakers and producers care about filmmaking. This is hardly surprising, as cinema is a constant reference point on Chapo and Will Menaker has an ongoing series called Movie Mindset . The anthology deliberately changes visual languages, including animation, public access pastiche, live stream presentation, and narrative shorts. The result is closer to an underground variety show than branded bonus content. Even people involved with the production have emphasized the diversity of form across the segments, while ColdFeet’s stated ambition was to provide resources for comedians and filmmakers to make larger projects in a style increasingly absent from conventional television. That matters politically as well as aesthetically. American entertainment has undergone the same process of consolidation that has affected the rest of the economy. Gigantic corporations spend enormous sums producing material designed to offend nobody, surprise nobody, and remain permanently available as intellectual property. Streaming promised unlimited choice and delivered menus containing thousands of nearly interchangeable objects. Algorithms, not artistic visions, determine cinema culture. Meanwhile, the economically precarious weirdos who historically made American comedy interesting are told to produce clips. Chunks offers a modest alternative: give funny people enough resources to make something strange. This delightfully utopian gesture will throw Generation X viewers into nostalgic melancholy. This is what punk was supposed to be. Let a hundred flowers bloom. The film’s satire is consequently more affectionate than nihilistic. Beneath all the grotesquerie is a conviction that people themselves remain funny, inventive, sociable, and worth watching. What deserves contempt are the systems into which they have been inserted. Shopping for America This distinction is increasingly important. Plenty of contemporary comedy gazes upon American stupidity and concludes that Americans are stupid. Chunks suggests something more interesting. People behave strangely because the world around them has become strange. They gamble because security has disappeared; they retreat into screens because public life has withered. They speak corporate nonsense because employment requires it. They consume endlessly because, ever since President George W. Bush told us to fight al-Qaeda by “shopping more,” citizenship has been reconceived as consumption. In America, reality has become impossible to parody because the absurdity is structural. This makes Chunks more than an entertaining collection of sketches. The film captures America in the peculiar phase when decline has ceased to feel exceptional and become an aesthetic, even though we can vaguely remember an era when things were less awful. Everything still works, more or less. The planes still fly, the apps still load, the office still opens. The shelves in CVS are stocked most of the time, but not fully. The commercial mascot continues smiling, and the live stream continues streaming. Somebody somewhere is always telling us that things are getting better, even though we have those intrusive memories of life not being so bad. As the tradition of all dead broadcasts weighs like a nightmare on the brains of the living, let’s hope that somewhere after midnight, Ruby McCollister is still in front of the camera, introducing the next offering from Chunks .

France’s Left Faces the 2027 Election Divided

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Marlon Ettinger ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-19T12:56:32+00:00

Over Christmastime 2018, Jean-Luc Mélenchon met for lunch with Raphaël Glucksmann in France’s National Assembly. Mélenchon was an MP at the time, fresh off his strong 2017 presidential run for France Insoumise. Glucksmann was a liberal essayist with a murky background advising sometimes-dubious politicians in countries like Ukraine and Georgia, producing documentaries about human rights issues while looking to get into politics himself. The conversation stayed light and nonconfrontational, and they were joined by the former Socialist Emmanuel Maurel (at that time supportive of France Insoumise), and Thomas Porcher, an economist who’d voted for Mélenchon the year before. Porcher would go on to cofound Glucksmann’s new electoral outfit, called Place Publique, later that year. This new formation, Porcher told Le Monde at the time, was not founded to be “anti–Jean-Luc Mélenchon.” A decade on, though — and just eight months ahead of the 2027 presidential election — it’s hard to believe that’s true. Today hard-liners like Glucksmann and his wing of the Left have ruled out any potential alliance with Mélenchon, whether it’s in the presidential race or the parliamentary contest held directly afterward. This position raises the stakes for the Parti Socialiste primary, which will be held in mid-October. Once the leading force on the Left, the Socialists were in power between 1981–95 and 2012–17. Today the party is substantially weaker, but it’s still an electoral factor, and the primary will determine exactly what role it plays. Some contenders, like Maurel, favor unity on the Left. Glucksmann’s camp, however, pushed hard in the other direction, unsuccessfully arguing in favor of a written ban on any alliance with France Insoumise, today France’s largest left-wing party. Glucksmann’s Place Publique views Mélenchon as an antisemite, a “friend of Putin,” and a would-be autocrat. The glory days of the broad left-wing electoral alliances of 2022 and 2024 are over, and not everybody thinks that’s a bad thing. Others, though, worry it could cost the Left the presidency and even bring the far right to power. Broad Left In 2027, one thing is sure: there will be more than one candidate running for president on the Left. The Parti Communiste Français (PCF) designated its general secretary, Fabien Roussel, as its candidate in early September; the Parti Socialiste primary in which Glucksmann is running will choose a contender in the middle of next month; and another major force on the Left, the Greens, are still debating whether it’s appropriate to run their own candidate at all. “This is a unique presidential election,” the consensus-seeking president of the Greens’ parliamentary group Cyrielle Chatelain told me. “We could see the far right come to power, so we’re reflecting on what the best way is to present our ideas in this context.” In 2022, an electoral alliance spanning the broad Left denied Emmanuel Macron a governing majority, leading to parliamentary instability throughout his second presidential term. Despite the electoral success, the pact soon broke up over friction between France Insoumise and the other left-wing parties. Another alliance was forged for the 2024 snap parliamentary elections. Every poll and pundit ( including myself ) predicted the election would sweep Marine Le Pen’s far-right Rassemblement National into power. In 2024, that alliance, dubbed the Nouveau Front Populaire, as well as strategic stand-down deals with candidates from the center, delayed the far right’s march to power. But this alliance also quickly fell apart. Grievances over the political style and positions of Mélenchon and France Insoumise — in particular centered on unfounded allegations of antisemitism, in the context of Israel’s escalating genocide in Palestine — authored the demise of the left-wing front. Even still, this spring’s municipal elections saw alliances between left-wing forces. Many included France Insoumise, as in Saint-Denis where its joint list with the PCF won the election in the first round, or in cities like Grenoble and Lyon, where alliances including France Insoumise kept the mayor’s office in the Left’s hands. With Mélenchon now the Left’s front-runner in the presidential race, these parties are questioning what to do. Should these parties support Mélenchon and France Insoumise, despite their harsh criticisms of his party? Or should they run candidates who have little chance of making the runoff, and risk taking enough from Mélenchon’s score to ensure no left-winger makes the decisive second-round contest? And if Le Pen wins, will the Left unite, even around France Insoumise, if it’s the only chance of stopping her governing with a crushing parliamentary majority? Revolution Fabien Roussel’s coronation as the PCF’s candidate earlier this month came as no surprise. The PCF congress voted 72 percent in favor of rerunning their 2022 candidate, despite a current within the party called Alternative Communiste arguing that running him last election did nothing to staunch the party’s decline . “A communist candidate must allow us to rebuild our influence and to reestablish national visibility,” Alternative Communiste wrote ahead of the party congress vote. “[In 2022 our candidate Roussel] got 2.28 percent of the vote. Five years later, nothing allows us to think that a new candidature will fundamentally change the balance of power.” For these internal critics, it was important to recognize how “recent events. . . . have demonstrated that the most reactionary forces are converging.. . . . in 2027, it’s communists’ responsibility to contribute to putting forward a joint candidate who is capable of winning.” Some on the Left are not running to win. Small Trotskyist groupings like Lutte ouvrière or Révolution permanente have also named candidates, but they rarely get more than 1 percent of the vote. François Ruffin, a former France Insoumise lawmaker who dramatically broke with the party after winning reelection in 2024, is also running, though he has danced around with allying with the Parti Socialiste. He’s even reportedly flirted with an alliance with Dominique de Villepin — a conservative former prime minister who’s spoken out strongly about Israel’s genocide in Palestine and is considering his own cross-party bid. These candidates of the radical and revolutionary left once represented around 15 percent of the vote, before Mélenchon established electoral hegemony over similar voters. In 2012 and 2017, the PCF joined forces and supported Mélenchon’s runs, but the majority of the party’s voting membership no longer supports this. Despite the PCF’s serious policy differences with France Insoumise — including its support for nuclear energy and rejection of Mélenchon’s narrative on racism — the party has often voted with France Insoumise and the Greens to censure Macron’s governments. Reform The two candidates who best represent the Parti Socialiste’s divisions are its first secretary Olivier Faure and Glucksmann, who Faure recruited to lead its list in the 2024 EU elections. Glucksmann proposes reorienting the French left toward a more emphatic pro-EU line with paeans to democracy, human rights, and reindustrializing the EU. The aim: to head off both Russian imperialism and the Communist Party of China slyly taking over the world through trade. Wherever Mélenchon and Glucksmann occasionally still intersect on policy, Glucksmann quickly backs off. In a recent polemic following Mélenchon’s proposition to cancel some of France’s debt held by the European Central Bank, the president of Glucksmann’s party claimed that now isn’t the time for such a move — despite previously supporting it. A Glucksmann victory in the primary would amount to a referendum against any alliance with Mélenchon or France Insoumise. Always wary of France Insoumise’s hegemony over the Left, this week 292 leading figures and power brokers in the Parti Socialiste signed a letter calling for a Glucksmann vote. His main rival is Faure, who took charge of the Parti Socialiste after its electoral collapse in 2017, when it won just 6 percent of the presidential vote. In 2022, its vote fell below 2 percent. However, for Arthur Delaporte, an MP for Calvados who’s close to Faure, the party’s first secretary is the natural candidate representing left-wing unity — and who’s running on his record as the “artisan” of recent broad-left alliances. A Faure presidency, Delaporte told me, would look much different from the record of the last Socialist president, François Hollande, in 2012–17. On labor, Delaporte says, Faure would depart from the former president’s example of weakening worker power in the labor code. Delaporte cited rolling back the “inversion of the hierarchy of norms” — a reference to a provision in Hollande’s reform which allowed branches of companies to negotiate conditions worse than those already agreed at the company level. The pro-business argument behind the “inversion” was that it could give companies more space to maneuver in a constrained business environment. Unions warned that companies would use it to “blackmail” union negotiations by threatening layoffs unless workers accepted a worse deal. Delaporte also pointed to rolling back unemployment insurance changes under Hollande and Macron as a priority. In this primary, Philippe Brun — an MP from Eure — tried to distinguish himself by calling for changing France’s retirement system to capitalization. It’s a measure supported by the center and the Right, all of whose candidates have called for such change to France’s redistributive system. Brun repeated right-wing arguments that there are no longer enough active workers to fund pensions. But on September 16, Brun was excluded from the party and its primary after allegations arose of violence against a parliamentary assistant, which he denies. On capitalization at least, Mélenchon and Faure are aligned. “We will never pass to a system of retirement by capitalization,” thundered Mélenchon in one speech to supporters, arguing that individually paid-for retirement plans would require much higher contributions, and that market instability could delay or wipe out workers’ retirements. Is Faure open to capitalization? I asked Delaporte. “No, that’s not one of the themes we’ve defended,” he replied. Popular Greens During the Paris municipal elections this spring, a small group of local politicians broke from the Greens’ official alliance with the Parti Socialiste and formed the Verts Populaires, which supported France Insoumise. Some were purged from the party, but their current is growing, with the Greens anchoring themselves more firmly on the Left over the past five years. However, other Greens are moving away from the radical left. At a Glucksmann r ally in June, the Greens’ 2022 presidential candidate Yannick Jadot told reporters that Mélenchon isn’t the candidate he wants leading the Left. Jadot quickly joined Glucksmann’s campaign after he entered the race last month. But some Green politicians see Mélenchon as having a better chance of winning than any other left-wing candidate. Sandrine Rousseau, runner-up in the Greens’ leadership election in 2023, has openly criticized the party leadership’s ambiguity on pursuing an alliance with France Insoumise and criticized talks to ally with other sections of the Left instead. “I don’t know what the non-Mélenchonist left is, because we don’t really know what their proposals or main principles are,” Rousseau said in August. Chatelain, the head of the Green parliamentary group, is reportedly in favor of an alliance with Mélenchon. She appeared alongside him as a guest at France Insoumise’s summer conference, with Le Canard enchaîné reporting that Jadot believes she’s made her choice and is just waiting for the right time to announce a decision. Chatelain told me that for now there are no formal or informal discussions between the Greens and other parties about a presidential alliance; she was coy about what choice the party should make, deferring to the democratic desires of the membership. She said that any presidential campaign the Greens joined would have to have a strong ecological program, ending fossil-fuel usage and transitioning to 100 percent renewable energy. Still she added that she doesn’t talk in terms of programmatic red lines, given that France’s left has campaigned jointly twice in recent elections and each time succeeded in reconciling its differences. “I don’t think there are any insurmountable issues on the Left,” she said. Grand Coalition While many on the Left are still weighing the best path to take, centrists see winning part of them over as a necessary step to avoid a Le Pen presidency. Clément Beaune and Philippe Grangeon, two early-hour Macronists, argued earlier this month that a grand coalition should be organized even before next spring’s election. “Excluded, of course, are the two extremes; those on the Right who make the rule of law a scapegoat, and those on the Left who have never excluded a compromise with France Insoumise,” Beaune and Grangeon wrote. Of particular worry, they say, is the Rassemblement National’s plan to halve France’s contribution to the EU ahead of negotiations for the bloc’s 2028–34 budget, claiming that “Europe. . . . [is] the only space of effective sovereignty in the twenty-first century.” Former President Hollande believes he could be a candidate of last resort for this camp, if no consensus is established by the winter. Speaking on a radio show in early September, he argued that if a candidate wants to make it to the second round, they have to not only be capable of rallying the Left but also conscious of the need to reach beyond their own political family. “Does that mean potentially governing with the center?” “Yes!” Hollande exclaimed. “It’s maybe the best manner, this broad coalition, to have the stability to transform, to act.” Hollande has struggled to reach double digits in polling scenarios that count him as a candidate. While at the start of his presidency he headed a broad left-wing coalition with a huge majority, by the time he left office in 2017 his approval rating fluttered above the bottom of the Mariana Trench at 4 percent. The fact that he is today seeking allies on the Right and in the center is proof that France’s left has learned the error of his ways. The question that remains is whether it can build a principled alliance of its own.

Debating Victor Serge

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Mitchell Abidor ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-18T20:14:25+00:00

When I began writing Victor Serge: Unruly Revolutionary , I expected to write a biography in keeping with the image common on the left of Serge as an uncompromising enemy of Stalinism who never fell into anti-communism and fought all his life for revolutionary socialism. This is what we find in the many writings of Richard Greeman, who single-handedly rescued Serge from oblivion by translating his novels and tirelessly writing about him. In fact, my biography began as a collaboration between me and Richard, one we abandoned due to differences in interpretation. Suzi Weissman presents that image as well in her biography of Serge, The Course Is Set on Hope . A fuller account of this Serge was what I thought I was going to write. Things did not turn out that way. I worked mainly from primary sources from archives around the world as well as long-forgotten newspapers and magazines for which Serge wrote. What I found was a man far different from the one we find in Greeman’s and Weissman’s books (and all those who draw from their work) but also the one Serge presented in his justly famous Memoirs of a Revolutionary, 1901–1941 . I found a man who ceaselessly evolved politically, which I found admirable. But the evolution I found went in directions even I was shocked to find: that Serge was a man who came to believe in his final years that socialist revolution was no longer in the cards, and that those who believed it were frozen in the past. I was also shocked to discover in his correspondence, articles, and notebooks that Serge became an anti-communist and in certain regards an apologist for the West in the early days of the Cold War. There was no denying the evidence, and everything I wrote is documented in my book. I knew I’d upset people, and I offered to debate some of those I told of my discoveries. I was never offered the chance to do so, and the recently published critique of me and my findings first appeared in several European publications and now in English in Jacobin . To fully respond to the attack by Suzi Weissman, Carlo Albertani, and Christian Dubucq on Victor Serge: Unruly Revolutionary , I would have to quote the entire book. I respond here, however briefly and incompletely. An example of their hasty and incorrect reading is their claim that I imply Serge was a police spy at the time of the Liabeuf Affair. The trio writes that “Abidor notes that the socialist journalist Gustave Hervé was prosecuted for public statements, whereas Serge was not. Although he acknowledges that no documentary evidence explains why Serge escaped prosecution, he nevertheless invites the reader to infer that his immunity reflected dubious dealings with the police.” I wrote or implied nothing of the sort, and what I said is unambiguous. In fact, I very specifically said that in writing about the Liabeuf case, “Gustave Hervé took a similar tack [to that of Serge], and in fact used many of the same words that Serge would a couple of weeks later. It is indicative of the relative threat posed by Hervé and Serge that, though they wrote the same things, Hervé went to jail for his opinions while Serge went undisturbed by the police.” Yes, I note Serge went undisturbed by the police, but I clearly say that this was because he was not a threat, while the well-known Hervé was. How this can be read as an accusation that Serge was a police informant is a mystery. I’m not even certain they read the book. My critics write, “As an anarchist, one might reasonably have expected Abidor’s criticism to focus on Serge’s decision to join the Bolshevik Party in 1919, marking his break with the anarchism of his youth. But this is not the case.” Had they actually read the book, they would have seen that the lengthiest and densest chapter is on Serge’s dishonesty about the Soviet Union in his writings on the new state and anarchism. I make the case that Serge lied when writing propaganda pieces for the international Communist press. In these articles, he claimed that the USSR was the fulfillment of the anarchist dream. As I show, using contemporary accounts in the anarchist press by anarchists from several countries who met Serge in Moscow in the early 1920s, Serge privately told his former comrades that the Soviet state was awful, while writing articles that claimed the opposite and encouraged anarchists to join him there. This is part of a chapter in which I also detail the Bolsheviks’ brutal and underhanded war on anarchists and anarchism. I will plead guilty to one thing that this group didn’t bring up. I attempt to explain why Serge might have knowingly written falsehoods. Is this an incidence of the “psychologizing” I’m attacked for? I look at this matter from many angles, all sympathetic to Serge, who was truly in a bind. Anarchists have told me that Serge’s conduct was inexcusable, and that my search for extenuating circumstances was unacceptable: lies are lies, and leading anarchists to move to the USSR was leading them to their deaths. Sometimes a biographer just can’t win. Weissman and her friends write, “Abidor treats the anarcho-individualist movement, in which Serge was an active participant for nearly a decade, with scant sympathy.” This is absurd. I would have hoped that people who admire Serge would be aware that I translated and edited Anarchists Never Surrender , a collection of Serge’s anarchist writings in 2015, the first and only one of its kind in English. That book is an admiring one, and in both that volume, the biography, and two other books I’ve dedicated to French individualist anarchism, I defend this tradition that Serge once so ably represented. I do subject the school to lengthy analysis and draw some critical conclusions, but that’s not a sign of “scant sympathy”; it’s proof I take it seriously. Had my critics actually read every page of Unruly Revolutionary , they’d have seen that I also note and criticize Serge’s harsh treatment of anarchist individualism in his novel The Birth of Our Power . I even located and approvingly quoted the review of that book by his former coeditor of the anarchist paper L’Anarchie and first wife, Rirette Maîtrejean, who condemned her former companion for the way he had “evolved’ and for his “disdain” for his former individualist anarchist comrades. In any case, criticizing my treatment of anarchism is odd coming from Weissman, who dedicated three pages to Serge’s anarchism in her book on him, none of it showing any familiarity with Serge’s writings in French. Credit where credit is due, Carlo Albertani is not guilty of this error in the first volume of his biography of Serge, published in France last year. Serge’s ideas evolved greatly over the course of his life, and his attachment to revolutionary socialism took a blow during and especially after World War II. He grew increasingly exasperated with his fellow left-wing anti-Stalinist exiles in Mexico, who thought World War II would end in revolutions, just as World War I had. He turned his back on Marxist orthodoxy and denied that possibility. I write about this approvingly and at length in the chapter “The Revolution Revised.” The working class, in his eyes, had suffered an enormous defeat and was not in a state to carry out revolution. In this book and in my other writings on Serge, I have praised him for this change in point of view. He wrote extensively of his shift in his Notebooks , and I highly recommend that anyone who hasn’t already read that essential volume do so. In it, we find this diagnosis by Serge of the politics of the revolutionary socialists among his fellow exiles: “Their extremely optimistic and schematic conviction that the Russian Revolution will soon be repeated in Europe. ‘The workers will occupy the factories (Pivert), they’ll take power (Gironella), etc.’ So deluded are they that the POUMists think that they’ll be back in a free, socialist Spain in six months, and Pivert speaks proudly of the potential for the formation of a Red Army in France!” All of this revolutionary sloganeering was detached from reality, and Serge told his comrades just that and did so bluntly. The final years of Serge’s Notebooks are full of evidence supporting the case for Serge’s abandonment of the “revolutionary” in revolutionary socialism. So bad had things become that Serge was informed in a letter that “the comrades of POUM . . . and Marceau [Pivert], Stein, and Jacob unanimously consider that, for ideological and political reasons, collaboration with you is impossible.” It’s certainly not by chance that Weissman and her friends refer only to the French edition of the book, which is an essential and impressive piece of work. To speak of it as Serge’s Notebooks , published by New York Review Books, would require that they acknowledge that the translator, who would necessarily know precisely what is contained in a book that details Serge’s evolution was . . . me. The immediate postwar period proved Serge right about revolution and permanently soured his relationship with the revolutionary left. His ignoble physical fight with fellow exile Jean Malaquais in 1944 was over the latter’s accusation that Serge called for an alliance with the Tories. This was an exaggeration of Serge’s actual position, since he hoped for a “socialist-leaning” future, but Serge was in no way a revolutionary socialist at the end of the war and in his final years. As I show, in his final interview, coincidentally published the day he died, he nowhere even mentioned socialism. His primary concern, one that recurs in his public and private writings, which I discovered in his archives, was the protection of the human person. Personalism had become his guiding philosophy. Victor Serge, the Anti-Communist My most controversial claim is that Serge was an anti-communist, for which I provide ample evidence, too ample to lay out here. I don’t speculate about what his future direction would have been. I wrote, “To say Serge would have evolved in the same directions as his comrade in anti-Stalinism Boris Souvarine, who eventually supported the US in Vietnam, might be going too far. But it’s hard to deny, given the positions he took and the language he employed in expressing his opinions . . . that Serge agreed with the point of view of ex-communist anti-communists like Arthur Koestler.” I spoke only of where he was, not where he might have gone. And where he was was obsessive anti-communism. A large chunk of the book is dedicated to this period and this phase in Serge’s political evolution. To spell everything out would extend this response to dozens of pages. My claim is not based solely on his writing for the anti-communist press, like the liberal anti-communist New Leader . His private correspondence is replete with references to “reds” and “commies.” He considered communism “the worst danger, a mortal danger” to humanity, more of a danger than the West, which he considered a benign, even positive force. Weissman and co. argue he wrote for the anti-communist New Leader because the communists blocked him from publishing elsewhere. Were the communists really that powerful everywhere that they could sway publishers at will? As I ask in the book, how is it that the French Communists were unable to block Koestler from publishing Darkness at Noon , which appeared with no difficulty and to great success? No less a figure than Dwight MacDonald , as I show in my book, warned Serge against the path he was taking. His correspondence with Emmanuel Mounier, the brilliant philosopher and founder of the variant of personalism to which Serge adhered, is filled with anti-communist screeds and attacks on Mounier for being soft on communism. When Serge died, Mounier wrote, “Since the war his letters were polarized around a single concern: the Soviet dictatorship and the camps in the USSR. . . . Far from France, he spoke inaccurately of realities he didn’t know. But we forgive him this, for men of his mettle and humanity are few.” Serge also suffered from the paranoia of a Cold Warrior, seeing the communist hand everywhere, particularly within the United States and its government. In a 1940 letter, he wrote, “I know that the Stalinist work is being done in the United States on a large scale by a goodly number of journalists, and Stalinist intellectuals who have arrived there. They even have a certain amount of support in the Administration. . . . Their action is and will be camouflaged. . . . They would do anything to get all their personnel installed in the United States.” Elucubrations like these are clearly the work of an anti-communist or, to phrase it better, a premature Cold Warrior. See this quote from a 1946 essay in a collection of Serge’s articles called The New Russian Imperialism . I wrote in the biography: He downplayed the threat and even importance of Western imperialism. In that publication he insisted that the British Empire, led by the Labour government, “is evacuating from Egypt and offers India its independence. The old methods of colonial exploitation are exhausted.” As for the exemplar of the new form of imperialism, the United States, Serge claimed that he “knew Americans sufficiently well to know that they don’t contemplate imposing tyrannies on the five continents and on the contrary desire a sustainable reorganization of international relations.” Leaving aside how poor this all was as a prediction, especially as regards America, this is hardly revolutionary socialism, which I think we can all agree would be anti-imperialist. The “exhaustion of the old imperialisms” Serge spoke of didn’t prohibit France from fighting for years to hold on to Indochina and Algeria. The Mau Maus of Kenya would have been surprised to learn that England was gladly setting their colonies free. Weissman and co. omit any mention of Serge’s 1947 letter to André Malraux, in which Serge says that if he were in France, he would have voted for de Gaulle. Though his son claimed the letter was a fake, there’s no reason to think it was, and the sentiments it expresses are in keeping with his beliefs at the time. He so hated communists and communism that he would have been willing to support France’s main barrier to Communist victory in France’s elections. This shocked the revolutionary left of the time, none of whom cast any doubt on the reality of this letter or Serge’s authorship. Two final things: As I did with Weissman and Richard Greeman, whose lifelong work on Serge I benefited from, I informed Birchall while I was writing the book of the conclusions I was reaching. He expressed his disagreement, and before reading the book told me that my thesis was incorrect. Ian and I disagree about many things, but he is an honorable man. He read the book with an open mind and was convinced by what I had to say about Serge’s anti-communism. Ian is a lifetime Trotskyist and a respected scholar and activist. The baseness of Weissman and her friends is revealed by their attack on him for being convinced by my documentation. Suzi Weissman has held fast over the years to a mythical version of Victor Serge, revolutionary socialist sans peur et sans reproche, one that was also propagated by my friend and mentor, Richard Greeman. I did not start writing Victor Serge: Unruly Revolutionary thirteen years ago as the critical volume it turned out to be. It was my reading of his anarchist writings, which contradicted his claims in his memoirs about not supporting anarchist illegalism; the transcript of the Bonnot Gang trial; in which his conduct was less than stellar; and what I found in archival sources that led the book to go in an unexpected direction. It was not my “anarchism” that led me to write the book that so displeases those who cling to the myth. It was the facts and, as I say in the introduction, my lack of the phrenological bump for veneration that forced me to write the book I wrote. Serge was at one time a revolutionary socialist, and at one time he wasn’t an anti-communist. He ceased being the former and became the latter in his final years. There’s no shame or surprise in the fact that Serge’s ideas changed with circumstances: that was a constant throughout his life. Let me end by quoting some apposite words of Serge’s concerning his fellow exiles who remained attached to out-of-date schemas. He said they were “idealists hemmed in by the sclerosis of doctrines and circumstances, and dominated by their convictions and their emotional attachments; in short, by fanaticism. Under such conditions the person who disturbs the inner security of the others is a hateful heretic.” Weissman, Albertani, and Dubucq cling to an imaginary Serge, one who fits their political needs and desires. Stiffing Workers, Rationing Toilet Paper: Who Really Makes WPI Run?

Resident Advisors on strike in 2024 take their picket line to Earle Bridge. Worcester DSA members join with a banner that reads: “Workers on strike, unite and fight!” By: Julia Z & Jake S WORCESTER – Students have returned to Worcester Polytechnic Institute (WPI). Freshmen are being shuttled through new student orientation, professors are dusting off their syllabi, and returning students have long soaked in their remaining days of freedom. Many new students were no doubt won over by WPI’s aggressive promotion of its cutting-edge Robotics Engineering, Computer Science, and AI undergraduate programs along with its highly-celebrated R1 Research University status. Many are surely excited for the “project-based learning” described by eager campus tour guides, ready to sell them a vision of high-tech projects on a charming New England campus. But a university campus is much more than its students, teachers, and overpaid administrators. WPI may claim non-profit status on its tax filings, but in order to compete in a cutthroat higher education industry, it still carries all the fixtures of a corporate enterprise — in many ways, becoming more a landlord/buffet/hedge fund/private police department conglomerate that offers college degrees on the side — including a massive staff force to keep the lights on, the students learning, and the tuition dollars rolling in. So who are the faces that keep those lights on? Who does everything from preparing curriculum, to the research that won WPI its R1 status, to taking out the trash and setting the tables for big donor wine-and-dine sessions? And, more importantly, why are they increasingly becoming self-organized into trade unions to represent them against the interests of WPI’s endowment managers? Working Mass sat down with representatives of three organized labor unions at WPI, plus the campus chapter of the American Association of University Professors (AAUP), to talk about the importance of their work, their organizations, and their conflicts with the bosses on campus. Trades, Groundskeeping, and Custodial Staff Service Employees International Union (SEIU) 32BJ SEIU 32BJ is the international union representing facilities workers at WPI. Without their labor, repairs and maintenance on buildings would go unaddressed, trash would pile up in every classroom and office, and the grass on the Quad wouldn’t get cut — in other words, the campus itself would probably become an overgrown ruin. Every few years, by being organized in their union, facilities staff negotiate a contract with WPI to lay out fair wages, decent benefits, and dignified working conditions. Between every contract cycle, the contract needs to be enforced; the staff, through their union, sometimes find themselves continuing to battle with WPI’s administration to follow the terms of the agreement that both parties have already signed. Mark, a union steward elected by fellow custodians to address such problems on the job, told us about a recent fight, and how the union united his coworkers to push WPI toward a fair resolution that protected their jobs and made their lives easier. About a year ago, the administration brought in consultants from Core America to evaluate the campus practices, supposedly as a checkup to make sure everything was properly staffed and maintained. Core America went to work to cut good union jobs and clamp down on upkeep costs. Part of what they developed was a proposal for communal trash bins in office buildings: rather than entering individual offices to pick up trash and recycling, office staff themselves would take on the work of custodians and deposit their trash in a larger, central bin. Less time spent gathering trash meant WPI could try to get away with paying fewer custodians to do it. The plan would cut five jobs and open the door to more “reorganizations” (i.e., more cuts). As Mark said, nobody thought to talk to custodians themselves about the change. Not only was WPI trying to skip past a contractual obligation to negotiate over these changes with the people who do the work, it wouldn’t even have worked out the way they wanted. “They don’t know anything about our jobs,” Mark said, of the administration and their outside consultants. “If you had office staff doing this, I’d be spending more time getting everything into the right bin than I would have spent just walking through their offices anyway. President Wang used to work at a school that tried this idea out, and that’s exactly what happened.” Union representatives called a meeting with management and were quick to demand the plan be reversed. Union members took collective action by delivering a petition to back up what their reps were saying, and WPI backed down. Core America’s recommendation wouldn’t go through. Mark reflected on the outcome: We got them to open those five positions back up. Did you know they don’t approve purchases in May until the fiscal year ends? At certain points in the year, we have to be really careful with the supplies we have left. Students pay tens of thousands a year to go here, and management has tens of thousands to spend on these consultants, but they want to cut our jobs and make us ration toilet paper? WPI estimates the annual cost of attendance while living on campus — which is required of first- and second-years — to be just shy of $85,000. The total evaluation of its endowment sits at well over half a billion dollars. According to Propublica, in the fiscal year ending June 2025, WPI’s Chief Finance Officer — who reportedly interfaced with Core America consultants — was paid roughly $600,000, while the school’s President took home just over $1 million total. Resident Advisors United Auto Workers (UAW) Local 2322 Resident Advisors (RAs), Residential Mentors (RMs), Community Support Assistants (CSAs), and Community Development Mentors (CDMs) come together to form a union affiliated with United Auto Workers Local 2322. Student workers in any of these four positions perform key roles in keeping students safe, submitting requests for maintenance and repairs, and building community amongst undergraduate students. Without their labor, the dorms would be less safe, less maintained, and much more lonely places to live. We met with Zoey and Dom, two union stewards for this group of workers, which voted to unionize in 2024 and won its first contract with WPI the following year. One core reason the Res Advisor Union (RAU) formed was to challenge constant changes to job descriptions imposed by WPI. In fact, WPI only had a single Res Advisor role, but very soon after RAs won their union election, the administration proposed breaking the job into its current roles, removing most RAs from on-campus housing. Admin claimed this would lighten the workloads of RAs, but Zoey and Dom know it was another cost-cutting measure. Most of the new roles would receive no meal plans, no housing, and less pay. Moreover, RMs would be charged with developing a meaningful relationship with 75 or even 85 students at a time, where in their old role, RAs were only expected to handle around 35. “This seemed to be the personal project of the Dean of Undergraduate Students,” Zoey told us, “but nobody wanted to actually engage with us to figure out a solution that would actually work.” During intense contract negotiations, RAs went on strike from October 31 to December 15, 2025, after which the RAU won a decent contract with WPI that includes just-cause firing, increased pay, housing vouchers, meal plans, and workload caps. And while many of the job description changes were ultimately accepted, they’ll be implemented gradually over a 2 to 3 year phase-in period with a joint Union-Management Committee to oversee the transition. If the new roles aren’t working for Res Advisors themselves, the union will return to the negotiating table in 2028 with an opening to fight again for a better system. Graduate Student Workers United Auto Workers (UAW) Local 2322 Several hundred graduate student workers are organized under the WPI Graduate Workers Union, WPI-GWU, a UAW Local 2322 affiliate. Most doctoral students, and many master’s students, often work as Research Assistants (RAs) or Teaching Assistants (TAs), with others working as administrative staff while pursuing their graduate degree. As a teaching and research institution, the value of this work is obvious. Without TAs, grades wouldn’t be submitted; without RAs, WPI would attract no grant funding; without administrative staffers, very little work could get done at all. Although faculty generally direct teaching and research, graduate workers perform an enormous share of the day-to-day work: office hours; proctoring exams; grading student work; running experiments; and applying for research funds. Stephen is a doctoral student in Biomedical Engineering, a co-chair of WPI-GWU, and an officer in Local 2322, which not only represents grads and res advisors at WPI, but thousands of academic and non-academic workers across New England, including similar workers at UMass Amherst and the University of Vermont. The GWU won its first contract in 2023 after several months of tough negotiations. Prior to the contract, grad workers could barely afford to live in Worcester, and a litany of safety problems plagued their research labs. Stephen told us that some of his colleagues were forced to live on food stamps and simply couldn’t make rent. The ambiguity of their student-worker status even led to delays and complications in receiving worker’s compensation for lab injuries. Their first contract changed everything. Stipend and hourly pay increased as much as 20%; graduate workers were guaranteed paid time off for the first time; WPI now covers the cost of many grad workers’ health insurance, and they now enjoy clear definitions of what’s expected of them at work. Workload caps, just-cause firings, and even new protections and benefits for international students were enshrined in the agreement. Teaching and Research Faculty American Association of University Professors (AAUP) Professor Dan DiMassa is Vice President of WPI’s chapter of the American Association of University Professors (AAUP). At WPI, the AAUP doesn’t function like a traditional labor union, but still represents the organized interests of full- and part-time faculty. Despite the reality of a totally unequal power imbalance between faculty and the university administration that holds hiring and firing authority over them, labor law treats faculty as “managers,” and restricts their rights to organize unions at private institutions like WPI. According to Professor DiMassa, the AAUP chapter at WPI formed in 2018 to deal with the administration’s attempt to rewrite parts of the faculty handbook. Their changes would have undermined academic freedom, putting the right to adjudicate disputes over academic integrity into the hands of trustees and administrators. Since then, the AAUP has also been forced to confront management over COVID-era cuts to faculty retirement benefits, which have only been partially reversed. At WPI, the AAUP chapter often works through the faculty governance system, which is the main mechanism through which faculty can have a say in the development of university policies. Through this channel, despite lacking legal rights to negotiate a union contract, faculty organized under the AAUP have won new tenure-track jobs and enfranchised a new category of teaching faculty. Faculty governance isn’t a perfect way for professors to get what they need, but because they’re organized and can unite in collective action, their conditions stand a chance at improving. Dan told us that the AAUP truly sees the interests of faculty and students as aligned. Both share a common investment in the core educational functions of a university: teaching and research. For the whole system to work, students and faculty alike need to build and maintain an environment of academic freedom where their thoughts can be tested and challenged. Robust faculty governance — capable of struggling against the power of professional university administrators charged with driving up revenue while cutting down on costs — plays an important role in that process. Without its workforce of wage labor, WPI would cease to function, just like any other private enterprise under capitalism. And just like at any other workplace, it’s when workers organize for collective action in their own interests that they can challenge the boss to improve their lives, their workplace, and the world around them. Workers keep the world running, and the working class deserves to run the world! Texas Wasn’t Always a Right-Wing Evangelical Powerhouse

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: David Griscom ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-18T16:44:06+00:00

There’s a common view of religious history that holds that the further back you go, the stronger the smell of brimstone and the more conservative the beliefs. It isn’t true, and it obscures how much the human hand makes the world, including our ideas of the sacred. Joseph L. Locke’s new book One State Under God: A History of Religion in Texas is an important reminder that the conservative evangelical Christianity that dominates Texas, and through it has consolidated its influence over the country, was not a natural outgrowth of Christianity itself, but a product of historical developments. Texas today is at the forefront of the campaign to tear down the wall between church and state. The Bible has been reintroduced in Texas classrooms, along with a new law requiring classrooms to display the protestant version of the Ten Commandments. Texas gave the country the prosperity gospel preached by televangelists, including the megachurch pastor and multimillionaire Joel Osteen. The state has been the proving ground for a fiery marriage between evangelicalism and conservatism. Religion has been woven through Texas history, and in turn Texas has woven a particular form of religion into American life. But in One State Under God , Locke shows that the marriage of Texas conservatism and evangelicalism was never preordained. Indeed, Texas was once considered a “godless” land. Christianity did not come easily to Texas. The first imperial attempt to impose it failed. Spain saw Texas as a bulwark against French expansion but never conquered it, physically or religiously. The missions of San Antonio, ancient by American standards, were sparsely populated. When Mexico declared independence from Spain, only two priests remained in Texas — and neither, observed the Tejano revolutionary Juan Seguín, was particularly religious. A Spanish official lamented the failure to make Texas a Catholic land, writing that it remained “under the domination of the barbarian.” Locke takes us through Mexican and revolutionary Texas, where religious expansion ran into similar obstacles. For most of this period, the governance of Texas was an afterthought — the territory was administered as part of Coahuila y Tejas, literally tacked onto a neighboring Mexican state. And religion was an afterthought, too. There was scant religious infrastructure in early Texas. Most of the Anglo migrants were protestants of the frontier variety, whose faith ran on circuit riders and camp meetings rather than settled clergy and church buildings, and Mexico had little interest in supplying Texas with priests. Much of life was secular: children went unbaptized, marriages went unrecognized, and burials were conducted in secular graveyards. Texans today may be taken for a religious bunch, but in those years they were seen as living at the edge of civilization, godless and rough. As a Presbyterian minister wrote at the time, these Texans “generally appear in their personal depravity to have forgot if ever they did know or pretend to believe, that there was a God, or a devil,” and behaved as though “as soon as they had crossed the Sabine line, they considered also that they had passed over the line of spiritual accountability.” When conservative politicians call on Texas to embrace its Christian roots, then, it’s not entirely clear what roots they’re talking about. The Bible Belt Gets Its Buckle Anyone who has spent time in the South today can see the sway that Christianity holds over our politics. It wasn’t always so. According to Locke, a term like “Bible Belt” would have drawn puzzled looks in the early twentieth century. Nationally, “the South was not yet seen as an especially religious region, and religion did not seem particularly active in public life there.” Most religious Texans considered this a good thing. Separation of church and state was taken seriously by Texas politicians and Texas Baptists alike, who believed that it kept religion “pure and left the rest of the world to its hellish ways.” What, then, brought Texas evangelicals into politics? One State Under God supplies the answer: prohibition, the jingoism of World War I, and the Ku Klux Klan. The fight over alcohol was the opening salvo. Governor Oscar Colquitt defeated his prohibitionist challengers in 1910 by stoking the state’s long-standing opposition to religious rule, saying, “The place of a preacher is in the pulpit and not in politics.” But his victory was short-lived, as the forces that had amassed to outlaw drinking on religious grounds found they rather enjoyed politics as a means of social purification. By the early 1920s, the prohibitionists had won, the war had made loyalty a religious test, and the Klan had put a hundred thousand Texans in white robes. The preacher who best understood what all of that made possible was J. Frank Norris, who can be thought of as the nation’s first megachurch leader. Norris preached a fiery, reactionary Christianity, condemning communists, liberals, modernists, Catholics, and Jews. He turned on Baylor University, his own alma mater and the world’s largest Baptist college, hounding a professor out of his job for teaching evolution. He made common cause with the Klan. And he had a violent streak: Norris was tried for arson in 1912, and in 1926 he shot an unarmed man dead in his church office. He was acquitted both times. Starting with a congregation of little more than a dozen, Norris built a church of thousands, and his radio show and newspaper reached many more. He founded a second church in Detroit and spent the 1930s flying back and forth between the two, in the early days of aviation. Norris had influence, and he used it. When the Democrats nominated Al Smith for president in 1928, Norris raged against the Catholic from New York, prophesying that if a Catholic won power, “they would behead every Protestant preacher and disembowel every Protestant mother. They would burn to ashes every Protestant Church and dynamite every Protestant school.” Conspiratorial and paranoid religiosity had been let loose in Texas politics, and it would stay. God and Landlords The rise of conservative evangelical Christianity happened against the backdrop of two major mobilizations of working-class Texans. The first was the populist uprising, which united laborers and farmers, white and black, in a People’s Party that vied for power with the ruling Democrats. That movement was put down violently , and the defeat was followed by severe restrictions on the vote — not only for black Texans but for poor whites as well. Then the Socialist Party of Texas competed with the Democrats for the support of tenant farmers across the state. When James Edward “Pa” Ferguson won the governor’s mansion, he did it largely on a platform of tenant-farmer reforms lifted from the Socialist Party. Both movements leaned heavily on religion. Locke notes that Christianity shaped the populists, and that the movement borrowed organizational methods from the Methodists, like sending out traveling lecturers. As I observed in my own book on Texas’s populist past, Thomas Lewis Nugent, one of the movement’s major leaders and its candidate for governor, called the life of Jesus Christ the “beginning of Christian socialism.” Black populist leaders like John B. Rayner preached to rural Texans as well. But Locke argues that despite these more radical strains, “Texas’s new wealth infected the Baptists and the Methodists and the Presbyterians,” especially once the oil boom erupted. His account of the two Christianities that divided Texas through slavery and Reconstruction is the book at its strongest: one belonging to the slaveholders, which preached a paternalistic apologetics for chattel slavery, and one belonging to black Texans, which held out liberation for the enslaved and judgment for the wicked in the next life. It is a distinction he largely stops drawing once emancipation arrives — a shame, because the decades that follow are full of it. Take the example of John B. Rayner. After the defeat of the populist uprising, he began taking money from the same landlords and bosses he had once opposed and wound up in the employ of the lumber baron John Henry Kirby, no longer speaking of liberation or the cooperative commonwealth but of personal responsibility and the self-help programs of Booker T. Washington. Kirby at the time was trying to break the Brotherhood of Timber Workers, one of the first interracial unions in the South. What Rayner was hired to do, preachers were hired to do across the cotton fields of Texas and the South: attack the “sinful ways” of the poor and keep their attention off the men robbing them. This was a Christianity bankrolled by the wealthy to keep working people in line. Locke does not ignore the alliance between the evangelical movement and the rich; he shows in detail how religion in Texas was cultivated for political ends. What he leaves out is this particular use of it, as a weapon against the agrarian revolts of the populists and then the socialists, who had made religion a common language for uniting tenant farmers across the state. Texas is a big place with a long history, and any book about it has to make cuts. In the decades that followed, the familiar alliance between evangelical Christianity and free-market conservatism came into the open. As Locke notes, the change was captured in Ronald Reagan’s line to a gathering of evangelical leaders in Dallas in 1980: “I know that you can’t endorse me, but I want you to know that I endorse you.” That rally was organized by the Texas televangelist James Robison, and men like him had by then steamrolled what was left of the prohibitions on religion in politics. The long campaign to remake religion in the image of the Right was complete. Today Texans live in the evangelical right’s world: abortion is banned, the Ten Commandments hang in classrooms, and the Texas GOP has its eyes on ending no-fault divorce. But the great service of One State Under God is to show that this world was deliberately made — and that what was made can be unmade.

Una pequeña victoria frente a los mapas que sitúan al Norte Global en el centro y empequeñecen a África.

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Prabir Purkayastha ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-18T19:05:02+00:00

Read in English here La semana pasada, el 4 de septiembre, la Asamblea General de las Naciones Unidas aprobó una propuesta africana para un nuevo mapa mundial, que muestra el tamaño real de los países en relación con los demás. Su objetivo es corregir la visión del mundo moldeada por el mapa mundial ampliamente utilizado basado en la proyección de Mercator, creada por Gerardus Mercator, un cartógrafo flamenco del siglo XVI. El problema con el mapa de Mercator es bien conocido; por ejemplo, muestra a la isla de Groenlandia con el mismo tamaño que el continente africano, ¡a pesar de que África es 14 veces más grande que Groenlandia! En pocas palabras, los mapas de Mercator exageran enormemente el norte del mundo —los Estados Unidos y Europa— mientras que reducen África y la mayor parte de Asia. Tampoco trata con benevolencia al resto del hemisferio sur. Sudamérica, que tiene casi tres veces el tamaño de Europa, parece comparable a esta en un mapa típico de Mercator. Aunque no existe ninguna razón geográfica por la que el hemisferio sur deba ser más pequeño que el hemisferio norte, los cartógrafos lo hicieron significativamente más pequeño porque tenía muy poca masa continental más allá de los 40 grados y, por lo tanto, se recortó. Esto significó que, en la mayoría de los mapas de Mercator, debido a que los cartógrafos occidentales recortaron gran parte del hemisferio sur, el ecuador quedó significativamente desplazado hacia abajo. El doble efecto de comprimir las zonas ecuatoriales, donde se encuentra gran parte de África, y desplazar el ecuador hacia abajo hizo que el norte del mundo, particularmente Europa y América del Norte, parecieran mucho más grandes de lo que realmente son. Por lo general, en la mayoría de los mapas de Mercator, el hemisferio norte parece el doble del tamaño del hemisferio sur, aunque ambos hemisferios son idénticos en tamaño. Cualquier mapa, ya sea de Mercator o de cualquier otra proyección, presenta el problema de que se puede ser fiel al tamaño o a la forma, pero no a ambos. Se trata de un problema fundamental que surge al proyectar una superficie tridimensional sobre una hoja de papel bidimensional. No hay forma de hacerlo sin distorsiones. Solo un globo terráqueo puede representar fielmente la Tierra tridimensional. Pero entonces, ¿qué hacemos cuando queremos reproducir detalles geográficos para nuestros lectores en libros o artículos? Las distorsiones evidentes de la proyección de Mercator y su enfoque eurocéntrico dieron lugar a varias alternativas, particularmente entre los cartógrafos de la izquierda amplia o progresista. Otros, especialmente en los Estados Unidos y Europa, se han opuesto a ella, argumentando que el tamaño no importa y que no les molesta que se les muestre mucho más grandes de lo que realmente son. Sin embargo, muchos discreparon e intentaron desarrollar proyecciones alternativas. La proyección de Gall-Peterson, que contó con el respaldo del gobierno socialdemócrata alemán de Willy Brandt, es una de esas proyecciones. La proyección de Hobo Dyer, que se encuentra en el dominio público global —lo que significa que cualquiera puede usarla sin pagar regalías—, es otra. Finalmente, las Naciones Unidas han puesto fin a las guerras de los mapas , con una victoria rotunda a favor de la moción presentada por las naciones africanas para utilizar mapas de área igual. La votación fue de 164 a favor de la moción, con los Estados Unidos como único voto en contra. El objetivo del mapa de Mercator era facilitar la navegación de los barcos en mar abierto, donde ya no es posible orientarse reconociendo diversas características topológicas, como ríos, colinas, aldeas, pueblos, etc. En mar abierto, si identificamos nuestra ubicación en un mapa de Mercator y el destino al que deseamos llegar, podemos viajar en línea recta, tal como se muestra en el mapa. Conserva la dirección, lo que significa que, si trazamos un rumbo en un mapa de Mercator, podemos fijar nuestro rumbo según él. Es por eso que los países marítimos utilizaban proyecciones de Mercator para la navegación cuando contaban con pocos instrumentos, más allá de la brújula y el sextante, para determinar su ubicación con respecto a su destino. Es importante señalar que la dirección que se muestra en el mapa no es la ruta más corta entre el punto de partida y el destino. El camino más corto entre dos puntos en la superficie del globo terráqueo es el arco del gran círculo que va desde su ubicación hasta su destino. Una línea recta en un mapa bidimensional puede parecer la distancia más corta, pero no lo es, ya que no toma en cuenta la curvatura de la Tierra. En pocas palabras, el precio que pagamos por convertir una superficie curva en una plana y bidimensional es que distorsionamos la forma de la superficie. Estiramos ciertas áreas y encogemos otras. En una Tierra plana, la distancia más corta sería la que indica su brújula, pero no así en una esfera. Anteriormente, los barcos utilizaban las estrellas y el sol del mediodía para estimar dónde se encontraban y hacia dónde debían dirigirse. Fijar un rumbo hacia su destino utilizando un mapa de Mercator y una brújula resultaba mucho más sencillo que tratar de adivinar dónde se encontraba en un océano desierto, sin puntos de referencia, y cambiar constantemente de rumbo en busca de una ruta más corta. La línea constante en el mapa se denominaba “línea de rumbo”, y seguirla ayudaba enormemente a los navegantes. Los mapas de Mercator eran populares en Europa, particularmente en Europa occidental, ya que, aparte de los árabes, eran las principales naciones marítimas. Mientras que los árabes se enfocaban en el comercio, particularmente en el Océano Índico, los europeos occidentales se dedicaban no solo a la pesca, sino también, fuera de temporada, a la piratería, las incursiones y el saqueo de otras naciones. Los vikingos, importantes figuras románticas en Hollywood, eran particularmente brutales , ya que combinaban el saqueo con el comercio de esclavos. Por supuesto, como todos los piratas, una vez que se alcanza el poder, se puede convertir ese poder marítimo en conquista. Los hombres del norte, o nórdicos, conquistaron Francia y más tarde Inglaterra, y son conocidos por nosotros como normandos. No muy diferentes de las Compañías Holandesa y Británica de las Indias Orientales, que comenzaron con el comercio y la piratería y más tarde establecieron imperios coloniales. No es de extrañar que los reinos marítimos de Europa occidental, para quienes la guerra, la pesca para alimentarse y la caza de focas, morsas, etc., con el fin de obtener grasa y pieles, constituían las principales fuentes de riqueza, se centraran mucho más en la navegación y la construcción naval. Esto significaba que los barcos construidos para el agitado Océano Atlántico contaban con ciertos avances de los que carecían los barcos árabes e indios, aunque la brújula, el astrolabio, los mapas astronómicos y otros instrumentos náuticos procedían todos de Asia. Los barcos de Europa occidental contaban con cañones montados en cubierta, lo que les permitía disparar “andanadas”, es decir, disparar múltiples cañones simultáneamente, a diferencia de los cañones montados en la proa y la popa. Esa es una de las principales razones por las que un puñado de países de Europa occidental pudo aprovechar los océanos para, primero, hacerse con el comercio marítimo y, más tarde, construir sus imperios coloniales. A diferencia de los fuertes fijos, los barcos actuaban como fuertes flotantes, lo que proporcionaba a los europeos una movilidad en las guerras navales de la que carecían las fortificaciones terrestres estáticas. Como muestra la monumental obra de Needham sobre la ciencia y la tecnología chinas , muchos avances náuticos en la construcción naval y la navegación procedían de China. Los siete viajes de los barcos de Zheng He en el siglo XV (1405-1443), que partieron de China, el sudeste asiático y la India, y recorrieron la costa árabe hasta África, demostraron que los chinos habían construido enormes barcos y una poderosa armada. En aquella época, se encontraban muy por delante de Europa y de los árabes, tanto en la construcción naval como en los buques de guerra. Las diferencias internas entre la aristocracia terrateniente y los comandantes navales condujeron a la destrucción de la armada y a la prohibición de construir cualquier buque de alta mar. Aunque la India suministraba a los árabes la madera necesaria para la construcción naval y en algún momento contó con una poderosa armada, cedió el comercio de alta mar a los comerciantes árabes. Ni China, ni la India, ni los árabes consideraban que la armada tuviera importancia militar. Como dijo un emperador mogol: “Los océanos son para los comerciantes, no para nosotros, los guerreros”. Las divisiones de castas en la India también llevaron, en un momento dado, a prohibir a las castas superiores emprender viajes oceánicos. La travesía del Kala Pani , siempre prohibida por los brahmanes, no se respetó de manera estricta. La India experimentó una gran expansión marítima en el período budista, no a través de la guerra, sino mediante el comercio y la labor de proselitismo de los monjes budistas. Tras el resurgimiento del hinduismo en el sur, el tabú que prohibía a las castas superiores viajar a través de los océanos se aplicó de manera más estricta, dejando los océanos en manos de comerciantes y mercenarios. Por ejemplo, las principales flotas de los reyes indios – ya fuera el rey de Calicut, también conocido como Samundri o Zamorin, o Shivaji – dependían de comandantes navales y buques mercenarios, muchos de ellos al mando de árabes. Como demuestra la historia, los mapas, el comercio marítimo y la guerra están íntimamente relacionados. Los viajes marítimos y las guerras navales confieren a los mapas una importancia estratégica. No es simplemente la ubicación de las fronteras de cada país lo que importa, sino también su visión estratégica del mundo. Podemos observar cómo esto se está manifestando actualmente en Bab el Mandeb y en el Golfo Pérsico. Un vistazo rápido a un mapa habría convencido a los líderes estadounidenses de que Irán cuenta con una clara ventaja estratégica en el Estrecho de Ormuz, al igual que los hutíes en el Mar Rojo y el paso hacia el Canal de Suez. Descuidar los océanos repite los errores que cometió Asia en una época diferente, errores de los que ha aprendido. Debemos tener esto presente también hoy en día. A small victory against the maps which center the global north and shrink Africa

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Prabir Purkayastha ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-18T19:05:02+00:00

Lee en español aquí On September 4, the United Nations General Assembly adopted an African proposal for a new world map, which shows countries’ true size relative to one another. It aims to correct the view of the world shaped by the widely used world map based on the Mercator Projection, created by Gerardus Mercator, a 16th-century Flemish cartographer. The problem with the Mercator Map is well known; for example, it shows the island of Greenland as the same size as the African continent, even though Africa is 14 times the size of Greenland! Simply put, Mercator maps greatly exaggerate the global north – the US and Europe – while shrinking Africa and most of Asia. Nor does it treat the rest of the Southern Hemisphere kindly. South America, nearly three times the size of Europe, looks comparable to it on a typical Mercator map. Though there is no geographical reason the Southern Hemisphere should be smaller than the Northern Hemisphere, mapmakers made it significantly smaller because it had very little landmass beyond 40 degrees and was therefore cropped out. This meant that on most Mercator maps, because Western mapmakers cropped out much of the Southern Hemisphere, the equator was significantly displaced downward. The double whammy of squeezing the equatorial areas, where much of Africa lies, and shifting the equator down made the global north, particularly Europe and North America, seem much larger than they actually are. Typically, on most Mercator maps, the Northern Hemisphere looks twice the size of the Southern Hemisphere, though the two hemispheres are identical in size. Alternatives to the Mercator projection Any map, whether Mercator or any other projection, has the problem that you can be true to either size or shape, but not both. The problem is a fundamental one of projecting a three-dimensional surface to a two-dimensional piece of paper. There is no way of doing this without distortions. Only a globe can truly represent the three-dimensional earth faithfully. But then what do we do when we want to reproduce geographical details for our readers in books or articles? The blatant distortions of the Mercator projection and its Eurocentric approach gave rise to several alternatives, particularly among broad-left or progressive cartographers. Others, particularly in the US and Europe have opposed it, arguing that size does not matter and that they were comfortable being shown much bigger than they actually are. But many dissented and tried to work out alternate projections. The Gall-Peterson projection, which was backed by the German social democratic government of Willy Brandt, is one such projection. The Hobo Dyer projection, which is in the global commons, meaning anybody can use it without paying royalties, is another one. Finally, the United Nations has ended the map wars , with a resounding victory for the motion moved by the African nations for using equal area maps. The voting was 164 in favor of the motion with the US casting the only nay vote. Read more: US votes against UN resolution promoting maps reflecting Africa’s true size The objective of Mercator map was for ships navigating in open seas, where we can no longer navigate recognizing various topological features, rivers, hills, villages, towns, etc. On the open ocean, if we identify where we are on a Mercator map and the destination we want to reach, we can travel in a straight line, as shown on the map. It conserves direction, meaning if we plot a course on a Mercator map, we can set our course by it. That is why ocean-going countries used Mercator projections for navigation when they had few instruments beyond the compass and the sextant to determine where they were relative to their destination. It is important to note that the direction shown on the map is not the shortest path between where you start from and your destination. The shortest path on two points on the surface of the globe is the arc of the great circle from where you are to your destination. A straight line on a 2-dimensional map may look like the shortest distance, but it is not as it does not account for the Earth’s curvature. Simply put, the price we pay for converting a curved surface to a flat 2-dimensional one is that we distort the shape of the surface. We stretch certain areas and shrink others. On a flat earth, the shortest distance would be what your compass shows, but not on a sphere. Earlier, ships used stars and the midday sun to estimate where they were and where they needed to go. Setting a course to your destination using a Mercator map and a compass was much easier than trying to guess where you were on an empty ocean with no landmarks and constantly changing course for a shorter route. The constant line on the map was called the thumb line, and following it greatly helped the seafarers. Mercator maps were popular in Europe, particularly in Western Europe, because, apart from the Arabs, they were the major ocean-going nations. Trade and piracy in the colonial era While the Arabs focused on trade, particularly in the Indian Ocean, Western Europeans focused not only on fishing but, in the off-season, on piracy, raiding, and looting other nations. The Vikings, major romantic figures in Hollywood, were particularly brutal , combining loot with slave trade. Of course, like all pirates, once you become powerful, you can convert that ocean power into conquest. The northern men, or Norsemen, conquered France and later England, and are known to us as Normans. Not very different from the Dutch and the British East India Companies, starting with trade and piracy and later establishing colonial empires. Not surprisingly, the ocean-going kingdoms of Western Europe, for whom war and fishing for food and hunting seals, walrus, etc., for blubber and skins were major sources of wealth, focused much more on seafaring and shipbuilding. This meant ships built for the rougher Atlantic Ocean had certain advances that Arab and Indian ships did not, though the compass, astrolabe, astronomical maps, and other nautical instruments all came from Asia. Western European ships had deck-mounted cannons, which allowed them to fire “broadside,” meaning multiple cannons firing simultaneously, unlike the front- and rear-mounted cannons. That is one of the major reasons a handful of West European countries could use the oceans to first capture the ocean trade and later build their colonial empires. Unlike fixed forts, ships acted as floating ones, giving Europeans mobility in naval wars that static ground fortifications lacked. Ocean navigation and development in Asia As Needham’s monumental work on Chinese science and technology shows, many nautical advances in shipbuilding and navigation came from China. Seven voyages by Zheng He’s ships in the 15th century (1405-1443), which traveled from China, Southeast Asia, India, and down the Arab coast to Africa, showed that the Chinese had built huge ships and a powerful navy. At that time, they were well ahead of Europe and the Arabs, in both shipbuilding and warships. Internal differences between the landed aristocracy and the naval commanders led to the navy’s destruction and a ban on building any oceangoing ships. Although India supplied the timber necessary for shipbuilding to the Arabs, and once had a powerful navy, it ceded the oceangoing trade to Arab merchants. Neither China, India, nor the Arabs saw the navy as militarily important. As one Mughal Emperor said, Oceans are for traders, not us, the warriors. Caste divisions in India also led, at one point, to barring upper castes from undertaking ocean voyages. Crossing Kala Pani , always forbidden by the Brahmins, was not strictly followed. India saw major maritime expansion in the Buddhist period, not by war but through trade and proselytizing Buddhist monks. After the revival of Hinduism in the South, the taboo against upper-caste travel across oceans was enforced more strictly, leaving the oceans to traders and mercenaries. For example, the major fleets of the Indian kings, whether the King of Calicut, also known as Samundri or Zamorin, or Shivaji, relied on mercenary naval commanders and ships, many commanded by the Arabs. As history shows, maps, ocean trade, and war are intimately connected. Ocean voyages and naval wars make maps strategically important. It is not simply where each country’s borders lie that is important, but also how it strategically views the world. We can see this currently playing out in the Bab-al-Mandeb and the Persian Gulf. A quick look at a map would have convinced US leaders that Iran has a clear strategic edge in the Strait of Hormuz, just as Ansar Allah do in the Red Sea and the passage to the Suez Canal. Neglecting the oceans repeats the mistakes Asia made in a different age, mistakes from which it has learned. We need to bear this in mind today as well. Por el contrario, una nueva confrontación paralizaría al BRICS+ y permitiría que potencias externas se aprovecharan de las divisiones en toda Asia. El cauteloso acercamiento entre India y China es un ejemplo de dicha contradicción, ya que combina el reconocimiento de la dependencia mutua con una desconfianza estratégica sin resolver. El futuro del BRICS+ dependerá de si dichas contradicciones se gestionan de manera pacífica y de si la cooperación genera beneficios tangibles para los trabajadores, en lugar de limitarse a ampliar el margen de maniobra de las clases dominantes nacionales. Para la India, el camino a seguir es claro: restablecer relaciones normales y pacíficas con China, resistirse a ser incorporada a la estrategia militar de los Estados Unidos, oponerse a la agresión y a las sanciones unilaterales contra Irán, y trabajar por un orden multilateral genuinamente democrático. Solo habrá un nuevo equilibrio mundial cuando se restrinja la dominación hiperimperialista y se amplíen la soberanía y las posibilidades de desarrollo de los pueblos. La cumbre de Nueva Delhi marcó un paso más en esa transición controvertida, pero la dirección y el contenido social del orden emergente siguen siendo objeto de lucha política. Wars in West Asia, unilateral sanctions, tariff barriers, and the weaponization of finance and technology have exposed the fundamentally unequal character of the imperialist-dominated international order. Against this background, the continued expansion of BRICS reflects the desire of developing countries for greater strategic autonomy and a more democratic system of global governance. At the same time, the summit demonstrated that BRICS+ remains a heterogeneous grouping whose members differ substantially in their political alignments and economic interests. The New Delhi Declaration, the outcome of the summit, reiterated the demand for reform of the United Nations, international financial institutions, and the multilateral trading system. It opposed unilateral sanctions not authorized by the United Nations Security Council and advocated greater trade in national currencies. The declaration also supported increased representation for developing countries in institutions whose rules continue to be determined primarily by the United States and its allies. These positions have objective significance at a time when Washington increasingly employs sanctions, tariffs, export controls, and control over payment systems to discipline countries unwilling to accept its dictates. BRICS+ now includes countries subjected to sustained Western pressure, such as Russia and Iran, as well as the United Arab Emirates and other states retaining extensive economic and security ties with the West. Consensus can consequently be maintained only through formulations that acknowledge conflicts without always identifying their political causes. This ambiguity was especially apparent in the summit’s treatment of the widening war in West Asia and the position adopted by Iran. Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian used the summit to denounce unilateral sanctions and the war of aggression against Iran carried out by the United States and Israel. He correctly stressed that economic resilience cannot be separated from peace, sovereignty, and security. Iran also advocated trade in national currencies, greater use of the New Development Bank, and the construction of independent transport and energy corridors (as it has already created through Pakistan, which has provided six roadways for Chinese and Russian goods to enter Iran). For a country subjected to decades of sanctions, exclusion from Western financial networks, and repeated threats of regime change, membership of BRICS+ provides an important diplomatic and economic opening. Iran’s position, however, contains a marked ambiguity. Tehran presents BRICS+ as an instrument for resisting unilateralism and defending sovereignty. Simultaneously, it must seek accommodation with neighboring Gulf states, several of which are deeply integrated with Western capital and security arrangements. Iran signed a declaration calling for restraint and de-escalation even while accusing the United States and Israel of aggression and defending its own retaliatory actions. Its militancy against hyper-imperialist pressure is therefore combined with a pragmatic effort to avoid regional isolation, secure sanctions relief, and preserve channels of negotiation. Iran confronts a vastly superior military and financial coalition and must maneuver within severe constraints. But it also illustrates the broader problem facing BRICS+. Opposition to Western domination does not automatically produce a coherent anti-imperialist program. The participating states are capitalist states pursuing their respective national interests. Their ruling classes may resist particular aspects of US hegemony while remaining prepared to reach accommodations with global finance or with regional reactionary powers. BRICS+ is consequently an arena of struggle within the changing world order, not an already constituted alternative to hyper-imperialism. Partners The summit’s most consequential political development for Asia was the meeting between Prime Minister Narendra Modi and Chinese President Xi Jinping. Xi’s first visit to India in seven years confirmed the gradual improvement in relations since the military disengagement arrangements initiated in 2024. The two leaders agreed that India and China should regard each other as partners rather than rivals and approach bilateral relations from a long-term strategic perspective. They also discussed the boundary question, trade, market access and supply-chain problems. Any durable improvement in India–China relations must be welcomed. The prolonged confrontation following the Galwan Valley clash of 2020 damaged both countries, increased military expenditure and strengthened the efforts of the United States to incorporate India into its Indo-Pacific strategy. Washington has consistently sought to turn India into a strategic counterweight to China through the Quad and expanding military and technological cooperation. A continuing India–China confrontation therefore serves US strategy far more than it serves the interests of the Indian or Chinese peoples. Peace and tranquillity along the Line of Actual Control remain indispensable. Agreements on disengagement must be followed by sustained diplomatic and military-level engagement, clarification of disputed areas and strict observance of existing arrangements. Neither nationalist rhetoric nor competitive infrastructure construction should be allowed to trigger another confrontation. Differences over the boundary cannot be wished away, but they can and must be managed without subordinating the entire relationship to militarised rivalry. Economic relations present an equally difficult contradiction. China is one of India’s largest trading partners and an important source of machinery, electronic components, chemicals, renewable-energy equipment and other industrial inputs. India’s enormous trade deficit with China, however, reflects both the weakness of Indian manufacturing and the obstacles faced by Indian exports in the Chinese market. Addressing this imbalance requires improved market access, Chinese investment under transparent conditions and, above all, an Indian industrial policy capable of developing domestic productive capacity. Over the past six months, two-way bilateral trade has soared . The Modi government’s approach has alternated between dependence on Chinese imports and politically motivated restrictions on Chinese investment and technology. Its “China-plus-one” rhetoric cannot conceal the failure of its own policies to generate broad-based industrialisation. Nor can closer strategic alignment with the United States supply India with an independent manufacturing base. The US seeks privileged access to the Indian market while using tariffs, technology restrictions and political pressure whenever Indian policies diverge from its interests. The incoherence of the Modi government is best demonstrated in its inability to actually produce a national development agenda that takes advantage of the buoyancy of the Chinese economy and disengages from a United State more geared towards economic nationalism. China, for its part, must recognize that stable relations require sensitivity to India’s legitimate concerns. These include the unresolved boundary, unequal market access and the trade imbalance. The considerable asymmetry between the two economies makes reciprocity particularly important. Beijing’s call for partnership will acquire substance only when accompanied by practical measures enabling Indian goods and services to enter the Chinese market and preventing border tensions from repeatedly overwhelming political dialogue. Cooperation between India and China would give BRICS+ far greater weight in the struggle for democratization of the international order. Together, the two countries represent more than a third of humanity. Their cooperation could advance reform of international institutions, climate finance, technology sharing, public-health initiatives, and a fairer trading system. Conversely, renewed confrontation would paralyse BRICS+ and permit outside powers to exploit divisions throughout Asia. India must therefore pursue an independent foreign policy based on peaceful coexistence, not participate in a US project to contain China. The New Delhi summit produced proposals for institutional continuity, stronger financial cooperation, and greater resilience among developing economies. But declarations will have limited value unless translated into material arrangements. The New Development Bank must expand lending in national currencies and prioritize socially necessary infrastructure rather than reproduce the conditionalities and corporate orientation of the World Bank. Cross-border payment mechanisms must be developed transparently, without creating new forms of domination by the strongest BRICS economies. The strong statements on behalf of the Cuban Revolution and the Palestinian people’s aspirations for self-determination will need practical application. Will the BRICS+ find a way to break the cruel and illegal US embargo on Cuba, and will the BRICS+ provide support for the revival of Palestinian political life? BRICS+ embodies both the movement towards a new world equilibrium and all the contradictions of that movement. The cautious India–China rapprochement is one example of the contradiction, combining recognition of mutual dependence with unresolved strategic distrust. The future of BRICS+ will depend upon whether such contradictions are peacefully managed and whether cooperation delivers tangible gains to working people rather than merely expanding the room for maneuver of national ruling classes. For India, the necessary course is clear: restore normal and peaceful relations with China, resist incorporation into the United States’ military strategy, oppose aggression and unilateral sanctions against Iran, and work for a genuinely democratic multilateral order. A new world equilibrium will only be when it restricts hyper-imperialist domination and enlarges the sovereignty and developmental possibilities of peoples. The New Delhi summit marked another step in that contested transition, but the direction and social content of the emerging order remain matters of political struggle.
