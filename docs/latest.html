United Auto Workers (UAW) President Shawn Fain and the union’s newly elected International Executive Board (IEB) were sworn into office for a new four-year term in a ceremony honoring the will of the membership and reaffirming the union’s commitment to fighting for its members and all working-class people. Press is encouraged to use photos from the swearing-in ceremony . The ceremony marked the beginning of a new term for Fain and the International Executive Board, who pledged to faithfully serve the union’s membership and advance the interests of UAW members, families, and their communities. UAW member and longtime union leader Paul Caucci administered the oath of office to Fain, who begins his second term as International Union president. Fain then administered the constitutional obligation to the newly elected members of the International Executive Board. In remarks following the ceremony, Fain reflected on the responsibility of union leadership and outlined the priorities for the next four years: growing the UAW, building worker power and winning improvements for working-class people. Fain connected the UAW’s history of collective action to the challenges facing workers today, pointing to the legacy of the 1930s sit-down strikes and the union’s more recent Stand Up Strike in 2023. “Our union built the American Dream,” Fain said. “From the Sit Down Strikers of the 1930’s to the Stand-Up Strikers since 2023, regular working-class people have led this union and led this country from the ground up.” “Over the next four years, our job is to do more than just to ensure the survival of our organization,” Fain said. “We need to grow. We need to build. We need to win. And to do that, we need each other.” Fain emphasized that the UAW is a democratic, member-driven organization and that the union’s future depends on members being prepared and empowered to fight for their interests. “This is a democracy,” Fain said. “We are only in this room because the members put us in this room. And we will only win what the members are prepared and empowered to fight for.” The president also highlighted the broader stakes of the labor movement, citing wealth inequality and the influence of corporate power as challenges facing working people. He urged UAW leaders to meet those challenges through faith, solidarity and collective action. “The only path to turning this situation around is by abandoning fear and having faith that’s rooted in solidarity,” Fain said. Fain closed his remarks by calling on the board to approach the next four years with a shared sense of purpose and responsibility. “It’s not our individual legacies on the line. It’s not just our union on the line,” Fain said. “It’s the fate of the working class and the fate of humanity.” “And together, we will rise to the occasion—to meet our generation’s defining moment. Economics News for Workers, October 2026

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: UE NEWS Updates | UE ／ 著者: 不明 ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-16T18:40:23+00:00

On September 16, the Federal Reserve Board raised its benchmark interest rate by a quarter of a percent, the first rate hike in three years. Minutes from the meeting which were released in early October indicate that the board of governors expects to impose another rate increase before the end of the year. They meet again on October 28 and December 9. The rate increase, while justified as a means to fight inflation, was primarily meant to reassure international financial markets that the U.S. will make good on its debt, which has spiraled due to the combination of tax cuts for the wealthy and military spending. Higher interest rates are essentially a tax imposed on working people, who will pay higher rates on credit cards and mortgages while also bearing the brunt of slower job growth. (When interest rates are higher, it is more expensive for businesses to borrow money, and they are less likely to expand and hire new workers.) Rising Energy Prices Erase All Wage Gains Since Trump’s Inauguration New data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ September jobs report and Consumer Price Index numbers shows that over the past year, inflation has increased by 3.7 percent, while hourly wage rate increases have fallen to only three percent. This means that the average American worker has seen a 0.7 percent real decline in the value of their wages since September 2025. Or, to put it another way, there has been no net increase in real wages since Trump took office in January, 2025. McDonald’s Using AI to Gouge Consumers In another sign that mature businesses will attempt any tactic to maximize profit, fast food company McDonald’s is now using dynamic pricing based on AI-gathered information. Algorithms track sales across 14,000 restaurants and bring in outside data, such as publicly-posted prices by competitors, to determine how much locals are willing to pay for an item on the menu. A recent article in Reuters notes that comparing two restaurants two miles apart, one is selling a Big Mac for a 21 percent markup over the second. Dynamic pricing has become commonly used by online retailers and rideshare apps like Uber, but the expansion into brick-and-mortar retail is a relatively recent dynamic. Water Bills Up 62 Percent in Last Decade A new study by Food & Water Watch has found that water bills for U.S. households have increased by 62 percent over the past ten years. By comparison, food prices have increased by 30 percent and overall consumer prices by 39 percent in the same period. “Corporate water abuses, federal disinvestment and climate change are supercharging water rate hikes across the country,” said study co-author Mary Grant, the water program director at Food & Water Watch. “Corporate control of water systems exacerbates this affordability crisis, as big water monopolies take away local control, prioritize profit and hike water prices even higher.” The study found that corporate-owner, for-profit water companies charged, on average, 67 percent higher rates than publicly-owned systems. Damaging New Rules on Student Loans Take Effect A new Education Department rule finalized in September means that for the first time, the government will judge eligibility for student loans based upon the “earnings potential” of graduates of the program. Essentially, the department will now look at the average earnings of graduates of a major or degree. If, for example, graduates of a particular bachelor's program make less on average than those with only a high school diploma, the program will be ineligible for federal loans. A defense of the new rule published earlier this year by an American Enterprise Institute fellow who was one of its architects noted the bulk of impacted students were enrolled in “undergraduate certificates” (often at for-profit institutions) in areas like cosmetology and massage therapy, occupations that required no educational background prior to the last few decades of degree inflation. However, the author’s own study also notes that almost two thirds of Master’s degrees in areas like social work and mental health fail to meet the earnings threshold, as the average graduate makes less money than the median worker with just a college degree. Given the pervasive underfunding of social and mental health services, it’s unlikely that the pay scale of these degrees will improve, meaning a critical shortage of new workers in these fields is the most likely outcome. Scammers Rob SNAP Recipients While Trump Administration Does Little to Stop Them Propublica has published an expose on the growing issue of food stamp theft. This is not the theft fearmongered about in right-wing media — recipients using food stamps towards improper purchases — but organized theft of SNAP benefits from recipients . In 2025,this totalled $470 million in benefits, with nearly one million households having had some money stolen. Typically this happens when the mag-stripe information on a card is intercepted (often due to purchases at a disreputable store), with a third party draining the account by purchasing large amounts of baby formula, energy drinks, or other valid SNAP purchase. The thief then resells the purchased product for profit. The most outrageous aspect of this SNAP theft is it would be easy to solve if electronic benefit transfer cards integrated the same sort of chip technology now otherwise universal for credit and debit cards. Indeed, a handful of states, including California, have introduced said cards (which in the case of California, reduced SNAP theft by 83 percent). However, cuts to SNAP funding under the “One Big Beautiful Bill” mean that the vast majority of states are blocked from implementing similar programs. In addition, 2025 cuts made by Elon Musk’s short-lived but highly destructive “Department of Government Oversight” have decimated the number of staff who oversee SNAP. Finally, Trump reversed a Biden-era policy which allowed for recipients to be reimbursed for stolen benefits, meaning that even in the rare cases these scammers are caught, no restitution is paid back to those who had to do without food assistance due to theft.

Dattner’s Architects Say They Want a Union

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Alex N. Press ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-16T18:15:44+00:00

On the west side of Manhattan, near the entrance to the Holland Tunnel, there is a nearly seventy-foot-tall concrete building containing five thousand tons of road salt. The walls of the Spring Street Salt Shed slope inward and outward in enormous pale planes; the whole thing is meant to resemble a grain of salt. Across the street stands a much larger garage that accommodates more than 150 sanitation vehicles, its long facades covered in glass and aluminum louvers. The two buildings were designed together, an attempt to make a sprawling sanitation facility fit into a residential neighborhood where residents had initially fought its construction. Writing about the complex in 2016, Architectural Record called the shed “as compelling as many pieces of public art in Manhattan.” The Salt Shed is a Dattner Architects project, designed with WXY Architecture + Urban Design. So are the 34th Street–Hudson Yards subway station, the Bronx Library Center, parts of Hudson River Park, Riverbank State Park, and thousands of units of housing around New York. Richard Dattner founded the firm in 1964, and public architecture and infrastructure have been central to its work ever since. Now the more than one hundred workers at Dattner are taking an unusual step in their industry: organizing with the International Association of Machinists and Aerospace Workers (IAM). The proposed wall-to-wall bargaining unit includes designers, architects, and operations staff across the firm, excluding management and certain other positions. Organizers say they have support from a supermajority of workers. On October 21, Dattner workers will gather at city hall for a listening session with Deputy Mayor Julie Su to discuss the union effort. The meeting comes as workers are pressing the firm’s partners to agree to a process for recognizing their union without a National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) election. “The City has long worked alongside union employers, and we’ve seen the value that worker voice brings to public projects,” said Cassio Mendoza, a spokesperson for Mayor Zohran Mamdani. “We support every worker’s free and fair choice on this question, and the decision belongs to them.” The campaign has been quietly underway for years, extending across Dattner’s three studios and among employees at different points in their careers. Building a supermajority at a firm of this size took time, particularly with workers coming to the campaign for different reasons. “Some might be personal or specific to the firm, while for others, it might be about broader concerns with the industry and previous experiences that they had,” a Dattner designer said. Three Dattner employees spoke to Jacobin for this story. We would ordinarily identify workers discussing their organizing efforts, but there have been allegations of retaliation against union supporters in the architecture industry in recent years. At the firm Snøhetta, eight employees lost their jobs shortly after a 2023 union election. The NLRB later accused the firm of firing them for their union activity. The case has yet to be resolved. We are withholding the Dattner employees’ names so they can speak freely about the campaign. Dattner is, by the accounts of the workers I spoke with, a relatively good place to work. A licensed architect at Dattner described it as a leader in working conditions within the industry. That makes its union campaign an opportunity to set a precedent. “If we don’t lead this effort, if a firm like ours just can’t or won’t lead this effort, who’s going to?” the architect asked. Dattner calls itself a mission-driven firm . Riverbank State Park puts a park atop a wastewater treatment plant; Via Verde, designed with Grimshaw, is a mixed-income housing development in the South Bronx; the 34th Street–Hudson Yards station was the first new subway station to open in the city in more than twenty-five years. Much of the firm’s work is commissioned by public agencies. “We’re trying to create in our work a more democratic city, more democratic spaces for people to live in,” the architect said. “If we want to make more democratic spaces, that starts in the office. And having a more democratic process in the office is only going to allow us to do our jobs better, fulfill our mission better.” The Work Behind the Buildings The architect is easy to imagine somewhere above the city, a kind of deity with the vision to change the landscape of a metropolis and the power to make everyone else go along: scores of people to build it, and the rest of us to live in it. A line on an architectural drawing can eventually become an instruction for many others to build something. But getting from that line to the building requires engineers, contractors, construction workers, clients, and public agencies, along with plenty of work inside the architecture firm itself. The work can mean navigating building codes and zoning rules, responding to clients, and producing and revising digital models and drawings. “There is drudge work,” an associate at Dattner said. “It’s not all drawing pictures and picking colors.” Becoming a licensed architect can take a long time, too. According to the National Council of Architectural Registration Boards, the average person who earned a license in 2025 took 12.3 years to get there, including their education. For all that education and training, architecture is not an especially lucrative profession for many of its practitioners. The profession nevertheless retains a powerful image of the architect as a singular creative genius, a Frank Lloyd Wright whose talent might eventually bring wealth and acclaim. For those who imagine they might someday join his ranks, long hours and modest pay can seem like dues worth paying. “There’s this culture of suffering for the work, of pouring yourself into it because it is this creative discipline,” the Dattner architect said. “I’ve been in situations before where we felt like we needed to explore another idea, we needed to explore another scheme, and it’s just not acceptable to say, ‘We don’t have time for that.’” Long hours are familiar to architects well before they enter the profession. Architecture students routinely spend nights in the studio, and the hours don’t necessarily improve once they’re employed. Firms compete for commissions, often on price, and employees can end up working nights and weekends to get projects finished. “I know many of my classmates who have already left the profession before they got licensed because it wasn’t sustainable for them,” the designer said. The cost of living in New York has made the profession’s pay harder to accept. “My rent costs more money, and I’m more exhausted than I’ve ever been in my life,” the Dattner associate said. “We were all sold this bill of goods: go to college, get educated, become a white-collar professional, and you’re going to have a good life,” said the Dattner architect: We love what we do. I do have a good life, and there are certainly many working-class people who are being squeezed much more. But between the affordability crisis, AI, and discussions of how things need to change in our industry that have been going on for the past decade, it has created a moment where folks like us see an opportunity. People who came before us fought for labor rights, fought for the ability to bargain, and now it’s our time to take up the charge. How Architects Started Organizing Architects have not traditionally thought of themselves as the sort of workers who form unions. But neither did many other white-collar professionals — including graduate students, journalists , and tech workers — who have been organizing in recent years. C. G. Beck, an architect and writer involved in organizing in the industry, welcomed the Dattner campaign. “Dattner’s unionization campaign is a step forward for all workers in our industry,” Beck said. Peggy Deamer, an architect and a founder of the Architecture Lobby , a grassroots organization advocating for better working conditions and unionization in the profession, has spent years urging architects to think of themselves as workers, with the same concerns about pay, hours, and working conditions as anyone else. Through its Architectural Workers United campaign, the IAM has been involved in a small wave of organizing efforts at New York City architecture firms, where private sector unionism had barely existed for generations. In 2021, workers at SHoP Architects filed for an NLRB election with the IAM. They withdrew the petition shortly before the scheduled vote after what the union described as a “powerful anti-union campaign.” In 2022, workers at Bernheimer Architecture took another route: the firm voluntarily recognized its union, the first union at a private sector American architecture firm in decades. The workers ratified a first contract in 2024, winning a thirty-six-hour workweek, compensatory time off for additional hours, and overtime pay after forty-five hours. Workers at Snøhetta voted thirty-five to twenty-nine against unionization in 2023. At Sage and Coombe Architects, workers won voluntary recognition that year and ratified a first contract this spring. Of those campaigns, the two that have reached a first contract were recognized voluntarily. At Dattner, workers have asked management to agree to a process for recognizing the union without an NLRB election. Under the proposed agreement, Dattner would remain neutral while a third party checked whether a majority of employees had signed union cards. The Fight for Recognition On September 23, workers sent Dattner’s partners a letter requesting a card-check neutrality agreement. Six days later, the partners circulated a response to employees, reviewed by Jacobin , calling the IAM’s approach “secretive” and arguing that union representation should be decided through an election, “just as the way we pick a mayor or senator.” Architecture firms have been struggling with a prolonged downturn. According to the American Institute of Architects, firms have reported declining billings for most of the past three and a half years. In August, firms in the Northeast reported their steepest decline since 2020. The partners, meanwhile, warned that unionization could cost Dattner clients, claiming that the small architecture firms where the IAM had won recognition “have not thrived in the wake of unionization and the employees do not seem to have benefitted.” They also claimed that once employees unionized, it would become illegal for management to make individualized accommodations for employees, although federal labor law does not categorically prohibit such arrangements. They further disputed the idea that collective bargaining would give workers a say in which projects the firm pursued or how it conducted business. Dattner’s management did not respond to a request for comment. The organizing committee sent a second letter on October 5. “We are not requesting voluntary recognition, nor an election agreement,” the workers wrote. “Considering the current federal administration, we believe that it would be best for everyone to find a process outside of the NLRB.” They also emphasized that they were not seeking a predetermined union contract. “Our contract is our own,” they wrote. They asked the partners to meet with their union representative and respond by Friday, October 9. In an email to staff on that date, Dattner management wrote that it did not believe card-check neutrality “would be in the best interest of Dattner Architects or our employees.” “Meet us at the table,” the Dattner associate said when asked what she would like to say to Dattner’s management. “We’re ready and excited to talk about the ways that we see this being beneficial, not just for the firm, but for something larger than ourselves.” If the union is recognized, the IAM says, Dattner would become the largest unionized architecture firm in the United States. Workers argued that unionization could help the firm retain employees and appeal to clients that share its commitments. They also hoped to have more influence over how the firm operates, including greater transparency around compensation. “We can’t see the books,” the Dattner associate said. “The office purports to have positive values, including equity, and they tell us that we have pay equity. But we can’t confirm that without really having a seat at the table.” “This is not a movement against anything,” she said. “It is a movement for something. There’s a lot of positive energy among the group about how working as architects doing important work for the city and beyond can be something that also includes a great quality of life and a great working experience.” This article originally appeared in Jacobin , a democratic socialist magazine publishing long-form essays and analysis on politics, economics, and culture. More Than Ball Fields

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Bill Gould ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-16T17:26:32+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. For generations of Altadena families, Charles S. Farnsworth Park has been much more than ballfields at the foot of the San Gabriel Mountains. It has been one of the places where Altadena comes together. Children learned to throw and catch there, parents watched from the stands, coaches became mentors and neighbors became friends. But Farnsworth has always served a broader community. Its playgrounds, tennis and pickleball courts and basketball courts provided recreation for all ages. The historic Davies Building and amphitheater hosted plays, concerts, voting and community celebrations. Farnsworth was not simply a park; it was part of Altadena’s civic life. As someone whose family lost our home and all of our belongings in the Eaton Fire, I have spent much of the past year thinking and writing about what genuine recovery should mean. I have argued that rebuilding must be centered on people and that survivors should not be left to navigate recovery alone. For many families, this process will take years. That is why rebuilding places like Farnsworth matters. Before the fire, I wrote about my family’s experience with baseball in Altadena. Our son grew up playing with Central Altadena Little League (C.A.L.L.) and the Altadena Baseball Academy, much of it at Farnsworth. I described the park as a “truly picturesque backdrop,” with the San Gabriel Mountains giving Altadena baseball a home-field advantage that had little to do with the scoreboard. Related: Rebuilding After the Eaton Fire: Disaster Recovery Isn’t Working. Here’s What Survivors Really Need The Eaton Fire devastated Altadena in January 2025, destroying thousands of homes and severely damaging Farnsworth. C.A.L.L. suddenly found itself without its longtime home. Yet baseball became one way Altadena refused to surrender its sense of community. That did not happen by accident. C.A.L.L.’s board and league leaders — many of whom lost homes or were displaced — made the remarkable decision to keep the league going. Working with coaches, parents, donors and neighboring leagues that offered fields, they put together a 2025 season and created some normalcy for children experiencing extraordinary disruption. That story became the documentary Going for Home. The Los Angeles Dodgers Foundation’s announcement that it will invest $1.4 million to restore two fields at Farnsworth therefore represents something larger than a sports construction project. Dodgers Dreamfields 69 and 70 will include improved playing surfaces and irrigation, fencing, shade structures, solar-powered scoreboards modeled after those at Dodger Stadium and a STEM Trail incorporating the foundation’s Science of Baseball curriculum. Groundbreaking is scheduled for Nov. 5. For Los Angeles Dodgers Foundation CEO Nichol McKenzie-Whiteman, the devastation gave the organization’s work a different meaning. “The Los Angeles Dodgers Foundation has been revitalizing baseball fields since 2003, but we’ve never had to restore fields due to events like the ones that occurred last year,” McKenzie-Whiteman said. “For me, it put our work in a new light; we’re restoring places that held deep sentimental value for generations of families who gathered and played in these parks.” Norma Garcia, Director of the County of Los Angeles Department of Parks and Recreation, asked the Dodgers Foundation to help with Altadena’s parks. Her department also raised $100,000 from DICK’S Sporting Goods to help pay for stands and shade — important additions for families spending hours at the fields. Garcia said the County has requested FEMA assistance for Farnsworth but, so far, has received no FEMA help for the park. Garcia described the new fields as representing “hope, healing, and renewed opportunities” for Altadena’s young people and families. For Matthew Milton, whose family has deep roots in C.A.L.L., the announcement carries special significance. “This is a blessing,” Milton said. “I’m glad the history of Jackie Robinson from this area has led the Dodgers Foundation to make this meaningful contribution to Altadena.” That connection is fitting. Jackie Robinson’s roots in the Pasadena-Altadena area are an important part of the region’s African American history. C.A.L.L. has also reflected Altadena’s rich African American and Latino history, with generations of players, families and coaches sharing the fields and sustaining youth baseball in the community. The Dreamfields connect that history to another generation of children — perhaps another Jackie Robinson among them — who need places to play, gather and simply be children. C.A.L.L. President Phillip Pollerana said Altadena is excited to get Farnsworth operating again. For Little League families — and a community filled with Dodgers fans — the Dodgers organization’s investment carries practical and symbolic meaning. Dart Cathirell, founder of the Altadena Baseball Academy, is especially pleased the project will include new scoreboards and shaded stands. Cathirell and his coaches have spent years providing young players with instruction, mentorship and opportunities to develop their skills and love of baseball. Those details — scoreboards, shade, fencing and quality playing surfaces — might seem small compared with the enormous challenge of rebuilding Altadena. They are not. Recovery is not simply the reconstruction of houses. Parks, schools, churches, libraries, ballfields and local businesses are connective tissue. They transform a collection of houses into a community. Restoring them gives displaced families another reason to return and gives children back places that defined their lives before the fire. We have already seen that power at Loma Alta Park. Its two Dodgers Dreamfields gave C.A.L.L. a place to play while Farnsworth remained unusable. The 2025 season became extraordinary: Central Altadena’s Braves won the District 17 Tournament of Champions, the first division championship in league history. Altadena’s recovery cannot be measured only by building permits or reconstructed houses. It must also be measured by whether families return, longtime institutions survive, children have safe places to play and neighbors again have places to engage one another.The Dodgers Foundation’s investment cannot solve all of Altadena’s rebuilding challenges. But it demonstrates what meaningful partnership can look like: listening to residents, investing substantial resources and building something intended to endure after the immediate disaster response has faded. Children will run onto the fields at Farnsworth again. Parents will settle into shaded stands. Baseballs will hit gloves, runs will illuminate new scoreboards, and coaches will teach another generation the game. Beyond the ballfields, families will return to Farnsworth’s playgrounds and courts and community life will return to the Davies Building and amphitheater. Against the San Gabriel Mountains, Farnsworth can once again become a place where Altadena gathers. For a community rebuilding from unimaginable loss, we should expect that future, invest in it and work together to make it happen. It will mean a piece of Altadena has returned home.

In India, “Cockroaches” Are Leading the Fight for Democracy

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Prajwal Bhat ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-16T17:19:58+00:00

On October 10, India’s capital city, New Delhi, was turned into a fortress as protesters tried to oppose a countrywide exercise to revise the electoral rolls. The protests were called by the Gen Z movement known as the Cockroach Janta Party (CJP), working with left-wing student groups, which forced the resignation of the minister of education in July. In response, the Indian government deployed more than twenty thousand police officers and paramilitary personnel, suspended internet access in central Delhi, and barricaded the city center. Thousands of protesters, including the leaders of the CJP and prominent opposition politicians, were detained. The protests stem from a massive government effort, known as the Special Intensive Revision (SIR), to revise India’s electoral rolls by removing dead, duplicate, and ineligible voters. Concerns intensified in the last month that under the SIR eligible voters are losing their right to vote, with 130 million names left off the draft electoral rolls since the start of the program. The events of the last year are part of a larger pattern that has cast doubts on the legitimacy of India’s elections. There are growing fears the SIR was designed to advance Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s goal of a centralized and authoritarian government. Evidence suggests that it handed Modi’s Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) an electoral advantage in recent state elections by removing voters from marginalized communities, including Muslim voters. The Gen Z protests have garnered support from a broad spectrum of opposition parties, including those on the Left. But the real energy of the protests is coming from the ingenuity and irreverence of young Indians who have flooded the streets of New Delhi despite the violent crackdown. In a country that has long touted itself as the world’s largest democracy, the developments are striking and could prove a turning point for its future. How Modi Changed India’s Elections While the electoral rolls in India are revised every year to add new voters and remove people who have died or moved away, the SIR, which launched back in June 2025, has inverted the voter registration process for the first time in the country’s history. Instead of election officers verifying voters at their homes, the SIR asked voters to fill out a form and prove their eligibility to vote. Voters are matched against records from a previous SIR conducted between 2002 and 2004 and in many cases asked to provide documents showing they are eligible to vote. Critics of the SIR claim that it is stripping eligible voters from the rolls. The SIR is the work of the Election Commission of India (ECI), the country’s chief electoral body, which oversees national elections and provincial elections, including in states with populations larger than California’s. The commission is led by a three-person panel whose chief, like a Supreme Court judge, can be removed only if the Parliament impeaches them. In a key development in December 2023, the Indian government passed a law making changes to the selection process for choosing election commissioners. The result is that the executive branch of the government now has the most influence in the process. The same law also quietly inserted a clause giving these commissioners legal immunity. Making matters worse, the election commissioner responsible for introducing the SIR, Gyanesh Kumar, just so happens to be the first chief election commissioner appointed under the new law, raising questions about what’s really going on. How Centralization of Voter Rolls Could Thwart Democracy in India Criticism of the SIR intensified after The Indian Express reported on September 23 that two of the three election commissioners were sidelined despite raising objections at least fourteen times about the exercise. Among their criticisms were concerns about the process in which new voters were registered, how names were deleted and restored, who had the power to file appeals against restored voters, and who had access to the voter database. The electoral body has maintained that access to the voters’ list is secure and decentralized, meaning that local electoral officers make the judgment on who stays on the voter list. But the revelations in The Indian Express showed that the SIR was relying on ECINET, a digital platform that logs voters. The result is increasing centralization, giving more control over the voter rolls to the commission’s headquarters in Delhi. In one instance, the newspaper documented that officials in the state of Goa flagged ninety-seven voters who were mistakenly removed. Despite eight emails from Goa’s chief electoral officer to officials in New Delhi, the voters were knocked off the rolls when the deadline passed. India’s principal opposition leader, Rahul Gandhi of the Indian National Congress, alleged that the additions and deletions of voters were undertaken as an exercise to disenfranchise a section of voters critical of Modi’s BJP. In the state of West Bengal, the exercise removed some nine million names from the electoral rolls in the months before the state elections in April 2026. Modi’s BJP won power in the state for the first time in the election. Thirty-four percent of those removed from the rolls were Muslim even though they make up just 27 percent of the state’s population. The BJP has maintained that many Muslims eliminated from the lists were “illegal Bangladeshi migrants,” even though evidence has shown scores of Indian nationals were also removed. Of the few appeals tribunals have decided — under 3 percent of some 2.5 million — about 90 percent ended in restoration. With the BJP falling short of a majority in the 2024 general election, the stakes are high for Modi. The party was forced to form a coalition with regional allies that do not share a Hindu-nationalist ideology. The opposition alliance led by Gandhi’s Congress won 234 seats, thirty-eight seats shy of a majority, despite the Congress having its bank accounts frozen and being comfortably outspent . The result is that for the first time in years many Indians are no longer afraid to openly criticize their government — they are even prepared to fight back. The Rise of India’s Cockroach Movement In May, the curiously named Cockroach Janta Party (CJP) first made global headlines after it hit roughly fifteen million followers on Instagram in a week — then again at the end of July after it forced the resignation of India’s minister of education, no small feat. Though the CJP appeared to come out of nowhere, it was the result of long-simmering tensions in India. It started as a satirical joke made by Abhijeet Dipke, an Indian student in Boston, after India’s chief justice, Surya Kant, likened unemployed young Indians questioning the government to “cockroaches” and “parasites.” Dipke posted a comment on X, “What if all cockroaches came together?” and millions responded. He returned from Boston to New Delhi on June 6 and headed directly to Jantar Mantar, launching a protest against the government’s mishandling of a competitive public exam. Though he had limited experience in political organizing, Dipke joined hands with left-wing student organizations in New Delhi. Their first protest lasted thirty-six days and attracted people from all over India and from all walks of life including a vast number of students, exam aspirants, schoolteachers, construction laborers, activists working with civil society organizations, Dalits, Muslims, and other social minorities. By taking up the issue of competitive exams, the movement’s leaders tapped into frustration among young people in India who have seen pathways to social mobility fall apart in recent years. Among those with graduate and postgraduate degrees, the rate of unemployment is particularly high. More than twelve million candidates applied for 8,868 openings in the state-owned Indian Railways company in 2025. The unemployment crisis is further compounded by the high cost of living, hikes in petrol prices, and the effects of pollution, which has left a near-permanent industrial haze over many Indian cities. The country has touted 7.7 percent growth in GDP numbers for the last fiscal year while ignoring persistent inequities on the ground. A Democracy Under Mortal Threat Despite well-documented inequalities in the country, India has long maintained an orderly and transparent electoral process. But a generation of political observers have now witnessed the erosion of once powerful institutions and the threat of detentions and police action against dissenters. In 2019, as Modi ran for a second term, the Election Commission cleared him in six separate complaints that he had violated the election code of conduct. In his second term, he further undermined the independence of the selection of election commissioners, seen as a fresh attempt to tip the political playing field in favor of his party and turn India into a one-party state. Then in 2024, Modi led an even more divisive campaign even as his opposition decried inaction from the election commission. The SIR exercise too has invited widespread public anger since it asked citizens to submit multiple documents simply to prove their eligibility to vote. In some cases, it even forced people identified as dead to come forward to prove that they were, in fact, alive. On September 24, a day after The Indian Express broke the story on the many inefficiencies of the SIR, Abhijeet Dipke and the Cockroach Janta Party called for a protest seeking the removal of the chief election commissioner, Gyanesh Kumar, and the rollback of the SIR process. And with that, the “cockroaches” kickstarted an even bigger public outcry. Mass Protests Are Needed to Protect Voting Rights As with the summer protests over education reforms, the Gen Z movement hoped to channel the growing anger in India over the undermining of democratic elections. Alongside the Cockroach Janta Party, the left-wing student organization All India Students’ Association (AISA) was credited with some of the more robust protest actions in the past week. AISA is the student wing of the Communist Party of India (Marxist–Leninist) Liberation, and its cadre have had to use all their experience, discipline, and political commitment in organizing mass collective actions in the protests. “[The protests] have opened democracy’s Pandora’s box to talk about all the other ways we can ask for accountability,” said Neha Bora, the president of AISA, speaking to Jacobin before this month’s protests. “The energy from this movement should be put into struggles to build our country anew. . . . We know that every mass protest is the possibility of something new and reinforces the idea there could be an end to this violent period.” The movement’s leaders, including Dipke of the CJP and Bora of AISA, announced a united mass protest on October 10. In response, police in India detained over ten thousand people across the country. Then on the day of the protest, the government deployed the overwhelming force of twenty thousand police and paramilitary personnel in order to prevent protesters from gathering in the city. The unprecedented crackdown has done little to stem the tide, as the opposition parties led by Rahul Gandhi held a protest of their own, marching to the Supreme Court on October 12 along with thousands of people. The sheer number who took to the streets this week was reminiscent of previous mass outcries in the Modi era against the disenfranchisement of India’s Muslim community, and against laws that would have left farmers vulnerable. But those protests faced a similar crackdown from the BJP: smear campaigns against the leaders, the use of excessive police force to stop demonstrations, and the eventual arrest of activists under anti-terror laws. Muslim activists and intellectuals like Sharjeel Imam and Umar Khalid have been jailed without trial for six years. However, the Gen Z movement has posed new questions to the BJP and has made it difficult for the party to control the narrative, despite its influence over mainstream television news channels in India. TV news has tried to paint the protesters as agents of India’s supposed external enemies like Pakistan and China. But the arrest of two of the Cockroach Janta Party’s leaders, Saurav Das and Ashutosh Ranka, while they were holding the Indian national flag has made that charge hard to sustain. The movement has not only been able to rapidly form a broad coalition on common social interests; it has also mobilized concerns shared by young Indians across religion, caste, and class divides. It has offered Indians the first real sign of lasting political change — little wonder that Modi is now throwing the full might of the state against them. Beginning in the marginalized banlieues, France’s high school revolt has become national in scope, taking on economic and racial oppression as well as police brutality. By October 13, the high school protest movement in France had mushroomed, prompting up to half a million people to take to the streets across the country. On that day, Swedish climate activist Greta Thunberg made a solidarity visit to one of the high schools where it all started, Lycée Paul Eluard, in the impoverished Paris banlieue (suburb) of Saint-Denis. Thunberg intoned that “the whole world is watching” and that it is “proud of the French students.” [1] With a two-week school vacation beginning on October 16, it remains to be seen if this remarkable movement, which has already lasted over three weeks, will be able to continue. Nevertheless, a new challenge from the left, and from the youngest of the youth, has been hurled at a racialized capitalist system which in France, as in so many other places, is moving in the direction of outright neofascism. Some three years ago, France experienced another nationwide uprising across its impoverished and ethnically oppressed banlieues and poorer city neighborhoods. On June 27, 2023, police gunned down Nahel Merzouk, a seventeen-year-old of Algerian descent who had refused to stop for questioning. As Nahel lay dead, the banlieues and other marginalized communities exploded, with teens and preteens in the lead. By the time the dust had settled five days later, 250 police stations and outposts had been attacked, 700 police injured, much property had been destroyed, and 3,000 youths had been arrested, many of them to be prosecuted mercilessly and jailed. For the French dominant classes, the mainstream media, the politicians, and the trade union bureaucrats, 2023 was seen as an irrational outburst of little long-term political or social import, something to be swept under the rug and forgotten. Little or nothing was done to address the underlying issues, least of all the country’s rampant police brutality and racial profiling. Related: Beyond Class Struggle: What Marx and Fanon Teach Us About Liberation In some ways, the September–October 2026 youth uprising has been a repeat of 2023. The communities where the current uprising originated are essentially the same as in 2023, and confrontations with the police, or rather vicious police violence against the youth, show that little has changed. The dominant rhetoric among politicians and the corporate media continues to characterize those in revolt as “savages” and the like. But 2026 is more than a larger version of 2023. First, this year’s uprising’s size, scope, and staying power have been greater. In the first week, it spread across high schools in poor and marginalized areas of the Paris region. As it did so, it became no longer only a spontaneous revolt, but also a movement making very concrete demands. Second, it has also spread from the banlieues and poorer city districts to more advantaged, even privileged high schools. It also involved parents, university students, and trade unions. All of this has helped break down the isolation of the banlieues , even if only for a time. Moreover, it has put into action the kinds of social forces that, if acting together, could really uproot the system. Third, partly as a result of these wider solidarities, the movement has been more organized and more political, and has expressed clear and comprehensive demands. This was facilitated by the fact that the uprising was centered in the schools, a more structured venue than an urban community, making its participants, even in the banlieues , less isolated and less vulnerable to demonization as street thugs, “savages,” and so on. Fourth, a greater number of key political figures have openly backed the youth than in 2023. Finally, all this has forced the center-right government to make a few small concessions, even though its main response has been demonization and repression. Because of these wider valences, 2026 is sometimes compared—both in the French media and globally—to the mass student-worker uprisings that were the high-water mark of the revolutionary year 1968, when ten million workers went on strike in the wake of university and workplace occupations. The comparison is surely an exaggeration. Nonetheless, 2026 is indeed a sign of wide and deep unrest of a type that cannot easily be contained by political regimes—like that of the neoliberal Emmanuel Macron in France—that represent a capitalism of austerity and whose support is plummeting. For the two sides of that conjuncture are a surge toward new leftwing political parties like La France Insoumise (LFI), and on the other side, toward the neofascist Rassemblement National (RN). Related: Trump’s “Annihilate Iran” Threat Raises the Specter of Nuclear War How the 2026 revolt unfolded On Friday, September 18, students at Lycée Saint-Exupéry in Créteil, a Paris banlieue , began to demonstrate at their school, soon after their return to classes after the summer break, and following a one-day teachers’ strike. This took place after climate change and massive wildfires had led to an unbearable summer across the country. Students experienced classrooms without air conditioning, in intolerable heat. In addition, classes were too large, school days too long, teachers missing due to lack of replacement hires, and buildings dilapidated. Even toilet paper was sometimes missing. An online student survey carried out by the government amid the ensuing protests found that school days as long as 8:00 am to 6:00 PM were one of the principal complaints, especially given climate change and lack of air conditioning. By the week of September 28, the mobilizations spread across the Paris region and then France as a whole. By Friday, October 2, protests had burst out in over a thousand high schools, with some two hundred being blockaded by students. Violence against property took place in a few locations, with a small fire set at Lycée Rosa Parks in Nantes in northwest France, to take one example. This was seized upon by the center-right government and the far right to brand the students as “savages,” rhetoric in turn used to justify the harsh police response. While the movement began and was most active in the banlieues and poorer urban districts, encompassing both trade schools and academic high schools, it became truly national and multi-class very quickly. For example, students at the prestigious Lycée Henri IV in a wealthy area of Paris—whose alumni include Jean-Paul Sartre, Michel Foucault, and President Macron himself—also went on strike, hanging a banner that read “Solidarity with the high schools.” [2] As journalists for Le Monde observed, “Some mobilized out of necessity, others rather from solidarity.” [3] In a number of extensively reported cases, police brutalized young students, among them a fifteen-year-old boy whose hand was torn off by a police “stun grenade.” This device is severely restricted or banned outright in many other European countries. After two weeks, the police announced that they had detained at least briefly some 6,877 people, 86 percent of them minors. Clearly, the goal of such a wide dragnet was to use fear to discourage participation. [4] Still wider solidarities emerged on October 6, when hundreds of thousands, perhaps even half a million people, took to the streets across France in support of the high schoolers. Some were university students and other older adults, many of whom wore white armbands as a sign that they were there to protect the country’s youth from the police—if necessary, by interposing themselves. Parents and other adults were doing the same outside many high schools. In Saint-Denis, another marginalized Paris suburb, Mayor Bally Bagayoko, a Black activist recently elected as a candidate for LFI, joined student demonstrators in an attempt to shield them from police attack. At universities, including the prestigious Sciences Po in Paris, where Palestine solidarity demonstrations over the past few years have faced surveillance and repression, students also engaged in solidarity actions. After taking over a nearby street, however, Sciences Po students were beaten back by a violent police attack. In Lille, seventeen-year-old Fanny (who prudently wanted to be quoted only by her first name) singled out “bad conditions in which to study, overcrowded classrooms, and buildings in need of repair.” But Fanny also went deeper, calling out “the climate catastrophe, artificial intelligence that is gobbling up jobs, and the risk that the RN will win the elections,” referring to Marine Le Pen’s neofascist party. An in-depth account of the protests in the Paris banlieue of Saint-Denis was published by Christophe Ayad, a journalist who has long distinguished himself by his coverage of both Middle East conflicts and France’s immigrant communities. As Ayad reported, student speakers at an October 4 rally asked, “Why do we have fewer chances than those from Paris?” Others declared, “There is a segregation dividing Paris and the suburbs due to Parcoursup”—a much-criticized online university selection system—“which is totally arbitrary. These inequalities are not normal!” Student speakers also decried media narratives that separated the banlieues from the rest of Paris and demonized banlieue students of color as the source of all violence: “There are the whites on one side, the Blacks and the Arabs on the other,” one said, adding, “When a student is non-white, police violence increases tenfold.” Other student protestors noted anti-Muslim discrimination: “Muslims are treated as enemies.” Still others raised wider, more political slogans, like support for Palestine. While expressing deep alienation, the students also referred to themselves as “the new France,” thus pointing to the more multiethnic and inclusive future society they envision. Mayor Bagayoko received huge applause when he told a crowd, “You honor the Republic; you honor its values; your demands are legitimate.” [6] Everywhere, the slogans reflected the anger, but also the imagination and verve, of a new generation. Referring to the militarized CRS riot police, students chanted, “You were sent an SOS and we received your CRS.” Adopting directly—and in English—a slogan associated with US Black Lives Matter movement, others chanted “ACAB” (All cops are bastards). This is particularly significant at a time when both social media and traditional TV and other mass media are increasingly under the control of rightwing oligarchs. The militancy and creativity of the youth have broken through those barriers, just as they have of late in India with the Cockroach Janta Party. The fact that such a militant and large-scale movement took place not in the Global South, but in Western Europe, illustrates the truly international scope of the struggle for human emancipation today. The French high school uprising of 2026 also shows, in a most dramatic manner, that the ballot box is far from the only way to enact social change, or at least to put it on the agenda. Taking to the streets and occupying key institutions is also a model, and a more radical one. Finally, the French youth revolt of 2026 has crossed barriers of race and class that have so often been used by dominant classes, from the days of slavery to today’s racialized capitalism, to keep oppressed sectors apart from each other and from critical intellectuals, thwarting the kind of cross-fertilization that is needed to truly challenge the system. What we have witnessed in France this year is not only an agenda of “unite and fight.” What has emerged is a movement in which the most oppressed sectors of society—youth of color, descendants of immigrants, and children of the working class—have taken the lead. The Social Reckoning Needed David Fincher

Reporting on how badly Aaron Sorkin’s Facebook drama The Social Reckoning is flopping, I won’t bother to do any better than USA Today ’s mild zinger , “Moviegoers are unfriending The Social Reckoning.” Meanwhile, the critically panned horror film Other Mommy is crushing it at the box office. But that’s unsurprising, because The Social Reckoning doesn’t have Jessica Chastain’s grinning, spider-crawling monster-mommy to draw people in during a Halloween season when the horror film pickings this year are rather slim. Of course, as The Social Reckoning ’s featured creature, Facebook CEO Mark Zuckerberg is pretty monstrous too, but even with Jeremy Strong playing him, he can’t convey the skin-crawling repulsiveness of this deeply weird billionaire tech mogul who strikes the public as so sociopathic, blank-eyed, and uneasy in his own skin that he seems likely to be a member of an alien species colonizing planet Earth. For Sorkin, it’s a very disappointing second bite at the Zuckerberg apple. The Social Network , which Sorkin wrote, had a major impact when it was released in 2010, because David Fincher made use of his remarkable gift for creating a miasma of dread in portraying the steady, willful, self-driven dehumanization of young Mark Zuckerberg. As astutely played by Jesse Eisenberg, Zuckerberg’s inner creep emerges and rapidly takes him over. It’s made aggressive by the competition from legions of other young male creeps around him at Harvard and later in the tech industry. The film gave us a nauseating look at how the most arrogant human scum rose to the top in the unwholesome nerdboy Olympics that gave rise to Silicon Valley and the global tech takeover of all our lives. It’s too bad Fincher wasn’t along for the ride this time. Here, Aaron Sorkin opted to direct his own script, which he’s insisting should not be considered a sequel to the ultra-successful Social Network . That’s a pretty futile stance toward a film that’s inevitably bound to be marketed and regarded as a sequel, especially because Sorkin himself keeps evoking the earlier film. As in The Social Network , for example, The Social Reckoning deploys a complex flashback structure framed by a team of professionals (played by Wunmi Mosaku and Bill Burr among others) serving as Mark Zuckerberg’s legal counsel and trying to coach him into appearing human before he testifies to congress. This involves such tasks as getting Zuckerberg to nod his head, smile, and show a modicum of empathy for anyone in the world, as a regular person might do. It’s a tall order, and Jeremy Strong goes all-out in portraying a man so pathologically icy and stiff that he’s the stuff of either nightmares or farcical comedy. The Sequel That Isn’t a Sequel — or Something Despite Sorkin’s claims that he’s not just made a sequel, he apparently had been fully confident that Jesse Eisenberg, the star of The Social Network , would be returning, noting rather bitterly that “The studio offered him every incentive you can offer an actor to do it.” Eisenberg turned the big-money role down flat, saying he no longer wanted to be identified with Mark Zuckerberg and “was tired or even aggravated by it,” according to Sorkin. Smart. Rising respect for Jesse Eisenberg. Obviously, Aaron Sorkin should never direct an Aaron Sorkin script, because that creates an exponentially greater seismic impact of Sorkin-ness on our quailing human consciousness. The Social Reckoning is a heavy plod of a movie that keeps attempting to weave in lighter qualities, with patented Sorkin rapid-fire dialogue and plenty of comic relief festooning a ponderously serious subject. As in all Sorkin works, like A Few Good Men , The West Wing , and Steve Jobs , the gradual bonding of fractious characters who are temperamentally opposed to each other plays out with maximum corny sentimentality. He undeniably has a good sense as to what works. And yet here, it simply doesn’t. In the end, the film winds up being an overly familiar narrative about real-life whistleblower Frances Haugen (Mikey Madison), who risks the wrath of Zuckerberg by joining forces with Wall Street Journal investigative reporter Jeff Horwitz (Jeremy Allen White) to expose what’s dubbed “The Facebook Files,” a series of articles on the deleterious effects of Facebook, Instagram, and other Zuckerberg-dominated internet platforms on an increasingly addicted public. It’s strange how belated a movie this seems, telling us all what we already know as the furious pace of terrible real-life events outruns The Social Reckoning by miles. As Frances, Mikey Madison plays a part completely opposed to her star-making, Oscar-winning portrayal of the vivid, confident, loudmouthed stripper in Sean Baker’s Anora (2024), which is probably one big reason she agreed to do it. As portrayed here, Frances is an introverted and dedicated data scientist who’s bought into the rhetoric about the democratizing ideal of Facebook and is made increasingly uneasy by what she’s finding in practice as a member of the Civic Integrity Team meant to guard the public’s interest. She keeps claiming to love her job at Facebook, but it’s impossible to see what she loves about it, besides the money and being able to eat in fancy restaurants. She works in a friendless hive of grim worker bees where paranoia reigns and no one will even glance at her findings on the dangerous effects of prolonged Facebook use on the public. Mikey Madison — an inherently vibrant performer and compulsively watchable — brings charming humanity and excellent timing to the role, keeping the movie tolerable if not adequate. She lightens up Jeremy Allen White, who’s been sinking into a morass of mockable self-seriousness since his star-making performance in the highly lauded FX series The Bear . He’s not playing one of his brooding depressives here, thank God. Together, Frances and Jeff arrive at the most alarming algorithmic tendency on Facebook, which is driving users toward ever more extreme content, stoking a tendency toward paranoia, self-righteousness, outspoken racism and xenophobia, and knee-jerk outrage that’s already led to the arrest of a former acquaintance of Frances’s. In the film’s opening sequence, we see this acquaintance, an overreactive blonde named Kayleigh (Portia Doubleday), filming her own traffic-stop encounter with the cops, which devolves into her fit of hysteria and ultimate arrest, shrieking racist insults and right-wing talking points as she’s cuffed and carted away. What made a monster of this young woman, whom Frances remembers as a sane and reasonable defender of progressive causes? Well, it’s the same monster-making Facebook practice that the film will present as the main motivating factor behind the violent January 6 attack on the US Capitol by lunatic Donald Trump supporters, many of them members of far-right groups like the Proud Boys and the Oath Keepers, trying to take back the presidency for their addled hero. And that climactic scene is shot here for massive slo-mo impact. Curiously, Sorkin avoids depicting some of the more absurd cosplaying figures, like Jacob Chansley, the “QAnon Shaman” in bison-horn headdress, and Trump entirely. Sorkin apparently wanted to ensure that it stayed a melodramatized American tragedy, uninflected by any element of the darkly ridiculous, that finally shocks even the uber-arrogant Mark Zuckerberg into repentant silence. And yet, of course, it’s ridiculous. A Political Film Hampered by Milquetoast Politics The Social Reckoning ’s politics are quite clear — Sorkin’s usual “progressive” liberal centrism versus the threat of right-wing conservative madness — which makes it a matter of teeth-grinding fury to read that Sorkin denies he has any such intention: I don’t have a political agenda. . . . I am not trying to persuade you of anything. I am trying to captivate you for however long I’ve asked for your attention in a dark theatre. Obviously, [ The Social Reckoning ] is dealing with something serious, but we’re not asking you to eat your vegetables. It’s not Homework: The Movie. We’ve heard this all before. Alejandro González Iñárritu just made the rounds denying any specific political agenda in his new film Digger , which is even more ludicrous in that case. Either the same cowardice seizes directors just as soon as it comes time to promote their films, or they’re getting coached by producers, studio executives, and PR flacks. Or, most likely, both. But Sorkin isn’t fooling anyone. The problem isn’t that the movie is political; it’s that its politics are not up to the task — not even close. Someone eventually will make a great movie about social-media-induced insanity. Unfortunately, The Social Reckoning is far from it, failing even to approach the power of its Fincher-directed (and Sorkin-penned!) predecessor. Maybe, just maybe, the guy who gave us The West Wing just isn’t the man for our times? Leftists Should Learn to Organize Like Evangelicals

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Eric Blanc ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-16T15:22:05+00:00

If progressives and radicals had all read Rick Warren’s The Purpose Driven Church when it came out in 1995, we’d be far stronger today. It’s the most important book on organizing that most leftists haven’t read. Luckily, it’s not too late to discover it. Warren founded Saddleback Church in suburban Orange County in 1980, starting with a Bible study in his home. Fifteen years later, more than ten thousand people were attending its services each weekend, and Saddleback had planted twenty-six other churches. The Purpose Driven Church was Warren’s attempt to explain how he’d done it, so that other pastors could do the same. It became a founding manual of “seeker-sensitive” Evangelicalism, which designs churches around the people who don’t yet attend one. By 2004, Warren’s book had sold more than a million copies, and it has since been translated into over thirty languages. Evangelical megachurches are among the few American membership institutions to grow during the past half-century of community collapse. Just compare the growth of evangelical megachurch weekly participation since 1970 to the decline in yearly attendance at union meetings. Roughly three decades ago, evangelicals like Warren systematized and scaled up organizing for our atomized era. The Left, by contrast, is still searching for a model. With a few partial exceptions, contemporary American liberals and radicals haven’t yet figured out how to build durable membership organizations capable of recruiting, retaining, and developing large numbers of people, beyond a small core of paid staff or self-selecting activists. We’re good at big moments like marches, viral campaigns, rallies, and the occasional electoral upset. We’re much worse at turning those moments into organizations that last — and grow. Warren’s book is a field manual, born from relentless trial and error, full of specific tips about greeters, parking spots, name tags, surveys, and membership classes. “Like a research and development center, we’ve experimented with all kinds of approaches to reaching, teaching, training, and sending out God’s people,” Warren notes. Of course, there are significant ideological differences between today’s evangelical churches and Left organizations; we face distinct structural challenges to succeed, and our organizations require a higher level of deliberative democracy. But what’s striking about Warren’s book is how familiar the problems he discusses are to our movements — and how useful his solutions are. Good labor and community organizers already use some of the following practices, but none of our organizations in the U.S. bring all of them together, much less as systematically as evangelicals. Those who doubt that we can replicate something like Warren’s systematic model need only look abroad to examples like the Workers’ Party of Belgium , which has leaned on similar methods to build thick organization in our socially thin era. Part 1 looks at what Warren teaches about recruitment. Part 2, which I’ll publish next week, turns to what happens once people are inside the door: how to retain and develop members to build an increasingly robust organizing machine. Here, then, are The Purpose Driven Church ’s top fourteen recruitment lessons for the Left. 1. Build the Belonging That Used to Grow Organically Warren’s starting point was that the old social glue was gone. For generations, churches (like unions and political parties) could count on people showing up out of habit and social pressure, since neighborhood communities were so tightly knit. “Instead, membership is now an act of commitment,” he writes. Organizing in hypersprawled Southern California, Warren saw that Americans had become mobile, lonely, isolated, and easily distracted by screens. “We’re experiencing an epidemic of loneliness in society,” he warns. But rather than simply mourn this, he spotted an opening: “This ‘longing for belonging’ provides the church with a timely opportunity. Positioning the church as an extended family, as ‘a place where you are cared for,’ will strike a sensitive chord in many lonely hearts.” Evangelicals like Warren grasped the organizing consequences of community collapse long before the Left did. Much of the progressive movement and the Left still organize as if community already exists and just needs to be mobilized or represented. In today’s atomized conditions, Warren convincingly explains, belonging has to be built deliberately, by design. 2. Let People Belong Before They Believe Belonging before belief is one of the core strategic insights of the seeker-sensitive evangelical movement. Warren’s approach starts from a fishing metaphor. “If your church is serious about reaching the unchurched, you must be willing to put up with people who have a lot of problems. Fishing is often messy and smelly. Many churches want the fish they catch to be already scaled, gutted, cleaned, and cooked. That is why they never reach anyone.” At Saddleback, newcomers were invited to “Come and see!” and weren’t expected to have their lives in order. “At Saddleback Church we do not expect unbelievers to act like believers until they are.” Warren would “rather have a southern California pagan attend our crowd service in shorts and a Budweiser® T-shirt than stay home or go to the beach.” He writes that he lost count of the unmarried couples who started attending while living together and then asked him to marry them once they converted; one pair had been living together for seventeen years. Nor did Warren care much why people first walked in the door. “It doesn’t matter why people initially come to Jesus, what matters is that they come. He can work on their motives, values, and priorities once they enter his presence.” While seeker-sensitive evangelicals let people belong before they believe, liberals and leftists tend to ask people to believe before they belong. This has major organizational repercussions. Warren sets a low bar at the door and a high bar for membership at the same time. “We expect very little from the seeker who is investigating the claims of Christ . . . . On the other hand, we require a major commitment from those who want to join our church.” Most of our organizations do the reverse. The bar at the door is high: you’re expected to know the right vocabulary and hold the right positions. Yet the bar for engagement is actually quite low: click a button, sign up for an email list, or, at most, pay modest dues. Nothing else is expected of you. 3. Study Your Terrain Warren repeatedly warns readers not to copy him. “To artificially plant a Saddleback clone in a different environment is a formula for failure,” he writes. “Even I wouldn’t do everything we do at Saddleback if I were in another part of the country. You must figure out what works best to reach seekers in your local context.” It’s a method, not a formula. Before holding a single service, Warren spent twelve weeks going door-to-door with five survey questions, starting with “What do you think is the greatest need in this area?” Reflecting on the responses he received to the question “If you were to look for a church to attend, what kind of things would you look for?” he recalls: “This single question taught me more about thinking like an unbeliever than my entire seminary training.” What he heard became Saddleback’s blueprint. Locals said sermons were boring, members were cliquish, churches only wanted their money, and the childcare was bad. So Warren rebuilt his preaching, trained greeters, announced every week that visitors weren’t expected to give money, and published strict childcare guidelines. Organizers know this as a listening campaign. The best unions do it before they pick their fights. But most activist groups decide their positions and messaging first, and only then go looking for an audience. Warren’s rule is blunt: “No book or demographic report can replace actually talking with the people in your community.” Asking questions is not just how you find out more; it’s also one of the best ways to get people to eventually open up to whatever you might have to offer. As Warren puts it, “I’ve learned that most people can’t hear until they’ve first been heard.” 4. Treat Recruitment as a Science Unlike much of the Left, Warren realized that just being “right” would not automatically translate into effective recruitment. Getting people through the door had to be treated like a science, full of experiments, rigorous assessments, and readjustments. Warren is refreshingly honest about how much failed. “The truth is, we’ve tried more things that didn’t work at Saddleback than did. We’ve never been afraid of failure; we just call everything an ‘experiment.’” He jokes that he could write another book called 1000 Ways to NOT Grow a Church . The Purpose Driven Church is mercilessly critical of self-flattering metrics. Warren recalls a Peanuts cartoon in which Charlie Brown is practicing archery. “Instead of aiming at a target, he would shoot an arrow at his fence and then walk over and draw a target around wherever the arrow stuck.” Too many churches, he says, do the same: “We just draw a bull’s-eye around whomever we reach and settle for that.” Nonprofits and left organizations do this constantly. Instead of questioning our priors, we declare success no matter what, based on whoever happened to show up. And we often count activity (doors knocked, emails sent, events held) or political righteousness instead of results. Warren insists on judging growth relative to what’s ambitiously possible: “To be realistic, you need to focus on the percentage of population being reached, not the actual numbers.” Above all, Warren refuses to let being correct serve as an excuse for being ineffective. He mocks congregations that treat their marginality as a badge of honor: “Some churches point to their lack of a crowd as proof that they are biblical, orthodox, or Spirit-filled.” Anyone who has spent time in small activist circles will recognize the type. 5. Win Hearts, Not Just Heads Warren, a Southern Baptist preacher who believes in biblical truth, is emphatic that arguments aren’t what win people over. “More people are won to Christ by feeling God’s presence than by all of our apologetic arguments combined. Few people, if any, are converted to Christ on purely intellectual grounds.” His biggest regret from his church’s early years was not taking this seriously enough. “In the first years of Saddleback, I made the mistake of underestimating the power of music so I minimized the use of music in our services.” Why does it matter so much? “A song can often touch people in a way that a sermon can’t. Music can bypass intellectual barriers and take the message straight to the heart.” True to form, Warren figured out what music to play by asking people. He handed out three-by-five-inch cards at a service and asked everyone to write down the call letters of the radio station they listened to. Saddleback soon stopped singing hymns in its services oriented to newcomers, and within a year attendance took off. The press took to calling it “the flock that likes to rock.” Warren admits the switch cost him hundreds of potential members, but he says it won thousands more. “Too many church services resemble a funeral more than a festival,” he writes. On the Left, too many of our meetings resemble a graduate seminar or a wake. It wasn’t always this way: the labor and civil rights movements sang, socialized, and celebrated. Zohran Mamdani’s campaign and New York City Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) soccer leagues have offered glimpses of that tradition’s revival. The challenge now is to make participatory joy the norm, not the exception. 6. Think Like an Outsider “The problem is, the longer you are a believer, the less you think like an unbeliever.” Warren saw two reasons for this. First, insiders stop spending time with outsiders. “One of the greatest barriers to evangelism is that most believers spend all their time with other Christians. They don’t have any non-believing friends. If you don’t spend any time with unbelievers, you won’t understand what they’re thinking.” Second, they start talking in code. Of such pastors, Warren quips: “Every Sunday they speak in an unknown tongue without being charismatic!” Warren warns against “developing such a close-knit fellowship that newcomers are afraid or unable to break into it.” He adds that “the problem with most small churches is that they are all core and nothing else. The same fifty people come to everything the church does.” Whether in church or on the Left, tight bonds are good; tight bonds that nobody new can penetrate are fatal. It’s easy for insiders to conclude that outsiders just aren’t interested, but what Warren saw in the survey results suggested otherwise. None of the complaints were theological, and he didn’t meet many who said they skipped church because they didn’t believe in God. “Most of the unchurched aren’t atheists: They are misinformed, turned off, or too busy.” His conclusion: “Churches need to stop saying people are closed to the Gospel and start finding out how to communicate on the unbeliever’s wavelength.” All of this translates directly. Progressive and left activists often have few apolitical friends, our rhetoric is thick with jargon, and when our efforts fall flat, we’re tempted to blame external conditions or the people we failed to reach. “They’re just not yet ready for our message,” we tell ourselves. “They’ll come around eventually.” But what if the problem is our less-than-effective organizing? 7. Orient to the Unorganized From day one, Warren focused on what we in the labor movement call organizing the unorganized. In his membership class, Warren told transfers from other churches bluntly that “if all you intend to do is attend services, we’d rather save your seat for someone who is an unbeliever.” Being a member of Saddleback meant you were expected to spread the gospel. Ten days before Saddleback’s first Easter service in 1980, Warren and his tiny group hand-addressed and hand-stamped fifteen thousand invitation letters to local homes. The letter opened: “AT LAST! A new church designed for those who’ve given up on traditional church services!” It didn’t mention Jesus or the Bible, and this didn’t go over well with the faithful: “In fact, all of the critical and angry letters I received in response to this first letter came from Christians who questioned why I had not mentioned Jesus or the Bible.” Some even questioned whether Warren himself was saved. But 205 people showed up for that first service, and eighty-two of them converted over the next ten weeks. Warren’s takeaway: “You have to decide who you want to impress.” Leftists face the same choice, and we usually choose wrong. Progressive organizations and nonprofits spend much of their time building coalitions with other existing organizations, most of which are similarly small and/or staff-heavy. Socialist groups tend to orient toward the small existing milieus of the already radicalized, and we struggle to recruit broadly, in part because we’re so focused on looking over our left shoulder. The overwhelming majority of Americans of all racial backgrounds remain unorganized. We have to decide whether our main concern is recruiting those millions or orienting to the thousands of existing leftists and nonprofits. 8. Love the People You’re Trying to Organize For Warren, the most important ingredient in church growth isn’t a technique at all. “Loving unbelievers the way Jesus did is the most overlooked key to growing a church. Without his passion for the lost, we will be unwilling to make the sacrifices necessary to reach them.” Recalling what new converts told him had drawn them to Saddleback, Warren writes, “I’ve never had anyone say, ‘It was because of the Reformation theology you believe.’ . . . Instead, the most common response was, ‘I felt an incredible spirit of love toward me that drew me in.’” The catch is that it’s easy to love those who already come week after week. “Of course, every congregation thinks their church is loving. That’s because the people who think it is unloving aren’t there!” At Saddleback, love for unbelievers was measured above all in the consistent focus placed on bringing in new people. But it also took the form of small sacrifices made for newcomers. Members stood through packed services so visitors could sit. On cold days in the church’s tent, they offered visitors their coats. Progressives and leftists often profess love for the marginalized and the working class in the abstract. Actual people, however, are another matter. Those who disagree with us too often face condescension or outright contempt. People can feel that from a mile away, just as Warren’s visitors could tell whether a church wanted them. In Warren’s words, “There is no method, no program, and no technology that can make up for a lack of love for unbelievers.” The uncomfortable truth behind many nonprofits and socialist organizations is the one Warren saw in small churches: on some unconscious level, we don’t want a crowd — or at least we don’t want it enough to shake up our routines and assumptions. A tight circle of the already convinced is comfortable, as is being a big fish in a small sea. 9. Make Every Member a Recruiter For Warren, growth ran through members’ own relationships. “The fruit of a believer is another believer,” he writes. Saddleback needed advertising only at the start. “Today, with thousands of members inviting friends to our church, advertising is unnecessary.” Warren offers a simple metric: a church should have at least 25 percent more regular attenders than members. If it doesn’t, “it means almost no one in your church is inviting unbelievers to come with them.” At Saddleback, twice as many people attended as were members. And if members don’t bring friends, that should worry leaders: “If most of our members never invite anyone to come to our church, what are they saying (by their actions) about the quality of what our church offers?” Very few liberal or left groups even track whether their members are bringing anybody in. Warren is just as clear about what doesn’t bring in newcomers: “By making members feel guilty for not inviting friends? No. By putting up a big sign that says ‘Visitors Welcome’? No. By cold-calling on homes in your community? Probably not.” That last line should give us pause. Even a big success like Zohran’s campaign relied mostly on volunteers knocking on the doors of strangers, rather than recruiting their coworkers, neighbors, or fellow congregants. Knocking on strangers’ doors can win an election. It’s a much weaker way to build a deep, lasting organization. 10. Build a Front Door for Newcomers As a kid, Warren would sometimes bring non-Christian friends to his father’s church. Inevitably, that would be the Sunday his dad preached on tithing or a visiting missionary showed slides. Eventually he gave up inviting anyone: “I wouldn’t think of inviting my unchurched friends from the office because the service wouldn’t make any sense to them. The messages are for me, the songs are for me, the prayers are prayed in terms I understand, and even the announcements are for me.” The root problem, Warren explains, is that “in every church there is constant tension between the concepts of ‘service’ and ‘serve-us.’ Most churches end up tipping the scales toward meeting members’ needs because the members pay the bills.” His solution: “Create a service that is intentionally designed for your members to bring their friends to.” Saddleback’s weekend services were built for newcomers, while a separate midweek service fed the committed. He also urges churches to keep their internal business far away from visitors. Many of our organizations don’t have a front door at all. There’s often no regular, in-person gathering designed for visitors, and very little serious onboarding. A newcomer’s first experience is often a digital mass call where they make no human connections or a general meeting run for insiders: committee reports, points of order, or faction fights over resolutions they’ve never heard of. That’s a believers’ service, being used as a seeker service. No wonder that so many never come back — or that so few of our members are inclined to invite their friends, family, and neighbors to join in. 11. Engineer Warmth, Don’t Assume It Warren knew that warmth doesn’t happen by accident: “While some relationships will spontaneously develop, the friendship factor in assimilation is too crucial to leave to chance. You can’t just hope members will make friends in the church. You must encourage it, plan for it, structure for it, and facilitate it.” So Saddleback engineered it. The best parking spots were reserved for visitors, and pastors and staff parked on the dirt. There were four distinct types of welcome ministers: parking attendants, greeters, hosts, and ushers. Greeters wore no badges, so newcomers would feel welcomed by friendly members rather than officials. Warren made flash cards to memorize names and knew everyone in his church by name until the church grew past about three thousand people. My favorite of Warren’s practices is the simplest. “In the early years of Saddleback, the members practiced what we called the ‘three-minute rule.’ We all agreed that for the first three minutes after the service was over, members would talk only to people they’d never met.” And Saddleback measured whether it was working. Every first-time visitor got a postcard asking what they noticed first, liked best, and liked least. Nearly 90 percent said the first thing they noticed was the warmth and friendliness of the people. “That response is not an accident. It is the result of an intentional strategy to express love to visitors in a manner that they can comprehend.” But a warm welcome at a big event is not enough for people to belong. Getting newcomers into small groups of about a dozen people that meet up weekly is what keeps them. At Saddleback, such groups brought members together, usually at someone’s home (and often with good food), to study the Bible, forge friendships, support one another, and work on shared projects. “We tell people, ‘You won’t really feel a part of this church family until you join a small group.’” And it paid off. “Small groups are the most effective way of closing the back door of your church. We never worry about losing people who are connected to a small group.” 12. Offer Good News “A lot of preaching is what I call ‘Ain’t it awful!’ preaching. It just complains about our society and makes judgments about people in general. It is long on diagnosis and short on remedy.” Sound familiar? Warren’s point is that people already know things are bad. “They are looking for solutions, not a scolding.” That’s why, he argues, “What people need today are fewer ‘ought-to’ sermons and more ‘how-to’ sermons.” Much of the Left specializes in “Ain’t it awful!” politics: doom-posting, moralizing, and endless exposés of how terrible things are. Though diagnosis matters, people join organizations that offer hope and a practical path forward. This positive vision also shapes how you motivate members. “Never use guilt or pressure to motivate people for ministry. It’s vision that motivates; guilt and pressure only discourage people.” The same goes for money. Warren notes that “people give to vision, not to need” and concludes that “churches with money problems often actually have a vision problem.” As Warren puts it, “People want to join a church that knows where it’s going.” 13. Repeat Your Most Important Points Routine, repetition, and ritual are how we absorb important truths. Referencing the Book of Nehemiah, Warren coined what he calls the Nehemiah Principle: “Vision and purpose must be restated every twenty-six days to keep the church moving in the right direction.” Saddleback repeated its five purposes everywhere: in every membership class, on banners and brochures, and in songs. They were even etched into the glass of the worship center’s foyer. Warren preached the same “state of the church” sermon every January, updating only the illustrations. “Don’t be afraid to repeat yourself, because nobody gets it the first time. I call repeating things over and over in fresh ways ‘creative redundancy.’” He also invested in catchy phrases like “Every member is a minister,” reasoning that “slogans, maxims, mottoes, and pithy phrases are remembered long after sermons are forgotten.” 14. Build Structures That Last Warren closes his recruitment case with a tale of two preachers. George Whitefield preached over eighteen thousand sermons, once addressed nearly one hundred thousand people near Glasgow, and helped spark the Great Awakening in the 1730s. But “Whitefield often left his converts without any organization so the results of his work were of short duration. Today, very few Christians would recognize George Whitefield’s name.” John Wesley preached to huge crowds too. “But Wesley was also an organizer. He created an organizational structure to fulfill his purpose that far outlasted his lifetime.” That structure is called the Methodist Church. The lesson: “For any renewal to last in a church, there must be a structure to nurture and support it.” The American left has been in its Whitefield era for a generation. Since the giant protests against the Iraq War in 2003, we’ve seen one ephemeral explosion after another: the immigrant rights marches of 2006, Occupy, the Women’s March, the 2020 uprising after George Floyd’s murder. Each drew and inspired millions. Few built or strengthened lasting institutional power. Warren saw where that road leads. “If you don’t have a system and a structure to assimilate and keep the people you reach, they won’t stay with your church. You’ll have as many people going out the back door of your church as are coming in the front door.” The deeper lesson is that recruitment is not a one-way funnel but a cycle: recruit, assimilate, develop, deploy, recruit again. The people who come through the front door have to be absorbed into relationships, developed into committed members, and given meaningful work. Then they become the people bringing in and building the next wave. That’s how to turn growth into self-sustaining growth. And it’s where Part 2 — on developing an organizing machine — begins. Sweden’s Left Faces a Political Deadlock

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Rikard Warlenius ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-16T14:26:22+00:00

After four years of a right-wing government that the far-right Sweden Democrats (SD) heavily influenced, the parties that refuse to cooperate with the SD secured a narrow victory in the general election on September 13. However, the winning side consists of four parties that are politically very far apart and have so far been unable to agree on a new government. While the Left Party refuses to support a government in which it does not hold cabinet posts, the neoliberal Center Party refuses to support a government that includes Left Party ministers. Nobody wants a snap election or a repeat of the extremely slow, 134-day process of forming a government that followed the 2018 election. But there is still no solution in sight to this stalemate. Ultimately, Sweden’s dilemma is bigger than the question of which parties receive which portfolios. It is whether the parties that claim to defend democracy against the inroads of right-wing populism can win a positive majority for a different economic policy agenda rather than a negative majority against the SD’s influence. Swedish Lessons? For much of the postwar period, Sweden seemed to offer a reassuring lesson for the rest of Europe: a strong labor movement could turn social conflict into universal security, while the far right remained at the margins. That settlement rested not only on strong social ties but on a universalist welfare state whose erosion has not been a single event. It has been a piecemeal dismantling . The SD emerged from the neo-Nazi milieu around Keep Sweden Swedish ( Bevara Sverige Svenskt ) and rebranded itself to take far-right, xenophobic ideas into the mainstream. The other parties of the Right progressively abandoned the cordon sanitaire around it as the SD’s voter support grew. In the 2018 election, the Right still held firm, and a weak centrist government took office. But in the run-up to the 2022 election, the temptation to capitalize on the right-wing majority to form a government proved too strong. Three out of four center-right parties formed an alliance with SD and took over the reins of government. This was a three-party coalition that the Moderate Party led, in which the SD formally did not participate. However, as the largest party within the alliance, the SD exerted a very strong influence over government policy through the Tidö Agreement, named after the palace where the parties negotiated it. Before the 2026 election, Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson went further, promising SD ministerial posts if his bloc won a majority. In exchange, the post of prime minister would remain with the Moderates, even though they were still polling below the SD. We should measure the far right’s advance in Sweden and elsewhere not only in terms of who holds a particular office but also in terms of who shapes the broad political agenda. By that measure, the SD has been remarkably successful: politicians across the Right now voice views that would have been beyond the pale a decade ago. The SD’s growth and the prospect of its formal entry into government have polarized Swedish politics. The Swedish Social Democratic Party (SAP) and Center Party — the only traditional right-wing party that still refuses to govern with the SD — presented themselves above all as guarantors against the SD entering any government. Yet they offered voters few clear political distinctions from the governing bloc beyond that pledge. The four-party opposition grouping of SAP, the Left Party, the Center Party, and the Green Party won 176 seats against 173 for the parties of the Tidö Agreement. Swedes often call this grouping red-green, despite the Center Party’s liberal and economically right-leaning profile. But the arithmetic has not produced a government. The Center Party campaigned on keeping both the Left and SD out of government, while the Left Party’s congress had committed the party to refusing to support a government in which it did not participate. Neither party has yet moved. The result is a political deadlock: there is a parliamentary majority opposed to SD’s entry into government but no agreement on what should replace the current administration. The January Agreement’s Afterlife There is a history behind this impasse. After the prolonged stalemate in 2018–19, the Social Democrats and the Green Party eventually formed a government with the active support of the Center Party and the Liberals. The January Agreement promised to keep both the far right and the Left away from political influence. However, this agreement depended on the passive support of the Left in parliament. To secure the support of the Center Party and the Liberals, SAP and the Greens accepted a program of liberal economic reforms, such as the weakening of employment protections and tax cuts for high-income earners. One clause in the agreement — coined the “humiliation clause” by politicians and analysts alike — stated explicitly that the Left Party was to have no influence over government policy. The Left Party nevertheless allowed the government to form on this basis, because its leaders believed that the Liberals would otherwise switch sides and form a government with the support of the SD. The supporters of the January Agreement presented it as a firewall against the far right. But a firewall built by moving the house to the right is a curious defense. The Left Party declared that its support had limits: if the government moved to implement the agreement’s provisions toward the elimination of rent controls or weakening of labor law, the Left would bring it down. In the summer of 2021, it did just that. Although the SAP soon formed a new government, the episode left a legacy of deep mistrust between the two parties of the labor movement. An SAP-led government, with passive support from the Left, nevertheless presided over tax cuts for high earners and the weakening of the right to strike. Critics warned that, while this arrangement may have temporarily prevented an SD government, in the long term it would be more likely to strengthen the far right. In the end, the January Agreement collapsed, but its governing logic survived. This is a case of technocratic centrism at its worst, where there are few substantial differences between the proposed governing alternatives, and the parties largely ask voters to choose the more popular prime minister. It is also the logic of triangulation. According to this logic, erstwhile left-wing forces should concede the economic terms that the center demands, borrow the language of the Right, and hope that competence can compensate for the absence of a distinctive social project. SD remained outside the cabinet, but the party system increasingly organized itself around its priorities: migration, crime, national identity, and the supposed impossibility of a more generous welfare settlement. The Left Party has spent more than a century outside the national government since its origins as the Communist Party of Sweden in 1921. In that time, it has often supported SAP administrations, because the alternative appeared worse — though in recent times, it has increasingly felt as if the lesser of two evils was only slightly lesser. After the humiliation of the January Agreement, which did not stop SD from heavily influencing the next government, the Left Party’s congress this time decided that the party would not hand its seats to a government it did not join. By making that a congress decision, the party leadership sought to bind itself against the predictable pressure to back down in the name of preventing a right-wing government. The party leadership and its supporters argue that this is a necessary break with a pattern in which the Left supplies votes while the Center Party shapes economic policy. They also argue that ministerial posts are the best way to guarantee influence over the government’s day-to-day decisions. Others in the party suggest that the substance of policy is more important than ministerial posts, and that it may even be an advantage for a fringe party to exert influence without having to take responsibility for all government decisions. Overall, support for the prevailing line remains strong within the party. SAP’s wider political ecosystem is pressing the Left to accept a policy agreement without insisting on entry to government. The left wing of SAP, for example Daniel Suhonen of the think tank Katalys , argues that since the SD grew under a similar arrangement, the Left could do the same. But the comparison misses a crucial difference: the SD benefited from a continental tide of right-wing populism, and parties across the Right were normalizing its political project. Repeating the argument that the Left must always accept less to keep SD out of power will not solve its dilemma. It might also turn out that the SD’s tactics delivered a Pyrrhic victory, at least in terms of voter support. The party saw its support decline for the first time in a general election, finishing at 17.5 percent — significantly lower than the Moderates, who thus regained their position as the largest right-wing party. The Sweden Democrats appear to have suffered a loss of support from both ends. Some voters returned to the center-right parties, presumably satisfied with the SD-inspired tightening of migration policy that the Tidö government implemented. At the same time, the most radical supporters felt that the SD had become far too willing to compromise and opted for smaller parties even further to the right. A Left Under Pressure Despite achieving a respectable 8.4 percent of the vote this time, the Left Party has its own unresolved crisis. The current leadership comes from a close circle of colleagues whose ties go back to the party’s youth organization. For more than a decade, their project has been to show that the party has put its unruly radical past behind it, and that the Left is ready to govern alongside SAP as a junior partner only slightly to its left. Internal critics argue that this drive to make the party both easier for its leadership to control and fit for government has repeatedly undermined its support for traditional left-wing positions. Long-standing members have left amid disputes over the Left Party’s support for arms to Ukraine, its acceptance of the arms race and an expanded military budget, its softened opposition to the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, and its retreat from a more assertive form of internationalist solidarity. The party’s relationship with the Palestine solidarity movement has become an especially visible flash point. Members have criticized its failure to take a stronger stance on Palestine as well as the absence of the party leadership from weekly demonstrations. These disputes have also sharpened tensions over how the party handles questions of internal dissent, discipline, and public protest. As the election approached, media scrutiny focused on Left candidates — often low on the lists in smaller municipalities — over statements they had made about Israel and Palestine. The cases ranged from explicitly antisemitic remarks to statements supporting armed resistance by Palestinians. The party responded with repeated expulsions. In the heat of the campaign, many activists felt that the party was distancing itself from the Palestine solidarity movement rather than standing with it. Similar conflicts have arisen around other issues. At times they have led well-known members, and some members of parliament, to break away and form new parties or organizations. These breakaways explicitly claim that their new ventures will be more attuned to the demands of social movements. These are recent examples of an age-old tension within the socialist movement: Can a parliamentary party remain rooted in social movements without trying to domesticate them? Despite these incidents, which often involved members with an immigrant background, the party achieved remarkable success precisely in those stigmatized suburbs where first- and second-generation immigrants are most concentrated. Voter turnout is usually low in these districts, and the people who do vote most often support the SAP. But turnout rose this year, and the Left made significant inroads. In many such places, the Left became the largest party. If we put its supporters together with the still substantial number of votes for the Social Democratic Party, the two “red” parties often accounted for over 90 percent of the vote. Statistics from exit polls show that the difference in voting behavior has widened even further. If only “ethnic Swedes” had voted, the Tidö parties would have won, and the same would have been true if only men had voted. Rural areas are now right-wing, while the major cities have a red-green majority. Those who self-identify as workers voted in equal measure for the red-green parties and the Tidö parties. The SAP remains the largest workers’ party with 31 percent, but the SD is the second largest with 25 percent, while the Left has 10 percent. The fact that so many people from migrant backgrounds voted for the Left fueled menacing postelection debates in which both Sweden Democrats and some Moderates claimed that migrants in general and Muslims in particular had decided the election. They often combined this with insinuations of electoral fraud. But it should come as no surprise that this section of the population now votes for the opposition. For the past four years, the government parties have constantly singled it out as problematic and made successive attempts at weakening or even revoking residence permits for recent migrants and their families. The Arithmetic of Exclusion The parliamentary deadlock reflects the existence of two different majorities. There are enough seats to keep SD out of a government, yet on economic questions, a cross-party majority often leans to the right. The Center Party’s red lines on taxation, welfare profits, and public investment would pull any government dependent on it in that direction. The anti-SD majority is therefore a negative one: it can block one outcome without agreeing on a positive program. The Left’s choice is not simply whether to demand ministerial posts. It is whether to accept a policy agreement that leaves it outside government, with the Center Party setting much of the economic direction, or to insist that the party’s voters should also have a share in executive responsibility. The stakes are material as well as symbolic. Without ministers and a role in setting priorities, the Left could again find itself endorsing compromises whose costs fall on its own program and electorate. But holding firm also carries risks: critics may blame the party if the parties cannot form a government, resulting in an early election and possibly the return of the Right. There are two familiar ways to describe this dispute. One is that the Left Party must be responsible and accept a lesser role to keep the SD out. The other is that the Center Party must be responsible and accept the Left Party in government, because the parliamentary numbers require it. Both framings moralize what is fundamentally a political conflict over who gets to govern and whose program counts as reasonable. The conflict has already produced an extraordinary opening scene. The Left Party voted with the Moderates to reelect the Moderate Andreas Norlén as speaker of the parliament. SAP leader Magdalena Andersson then returned her first exploratory mandate, saying she could not form a government under the new parliamentary conditions. On October 2, the speaker gave her a fresh mandate which expired yesterday, October 15, when the SAP proposed a vote on a prime minister and a government consisting only of their own party. It did not pass, since no agreement between the four parties was reached. (Both the Greens and the Left had already stated that they would vote no in this round.) The speaker will now likely pass the exploratory mandate on to the leader of the Moderates, Ulf Kristersson — the recently resigned prime minister — who will have one or two weeks to attempt to form a government. That too will likely end in a vote in which the candidate does not secure a majority, and the ball will once again be in the court of SAP, the Left, the Greens, and the Center Party. After four unsuccessful attempts to elect a prime minister, the law requires that a general election be held. That prospect gives every party a reason to hold its line — and a reason to blame someone else if negotiations collapse. The Center Party’s refusal to govern with the Left also leaves open a dangerous escape route. If talks fail, the Center Party could face pressure to tolerate a center-right government dependent on SD’s support, or even to join a right-wing majority, while SD remains outside the cabinet. The party leaders have explicitly said they will not support a government that relies on SD votes, even if SD receives no ministerial posts. But there may be a clear incentive, in a crisis, for the party to redefine what it means by “reliance.” The Center Cannot Hold by Imitation For SAP, the deeper problem is older. For years it has tried to neutralize the challenge from the Moderate Party by imitating its approach to government. This has meant favoring fiscal caution over public investment and market discipline over democratic control, combined with punitive responses to social insecurity and increasingly restrictive language on migration. That strategy has not defeated the Right. It has merely helped legitimize the discourse of the Right while leaving the Left with fewer convincing answers of its own. It has also led to the worst electoral result for the SAP since the advent of universal suffrage. The same dilemma faces social democracy across Europe. Centrist parties can win office by promising stability and borrowing the Right’s language, but the strategy gives voters little reason to expect a different future. When the mainstream says no alternative is possible, the far right offers the most dramatic alternative of all. There is no denying that the situation is bleak. Nor are there clear-cut answers about what the Left’s strategy should be, either in the broad sense of a political program or, more narrowly, in terms of parliamentary tactics for the Left. The recent election gave a majority to right-leaning economic policies, although the Center Party opposition to SD cuts across this majority. The parliamentary strategy of the Left Party has resulted in serious internal conflicts. Although its vote share of 8.4 percent is slightly better than usual, it does not represent a major success. At the same time, this has been the general trend for parties of the radical left in Western Europe: even when they have not been losing support, they have not been making major breakthroughs. In the absence of a surge of civil unrest or the emergence of a charismatic figure to lead the Left, it’s not clear how things could have been different. Under prevailing conditions, we face the prospect that governments will continue dismantling Sweden’s welfare institutions — either quickly and aggressively with the Right in charge or at a somewhat slower pace under the Social Democrats and their centrist allies. Since the outbreak of high school blockades and students protests in French Guiana and Mayotte this September , Jean-Luc Mélenchon’s party, La France Insoumise, has continued to rise in the polls. After a rally in Clermont-Ferrand last Sunday, filling the city’s biggest arena — the third-largest in France — Mélenchon was for the first time projected to make it to the runoff of the 2027 presidential election, in all possible scenarios lining up different candidates. Marine Le Pen, projected to win 35 percent of the vote in the first round, will almost certainly be his opponent in the final showdown for power. As the movement has yet to reach its peak, these figures could still radically change. On Tuesday, 480,000 people protested across France . With over a million people having already taken to streets in a mere three days of strikes, May ’68 has become a recurring reference by the press and protesters alike . As calls for a convergence between the “yellow vests” ( gilets jaunes ) and the student movement multiply, this Saturday will see one of the most impressive social movements in France gain further momentum. The Left is now significantly better organized than in successive recent waves of protest. It is quite possible that Mélenchon could become president next year on the wave of this social movement. In anglophone media, some are quick to sound the alarm about the dangers this involves, revealing their blatant disconnect from the material conditions not only of students but from most working people in France and their disregard for the frustrations with a centralized, top-down political system. Talk of twin “extremes” of right and left is rampant , yet seemingly ignorant of the extreme exercise of power under Emmanuel Macron. The infamous Article 49.3 of the constitution, which allows the government to pass legislation without having to seek the Assemblée Nationale’s approval, has been used a record number of times to push through extremely unpopular budget cuts under Macron, including the pension reforms of 2023 (a decision that has since been suspended). Comparisons are often drawn with Michel Rocard, prime minister under Socialist President François Mitterrand, who still holds the record for invoking the much-contested provision twenty-eight times. The crucial difference, however, is that Rocard never resorted to it amid mass protests. With France Insoumise’s new program due to be published on November 6, it is likely to reassert its proposal to abolish Article 49.3 as part of a broader effort to put an end to what the party calls the country’s “presidential monarchy.” Applied to a party broadly comparable to the vision of Zohran Mamdani, or reminiscent of Jeremy Corbyn’s leadership of the Labour Party, this ill-founded demonization of “extremism” tastes suspiciously of newspeak. For we know that France Insoumise’s campaign will run on tackling inflation, investing in the green transition, and reinvigorating left-behind rural areas . The fear of the far right is understandable. However, seeing it as all-powerful reflects a misreading of the French political landscape and of the waves of social resistance that have emerged over the past decade. In this sense, it is also important to realize that the current protests cannot be understood in isolation from the yellow vests movement. The Left, and especially trade unions, are still living with the guilt of not having sprung into action when the yellow vests’ protests and occupations first erupted in November 2018. Due to the unrecognizable nature of its protesters, some of whom had never been politically active before their involvement in this movement, there was considerable political disdain for the yellow vests, who mainly came from France’s nonurban populations. The Confédération Générale du Travail (CGT) trade union, generally considered to be the furthest to the left, was initially very cautious, referring to the outbreak of protests as an instrumentalization of people’s exasperation, orchestrated by an ill-defined far right . The CGT is now taking steps to avoid repeating the same mistake, calling for a “yellow-vest–inspired” mass mobilization against the rising cost of living this coming Saturday. Likewise, whereas the Left, politicians, intellectuals, and activists largely remained focused on urban centers during the yellow vests movement, France Insoumise now visibly addresses these past shortcomings by giving greater attention to rural France. One strand of France Insoumise’s program specifically addresses France’s left-behind rural areas and recalls the yellow vests’ contribution in revealing the inequalities that divide city and country. This also answers criticism of the party being too focused on the main cities. The countryside has been crippled by reduced access to health care — commonly referred to as France’s “medical desert” — public transportation, education, and an erosion of local stores, businesses, and social isolation. In order to invest in these areas, France Insoumise plans to decentralize decision-making on the regional level by prioritizing local politics, investing in public meeting spaces, and by making the countryside the driving force behind the party’s ecological planning ambitions . This also concerns the overseas territories where the current protests first emerged. In the first round of the 2022 presidential elections, Mélenchon was the top candidate in these territories, which the party also described as left-behind, neglected areas. The second round, however, saw the reversal of what happened in metropolitan France, with Marine Le Pen winning by a landslide over Emmanuel Macron. Given the remarkably high abstention rates among these same populations, France Insoumise should not forget these overseas territories . With the protests having started in French Guiana and Mayotte, the student movement has significant potential to drive up the vote. In this respect, it is crucial for the Left not to remain focused on those already politically closest to it but to stand in solidarity with the social movement overseas. Given Le Pen’s strong polling position, the prospect of her winning in 2027 cannot be ruled out. Yet it should also be recognized that the conditions are now radically different from those of the previous presidential election, where she again came in second place. In 2022, when Mélenchon already came close to reaching the second round, there were no ongoing mass protests coinciding with the presidential campaign. By then, the yellow vests movement had already begun to lose momentum, with participation dwindling rapidly following the mass police violence on May Day 2019, before eventually fizzling out during the pandemic. The arrival of COVID-19 also, perhaps ironically, weakened strikes in the health care sector that had overlapped with the yellow vests movement. Now the ground has shifted. The Left appears to have understood whose voices it needs to listen to: those of the voters who formed the backbone of the yellow vests movement — working-class people living outside major urban centers. After all, young people are already primarily voting on the Left and for Mélenchon , discrediting claims that the far right is dominant among young people. A clear direction is emerging, spearheaded by young people who, like the yellow vests before them, have little reason to feel optimistic about their future. Republicans hope—and Democrats Fear—that the recent pattern of polls underestimating Republican (and Trump) support will save them this year. And there are certainly grounds to hope that. In 2016, most polls had Trump losing, but of course, he won. Polls overestimated Hillary Clinton’s support by 3 percent. In 2020, polls overestimated Democratic numbers by 4.7 percent (but they won anyway). In 2024, Democrats were overestimated by 2.9 percent, and of course Trump won a narrow victory (not the landslide he claims). The mid-term bias has been smaller. In 2018, GOP support was overestimated by a mere 0.5 percent; in 2022, Democratic support was overstated by a scarcely larger 0.8 percent. There is thus some basis for the hypothesis of anti-Trump bias, but very little to suggest a major pattern in the midterms. Trump may ask us to pretend he’s on the ballot, but he’s not. The polls this year all show a durable and increasing deficit for both Trump and the GOP, including polls that are consistently more pro-Trump than others (e.g., Rasmussen). Little doubt exists that Trump is the most unpopular President in the history of modern polling (post-World War II). Other low-end Presidents included Truman, Carter and G.W. Bush. Related: Receivership: Agenda for a Democratic Congress We’re talking disapproval ratings of over 60 percent, and approvals of under 40 percent. If we take the mid-term pattern of the Trump Era (2016-2026), poll bias would be 1 percent or less. While some close House or Senate races might be tipped by such a small bias, other local factors could well be more important than poll bias in determining the outcome. [[{“value”:” By: Matt Wolfinger BOSTON – There’s a loophole in our labor laws spreading across the economy. From delivery drivers to nurses, corporations are classifying workers as independent contractors and using the promise of flexible work to cudgel labor rights. Nearly 12 million people in the United States, or seven percent of the workforce, describe independent contractor work as their primary source of income, according to the most recent data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Independent contractors aren’t protected under the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA), so they can’t unionize, aren’t entitled to benefits, a minimum wage, or overtime and can be fired at will. Companies like Uber, Lyft and Amazon have spent hundreds of millions to keep their workers classified as self-employed independent contractors, allowing them to cut costs and indirectly union-bust. By blurring the line between employee and contractor, employers save money they’d otherwise be required to spend on unemployment insurance, benefits and employer-paid payroll taxes. Meanwhile, workers lcassified as contractors then pay exorbitantly higher taxation to account for both employee and employer contributions. If corporations can successfully “gigify” their industries, they can circumvent responsibilities and laws that would otherwise prevent them from mistreating their workers. 1099 tax filing, the paperwork for independent contractors (Working Mass) Uber, Lyft and the Legacy of Gigification Delivery and rideshare apps like Doordash, Lyft and Uber are poster children for operating in this gray area. Emerging in the shadow of the Great Recession when jobs were hard to come by, companies like Uber, Lyft and the now-defunct Sidecar pioneered the modern understanding of gig work by taking advantage of the dire economic landscape. In Massachusetts, most workers are considered employees unless a company can prove their work meets three different criteria. According to Massachusetts Attorney General Andrea Joy Campbell’s office, the work must be: Done without the direction and control of the employer Performed outside the usual course of the employer’s business Done by someone who has their own, independent business or trade doing that kind of work Companies constantly find workarounds. In one landmark case, Massachusetts residents voted in late 2024 to pass a ballot measure affording rideshare drivers the right to unionize. Notably, neither Uber or Lyft spent a cent to oppose it. In contrast, they paid more than $200 million in California to help pass Proposition 22, a bill cementing California gig workers’ misclassification as independent contractors. The reason why these companies did not perceive Question 3 as a threat became clear in May, when over 70,000 Lyft and Uber in Massachusetts drivers successfully voted to certify their App Drivers Union (ADU). As the first union of its kind in the country, it’s a big deal—but there’s a catch: The union ceded on classification. Their official stance is that they’ve chosen to remain independent contractors. This limits the union’s ability to advocate for its members, since benefits like healthcare or a minimum wage are off the bargaining table. As Working Mass’s Editorial Board reported at the time, Question 3 “allowed drivers to vote for a collective bargaining representative, with which Uber and Lyft would then have to negotiate over wages and working conditions… [but only covers] app workers transporting passengers, leaving out take-out and grocery delivery workers.” As a result, “the system as written allows drivers to vote for or against collective bargaining but does not mandate any driver rights within the “union” once it is recognized.” This has led to credible accusations against the ADU of company unionism. The ADU’s FAQ page on their website states that remaining independent contractors allows members to “keep our flexibility and independence,” since bosses cannot legally dictate when independent contractors work and do not work. The ADU did not respond to requests for comment on how they reached this decision. Uber uses this misclassification to avoid covering expenses like gas, insurance, and vehicle maintenance, since they were the responsibility of their “self-employed” contractor. It’s argued that the three qualifications in Massachusetts for independent contractor status aren’t met because: Uber controls the routes and pay of their drivers while requiring them to meet a set of standards dictated by the company, so the work is not done “without direction or control of the employer.” The services provided are not “performed outside the usual course of the employer’s business” (Uber is a company providing transportation to riders, which is fulfilled through the labor of their drivers). Drivers do not have “their own, independent business or trade doing that kind of work” as Uber sets pay rates, can change them at their sole discretion and requires a set of standards its drivers and vehicles must meet (and can change those at will). Who Gets to Turn Numbers into Evidence?

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: [:en]THE TRICONTINENTAL[:es]TRICONTINENTAL[:] ／ 著者: Author and Editors ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-16T09:16:34+00:00

Turning numbers into evidence is a form of labour – and in the Global South, that labour is unevenly distributed. 1. Who Gets to Question the Numbers? By the end of 2018, India’s National Sample Survey Office had completed its 2017–18 Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) – the government’s own instrument for measuring employment. The report was not released. Weeks later, a version leaked to the press showed unemployment at 6.1%, its highest level in forty-five years. Two members of the National Statistical Commission, which had approved the report, resigned over its withholding. 1 The immediate dispute was not over a mathematical error in the figure, but over whether the public would be allowed to see it. The PLFS controversy showed how much political weight a number can carry. Numbers are central to public debate: debt ratios, growth rates, unemployment rates, poverty lines, price indices, and export values help decide whether an economy is ‘recovering’, whether workers are ‘employed’, and whether public spending is treated as ‘affordable’. But withholding a number is only one way to control its political meaning. Numbers can also obscure reality through the definitions, classifications, comparisons, and formats that make them appear neutral. Unemployment rates are a good example of what numbers can reveal and obscure at the same time. An unemployment rate is never a simple head count of the jobless. It is a ratio between those classified as unemployed and those counted as part of the active labour force. That ratio depends on definitional boundaries: who qualifies as employed, who is actively seeking work, who has stopped searching, who is immediately available to work, and which forms of informal or unpaid labour fall outside the metric. The rate can fall because employment has genuinely expanded, but it can also fall because discouraged jobseekers stopped searching, because precarious informal work is counted as employment, or because underemployment has been swept into a different statistical bucket. On its own, the number cannot tell us which underlying social process it records. These examples point to a deeply political problem. Accuracy is only part of it. The deeper question is who has the power to interrogate a number. Who can probe its definitions? Who has the means to audit the denominator, distinguish a count from a rate or an index, or tell a nominal value from a real one? Who can trace a polished indicator back to the realities of labour and class, gender and region, debt and the world market? These questions have real-world implications that affect the lives of billions. International Monetary Fund (IMF) surveillance and its formally voluntary data-dissemination standards shape the formats through which states report economic data; World Bank lending has financed and shaped the development of national statistical systems; the United Nations’ System of National Accounts defines the international standards for measuring gross domestic product (GDP); and sovereign credit ratings from Moody’s, S&P, and Fitch influence borrowing costs through methodologies whose final judgements are not fully open to public scrutiny. 2 This concentration of statistical authority leaves researchers in the Global South in a bind. They may ask how sovereign debt constrains public spending, or how commodity prices bind national production to an unequal world market. But the data through which they must answer those questions are often organised elsewhere, sometimes by the very institutions and standards they seek to interrogate. The pattern is not new. Raúl Prebisch’s account of core–periphery terms of trade 3 and Walter Rodney’s account of colonial economies organised for extraction 4 point to an older problem: economic domination shapes not only the movement of commodities, labour, and capital, but also the terms through which those movements are counted, compared, and understood. The question today is how that older problem reappears in datasets, indicators, metadata, and institutional standards. Nor does the Global South confront this problem from a single standpoint. A finance ministry technocrat, a business lobby, and a union research department do not approach IMF numbers with the same interests. The question is therefore not only where statistical authority is located, but whose interests its categories and measures serve. For researchers, movements, and public institutions that have long had to rely on metrics pre-packaged by dominant institutions, the challenge is not simply a lack of data but unequal power to turn raw numbers into evidence. The emergence of AI tools changes this terrain by lowering the cost of that work. But AI’s political significance can only be understood by first looking at the labour that comes before analysis: the choices, checks, and transformations that decide what a number can be made to show. 2. Numbers Are Not Evidence Until Someone Makes Them Evidence Quantitative research is often imagined as a linear process: the data exist, then they are cleaned, analysed, visualised, and interpreted. This sequence feels intuitive, but it starts too late. Much of empirical inquiry happens before formal analysis begins, in the laborious effort to determine whether a given figure can answer the question in front of it. A cell value in a table is not yet evidence. It becomes evidence only when connected to a specific research question, a rigorous definition, a unit of observation, a standardised measure, and a basis for comparison. A number may be valid inside its own statistical system and still fail to answer the political question being asked. Recognising when a national total hides deep distributional disparities, or when a trend in money terms hides a change in what that money buys, is part of the conceptual work of quantitative inquiry. So is seeing when a formal head count of workers leaves out labour that is socially necessary. This connective labour – the work that bridges raw figures and substantive claims – is measurement work. It is the labour of transforming raw administrative or statistical outputs into evidence. Its inputs might be a PDF table, a brief metadata note, or an isolated time series. Its outputs are explicit variables, verified transformations, documented sources, and defensible comparisons. The term is specific to data, but the kind of translation it refers to has a longer history. Bowker and Star showed that classification systems are built through political and institutional labour. 5 Marx showed how capitalism translates different kinds of concrete labour into abstract, exchangeable value. 6 Measurement work names a related translation: the process by which raw figures are made into evidence that can circulate as public truth. Naming this labour matters because it reframes the primary question from ‘do we have the numbers?’ to ‘what has been done to make these numbers usable as evidence?’ GDP, for instance, can be expressed as an absolute level, a year-on-year growth rate, a per capita value, a nominal series, or a deflated real index. A poverty line can make material deprivation visible, but where it is drawn also decides how many people count as poor at all. A consumer price index captures inflation, yet its political implications diverge dramatically depending on whether the focus is on food security, fuel costs, real wages, or basic household survival. These are not minor technical adjustments. They decide which parts of social life a number can show, and which it leaves out. Take the World Bank’s ‘Pink Sheet’ on commodity prices. 7 The downloaded annual workbook is a complex file containing multiple sheets: nominal prices, real prices, price indices, and weight structures. A researcher studying agricultural dependency has to choose which version of the price to use. Should they track the price as it appeared at the time, or adjust it to show its purchasing power across years? Which commodity and unit of measure fit the question? This selection is measurement work. It can make the same decades look like recovery – or like a long decline in what those prices could actually buy. Similarly, a basic GDP growth series can support very different, even contradictory, arguments depending on how it is handled. While a raw annual rate highlights short-term volatility, a three-year moving average smooths out short-term fluctuations to expose cyclical trends, and a five-year average makes longer trends easier to see (Figure 1). The underlying data remain the same, but the choice to aggregate or smooth them is a conceptual intervention. Without the technical capacity to construct these alternative measures, a research team is forced to inherit the version used in official press releases to frame national performance. Figure 1. Real GDP growth, read as the annual rate and three- and five-year moving averages (illustrative constructed series, per cent per year; not any specific country). None of this makes statistical metrics meaningless. It means their authority rests on the chain that connects a raw value to a public claim. Yet this chain is deeply unequal. Well-funded institutions can spread the pre-analytical labour across dedicated data teams, advanced software pipelines, and robust documentation systems. Researchers, social movements, and public agencies in the Global South often have to carry the sequence themselves: locating raw files, auditing sources, deconstructing tables, writing code, validating totals, and defending their findings in public debates. This division of labour determines which questions can be asked in the first place. If researchers cannot verify the construct behind a number, an inquiry may never progress to formal analysis. If a spreadsheet cannot be reshaped into structured evidence, the patterns inside it may remain locked away from public debate. When only a handful of well-funded organisations can perform this translation at scale, they retain the power to define economic and social reality through numbers. Measurement work is a cornerstone of quantitative research capacity. It is not a preliminary chore, but one of the conditions that make empirical inquiry possible. Yet this foundational labour is routinely erased. The polished indicators in global databases appear as finished facts, with the decisions behind them pushed out of view. This erasure creates two barriers. It discourages researchers from interrogating official statistics, and it leaves them without practice in handling raw administrative data. Often, the numbers exist, but they arrive in formats that are hard to question. A table may be easy to read and still be difficult to check, compare, or reuse. 3. A Readable Table Is Not Yet Data The gulf between a presentation table and an analysis-ready dataset is visible in a typical worksheet on labour-force indicators (Figure 2). The sheet looks orderly: the two sexes run across the top, sectors down the left, and three related blocks – labour-force participation, work, and unemployment – are stacked one under another. A researcher can read it and a spreadsheet program can display it. But tools such as R, Python, or SQL cannot safely treat it as data, because the meaning of each value is carried by position, formula, and block order rather than by explicit variables. Figure 2. Labour-force indicators by sector and sex, India PLFS 2017–18 (presentation worksheet; usual status – principal plus subsidiary activity; persons aged 15 and above; rates in per cent). 8 In the example above, the labour-force participation rate, the worker–population ratio, and the unemployment rate appear in matching positions across separate blocks. To understand how they relate to one another, the reader has to follow the layout and formulas by hand. That may be enough for one careful reading, but it is not enough for analysis that another researcher can repeat and verify. An analysis-ready dataset has to be organised differently. The meaning of each number must be built into the dataset itself: the sector, sex, measure, unit, and value should appear as explicit fields, not as clues scattered across the layout. Definitions and formulas should travel with the measure as metadata, rather than be inferred from where a number sits in the grid. The question shifts from ‘can I see the right number near the right label?’ to ‘can another researcher check, group, compare, and reproduce the record without guessing?’ Reshaping the sheet into that form (Figure 3) turns a human-readable layout into machine-checkable evidence. Once the measure is explicit, the evidence chain can be verified and inspected. Code can test whether totals add up, whether categories repeat consistently, whether rates match their inputs, and whether cases are missing. The record that travels with the dataset – source, layout, definitions, formulas, exclusions, checks – lets another researcher trace each step. Without that structure and record, a chart may look polished while resting on a number whose meaning was guessed from its place in the table. The evidence cannot be independently checked or reliably reused beyond the person who cleaned it. The same opacity recurs across government reports, international databases, survey summaries, commodity-price sheets, and statistical PDFs – wherever the conditions that make numbers usable as evidence are hidden in layout, metadata, definitions, and institutional practice. 4. AI Lowers the Cost of the Work before Analysis In this context, AI matters because it lowers the cost of pre-analytical labour. It is most useful for researchers who have a clear question, understand the statistical constructs, and can judge the political stakes, but lack years of formal training in data engineering. For resource-constrained research teams, a major bottleneck is the difficult transition from a published table to a dataset that can be searched, checked, and analysed. Researchers have to identify the implicit variables, write the code that reshapes the table, verify the formulas, and record where each value came from. Many promising inquiries are abandoned at this stage not because the question is weak, but because the path from source format to evidence is too costly. AI can help at several points in this transition, so long as each step remains open to human inspection. Reading the Layout. AI can reduce the friction by helping researchers map the structure of the source data. A researcher can describe a table in plain language – quarters across columns, age cohorts down rows, sex categories repeated under each period, and measures stacked in blocks – and ask the system to propose a structure: period, age group, sex, measure code, unit, and value. The point is not to outsource the design, but to get a first draft that can be inspected, corrected, and finalised faster than starting from a blank script. Drafting the Code. AI can help turn the researcher’s plan into a first working script. A researcher can explain what needs to happen to the table: move repeated headings into their own columns, combine related tables or lookup lists, remove footnote markers and other recurring text patterns, make labels consistent, turn numbers stored as text into usable values, or record where the original figures came from. The script still has to be checked against the source. But AI can lower the barrier between knowing what needs to be done and being able to run the code that does it. Running the Checks. AI can also help with verification. A researcher can ask it to generate validation checks: Do sex categories add up to the listed totals? Do regional sub-aggregates reconcile with national aggregates? Does a rate match its numerator and denominator? Were any rows dropped during the reshaping process? These checks are the primary way in which a small team can prevent an empirical claim from drifting away from its material grounding. Documenting the Work. AI can help turn a messy, interactive data-cleaning session into a structured record of the work: which sheet and cell ranges were read, which rows were excluded, how variables were renamed, what assumptions were made, and which validation tests succeeded. That record can be written in plain Markdown, JSON, or any other format the team can review. Without it, the analysis remains an isolated craft. Researchers are forced to reconstruct the same steps again, while institutions with stronger data-management systems retain the authority to define the mainstream narrative. Diagnostic visualisations extend the same logic. Line charts, bar charts, and histograms can reveal structural breaks, duplicated periods, missing categories, or unusual outliers while evidence is still being made, before a claim has been written around a mistaken pattern. One political caution belongs here: cleaning sensitive administrative data with a foreign AI tool can make those data newly legible to the company running the model, depending on its retention practices. The same act can build research capacity and open a new point of extraction. Taken together, these steps place AI inside the measurement workflow rather than above it (Figure 4). AI can lower the cost of moving from a source to a structure, a set of checks, and a record. But the work that gives numbers public meaning – confirming the source, checking definitions, and interpreting the result – stays in human hands. Figure 4. Where AI enters the measurement workflow (schematic; no data). AI can automate parts of the quantitative workflow, but it cannot replace quantitative research as a whole. Nor does it make programming obsolete. The political opening is that researchers can spend less time fighting syntax and more time on statistical definitions, conceptual design, and political-economic interpretation. In practice, this means that local research teams, trade unions, and regional public institutions in the Global South can now transform dense, poorly formatted administrative reports into auditable databases that others can inspect, reuse, and contest. This extends the Bandung Circuits’ AI for Social Science (AI4SS) framework into the terrain of measurement, applying its concern with research sovereignty to the work of turning numbers into evidence. 9 5. AI Cannot Replace Judgement Making data preparation easier is not the same as replacing intellectual judgement. An AI system can reshape a file without comprehending its substance. It can infer a tabular layout without knowing whether a given value is a numerator, denominator, total, rate, share, or index. It can produce a clean dataset from a flawed premise. It can accelerate research, but it can also accelerate structured error. The core principle remains: AI can transform the file, but the analyst must verify what the file actually represents. Verification is where statistical definition, economic interpretation, and political responsibility converge. Researchers must still decide what activities qualify as labour, which denominator belongs to a rate, whether historical categories remain comparable over time, whether a nominal series must be adjusted for inflation, and whether a chosen price index genuinely addresses the research question. They must also ask whether an official indicator obscures informal work, unpaid care, ecological degradation, or class distribution. The same limit appears in research on informal work. An AI system can isolate categories such as self-employment, own-account work, or contributing family labour. But it cannot determine whether a category is best understood as an independent livelihood, disguised wage labour, a household survival strategy, or a position of acute subordination within a broader chain of extraction. Translating a statistical category into a concrete social relation requires political-economic judgement. No machine can assume responsibility for how researchers interpret class, value, dependency, or exploitation. AI can recommend validation checks, but it cannot discern which discrepancy matters politically. A small mismatch in a total may be a rounding issue; a denominator that quietly excludes migrant workers may change the social meaning of the entire rate. AI can render a chart, but it cannot take responsibility for the public claim attached to it. Using AI responsibly does not mean outsourcing judgement to an algorithm. It means embedding the machine within a disciplined research workflow – checking sources, reconciling totals, documenting each step – and interpreting the result politically. The more powerful these computational tools become, the more clearly researchers must understand their limits. 6. From Data Access to Research Capacity By this point, the problem is no longer only whether data can be accessed. It is whether researchers have the capacity to turn those data into evidence that can be tested and defended. A dataset may be public and still be politically unusable if researchers lack the time, tools, concepts, and institutional support to work with it. What matters is who can sustain the whole chain: formulating the question, structuring the data, validating transformations, interpreting results, and carrying findings into public debate. If knowledge production has its own ‘means of production’, then quantitative inquiry is no exception. It requires data and software, statistical concepts and economic theory, infrastructure and documentation – and, above all, institutional time. We call this broader ensemble of conditions the means of quantitative inquiry. It is not a single software package, but the collective capacity to interrogate numbers. Measurement work is a foundational, yet often hidden, layer of this capacity, the point where the structural conditions of quantitative inquiry first become labour. The central change is not in the questions researchers ask, but in the resources required to answer them with evidence. By lowering that resource threshold, AI can change who builds and defends an evidence base. The first shift is in wider access to materials. A research team that once stopped at a scanned PDF table can now build an auditable database. The second is in deeper access to statistical constructs. A civil society researcher who once had to accept a pre-packaged external indicator can now dismantle and examine its numerator, denominator, and measurement logic. The third is the capacity to build an evidence base of one’s own. A local public institution that once passively consumed international classifications can now test whether those frameworks align with its mandate and build rigorous counter-evidence when they do not. The United Nations Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean shows what that third capacity looks like when sustained at an institutional scale. Prebisch directed it from 1950 to 1963, and it has produced alternative development statistics for the region for nearly eight decades. 10 This is, fundamentally, a question of methodological sovereignty. It does not mean that every institution should invent its own statistics or ignore comparability. It means having the capacity to examine inherited measures, test whether they answer local and regional questions, document alternative choices, and make those choices accountable to public argument. For a graduate student, this may mean reproducing an official chart instead of simply citing it. For a union, research institute, or public agency, it may mean maintaining an alternative evidence base that can survive public scrutiny. Kerala’s People’s Plan Campaign, launched in 1996, is a working example of that balance. Local bodies gathered their own data and set their own development priorities, within a common planning framework that kept plans comparable across the state – and the decentralised planning it built continues under the Kerala State Planning Board. 11 Methodological sovereignty matters most where statistics decide a country’s future. In Pakistan – as Tricontinental: Institute for Social Research documents in dossier no. 69, How the International Monetary Fund Is Squeezing Pakistan – the IMF has made its lending conditional on austerity: removing electricity, gas, and fuel subsidies, raising interest rates, and letting the market set the exchange rate. 12 The Fund justifies these conditions through an aggregate assessment of debt sustainability. Its judgement of what is ‘sustainable’ reaches the household as the price of cooking gas and electricity. To show how that burden is distributed, local researchers and trade unions need the capacity to work with household survey microdata, reweight price indices according to local consumption patterns, and estimate the effects of the proposed cuts on different groups, particularly working-class households. Owning the means of quantitative inquiry allows researchers to do more than react to the IMF’s claims: they can challenge its arithmetic with evidence that withstands technical scrutiny and equip unions, movements, and governments prepared to contest its prescriptions. What AI changes depends on who deploys it. In the hands of dominant institutions, it can produce the same pre-packaged indicator narratives at a faster rate. In the hands of scholars, trade unions, social movements, and public agencies in the Global South, it can widen who is able to build and defend an evidence base. That wider access does not mean lower standards. It requires clearer definitions, more traceable data lineages, more reproducible transformations, more explicit verification protocols, and more responsible interpretation. The objective is not to bend numbers to a predetermined narrative, but to let more researchers check what the numbers can and cannot say. 7. Who Sets the Terms of Counting? Rigour matters because numbers carry power in public life. But not everything of value should be turned into a metric. Some relations of solidarity and subsistence resist counting, and should. The narrower claim is that whoever sets the terms of counting holds real power, and that power should not sit only in the North. That is the test to hold AI to as well: not AI thinking for researchers, but more researchers gaining the conditions to think with numbers. Who gets to interrogate the numbers? Who gets to turn numbers into evidence? Who gets to use that evidence to challenge frameworks arranged by others? In the Global South, methodological sovereignty begins there: in the capacity to question inherited numbers, defend the evidence built from them, and mobilise it for political and social emancipation. Brazil’s Congress ends 6×1 work schedule

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Brasil de Fato ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-15T23:40:25+00:00

The approval of Constitutional Amendment Proposal (PEC) 221 of 2019 by the Federal Senate establishes the most profound alteration in the organization of the Brazilian work schedule since the promulgation of the 1988 Constitution. The text eliminates the model of six days of work followed by one day of rest and sets a maximum work week of 40 hours with two paid weekly rest days, without salary reduction. Because it is a constitutional amendment, the text does not depend on the sanction or veto of President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva (PT). Promulgation is the responsibility of the presiding officers of the Chamber of Deputies and the Senate in a joint session of the National Congress. Once promulgated and published in the Official Gazette of the Union (DOU), the amendment enters into force in a phased manner, with specific adaptation periods for companies, professional categories, and the public sector. Transition timeline The transition from the 44-hour work week to the definitive model of 40 hours per week and five working days followed by two days off (5×2 schedule) will occur in two main stages: First stage (60 days after publication) : Two months after the amendment is published, workers become entitled to two paid days off per week, preferably on Sundays. At the same time, the weekly working hours limit is reduced from 44 to 42 hours. Second stage (14 months after publication) : after 12 months from the implementation of the first stage, totaling 14 months from the official publication, the maximum weekly workload is definitively reduced to 40 hours, with a limit of eight hours per day. During the 12-month period in which the 42-hour weekly limit will be in effect, employers must distribute the two extra hours across the weekdays. If distributed evenly across the five working days, the daily work schedule becomes 8 hours and 24 minutes. Collective labor agreements and conventions may authorize daily work schedules exceeding eight hours during this transitional period to accommodate the 42-hour schedule, provided that the two weekly days off are strictly maintained. Once the 14-month transition period is complete, any activity exceeding eight hours per day or 40 hours per week will require the payment of overtime or compensation through a time bank, mechanisms that remain permitted under labor law. Special schedules and shifts The new constitutional guidelines do not impose a single work schedule format for the entire market and safeguard the operation of continuous activities and essential services. Uninterrupted services, such as hospitals and health units, public safety, public transportation, urban cleaning, and surveillance, may adopt specific models for organizing and compensating working hours through collective bargaining agreements or conventions. In these areas, the traditional 12-hour work shift followed by 36 hours of rest (12×36 shift) is not automatically eliminated by the PEC, but will need to be adapted at the negotiating table between employers’ and workers’ unions. The reorganization of shifts must take into account the reduction of the weekly workload and the minimum rest and meal periods stipulated in the Constitution. The text also refers to future specific laws for the complementary regulation of conditions for categories with their own operational characteristics. In the case of workers subjected to prolonged confinement , such as on offshore oil platforms, amendments proposing prior exceptions in the constitutional text were rejected by rapporteur Omar Aziz. The parliamentarian pointed out that the work regime for offshore oil workers should be subject to subsequent legal regulation. Compensation and average monthly days off The proposed amendment allows companies and unions to establish, exceptionally and through collective agreement or convention, work schedule compensation regimes with shifts different from the 5×2 format, including enabling the eventual continuation of the 6×1 shift schedule. For this flexibility to be valid before the Labor Court, the proposal requires compliance with two mandatory criteria: the worked days off must be fully compensated within the same calendar month; and the worker is entitled to at least one day of rest within a maximum period of one week of service, and must reach, at the end of the month, an average of two paid days off per week. The three express exceptions The text approved by Congress establishes differentiated rules and specific conditions for three groups of workers and economic sectors: Outsourced public administration workers : To safeguard the continuity of essential public services and preserve the economic and financial balance of administrative contracts, outsourced companies providing services to the State will have up to 12 months after the enactment to adjust their work schedules, instead of the general 60-day period. Contracts amended after 60 days from the publication must already comply with the new schedule. If the public authority and the service providers do not formalize an addendum within one year, the reduction in working hours for outsourced workers will automatically take effect, without a reduction in salary. Professionals with a university degree and salaries starting at R$ 21,100 : the so-called “highly qualified workers,” who are employees with a university degree and a monthly salary equal to or greater than 2.5 times the ceiling of Social Security benefits (currently R$ 21,188.87), are exempt from general daily working hour limits and timekeeping. These professionals can negotiate their schedules directly with the company, but retain the constitutional right to two days of paid weekly rest. This exception does not apply to public employees. Brazilian labor law already provides for situations where there is no traditional control of working hours, such as in the case of employees who hold positions of trust or who work outside the company in activities where it is not possible to control their hours. Individual micro-entrepreneurs and small businesses : the proposed constitutional amendment (PEC) stipulates that a subsequent complementary law will create transitional measures for mitigation and fiscal or credit support for individual micro-entrepreneurs, micro-enterprises, and small businesses. The granting of these benefits will be expressly conditional upon maintaining jobs and employment levels in these establishments. Current contracts and old agreements The application of the new work schedule to existing employment contracts is direct and immediate, requiring no formal re-hiring or termination of employment. The Constitution guarantees the principle of non-reduction of wages, prohibiting any nominal cut or proportional reduction in employee salaries or in the minimum wages of professional categories. For workers who already work 40 hours a week or less, the amendment does not create a further reduction in weekly working hours. However, these employees will now have a guaranteed right to two paid days of weekly rest. In the labor union sphere, clauses in previously agreed collective bargaining agreements and conventions that address working hours or weekly rest periods and are incompatible with the new constitutional parameters will lose all legal validity 60 days after the amendment is published. Impact on the labor market The shift to a new work schedule affects tens of millions of workers in operational, customer service, and ongoing production roles across the country. A technical survey prepared by the Inter-Union Department of Statistics and Socioeconomic Studies (Dieese), based on the Continuous National Household Sample Survey (PNAD) of the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE), indicates that the measure will directly benefit 26.8 million workers . The contingent consists of 22 million employees with formal contracts in the formal sector (equivalent to 54% of the country’s formal workers) and approximately 4.8 million informal workers who register weekly work schedules exceeding 40 hours. Data from the Ministry of Labor and Employment (MTE) indicates that 14.8 million formal workers were directly subject to the 6×1 work schedule in 2025, while another 29.7 million were already working on the 5×2 schedule. The scope of the change is also reflected in the profile of contracted work schedules in Brazil. Studies by the National Confederation of Commerce of Goods, Services and Tourism (CNC), structured from the Annual Social Information Report (Rais), reveal that 35.7 million workers under the CLT (Consolidation of Labor Laws), about 62% of the formal Brazilian workforce, currently work schedules ranging between 40 and 44 hours per week.
