Stewards Corner: Laying the Foundation for Change in Your Union

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: 不明 ／ 著者: Labor Notes Staff ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-17T21:22:20+00:00

Revitalizing a local union may require electing new officers with a different vision. But running for office should not be your first step. Successful campaigns for union office usually start by organizing against management first, often years in advance of any eventual run. A group of workers wants to accomplish something—better pay, more control over their conditions on the job, an end to tiered wages or benefits—but their officials, or something about the way their union operates, is in the way. So they begin to change the culture of the union, getting more members involved in building power on the shop floor. Your ultimate goal, after all, is not just electing new leaders, but building a stronger union. And doing that means involving as many members as possible. WHERE TO START The good news is you can begin organizing for change in your union any time. “Your campaign should start with workplace fights that involve your co-workers,” says John Pearson, a nurse at Highland Hospital in Oakland, California, and former president of the SEIU Local 1021 chapter there. “You need that type of credibility and trust from people to be able to lead successfully.” Before running for office, Pearson helped lead a campaign to defend breaks, involving nurses throughout the Alameda County hospital system, which resulted in a big back pay settlement. He also led an effort to save a substance abuse clinic from defunding. “The word spreads if you’re effective at fighting in the workplace—people know to ask you for help,” says Pearson. This type of organizing shows members that you have a track record, and that collectively members can make a difference. The Caucus of Rank-and-File Educators (CORE), which won leadership in the Chicago Teachers Union in 2010, built up strength in campaigns like a fight to stop the school district from closing dozens of neighborhood schools and replacing them with privately run charters. “So much of our caucus was really forged in the fight against school closings,” says Norine Gutekanst, who was a third-grade teacher at the time. Caucus members attended school closing hearings and teamed up with community activists to camp out all night in front of threatened schools. The leadership of the union, meanwhile, was absent from the fight. CORE was already “doing what the union should have been doing,” said Chicago social studies teacher Bill Lamme. “So when the election came, [CORE activists] didn’t say ‘Elect me and I’ll do this.’ They said, ‘This is what we’ve been doing and we’ll keep doing it.’” ATTEND MEETINGS If you’re serious about getting involved in and transforming your union, you should be regularly attending union meetings, even if the meetings don’t allow for much debate. Bring other members who share your views. By participating, your group can shake things up. “The first time you attend a meeting, just listen,” says Ken Paff, longtime national organizer with Teamsters for a Democratic Union. “Later on,” Paff says, “you can make a proposal, and organize your group to present it. Talk to members before the meeting; draw them in. Get things out to the members, not only to make your group transparent, but also to make union meetings that way. “Tell people there will be a motion on the pension plan, or another burning topic. Ask them to come support the motion. Put it out in writing. Get them to sign a petition in support of it.” In Letter Carriers (NALC) Branch 9 in Minneapolis, a group of members started meeting to talk about how to change the culture of their branch, including its meeting agendas. “We had a president of over 30 years who would just use Robert’s Rules to keep people down,” says letter carrier Chris Pennock. But the group of reformers kept bringing more members to meetings. Eventually their motions started winning. Pennock got 10 minutes on the agenda to talk about fighting post office closures. It was a small step, but a co-worker told him: “I don’t know if you know how historic that is—I’ve never seen a motion pass that the executive board not only didn’t support but were actively opposed to.” Their organizing helped lay the basis to run a reform slate; Pennock is now vice president of Branch 9. “Have faith that you can change the culture,” he says. At the same time, “it’s a mistake to think that meetings are the important thing,” says SEIU Local 1021’s Pearson—especially since most members won’t attend meetings. You want to find other ways to get them involved in the union. “Talk to people in the workplace, especially while you’re working, if you can,” advises Pearson. Some other activities to consider: Holding classes or discussions on issues the union is not addressing Getting members to join rallies or picket lines in solidarity with other unions Organizing a “Vote No” campaign on a subpar contract Campaigning to change your bylaws to make the union more democratic BE AN INFORMATION SOURCE Is your local union run like a small club or clique, with a few insiders making all of the decisions and everyone else wondering what’s going on? Do members feel they don’t have a voice in critical decisions about their jobs, benefits, and contracts? Members want information, and informed members are more active members. You can start building a campaign for change by becoming an information source. Here are some ideas: Inform members about the contract and how to enforce it Take on workplace issues and organize contract enforcement campaigns Organize short gatherings at lunch or after work to talk about workplace or union issues Distribute bulletins on workplace and union issues Hold online or in-person workshops or an educational conference Use an app like WhatsApp to build communication and spread information among members Start a Facebook group to share news about your employer and give members a space to communicate with each other Start an email and text list to send other members news about your local Don’t spread rumors or conspiracy theories. Be reliable and provide information that the members can use. Set a positive and constructive tone, especially if you’re using social media. Remember, your credibility is your most important asset. GETTING MEMBERS INVOLVED A one-person crusade isn’t going to change your local union, and it isn’t what the union needs. You need teammates and helpers if you want to accomplish long-term change. “Every day you need to ask yourself, ‘Who did I talk to today?’” says Vance Smith of Teamsters Local 135 in Indianapolis. “Get out in the field and have one-on-one, personal conversations.” Have realistic expectations. Not every member is going to want to become as active as you are. If someone wants to get involved, start them off with small jobs first, like talking to you about problems where they work or introducing you to other members. Thank them for their efforts and let them know their work is valuable. “In every workplace, workers show a range of knowledge about and interest in the union, from A to Z,” says longtime organizer Joe Fahey. Don’t try to move people from A to Z immediately—instead, move them from A to B, and so on. Once they’ve gotten their feet wet, ask them to do bigger jobs, like passing out a leaflet or standing with you in the parking lot while you do that. Give them options. Remember, people’s lives are complicated. If someone doesn’t want to get involved at first, don’t write them off. Stay in contact and ask them again later. You’re building a network here, so cast as wide a net as you can. People can surprise you. The more members you have working with you, the more work you can get done. Especially in a local that represents multiple workplaces or industries, getting lots of members involved in your campaign can be critical. Is It Working? Here are some of the ways you can tell your organizing is having an effect: More members are involved in a passive way, like reading a leaflet. More members are taking a small action step, like wearing a button or signing a petition. Some members are taking on organizing tasks, like passing out flyers or making phone calls. Some members are becoming leaders and taking initiative. If these things aren’t happening, take a step back and re-evaluate your strategy. Are you doing too much yourself? Do you need to reach out to more departments? Do you have the right message? This advice is drawn from the Labor Notes book Democracy Is Power by Mike Parker and Martha Gruelle. Do Peasants Hold the Key to Humanity’s Future?

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Daniel Colligan ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-17T20:14:34+00:00

In 1966, the sociologist Teodor Shanin boldly asserted that “peasants are the majority of mankind.” This declaration coincided with the beginning of a decades-long increase in academic focus on the peasantry, which, in addition to its demographic weight, was the driving force of some of the most important political movements of the twentieth century. Classic works such as anthropologist Eric Wolf’s 1969 Peasant Wars of the Twentieth Century chronicled the various ways in which subsistence farmers asserted themselves on the world stage. In the sixty years since Shanin made that declaration, the situation of world agriculture has changed dramatically. On large farms, highly mechanized operations have raised productivity and output to unprecedented levels. Smallholder agriculture has been transformed by the adoption of Green Revolution technologies such as synthetic fertilizer, high-yielding crop varieties, and pesticides. A continuous flow of migrants exiting agriculture has swelled the urban areas of both rich and poor countries alike. The epic overhaul of the global countryside has caused the peasantry to recede as an object of interest. Historian Eric Hobsbawm ’s assessment that the twentieth century witnessed the “death of the peasantry” is often taken as a final judgment on the peasantry’s political relevance. That epitaph was reiterated two years ago in the most recent work on (European) peasants targeted at a general audience. A new book attempts to counter this conventional wisdom on the peasantry by arguing that reports of the peasantry’s death have been greatly exaggerated. Maryam Aslany’s Peasants: Makers of the Modern World insists that there are actually more peasants living now than at any point in history: two billion who produce a third of the world’s food. Drawing on her fieldwork in India, Cambodia, Peru, and Ghana, Aslany attempts to “restore to the countryside the visibility it demands.” And the visibility it demands is, in her view, superlative. In an article previewing the arguments of the book, Aslany argues that the present crisis of the peasantry is “the most important story of the 21st century.” Aslany believes peasants to be a supremely important topic, because she thinks these agriculturalists are humanity’s final defense against total ecological ruin. Peasant cosmology’s sacral reverence for nature and peasants’ unique capability to steward natural resources, she claims, provide “our last protection against environmental collapse.” The peasantry’s eco-preserving essence is under continuous attack from urban-based and purportedly progressive forces that do not appreciate the indispensable role peasants play in environmental conservation. Humanity’s survival, then, depends on a global shift in agricultural policy toward supporting and strengthening the peasantry. This is all the more true, in her view, because intolerable urban conditions must lead to future repeasantization. But Aslany’s revisionist perspective on the peasantry suffers from critical flaws. Her misleading historical account of global economic development confounds the role of both states and peasants in it. These distortions lead her to erroneously inflate the peasantry’s potential future importance. Ultimately, her peasant exceptionalism is unconvincing: it is highly doubtful that peasants possess a unique solution to the world’s environmental dilemmas. What Is a Peasant? Where does the two billion peasants figure come from? The source that Aslany cites for her claim regarding the existence of two billion peasants is her article, where she makes a slightly different claim — namely, that the rural populations of the three less-developed continents comprise two billion people: “By my calculations, as many as 2 billion people live in the countryside of Africa, Latin America and Asia, where small family farms dominate.” The article itself does not give any sense of how this particular statistic was derived, but two billion is in line with other estimates of the number of people involved in smallholder agriculture globally. Nevertheless, peasants, smallholder agriculturalists, and rural dwellers are overlapping but distinct categories whose differences should be preserved. Defining peasants is a controversial endeavor. Aslany’s definition of a peasant — “a smallholder farmer who lives off the land . . . relies mainly on family labour, and farms both for subsistence and for the market” — blurs the distinction between farmers who primarily produce for exchange and those who are not fundamentally dependent on markets for their survival. This move allows her to to include a larger swath of people in her study, beyond only those rustics who raise crops and animals primarily for their own family’s consumption. In fact, the cultivators Aslany studies are often highly dependent on markets, either on the input side, to purchase seed, fertilizer, and chemicals, sometimes via financial markets, and/or on the output side, through income generated by sale of their produce. They often cannot subsist on their own produce since they are husbanding nonstaple crops (e.g., coca, cocoa, or sugar). Farming is not even the main income generator for some of the people Aslany profiles. One Cambodian farming couple’s income is overwhelmingly provided through the remittances generated by their children working in South Korean factories. Another farmer, this one in India, holds onto his land only though revenue earned from a transport business. The point here is not to argue for one or another sociological definition of the peasantry or attempt the near-impossible task of precisely enumerating how many people fit any such definition worldwide. Rather, it is to note that a less expansive conception of what a peasant is would lead to an estimate of the peasantry’s size significantly lower than her marquee two billion figure. Repeasantization? Part of Aslany’s desire to stress the continuing importance of the peasantry derives from what she considers the failure of cities and industry to provide a tolerable existence for the future of humanity. The increase in world population over the next several decades will, she predicts, see many return from cities to rural areas and agricultural activities: “When all else fails, land is the only thing; in this sense, peasant cultivation must have a significant role to play in securing humanity’s future.” Aslany tries to buttress this prediction by arguing against the veracity of two “compelling — if unexamined — stories about [the peasantry’s] demise.” The first is that peasant agriculture will decline in tandem with the progress of industry, and the second is that cities will absorb the peasants departing the countryside. Since these are precisely the processes that the world has witnessed over the past century, readers may be perplexed when encountering Aslany’s pushback on these claims. Aslany attempts to rescue the first contention by writing that predictions of peasant agriculture’s demise was an “based on an extrapolation from the highly specific experience of north-western Europe” and “has still not happened.” But depeasantization is a phenomenon that has advanced far beyond the borders of the richest countries. For instance, taking as a sample the countries that Wolf profiles in Peasant Wars — Mexico, Russia, China, Vietnam, Algeria, and Cuba — the International Labour Organization reports the share of labor working in agriculture as under 10 percent in Algeria and Russia, under 20 percent in Cuba and Mexico, and, in China (22 percent) and Vietnam (25 percent), it is half of what it was mere decades ago. The countries Aslany visits in her book generally have higher labor shares in agriculture, but even they have come down rapidly in the twenty-first century: Cambodia’s, for instance, fell from 73 percent in the year 2000 to 33 percent in 2025. On the second claim, Aslany recognizes that some formerly peasant countries like China have seen tremendous rural-to-urban migration. But, she insists, “China is exceptional: it cannot become the general condition.” Again, the data intrudes on this perspective. United Nations figures show the Peasant Wars group with all non-Chinese countries at over 75 percent urbanization, with the exception of Vietnam, which has doubled its urbanization rate to 39 percent in the past generation. The countries Aslany visited, too, exhibit secular trends toward urbanization. In fact, of the three continents Aslany mentions as having significant rural populations, only in Africa is the rural population growing absolutely (although less rapidly than the urban population on the continent). Latin America ’s and Asia ’s rural populations have seen slight absolute declines over the twenty-first century. It is therefore difficult not to be skeptical about Aslany’s predictions of saturated cities producing a demographic movement to the countryside; her statement that “even Europe is seeing traces of what some experts call ‘re-peasantization’” is baffling. It is true that legitimate questions remain about how burgeoning urban populations in less-developed countries will be absorbed into the labor force, especially given that many of these countries are experiencing “ premature deindustrialization .” However, outside of some massive crisis of urban life above and beyond what is currently being experienced in Global South cities, in all likelihood current trends of depeasantization and rural-to-urban migration will continue into the near future. The State as Villain (Marx in Supporting Role) The sense that peasants are currently facing an existential crisis (despite a somewhat contradictory insistence on their endurance) provides the motivating drama for Aslany’s book. Peasants themselves, Aslany avers, are not responsible for their endangerment. Rather, that is the fault of the states that have persecuted them over the last century: “Peasants are in crisis today not because they are somehow unsuited to modern conditions but because they have been hunted to the limits of possibility.” In this “class war between humanity’s urban and rural factions,” deliberate anti-peasant efforts by states have driven many peasants off of their land and threaten to eliminate the peasantry as a category in years to come. The underlying reason that states have hit upon these anti-peasant policies, Aslany contends, is primarily ideological. Drawing on the English example, in which a vanquished peasantry coincided with rapid economic growth, the world’s technocrats adopted this experience as an ideal and, aided by British imperialism, exported it to the rest of the world. Thus, Aslany writes, “The world’s managers have believed their own urban fairy tale: that the peasantry no longer exists – or that it should, in the interests of ‘progress,’ cease to do so.” Chief among the ideologists was Karl Marx, “who did the most to convert the dying peasant of bourgeois fantasy into scientific ‘fact.’” This peasant-averse ideology reached its most grotesque expression in various episodes of the twentieth century, when Marxist-influenced governments, through collectivization or otherwise, waged lethal mass campaigns against the peasantry. This is a radical reinterpretation of the motives of state managers in the past centuries of global economic development, which completely neglects the more straightforward reasons that they pursued policies that did not prioritize peasant interests. State managers’ aspirations to increase economic prosperity derived primarily from the political pressures they faced both domestically and internationally. Domestically, populations clamored for increasing living standards, and businesses demanded assistance in competing with foreign goods. Internationally, state managers wanted to avoid being dominated economically and/or militarily by rival states. Any successful historical example of rapid national economic development has required a transition of the labor force out of agriculture and into services and, especially, industry. While not ignoring the suffering that peasants faced in historical episodes of persecution, it is also true that peasants themselves have found many reasons to voluntarily leave the country for the city. Urban wages are often higher than agricultural ones, and, for many, backbreaking labor in the fields is less preferable than employment in a factory or an office. Cities offer a more exciting cultural life and an escape from rural social conservatism and patriarchal family structures. Not every peasant who left their farm for the city over the past centuries was marched off at gunpoint by the state; migratory flows from countryside to city have existed both in times of acute crisis and in times of relative stability. Therefore, it is misleading to portray depeasantization as a wholly top-down enterprise devised by sinister state bureaucrats drunk on Marxism or modernization theory. Many peasants have voluntarily chosen, for various reasons, to take their chances in the uncertain city rather than on the familiar farm. The Thin Green Line The book’s ultimate argument for the importance of the peasantry lies in peasants’ ecological sensibilities. “The fact that a substantial proportion of the world’s cultivable land is controlled by peasants,” Aslany writes, “protects us enormously against the final exhaustion of natural systems.” Peasants’ sacral veneration of their land and environment, Aslany argues, is actually superior as a form of conservation compared to more conventional legal approaches: “Over prolonged periods, the sacred offers a much stronger form of environmental protection than the law.” Peasant cosmology amounts to a kind of Thin Green Line preventing the world from tipping over into environmental catastrophe. The balance sheet of the peasantry’s relation to the environment, however, is not exclusively on the side of conservation; peasants can play a very damaging ecological role. Smallholder agriculture requires land, which excludes the land from serving other purposes. The more small farms propagate, the more deforestation and biodiversity loss might result. On an individual level, too, small farmers are often compelled to adopt the latest potentially ecologically-damaging cultivation practices, whether it is synthetic fertilizer, pesticide and herbicide use, or others. In the extreme case, as Aslany documents in Ghana , small farmers might opt to despoil their plots with what is euphemistically called “artisanal and small-scale mining,” overwhelming a once-viable farm with toxic waste from gold extraction. In truth, peasant agriculture is not inherently ecologically superior to other forms of agriculture — or, for that matter, other forms of economic activity. Agriculture undoubtedly has an important place in any plan for environmental sustainability. But comprehensive solutions to the climate crisis (to mention just one pressing ecological challenge) must go far beyond the areas where peasants purportedly have expertise. Transitioning to renewable energy sources, for instance, and decarbonizing industry and transit are necessary components of a climate strategy on which peasant cosmology will have very little bearing. All the more reason to abandon notions of peasant exceptionalism when thinking about how to achieve our ecological goals. To the extent that Aslany has a political program in mind to address smallholder agriculture, she advocates placating the farmers who “would love a bit more capitalism in their lives” by deregulating state-sponsored commodity monopolies. But hers is not simply a laissez-faire program. Aslany also urges state support for “iron-clad protection for [peasant] land,” “forms of credit that are quite different from other kinds of businesses,” and “protection from excessive price fluctuations, or downward pressure applied by powerful buyers.” At base, the political program she outlines is one of subsidizing smallholder agriculture rather than an ecologically driven project per se. Perhaps this could be a small component part of a larger ecological strategy, but handouts to small agrarian businesses does not seem like a route to averting climate disaster. Makers of a Previous World Peasants raises pressing questions about agriculture, urbanization, migration, ecology, and economic development, particularly in the Global South. The stubbornly persistent absolute number of people involved in smallholder agriculture is certainly an important phenomenon that must be grappled with in both national and international contexts. But such an assessment is not aided by mangling the history and present of the peasant predicament. Peasants have not left their land over the past few centuries exclusively because they were victims of state persecution. And states have not undertaken industrialization programs only or primarily out of ideological anti-peasant animus. Rather, peasant attraction to cities and new forms of employment provided reason enough for many to migrate. It seems highly unlikely that present trends of urbanization and deagrarianization will sharply reverse in the decades to come. Finally, peasants do not possess a privileged position when it comes to formulating a comprehensive solution to the planet’s environmental crises. Peasants can be said to be “makers of the modern world” only in the sense that in the preceding epoch they provided the demographic foundation for agrarian empires. Today they account for an ever-dwindling share of the world’s population, a minority of global food production, and only really survive in the least developed countries. Extrapolating into the future, repeasantization on a significant scale looks far less likely than absolute peasant numbers dwindling further with the continuing advance of capitalism and falling fertility rates. Karl Marx is often criticized, including by Aslany, for proclaiming that “The country that is more developed industrially only shows, to the less developed, the image of its own future.” However, insofar as this premonition is read as sketching the broad historical arc of depeasantization and urbanization, it hews closer to developments across the subsequent centuries than his critics might care to admit. A History of Taxing the Rich in California

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Fred Glass ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-17T19:13:17+00:00

In the wake of the defeat of Proposition 40, the billionaire tax in California, observers are parsing the loss. The good news is that there were actually two progressive tax ballot measures before the Golden State’s electorate this year, and while we lost Prop 40, we won the other one, Prop 3. This point is key, because it’s not possible to grasp the reasons for the loss of 40 without knowing its intertwined history with Prop 3. Since economic inequality will not be disappearing any time soon, and taxing the rich is an integral aspect of fixing the problem, we need to devise a balance sheet of lessons drawn from these two campaigns to inform the next round. Prop 3 has made permanent a temporary state income tax in place since 2012 on the top 2 percent of income earners. These are people who make at least $370,000 per year. Prop 3 mostly supports public education but also supports other state-funded services. Last year, it brought in over $14 billion in revenue, close to 6 percent of the entire state budget. Prop 3 passed by a comfortable margin of better than 60–40 percent. Prop 40 — which lost 52–48 percent — proposed to tax California’s 250 billionaires on their bloated collective wealth (an estimated $2.4 trillion), a 5 percent levy to backfill cuts that Donald Trump and the Republican Congress made to federal Medicaid assistance to the states. Until now, California’s share came to $20 billion a year. Starting January 1, 2027, the loss of Prop 40 means that unless the state’s governor and legislature come up with a Plan B (and there is barely a glimmer of one at the moment), three million of the poorest Californians will lose access to health care. This goal of Prop 40, to save health care for the state’s indigent, was lost in mainstream media reportage on the campaign, which breathlessly — and in the worst tradition of fawning celebrity journalism — focused on the astronomical sums of money spent against Prop 40 and narcissistic wounds suffered by the ultrarich at the prospect of paying higher taxes. Example: a New York Times story in the final weekend of the campaign, “Inside the War Against California’s Wealth Tax,” managed to spill ink across two pages without saying a single word about the purpose of the tax that the self-absorbed right-wing billionaires were fighting. Little attention, too, was devoted to analyzing what the enormous spigot of political action committee (PAC) money against the billionaire tax purchased: a torrent of some of the most deceptive and deliberately confusing paid messaging ever delivered in a state ballot measure campaign (and that’s saying something). Prop 3, by contrast, received generally favorable coverage and flew mostly below the radar, while Prop 40 was being carpet-bombed. What accounted for the differing treatment? And does the result mean Prop 40 was a first step toward wealth taxes from which we can draw valuable insights and move forward? Or a serious mistake from which it will take a while to recover? What They’re Saying One postelection refrain is the assertion that the billionaires have way too much money and will spend so much (which they won’t even feel) that the people don’t stand a chance to tax the rich. The implication is that progressive forces should work on other issues that we can actually win. There is an obvious element of truth here, but it’s only part of the story. It’s also the defeatist lesson the billionaires would like us to internalize. A more nuanced narrative: Forget a wealth tax. Proposition 3, a top tier income tax, was on the same ballot and won. Only income taxes on the rich stand a chance. Again, a partial truth: the failure of one wealth tax doesn’t mean all will fail. But the devil is in the details. And income taxes on the superwealthy, as Thomas Piketty pointed out a dozen years ago, are so twentieth century. They are still necessary, but they are a mismatch with the ability of the rich to avoid tax scrutiny. And that focus ignores the big enchilada of where the real money is. Then there’s Governor Gavin Newsom’s self-serving mantra: You can’t do it in one state. It has to be a federal wealth tax. Which is half right — we do need that. But it’s not an either/or. Fully one-quarter of the country’s billionaires reside in California, and there’s plenty of need for a fair share of their taxes right here. Finally, for people closer to the labor campaigns for 3 and 40 — they were both led by unions — and tuned into the inside baseball, there are competing stories about why the campaigns did not work together, and crucially, why a handful of important labor and nominally progressive organizations opposed the billionaire tax. The division inflicted real damage on 40 and, with its defeat, perceptions of what’s possible along these lines in the future. Sorting through these viewpoints and explanations with the goal of doing better next time requires some background in California’s tax wars, beginning with the disastrous Prop 13. Had the major backer of the Prop 40 campaign, SEIU-United Healthcare Workers, absorbed the history and applied its lessons from the outset, things might have gone differently. That knowledge might have also given the leadership of the Prop 3 campaign a more expansive view of the state’s tax needs than the narrow picture they felt compelled to defend. Prop 13 No examination of California’s tax politics can avoid the legacy of Prop 13. The proposition was placed on the California ballot in 1978 by the Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association, an anti-tax group backed by big business. It turned out to be a small government, anti-tax program replicated by the Right all across the United States. Prop 13 capped property taxes at 1 percent of assessed value and limited increases in tax rates to no more than 2 percent per year. Although it was sold to the public in a time of high inflation as a measure to “keep grandma in her home” on her fixed income, it applied to both residential and commercial property. This meant, over time, that large commercial property owners would pocket billions of dollars in the difference between what they pay in taxes and what they would be paying if their property, increasing in value over the years, was reassessed upward to market value. Prop 13 didn’t stop with revisions to the property tax code. A state constitutional amendment, it changed the threshold for the legislature to pass any tax from a simple majority to two-thirds supermajority, and the same for local taxes. Meanwhile, it left a simple majority in place for reducing or eliminating a tax. It mandated that property value reassessments to current market rate could only take place when the property was sold, and even then, with loopholes that allowed corporations to avoid reassessment and thus higher taxes. Beyond the details of its mandates, two other facets of the measure bear mention. Its dog-whistle racist campaign message pitted white homeowners against renters and welfare recipients: as in, “Your hard-earned tax dollars shouldn’t be wasted on black and brown poor people” — a template for conservative campaigners elsewhere. It represented a backlash, in a time of high inflation, against the last gasps of social movements of the 1960s and ’70s, especially the gains of the civil rights movement and rise of public sector unions. Prop 13 was a traumatic event from which consultants and politicians couldn’t recover for more than three decades, preventing them from understanding that yes, it was actually still possible to tax the rich. What began as a political defeat morphed into a self-inflicted inhibition and common sense. Lesson One: Don’t Forget About the Money The first progressive tax campaign I worked on was in 1992. A combination of upper-tier personal income and corporate tax increases, Prop 167 was placed on the California state ballot by public sector unions seeking to draw business contributions away from support of Republican Governor Pete Wilson’s ballot-box attempt to concentrate power over the state budget into his office. It was the first effort in a decade and a half to tax the rich following passage of Prop 13 but, still suffering below its shadow, the plan was not completely serious. After raising the money to get the tax on the ballot, the unions directed staff like me — I was communications director for the California Federation of Teachers (CFT) at the time — to work the campaign but spent no money on media. Meanwhile, business interests treated the threat seriously, put together a war chest, and expended millions on media buys. California Governor Pete Wilson with then-President George H. W. Bush. (Records of the White House Photograph Office / George H. W. Bush Presidential Photographs) Yet until three weeks before Election Day, the two sides were polling dead even. At that point, the anti-tax forces pulled out all the stops. You could not turn on a radio or TV without teeth-gnashing boutique owners sobbing about how they were going to lose the businesses they had built with their own two hands. Ultimately, the business coalition outspent the unions by a margin of more than twenty to one. In actuality, no small businesses would have been affected by the taxes. But the tactic of corporations hiding behind mom-and-pop stores, deployed against progressive taxes since time immemorial, proved effective. That prevarication, coupled with the equally venerable blackmailing canard that all the “job creators” would leave the state — and multiplied by the huge imbalance in political spending — meant that we lost by a wide margin, 59–41. All the smart people said, “You see. We told you. California is an anti-tax state and will remain that way forever.” Some of us felt the story had a different moral. Studying the campaign timeline, we saw that progressive taxes were at first blush popular. Until the opposition’s river of negative media advertising widened into a flood, with no comparable answer forthcoming from our side, the public was far from convinced how evil Prop 167 was. We posited that if the unions were to pony up a reasonable amount of campaign funding for media alongside a robust field operation, we could win a future campaign. But that insight — blocked by the common wisdom among the political cognoscenti that California’s electorate had been “anti-tax” since Prop 13 and would remain that way until Judgment Day had to wait for some twenty years to be tested. Poor consolation: the unions’ feint worked, and Wilson’s measure went down to defeat. Lesson Two: The Right Message at the Right Time Two decades later, following the subprime mortgage housing crash and Great Recession, my union organized and led a successful campaign to pass a progressive tax. After three years of massive state budget cuts pummeling public schools and social services, the state still faced a record multibillion-dollar budget deficit. To ward off further harm, we went on offense, pulling together a coalition that included unions and community and faith-based groups. In our early meetings we came up with three criteria to test in opinion research: What tax would raise the largest amount of revenue, drawn from the most progressive source, with the best chance of winning? These deliberations and tests resulted in our proposal for a millionaire tax ballot measure to add two top-tier marginal tax rates to the state income tax schedule: an additional 3 percent on income between $1 million and $2 million, and 5 percent on any income above $2 million. We calculated that this would raise $5 billion — not enough to fill the entire state budget hole, but a good start and, following our criteria, winnable. The core of our message: “Tax the rich for schools and services.” We had tested similar sentiments a year earlier with a march from Bakersfield to Sacramento and found over six weeks that they resonated deeply in the communities of the central valley, supposedly a conservative bastion. Reinforced now by our opinion research, we began circulating petitions to gather the requisite number of voter signatures to place our millionaire tax on the ballot. Then-Governor Jerry Brown had a different idea. Notoriously averse to opinion research, and with a record of electoral success based on his gut instincts, he rejected our overtures to join the campaign and put a rival petition for signatures into circulation with a mix of progressive and regressive taxes, including increases on the lowest income earners along with a 1¢ sales tax increase. Our research told us (and we told him) that this would not fly with the electorate. Jerry Brown campaigns in 2010. (Bob Tilden / CC BY 2.0 ) Our proposed measure came out on top of his in five straight opinion polls over several months, but he remained unmoved until the first week of March 2012. During that interval, he shaved away the other unions in our coalition with a razor governors have at their disposal: the threat to veto any legislation sponsored by groups that refused to comply with his will. Despite the loss of these partners (who behind the scenes let us know their hearts were still with us), we retained sufficient resources and coalition partners to put ten thousand marchers carrying millionaire tax signs in the streets of the capital, parading past Brown’s windows. Two days later, we announced the results of a new poll showing our numbers going up and his going down. Brown finally caved. He called CFT president Joshua Pechthalt, a high school social studies teacher in Los Angeles, and worked out a compromise deal. This became Proposition 30, with two parts: three tiers of additional 1 percent tax bumps on individuals making $250,000, $300,000 and $500,000 a year, coupled with a modest 0.25 percent sales tax. Brown insisted on inclusion of the latter (we negotiated it down from his original 1¢ sales tax) due to his agreement with the Chamber of Commerce that they would stay out of the fight so long as Brown didn’t “demonize the rich.” With the sales tax, he said, everyone had skin in the game. We didn’t like the sales tax, nor that the Prop 30 tax would be temporary; our millionaire tax proposal had been for a permanent tax. But Brown agreed to limit the sales tax to four years and extend the progressive income tax to seven years. And the Prop 30 package was actually projected to raise more money than our millionaire tax (and it has). Lesson Three: A Big Coalition With Brown on board, and all of our former coalition partners returned to the fold, we were set up for success. But first we had to overcome a messaging hiccup. The governor had been campaigning mostly on the idea that Prop 30 would save education from further cuts. We agreed with that message, but based on our research — and the post–Occupy Wall Street injection of economic inequality into public conversation — we knew that there also had to be a strong emphasis on taxing the rich. Brown’s hodgepodge approach — save education, tear down the state’s wall of debt, shared sacrifice, and reluctantly, fair taxes — had to be redirected to what we knew were the winning messages. After some internal conversations, he dropped the distracting mishmash and became a disciplined messenger, going straight to stripped-down messages on education, fair share of taxes for the rich, and emphasizing that the sales tax (our biggest worry) represented just one tiny penny on a four-dollar sandwich. Prop 30 made it across the finish line with votes to spare, 55–45 percent, overcoming more than $50 million in opposition spending and decades of formerly unshakeable warnings that California voters would never ever vote for a tax. Thanks to Brown’s hefty campaign fund, we slightly outspent the rich conservatives who bankrolled the other side. That, plus our robust ground game (door-to-door canvassing and earned media from demonstrations, town halls, and other public events) and late course correction on messaging made the difference. Prop 30 was the largest income tax increase on wealthy Californians since World War II. Four years later, as the sales tax expired, the coalition brought Prop 55 to the voters to extend Prop 30, this time as a pure progressive tax for another fourteen years. Brown didn’t bother participating, nor did we need him, as the wealthy reactionaries, having had their butts handed to them four years before, stayed home. A unified labor movement, with adequate funding and support from a wide spectrum of progressive community partners, executed well, and Prop 55 won in a landslide, by 64–36, demonstrating the popularity of taxing the rich if an undisturbed electorate makes its choice without a thumb on the scale from a well-heeled opposition. The above are key pieces of the ancestry to this year’s Proposition 3 victory, which made the Prop 55 tax permanent. But there’s one more election story we should review, the lessons of which help explain Prop 3’s success and the defeat of Prop 40 on November 3. Split Roll In March 2020, now retired, and prey to the notion that “idle hands are the devil’s workshop”, I was gathering signatures with my local faculty union for the “Schools and Communities First” ballot measure, ultimately Prop 15. Generated by the same progressive tax coalition behind Props 30 and 55, Prop 15 was the long-anticipated “split roll” tax meant to overcome the choke hold placed over the state budget via property tax limitations in 1978’s Prop 13. Prop 15 proposed a simple change: resetting the tax basis of commercial property from its last purchase price to current market value. It explicitly excluded residential and agricultural property and, attempting to neutralize small business opposition, set the bar for reassessment to property worth $3 million or more. It would have drawn more than 90 percent of its revenues from less than 10 percent of commercial property holders — entities like Disney and Chevron whose owners paid property taxes based on long-past-shelf-life assessments, robbing the state and its public services of billions of dollars each year. At the outset, the measure polled well, although not as strongly as the millionaire tax had. But it ran into three problems. The broad labor/community progressive tax coalition, well prepared, had gathered the most signatures ever for a California ballot measure campaign — 1.7 million — by mid-March. It would have been even more, but the intervention of the COVID-19 pandemic put a premature end to that activity. As it turned out, this represented but the beginning of how the pandemic undercut the yes campaign. With the imposition of restrictions on public events and face-to-face contact, one of the main tools of labor-community coalitions against big money, grassroots people power, largely evaporated. Door-to-door canvassing and large rallies to draw earned media were out, as was tabling and leafleting in public spaces. The campaign thus developed a decent virtual speakers bureau program, including relational organizing efforts and battalions of letter-to-the-editor writers. Although Prop 15 phone banking was well staffed with volunteers, it became tough for voters to distinguish volunteers from paid callers funded by oil corporations, realtors, and large commercial property owners like Chevron. Already weeks before Election Day, phone banks had been rendered less effective due to voter fatigue at the tsunami of calls. A second factor was the problematic role of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP). Its president, Alice Huffman, steered the state organization into a no endorsement on the campaign, while her political consulting firm was accepting $740,000 in No on 15 contributions. The Huffman-led NAACP’s endorsement figured prominently in the massive direct mail operation that sent deceptive “No on 15” literature streaming into African American households, which claimed falsely that 15 would tax homeowners. The NAACP position was partly countered by Prop 15 endorsements from other institutional pillars of black communities, but it sowed confusion among African American voters. Current California Governor Gavin Newsom speaks at the California Economic Summit in 2019. (Government of California) Third, Governor Gavin Newsom endorsed the measure late and declined to campaign for it. By contrast, Jerry Brown had been out every day in October 2012 calling for the passage of Proposition 30. Governors are reliable media magnets. The no side outspent the pro-15 forces by some $20 million, most of which went to TV advertising. Newsom missing in action meant the loss of earned media opportunities that could have counterbalanced the negative ads. Had any one of these factors gone the other way, Prop 15 would have won despite its funding disadvantage. Instead, it lost narrowly, 52–48. Between Election Campaigns The defeat of Prop 15 and the pandemic in which it occurred left a nasty dent in progressive tax work and on the general atmosphere of the political left in California. The Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) played a significant role in the Prop 15 campaign, mounting a coordinated statewide effort among its twenty chapters. Its activists, fresh from the 2020 Bernie Sanders primary victory over Joe Biden in the state, had regrouped after Biden clinched the nomination and enthusiastically staffed phone banks for 15, logging more calls than most unions. But after November, the two major political disappointments in a nine-month span sent the mostly youthful cohort into a demoralized space; membership and activism decayed over the next few years. A similar dysphoria enveloped the rest of the progressive tax coalition, which largely lost momentum, nursing its wounds through the 2024 election cycle. Beyond the general disorientation and social anomie fostered by the pandemic, the education unions (the California Teachers Association [CTA], CFT, and significant sectors of the Service Employees International Union) leading the coalition had to face unrelenting attacks from the Right for their support of school closures, diluting energy for other commitments. One of the coalition’s community organizations, California Calls, kept the faith and continued to host sparsely attended meetings. Only CFT, with little active support from the other groups, pushed a new effort in the legislature. The union worked with Alex Lee, a state assemblymember in Alameda and Santa Clara counties (and the only state representative at the time associated with DSA) to promote wealth tax legislation. Since everyone understood Newsom would veto any such bills, a paper Democratic legislative supermajority meant nothing (even if it had been unified on the issue, which it wasn’t) and these bills did not advance. Limited meetings and deferred decision-making brought no campaign in 2024. But heading toward the 2026 midterms, the coalition got its act together, tested the waters on several proposals, and decided its best move was to make Prop 55 permanent. This would ensure that the funding — an average above $10 billion a year from the progressive tax, or around 5 percent of the entire state budget — would continue, without which the public sector, especially education, would suffer serious budget reductions, and high-end income earners would receive an entirely unnecessary tax cut. Or so went the plan before Trump’s “Big Beautiful Bill.” HR 1 gave federal tax cuts to the rich and paid for it with massive reductions to Medicaid, including a loss of some $20 billion a year for California’s share of that program (called “Medi-Cal” here). The looming threat to Medi-Cal and by extension to health care jobs spurred SEIU-United Healthcare Workers into action. Enter the Billionaire Tax A 120,000-member statewide union representing frontline workers across numerous corporate and public health care entities, UHW stood to lose tens of thousands of jobs after January 1, 2027, if nothing changed. Its president, Dave Regan, had used ballot measures in the past — dozens of them — to leverage his union’s interests in Sacramento. Sometimes this meant maintaining the efforts all the way to November,and at other times agreeing to drop them from the ballot if negotiations with employers or the legislature gained improved outcomes. So no one was sure, when the billionaire tax entered the picture in September 2025 with a filing for title and summary, whether it was a straightforward entry into the ballot measure lists or a bargaining ploy for Regan with Gavin Newsom and the state legislature. What unfolded suggests an initial goal of a negotiating chip that, in failing, forced the union, fatefully, to repurpose it as a real ballot campaign. The measure itself, despite claims by opponents that it was poorly designed, was put together by a rock-star team of progressive economists and lawyers who fully understood the present and future perils of nonchalant-ing its construction and, at least from this layman’s point of view, gave it a solid foundation on both economic and legal grounds. Although Regan approached the progressive tax coalition with a request for support, the conversation did not go well. Its leaders felt the measure was ill-timed and could get in the way of Prop 55 renewal. Regan’s position was essentially a pointed question: What about the three million Medi-Cal recipients and thousands of union jobs? To which the coalition leaders gave an unsatisfactory answer predicated on by no means certain budgetary maneuvers and legislative goodwill. Regan’s blunt personality preceded him into the room and was a factor in the discussion, which ended in mutual antagonism. The Opposition Consolidates By late December, several of the wealthiest tech moguls in California, having created the “Building a Better California” PAC to oppose the tax, celebrated the season with a party in Marin whose guests included Sergey Brin (Google cofounder, worth $250 billion and a MAGA donor) and Newsom. Brin reportedly cornered the governor and gave him a piece of his mind about the tax, threatening to leave the state if it passed. Newsom promised to try to get rid of it and was as good as his word, quickly becoming its most high-profile non-right-wing billionaire opponent and reliably delivering their false talking points (all the billionaires will leave, innovation will be smothered, California will be left a smoking husk). As a signal to his left that he didn’t share an entire bed with right-wing sociopaths, he added his own two cents: billionaires do need to pay their fair share, but a) at the federal level, not via the state, and b) it’s a good idea but fatally flawed (repeating a commonly employed argument for campaigns against ballot measures). Google cofounder Sergey Brin poured massive sums into the campaign against Proposition 40. He is pictured here, alongside Elon Musk and Jensen Huang, receiving the National Medal of Science at the Donald J. Trump Institute of Peace on October 8, 2026. (Daniel Torok / The White House) The measure received its title and summary around the first of the year (late for this sort of thing; Prop 3 had been gathering signatures already for months), and UHW began campaigning at the same time as the billionaires and their puppet governor danced and sang before an eager audience of mainstream media editors and reporters — none of whom seemed to know how to ask uber-rich people questions like: What impact will passage of this measure actually have upon your life/finances/business?; What is a greater value, your keeping every penny or millions of poor people keeping access to health care?; What does it mean to “leave the state” when you already own multiple mansions all over the country and world?; or Without your tax dollars, what other action do you suggest the government should take to prevent this health care debacle from occurring? The small coterie of fuming billionaires unearthed and spent the quarters from their couch cushions (piling up close to $400 million to date and counting, the largest sum ever expended on a California ballot initiative) to pay for consulting firms, gather signatures for two poison-pill measures to nullify 40, launch salvos of direct mail. and reserve massive media buys. Regan met with Newsom and attempted to work out a deal. In exchange for the governor’s support, the UHW president offered to pull the measure from the ballot and drop to a new bottom line: from 5 percent to 2 percent. This would have had the effect of reducing the revenue from $100 billion over five years to $40 billion — enough to get through two years of covering the Medi-Cal cuts before (presumably) a Democratic administration would be in national office, which (again presumably) would restore federal funding for Medicaid. But Newsom declined the deal. There matters stood until the June midterm Democratic primary. When Tom Steyer, the sole gubernatorial candidate supporting the billionaire tax, failed to make it into the top two for November, the CTA, State Building Trades Council (BTC), and Planned Parenthood, among others, immediately announced their public opposition to the billionaire tax. CTA was the only organization offering a principled reason for its position. Prop 40 allocated 90 percent of its projected revenues to shoring up Medi-Cal, which violated the state budgetary rules put in place from an earlier voter decision, Prop 98, that mandates 40 percent of the state general fund to public education. CTA’s opposition to the billionaire tax was consistent with their long-standing defense of that line in the sand. If it had stopped there, its hands would have been clean. Instead its leaders signed onto the scary and false messaging flowing from the billionaires’ playbook: Prop 40 would cause the billionaires to flee. The state would lose substantial tax dollars. Because the tax is temporary, voters should reject it, because it doesn’t meet the long-term needs of the state. The last one took the cake, as CTA with its other breath was urging passage of Prop 3, which it had supported as a temporary tax beginning in 2012 up to this election cycle. At the time of CTA’s announcement, it posted its recommendations for the full slate of propositions on its website. Next to the no vote on Prop 40, it placed, without explanation, a link to the Building a Better California website. After a few weeks the link was removed. The public statements from the BTC, Planned Parenthood, and the others simply parroted the billionaires’ talking points and aren’t worth dissecting. It is important to note a common strand among these nominally progressive groups’ decision-making processes: none offered a plebiscite to their members on the question. The BTC is one of the most conservative sectors of the labor movement and reliably tails behind the promises of construction capital for jobs. It was Planned Parenthood’s foundation board, which relies on philanthropy, that voted for opposition. These moves were amplified by the billionaire media machine, which emphasized the split in the progressive community and labor when it wasn’t simply pretending that “teachers” were opposed. (The lie was exposed, to little media notice, when two of the largest local CTA affiliates, United Teachers Los Angeles and United Educators of San Francisco, came out for Prop 40. Shortly afterward the California Federation of Teachers, which had been neutral, and which has a more democratic governance structure than CTA, switched to a support position.) Momentum Returns and Recedes Things looked bad for Prop 40 until three days at the beginning of August. At that point, UHW’s organizing within two of the major institutions of California progressive politics paid off. First, the state Democratic Party leadership, expressing its displeasure with the governor, voted narrowly to endorse the billionaire tax. The next day, with President Lorena Gonzalez leading the charge, the California Federation of Labor Unions came overwhelmingly on board. As icing on the cake, the California Nurses Association joined the newly expanding coalition the following day. But this represented the high point of the campaign. Late endorsements and sporadic earned media emanating from such events as enthusiastic rallies featuring Bernie Sanders and Ro Khanna and cheerleading by Nobel Prize–winning economists turned out to be no match for the record-setting spending tide of the other side, which all along had been steadily eroding the early advantage in poll numbers among a public disgusted with Trump-supporting oligarchs. A no campaign has more messaging tools at its disposal than the pro side of a ballot measure. Where the latter has to make and hold its case before the electorate on the merits, the opposition — especially one with an unlimited pocketbook — doesn’t have to convincingly argue on the same ground. It only needs to cast doubt on the enterprise, showing (or claiming) that various trusted messengers are opposed, rolling out “experts” who “prove” it’s all wrong, and muddling the issues through fearmongering and misdirection. Communications from hell, summoned up by demonic billionaire-backed consultants performed those tasks well. Homeowners learned that they were the target of unnamed but terrifyingly evil “Sacramento politicians” who wanted their homes (like those in the 2020 split roll campaign). Old people found that their pensions were going to be taken away by Prop 40. “Stanford professors” (usually not identified for who they were: the right-wing Hoover Institute) demonstrated that the tax would strip California of innovation and tax revenues. And so on. Progressive tax campaigns produce time-tested, predictable reactions from right-wing rich people, which backers of the measures do well to inoculate against. While modulating with circumstances, they fall into three main themes: The “job creators” will all leave, taking all the jobs with them in their platinum-lined backpacks. The innovation this time around was substituting wealthy “taxpayers” and “tax revenues” for employers and jobs. This is a tax on “you.” This takes two forms: an outright lie making a levy on 250 people into a general tax, and “First they come for the billionaires, then they come for you.” Misdirection: pretending small business owners/homeowners/pensions are the targets, obscuring the point of economic inequality and the tax-dodging of the superrich. Inoculation is possible, but it requires a sufficient timeline before the campaign proper to manufacture the needles and serum and delivery, and sufficient financial resources to pay for it all. With a relatively popular governor actively opposed, and putatively progressive organizations gravely restating the false messages that billionaires couldn’t have convinced the public to believe on their own, the billionaire tax had too big a mountain to climb. A progressive tax campaign needs three things to have a reasonable shot at winning: the right message at the right time; the largest coalition possible; and sufficient cash for at least a modicum of paid advertising to augment its field campaign and earned media against the most well-funded opposition imaginable. Prop 40 had but one out of three, and it wasn’t enough. Compare and contrast with Prop 3’s advantages. Built on a long-standing and extensive coalition of unions and community organizations, with the full backing of the Democratic political establishment (including the governor, who otherwise never met a progressive tax he liked), and with a bank account supported by the biggest labor political contributor (CTA) in the state, and facing virtually no opposition spending, it offered a free lunch to the public (“not a tax increase” — technically true, just maintaining an earlier one) along with its backward, modestly progressive tax messaging (“If it fails, rich people will get a completely unnecessary tax cut”). Prop 3’s victory relied on keeping below the line of fire aimed at Prop 40. It might have been a larger target itself had the billionaire tax not existed. But in that event, as in 2016 with Prop 55, the progressive tax coalition would probably have prevailed anyway, because it was sufficiently prepared and resourced. CTA did not have to hand the billionaires a winning card against a historically justifiable tax on plutocrats for a good cause. Their action dismissed the possibility of a united two-proposition tax the rich campaign that could have provided much-needed consciousness-raising in the electorate about the evils of our New Gilded Age imbalance of wealth and power. Such a dedicated education campaign remains an urgent task. At one level, it’s pointless to second guess what SEIU-UHW might have done differently to get Prop 40 past the third of a billion dollars a couple dozen sociopathic tech oligarchs spent to keep every speck of their hoard and prevent a few million of the poorest Californians from gaining back the health care Trump stole from them. It’s done. But it’s also essential to understand the mistakes that could have been avoided so as to not commit them again the next time around. What’s Next The loss of Prop 40 does not mean the end of progressive tax conflict in California. It’s a battle lost, not the war. We can close this account with a note about the short term and the longer run. It remains to be seen whether the deal originally offered to the UHW will materialize, and budget maneuvers and modest corporate tax increases or loophole closures backed by the legislature and Governor-elect Xavier Becerra will result in enough revenue to reverse the loss of health care for millions of California residents caused by the cruel actions of Trump and the federal government. It’s within the power of the new governor and legislature, if they are committed to it; the tax increases (on income, since thanks to passage of the billionaires’ deceptive Prop 42, wealth taxes are off the table for the time being) will simply require a legislative supermajority of two thirds. If the “blue wave” of November 3 should mean anything to the legislators, it’s that now is the time to act without fear of electoral retribution on that score. Beyond the immediate need to plug the funding gap left by the failure of the billionaire tax, those interested in reversing the stratospheric economic inequality that allows a handful of spoiled oligarchs to thwart what would otherwise be the will of an electoral majority must rise beyond the narrow definition of success of the Prop 3 coalition. Future progressive tax victories cannot depend on an “I win, you lose” mindset between what should be coalition partners. The key here is that all potential interested partners need to get over sectoral myopia and understand the common problem, the common enemy, and common working-class interests. Or, as Martin Luther King Jr noted in another time and place, “The best way to solve any problem, is to remove the cause.” The other side is serious. Are we? Corrin Kearney had been delivering pizzas for a Domino’s in North Texas for a few months when their store asked them to install a new app on their phone and keep it open during their shift. Soon, Kearney learned the app was tracking and scoring every delivery route they drove, with the results displayed on a leaderboard back at the store for the whole crew to see. The app uses technology from Drivosity, a company that sells what it calls “cutting edge GPS and AI” driver-monitoring to fast-food chains, including Domino’s and Papa John’s. Each trip is scored from 0 to 100 based on factors including acceleration, braking, cornering and speed. According to Kearney, drivers at their store are expected to keep their scores above 85. Anyone below that score is written up by managers and eventually locked out of the Domino’s system. “Drivosity can effectively fire people,” Kearney said. As a driver captain, Kearney has to write up other drivers, even though they have found the app doesn’t recognize the nuance of many real-world situations. Drivers can be penalized for sudden braking to avoid a collision or rapid acceleration to merge on a highway, forcing them to choose between risking an accident or getting points docked, according to Kearney. “It’s infuriating,” Kearney said. “The AI can tell you you haven’t driven well enough and you get fired.” Drivosity also sells coaching and analytics features, including HustleTime®, which measures how fast employees walk from the store to their car, how long they take handing the pizza to a customer and how long they take walking back. In elections this year, 169 (and counting) candidates or ballot initiatives endorsed by the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) nationally or by its chapters succeeded at city, state, and federal levels. DSA member Zohran Mamdani is the mayor of the largest city in the country, and it seems increasingly possible that Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez will run for president of the United States in 2028. These exciting developments raise the question of the role of elections in the socialist project. Today some on the Left refer to socialists running for office as “electoralism.” But that term has a specific historical meaning, which much of the radical left has long viewed with disdain. It’s worth getting clear on what distinguishes electoralism in that sense from a transformative democratic socialist electoral strategy like the one many in DSA are trying to work toward today. Unlike anarchist or syndicalist approaches to organizing, which view militancy outside of the state as the sole mechanism for bringing about social change, both electoralist and democratic socialist electoral strategies see contesting elections as an important tool for helping bring about socialism. But while electoralism focuses exclusively on winning elections and making change through policy measures, a democratic socialist strategy understands elections and socialist governance as one important means among others of achieving reforms and facilitating the organization of the working class into an agent capable of transforming society. Electoralism and Social Democracy Electoralism has existed nearly as long as the socialist movement itself. In Evolutionary Socialism , published in 1899, German socialist Eduard Bernstein argued that capitalism could be gradually overcome through a peaceful electoral path and piecemeal legislative reform. In the early twentieth century, social democratic parties across Western Europe seemingly began making good on this vision, winning state power through elections and attempting to enact pro-worker reforms. Though most early socialists did not think of themselves as “electoralists” and understood the importance of organizing outside the state, particularly in the workplace, over time social democratic strategy focused more and more on winning elections and parliamentary power. Social democrats eventually became electoralists in practice, if not in name. When social democratic parties were on the rise in the early and mid-twentieth century, the industrial working class was increasingly joining unions and engaging in mass strikes, giving the parties significant political leverage . This combination of power inside and outside the state allowed socialists to pass significant policy measures that improved the lives of millions, like universal health care, vacation for all, and expansive labor rights. The growing power of the working class outside of the state at the time also helped conceal the problems that the social democrats’ increasingly electorally focused strategy would eventually face. As time wore on, however, social democrats in government found themselves constrained to merely managing capitalist economies — whose center of power lay not in the state but in the hands of private capital owners who could withhold or offshore their investments if they felt sufficiently threatened, causing economic turbulence and eventually leading to socialist electoral defeat. Many social democratic parties began abandoning the end goal of bringing about socialism entirely, focusing on achieving reforms within capitalism instead. The problem was worsened by the fact that the labor movement, social democrats’ traditional base of power, slowly ossified and came to represent an ever smaller proportion of the working class. In periods of economic stagnation, as in the 1970s and ’80s, this dynamic led socialist governments such as François Mitterrand’s in France to impose austerity measures in an attempt to appease capital and maintain growth. But this too ultimately undermined their popularity and brought about their electoral ousting. In recent decades, declining union density has accelerated this dynamic, leading desperate left-wing parties to try to cobble together broader populist coalitions in order to win a plurality of votes. But these parties have then found that they lacked the organized power outside of the state to overcome the leverage of capital once they entered government, as we saw with the collapse of Podemos in Spain and Syriza in Greece. The core reason that postwar social democratic parties failed, then, was not that party leaders betrayed their base, but that the structural economic conditions imposed by capitalist social relations give owners of capital significant power over the state, limiting socialists’ ability to implement policy reforms absent independent bases of popular power. Social democrats often took this structural imbalance of power between capital and workers outside of the state as an unchangeable given, adopting an electoralist approach at the expense of reconstituting working-class organization. An Alternative to Electoralism: a Democratic Socialist Electoral Strategy Observing the crisis of social democracy in the 1970s, the Marxist philosopher Nicos Poulantzas theorized a democratic socialist electoral strategy as an alternative to both Leninist insurrectionism and social democratic electoralism. For Poulantzas, the state was a terrain of struggle that could be used by socialists to enact transformative change, but only if it was used to help build independent working-class power that would solidify this change and shift power from capital to workers. Adam Przeworski developed this idea further with the concept of class formation. Przeworski argued that whether proletarians act on their class interests rather than their many other interests (as, e.g., homeowners or college graduates or Catholics) depends on whether a political struggle brings class interests to the fore. This struggle is not just about changing people’s ideas but also about changing the world to materially align people around their shared class interests. For example, socialists in Sweden significantly reduced the wage gap between different types of workers through their “ solidaristic wage policy,” thereby not only raising the floor for the lowest-paid workers and reshaping the economy around more efficient industries but also leveling material differences between different segments of the working class. Today the biggest challenge American socialists face is a politically fragmented working class and a weak labor movement decimated by decades of neoliberal attacks. A democratic socialist electoral strategy aims to use electoral campaigns and socialist governance to slowly rebuild working-class consciousness and organization in order to reconstitute the popular power needed to transform society. Such a strategy situates those efforts within a broader approach alongside equally necessary grassroots organizing in workplaces and neighborhoods. Using Elections to Agitate and Organize What does such an electoral strategy, as opposed to mere “electoralism,” look like in practice? For one thing, socialists can use campaign communications to clarify the lines of class struggle. Bernie Sanders’s presidential campaigns exemplified this approach to messaging, with his “Not Me, Us” language, his calls for political revolution, and his frequent finger-pointing at the billionaires who benefit from our current class society. Once in office, socialists can continue to raise people’s political awareness by pointing out when capital stands in the way of much-needed reform or is otherwise harming the interests of working people. Mayor Mamdani’s “Rental Ripoff Hearings” are an excellent example of this type of us-versus-them line drawing, as are Congresswoman Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez’s frequent viral denunciations of corporate greed in her House speeches. Electoral campaigns can also be used to build socialist organization, as DSA has done in recent years by building out volunteer-led organizing efforts that engage thousands of volunteers in outreach to the broader working class — not only reaching more voters but also developing activists who may continue organizing in the socialist movement for years to come. Socialists in office can go beyond changing peoples’ ideas too: they can use their political power to make it easier for workers, renters, and consumers to build collective organization, thereby increasing the potential power of the socialist movement in the long run. One way to facilitate working-class organization is to use the power of the state to bolster organizing directly, for example by helping tenants and workers organize through public offices dedicated to the task, as the Mamdani administration has begun to do with its Office of Tenant Protection and Office of Worker Power. When socialists have greater political power, they can also pass legislation that directly empowers organizing, like reforming labor law to make it easier to unionize or establishing enforceable collective bargaining rights for tenant unions. With enough power, socialists can start to change the broader structures of the economy to shift power from capital to the working class — for instance, by nationalizing key industries. Even smaller reforms along the way, such as rent freezes and reducing transit costs, can grow support for the socialist project while redistributing resources. Finally, a democratic socialist electoral strategy recognizes the limited power of elections alone on the road to socialism. While winning elections and passing legislation are an essential part of building political power, democratic socialists must equally prioritize building people power beyond elections through union, tenant, and social movement organizing. Recognizing what elections can’t do is as important as understanding how we can best use them to build power. Most socialists in the United States today believe in the importance of contesting elections and taking state power to make change. But as the movement grows, socialists should avoid the pitfalls of a pure-and-simple electoralism that focuses on electing candidates and getting policy passed to the exclusion of all else. Such an approach sets the movement up for disaster when capital brings its leverage to bear in the context of a disorganized working class. We should instead be pursuing a democratic socialist electoral strategy, using campaigns and elected office alongside grassroots organizing to help rebuild the independent bases of working-class power that will be needed to achieve socialism in the long run. The 1973 Athens Polytechnic Uprising Still Matters Today

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Christos Avramidis ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-17T15:22:45+00:00

A military dictatorship had ruled Greece for more than six years when, in November 1973, hundreds of student occupied the Athens Polytechnic, transforming their university into a center of resistance. Thousands of workers, school students, and ordinary citizens soon joined them. On the pillars of the Polytechnic’s central gate, a student painted two messages in large letters: “USA OUT” and “NATO OUT.” Within days, on November 17, a military tank would smash through that same gate. Why were young Greeks risking their lives to overthrow the military dictatorship also demanding the withdrawal of the United States and the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO)? The answer lies in the three decades preceding the uprising. During those years, Greece experienced Nazi occupation, British military intervention, civil war, and the consolidation of an anti-communist political order that American assistance sustained. The Greek ruling class was all too happy to allow US interference to consolidate its own rule. The Resistance That Britain Opposed During World War II, Greece developed one of Europe’s largest resistance movements. The communist-led National Liberation Front (EAM) and its armed wing, the Greek People’s Liberation Army (ELAS), mobilized hundreds of thousands of people and came to control much of the Greek countryside. EAM was far more than a guerrilla organization. In the territories it liberated from Nazi occupation, EAM established institutions of popular administration, organized schools, and introduced new forms of political participation. In 1944, women were able to vote and stand as candidates in elections the resistance organized, years before they gained equal voting rights in national Greek elections. By the time German troops withdrew in October 1944, the resistance had become one of the most powerful social and political forces in the country. But Winston Churchill’s government was determined to prevent the communist-led movement from dominating postwar Greece. The confrontation centered on the future of the armed forces. Under British supervision, the Greek government demanded the disarmament of ELAS while retaining military formations associated with the government in exile. EAM rejected an arrangement that would leave its supporters disarmed in the face of potentially hostile forces. In practice, the leadership of EAM and of the Communist Party of Greece had an ambivalent stance toward the British army and British agents operating on Greek soil. They had cooperated with the British against the Nazi occupation. But they were deeply skeptical of British intervention in liberated Greece. Events after the German withdrawal from Athens confirmed their suspicions: the forces of liberation (EAM/ELAS and their allies) would have to fight a new battle. On December 3, 1944, an enormous, initially peaceful demonstration gathered in central Athens. Police opened fire, killing demonstrators and wounding many others. The Athens police chief, Angelos Evert, later acknowledged ordering the shooting. He had remained in charge of the police during the Nazi occupation, maintaining relations with the occupying authorities as well as contacts with British intelligence. The massacre marked the beginning of the December clashes, which Greeks call the Dekemvriana . British troops clashed with ELAS in the streets of Athens, using tanks, artillery, and aircraft. Their Greek allies included formations containing former members of the Security Battalions, which had collaborated with the Nazi occupiers in the struggle against the resistance. Militants who had fought the Nazis now faced the British army — and, fighting alongside it, former collaborators of the occupation. Churchill himself arrived in Athens on Christmas Day to intervene in the political negotiations. The British military campaign eventually defeated ELAS in the capital. The Varkiza Agreement, which the government and EAM signed in February 1945, then required the resistance to surrender its weapons. The Left had suffered a military defeat and now had to accept a political settlement from a position of profound weakness. Its supporters would soon discover how little protection that settlement provided. White Terror and the American Takeover Widespread persecution of the Left, which Greeks remember as the White Terror, followed the disarmament of ELAS. Armed right-wing groups, often operating with the tolerance or cooperation of state authorities, targeted people associated with EAM. They murdered and tortured former resistance fighters or drove them from their homes. State authorities imprisoned many more. Contemporary figures that EAM compiled recorded 1,289 murders, 31,632 cases of torture and physical abuse, 84,931 arrests, and 265 rapes between February 1945 and March 1946. United Nations documentation later cited these figures, but they originated with the persecuted movement rather than an independent nationwide investigation. The political climate was hardly conducive to a free electoral contest. The government scheduled elections for March 1946, while thousands of leftists faced imprisonment, displacement, intimidation, and restrictions on political activity. The communist-led left boycotted the vote, arguing that the conditions made meaningful democratic participation impossible. The Right emerged victorious. A referendum later that year restored King George II to the throne, consolidating the monarchy’s influence in the postwar political order. Greece descended into full-scale civil war. By 1947, however, Britain could no longer afford to sustain its intervention. The United States took its place. President Harry Truman requested $400 million in assistance for Greece and Turkey, declaring that Washington would help free peoples resist communist domination. In Greece, this meant massive military assistance to the government fighting the communist-led Democratic Army of Greece (DSE). American weapons, money, and military advisers helped determine the outcome of the war. During its final stages in 1949, Greek government aircraft dropped American-supplied napalm on Greek territory, targeting communist positions in the mountains of northern Greece. The United States had used napalm during World War II, but its deployment in Greece preceded Washington’s much better-known use of the weapon in Vietnam. Government forces defeated the DSE in 1949. For their supporters, however, the end of the war did not bring freedom. The iconic image of Greek resistance fighter Nikos Beloyannis holding a red carnation. ( Femmes françaises / Gallica Digital Library) The state sent thousands of leftists to prisons and remote island camps. The camp on the island of Makronisos became particularly notorious for its system of torture, beatings, forced declarations of repentance, and ideological reeducation. The contrast with the treatment of Nazi collaborators was striking, with far higher numbers of executions. One execution became a symbol of that political order. Nikos Beloyannis had been a communist resistance fighter during the Nazi occupation. The authorities arrested him after the civil war, and a military court sentenced him to death. A photograph of him smiling with a red carnation during his trial became famous internationally, while an international campaign demanded that the Greek government spare his life. Nevertheless, on March 30, 1952, a firing squad executed Beloyannis and three other communists. Their deaths reflected the determination of the postwar Greek state to eliminate what it regarded as the communist threat, despite the formal restoration of parliamentary institutions. That same year, Greece joined NATO. Much of Greece’s political elite had fled the country during the Nazi occupation, while significant sections of its economic elite had collaborated with the occupying forces. After liberation, the former returned to power, while much of the latter retained its economic influence. The restored ruling establishment built new mechanisms of repression and presided over a new era for Greek capitalism, keeping wages low and suppressing trade unions. US imperialism, which had taken the reins from its British counterpart, underpinned this new configuration of forces. In the postwar years, US interference would take many forms; at the same time, the Greek left would prove much more resilient than anyone could have predicted. When the American Ambassador Intervened in Greek Elections American influence went beyond supplying weapons. Declassified US diplomatic documents reveal direct American intervention in Greece’s electoral arrangements. In March 1952, US ambassador John Peurifoy informed Washington that the American embassy’s political intervention and a public statement had blocked the introduction of proportional representation. The American ambassador was candidly discussing his government’s efforts to influence the electoral system of a formally sovereign democracy. The elections four years later vividly illustrated the political consequences of Greece’s unusual electoral arrangements. In the 1956 parliamentary elections, the opposition Democratic Union won 48.15 percent of the popular vote, compared with 47.38 percent for Konstantinos Karamanlis’s conservative National Radical Union. Yet Karamanlis secured 165 parliamentary seats, while the opposition received only 132. The party that won fewer votes formed the new government. The opposition alliance was not a communist electoral front. It brought together centrist and left-wing forces against the conservative government. Even this broad parliamentary challenge failed to dislodge the Right. Two years later, the United Democratic Left (EDA) became Greece’s second-largest parliamentary party, demonstrating the continuing electoral strength of the Left despite years of persecution. The result alarmed the conservative establishment and reinforced its fears about the political consequences of a more open democratic system. The Monarchy, the Para-State, and the Road to Dictatorship The Greek monarchy was far from a ceremonial institution. The palace intervened repeatedly in political affairs, exercised substantial influence over the armed forces, and helped shape the composition and survival of governments. Alongside the formal state existed an extensive anti-communist security apparatus and networks of right-wing organizations, some of which maintained close relations with elements of the police, military, and intelligence services. Greek and American security agencies also cooperated during the Cold War in covert structures, including a clandestine stay-behind network called “Red Sheepskin.” These networks formed part of the country’s anti-communist security architecture, although their existence alone does not establish responsibility for every episode of political violence. In 1965, King Constantine II’s confrontation with the centrist Prime Minister Georgios Papandreou over control of the military precipitated a government crisis. Papandreou had won a substantial parliamentary majority in 1964, yet his government collapsed amid royal intervention and defections from his own party. Greeks call these defections the Apostasia . The conflict was particularly revealing because Papandreou was no communist revolutionary. He was a moderate centrist politician operating within the parliamentary system. Nevertheless, his attempt to exercise authority over the military collided with the monarchy and the security establishment. By April 1967, Greece was approaching another national election. The army intervened before voters could go to the polls. The Colonels and Their American Allies On April 21, 1967, a group of military officers seized power. They suspended the constitution, banned political parties, imposed censorship, and arrested thousands of political opponents. The dictatorship established a system of exile and torture that particularly targeted communists, trade unionists, students, and other opponents. Its leaders justified their seizure of power as necessary to prevent a communist takeover. The precise extent of American foreknowledge of the coup, and of individual US officials’ involvement in it, remains contested. But the documentary record of Washington’s subsequent military cooperation with the dictatorship is extensive and undisputed. The Johnson administration suspended deliveries of certain major weapons systems. The suspension, however, was only partial, and restrictions on military deliveries were gradually eased. An October 1969 memorandum for President Richard Nixon acknowledged that the United States had delivered approximately $100 million in military equipment to Greece as grant assistance between April 1967 and June 1969. During the same period, the dictatorship held political prisoners in camps and prisons, and torture and repression continued unabated. American officials had a clear understanding of the nature of the dictatorship. They also regarded Greece’s position in NATO, its military installations, and its strategic location in the eastern Mediterranean as important to American interests. The contradiction was evident. Washington publicly called for constitutional government while maintaining significant military ties with a dictatorship that had abolished it. Greece was not an isolated example. In Spain, the United States had signed military agreements with Francisco Franco’s dictatorship in 1953, securing access to bases in exchange for financial and military assistance. The protection of Western strategic interests could coexist with the survival of authoritarian governments. The Tank at the Athens Polytechnic Uprising By November 1973, the Greek dictatorship was facing growing opposition. On November 14, students occupied the Athens Polytechnic and established a makeshift radio station. Their broadcasts called on the population to join the struggle for freedom. The most famous slogan of the uprising was “Bread, Education, Freedom.” But the messages painted on the pillars of the university gate revealed another dimension of the revolt. They read “USA OUT” and “NATO OUT.” For the protesters, these demands were inseparable from their struggle against the dictatorship. They had grown up in a country where American military assistance had helped defeat the communist left and where political prisoners had endured years of persecution. US diplomats had intervened in its domestic politics, and Greece’s military dictatorship maintained close relations with Washington. Thousands gathered around the Polytechnic. In the early hours of November 17, the dictatorship sent tanks against the occupied university. One crashed through the main gate. Police and soldiers violently suppressed the uprising. Historian Leonidas Kallivretakis has documented twenty-four confirmed deaths associated with the events, many of them in the streets surrounding the Polytechnic. In Greek popular memory, the uprising became the decisive event that brought down the dictatorship. Strictly speaking, however, the dictatorship survived for another eight months. On November 25, hard-line military officer Dimitrios Ioannidis overthrew dictator Georgios Papadopoulos and established an even more repressive military government. But Greek society had watched police and soldiers kill young people who had demanded freedom. The apparent restoration of military control could not erase the political consequences of the uprising. American officials continued to maintain diplomatic and military relations with the new junta, despite their awareness of its repressive character. US diplomatic reporting from 1974 acknowledged the resentment directed at Washington and the widespread Greek belief that American support had contributed to the dictatorship’s survival. The new rulers also maintained their interest in relations with the United States while seeking international support for their rule. The junta finally collapsed in July 1974, after it organized a coup against Cyprus’s president, Archbishop Makarios III, triggering the crisis that culminated in Turkey’s invasion of the island. Democracy returned. But the legacy of the Polytechnic uprising extended far beyond the overthrow of the colonels. The Bases That Never Left The anger against foreign intervention remained a powerful force in Greek politics after the restoration of democracy. In August 1974, even conservative Prime Minister Karamanlis withdrew Greece from NATO’s integrated military command structure in protest over the alliance’s response to Turkey’s invasion of Cyprus. The decision reflected both the government’s security concerns and the extraordinary public anger surrounding the Cyprus crisis. Greece returned to NATO’s military command in 1980. In October 1981, Andreas Papandreou, the son of Georgios Papandreou, led the Panhellenic Socialist Movement (PASOK) to victory. PASOK won 48.07 percent of the vote, the highest share in the party’s electoral history. Its program had promised a fundamental change in Greece’s relationship with the United States, including the removal of American bases and withdrawal from NATO, but neither happened. In 1983, Papandreou’s government signed a new agreement regulating the American military presence. The country remained in NATO, and American facilities continued operating. More than fifty years later, Greece remains an important part of American military strategy. Successive governments have expanded defense cooperation with Washington, including agreements granting American forces access to facilities across the country. Among the most significant is Souda Bay, on the island of Crete, a major logistical and operational hub for the US Navy and Air Force in the eastern Mediterranean. The implications became particularly visible during the American attacks on Iranian nuclear facilities in June 2025. Greek news outlets reported that American KC-135 aerial refueling aircraft operating from Souda Bay supported the mission involving US B-2 bombers. According to these reports, aircraft departing from the base participated in refueling operations over the Mediterranean. In March 2026, the United States, Israel, and Iran were once again locked in military confrontation. Greece’s defense minister, Nikos Dendias, publicly discussed the possibility of attacks on military facilities and the measures the government had taken to protect Souda Bay and other critical infrastructure. The Greek government has stated that Greece is not participating in operations against Iran. But the strategic importance of American military facilities on Greek territory has made questions about their use, security, and accountability increasingly consequential. The Polytechnic Uprising Lives On Every November 17, tens of thousands of Greeks take to the streets to commemorate the Polytechnic uprising. The annual march traditionally ends outside the American embassy. Even the statue of Harry S. Truman in Athens became a target of protest. Over the decades, demonstrators repeatedly attacked it and attempted to bring it down, sometimes succeeding. The monument to the president whose doctrine had initiated America’s postwar intervention in Greece became a recurring symbol of opposition to that intervention. The dictatorship fell in 1974. Parliamentary democracy returned, and governments of different political orientations have held power since then. Yet the country continues to host military facilities that form part of Washington’s wider strategic presence in the Mediterranean and the Middle East. For generations of Greeks, the Polytechnic has represented not only the struggle against military dictatorship but also the demand that the country’s future should be decided by its own people. Fifty-three years after the uprising, the words students painted on the pillars of its gate remain part of an unresolved historical debate. A tank smashed the gate. The demands written on its pillars survived. Question 2 ultimately passed November 3, with 64 percent of voters in favor. Massachusetts Democrats like Congresswoman Ayanna Pressley , Senator Ed Markey , and Boston Mayor Michelle Wu endorsed the measure leading up to the election. When someone accused of a crime can’t afford legal representation, entitled to all per the 6th Amendment, public defenders take the case – serving as the last line of defense between the state and the working class. Reliant on government funding and crushed under the weight of high caseloads, public defenders see little pay in return. Ballot Question 2 allows these 500 public defenders and many social workers, paralegals, interpreters and investigators at CPCS to collectively bargain. Previously, CPCS was one of the few state agencies whose employees could not unionize, as their name was left off the initial law permitting public sector unions in Massachusetts. More than 90 percent of state employees in the Commonwealth have unionized. The Right to a Union Does Not Mean the Union Crucially, the measure doesn’t automatically form a union, but gives workers the right to create one. As an independent entity operating within the state’s judicial branch, CPCS relies on government funding. Once the union and CPCS management agree on a contract, it would be sent to the Governor’s office and, once approved, be funded by the state legislature. The committee that helped get Question 2 on the ballot received most of its over $1 million in funding from the National Association of Government Employees (NAGE) and Service Employees International Union (SEIU) Local 888, who’ve both expressed support for the measure. The committee helped collect more than 86,000 signatures to secure a spot on the ballot. SEIU has worked alongside CPCS employees for decades supporting their efforts. Still in its early stages, the workers plan to organize with SEIU Local 888. “We look forward to partnering and forging a long lasting partnership with CPCS,” said SEIU Local 888 President Tom McKeever in a public statement . “Security, stability and predictability through a collective bargaining agreement for these state employees will create less turn over, retain the most talented and provide the best services to the most vulnerable.” SEIU Local 888 expressed interest in an interview but did not respond in time for publication. Indigent defendants (whose income level is below a certain threshold) are provided representation from CPCS defenders, social workers, and other employees who specialize in advocating for members of underserved communities. The exact requirements can vary by state, but in Massachusetts, one’s income cannot exceed more than 125 percent of the Federal Poverty Line ( $19,950/year or less for a family of one). Part of 1963’s landmark Supreme Court case, Gideon vs. Wainwright, recommended that each state set up their own system to provide legal representation to all under the 6th Amendment. The 6th Amendment Center explains that CPCS is “responsible for funding and administering all adult and juvenile trial-level indigent defense representation in [Massachusetts].” There are two kinds of CPCS attorneys: one is the private counsel division, consisting of private attorneys who subcontract with the state and are paid by the hour (bar advocates). The other is the public defender division, which maintains a full-time staff of attorneys. The CPCS workers employed by the state represent roughly 20 percent of indigent defendants while its bar advocates handle the remaining 80 percent. Since independent contractors can’t unionize , the latter are absent from the ballot. Bar advocates made headlines last year after engaging in a weeks-long work stoppage to demand better wages, refusing to take on new cases while continuing to work on their existing ones. This led to hundreds of cases being dropped as the remaining CPCS employees, already dealing with insanely high caseloads, were unable to shoulder all of the work that had been pushed onto them. If a client is left without representation for more than 45 days, the case is dropped per state protocol. The same goes for those in police custody, who must be released after seven days if they don’t receive representation. Conflict of interest policy plays a significant role in what happened, as it reduces the amount of cases public defenders can take on. For instance, one attorney can’t represent two people whose interests are in conflict with one another, even if their cases are unrelated. CPCS has committed to revising these policies, but doesn’t expect it to do much good since there are still firm ethical guidelines they must adhere to. The numbers just weren’t there to let CPCS absorb every case thrown over to them during the stoppage. The striking bar advocates asked for a raise of $35/hour. Instead, they received two $10/hour wage increases – one last year, and another this August – amounting to $85/hour. This is still far below other states in New England, according to recent data from the Massachusetts Office of Inspector General (OIG). But since independent contractors aren’t employees , they pay for things like benefits and malpractice insurance out-of-pocket. Some bar advocates reported last year that their final takehome pay was really closer to $20/hour . Bar chart showcasing hourly starting pay for bar advocates across states in New England, data courtesy of the Massachusetts Office of Inspector General (Working Mass) The decision to afford public defenders and bar advocates the pay and conditions they deserve will ultimately be in the hands of CPCS management and the legislators on Beacon Hill. Making public defenders state employees does come with one catch – public sector unions in the Bay State can’t go on strike , so by forming a union they forfeit the right exercised by bar advocates. This incident has been cited by critics as proof that passing Question 2 would somehow make things worse, placing the blame on the workers who had to twist the arm of their employer to be paid an increased wage that’s still far below every other state in New England. The stoppage resulted in a wage increase for bar advocates and additional funding for CPCS to hire 320 new public defenders by September 30, 2027. Daniel Werner, a staff public defender with CPCS for more than 14 years and organizer with the “ Yes on 2 ” initiative, told Working Mass that because of that funding, the number of attorneys at his office has “more than doubled” in the last year, from roughly 18 attorneys to 38. “We’ve had around 20 people in this most recent class one month ago, on top of 10 new people recently before that,” said Werner. “So it’s kind of crazy. I remember when the Malden office had eight lawyers, and now it has 38. It’s a huge expansion.” This growth has helped keep caseloads under control, but the rapid hiring rates have introduced their own set of issues. Currently, a lot of time and resources are spent interviewing and training waves of new employees. But without the means to retain these employees through competitive wages and benefits, CPCS has been plagued with high turnover rates, as many attorneys quickly burn out and migrate to jobs with better benefits and pay. Werner says a contract would help remedy this issue by ensuring their wages match those of nearby states and keep up with inflation. “People do this work for a couple years, then they marry and want to have kids, but they feel like they can’t plan their future,” said Werner. “So we do get turnover, and because of that, we have a high supervision ratio and a large training department because we’re constantly cycling people through.” As rewarding as the work may be, fields with more liveable wages in the current conjuncture become increasingly necessary to pursue for lawyers saddled with student loan debt and struggling to stay afloat. A CPCS representative told Working Mass that the current starting salary is $75,000 for a non-barred, recent grad and $80,598 for a barred attorney with no experience. Courtesy of CPCS) Beyond Public Defenders By their own admission , the compensation offered by CPCS is still below the competitive standard that could lower the high rates of turnover. Public defenders at CPCS receive the most attention in the press, but they’re only one part of the story. Social workers in the public defender office like Jeff Whiteside, who’s been there for more than a decade, haven’t benefitted from the waves of new hires at the same rate as public defenders. Whiteside and Werner both spoke to Working Mass on behalf of themselves. All opinions expressed do not speak for CPCS as an agency. “I don’t think it was intentional,” Whiteside told Working Mass . He continued: But the focus was on hiring attorneys, and not necessarily putting in the infrastructure as far as support staff like social workers, investigators, interpreters, in place at the same clip. So it created a very high social work to attorney ratio that was way out of alignment from what the ratios should be. The ideal for an office, Whiteside explained, would be an attorney-to-social worker ratio of 10 to 1. But at the moment, he said it’s closer to 25 attorneys for every social worker. This has spread the limited number of staff who aren’t public defenders thin as they struggle to provide the best possible representation for their clients. Whiteside works according to a “holistic defense model:” going to jails, prisons and courts to ensure the full story of their clients is properly told and they remain humanized throughout their journey through the legal system. Attorneys do not necessarily take that approach consistently: They basically just go buy a police report, which many times in our experience isn’t, you know, necessarily accurate. Social workers also connect clients with social services to assist with mental health and substance issues to meet their needs and develop comprehensive reentry plans. “We’re coming in contact with people at, many times, the most vulnerable and hardest time of their life,” said Whiteside. “More times than not, our clients really appreciate that they have someone in their corner that actually wants to get to know them as a person.” Can a Union Improve Service to the People? A proper union contract isn’t just about the money for CPCS employees. Rather, proper staffing and more room to speak their minds about working conditions would allow them to give their full attention to helping members of the Commonwealth’s most underserved communities. CPCS and the OIG have taken each other to court after an OIG report released in June alleged the public defense system in Massachusetts “is broken, expensive, and is resistant to oversight.” The report named CPCS directly, claiming its attorneys aren’t taking on enough cases compared to nearby states and recommended raising the limit on the number of hours bar advocates can take on per year – relying more on independent contractors (who can’t unionize and earn less for the same work) than the staff attorneys to shoulder caseloads. CPCS countered with a lawsuit of their own and a 439-page response to the OIG’s claims, admitting some aspects need work while committing to improvements. They disputed the OIG’s claim of a broken system, saying it “does not reflect the work of the people who built one of the nation’s most respected public defense systems.” They also allege that the OIG misrepresented their report’s own data, repeatedly pinning the responsibility on CPCS for issues they don’t have control over. The OIG argued that CPCS dropped the ball during last year’s work stoppage, and that its staff attorneys didn’t take on as much work as they could have. Between July and December 2025, the OIG’s report notes that 1,689 cases were dismissed and 213 defendants released without bail; they acknowledge that most of these cases have since been refiled by district attorneys and most defendants redetained. The lack of staff to account for the aforementioned conflict of interest policy barred CPCS from taking on every case, even if they wanted to. Those 320 new hires will give CPCS the numbers to ensure this won’t happen again in the event of another stoppage, but paying these employees decent, competitive wages will keep their numbers steady and decrease turnover. CPCS acknowledges their data keeping practices can be improved, as the lack of data gave the impression that attorneys weren’t taking on as much work as they could, stating that “staff attorneys took on substantially more work once the shortage hit, and how much more they could take on was set by constitutional and ethical limits.” Werner has observed that things like discipline, promotions and personnel decisions are largely based on personal relationships, so more rigorous and transparent data collection could result in more equitable outcomes for employees. “One of the things that the Office of Inspector General report noted is that [CPCS] isn’t really collecting any data,” said Werner. “So it’d make the agency more efficient and make personnel decisions more fair … based on objective criteria rather than personal relationships.” The unionization process moving forward may be “somewhat convoluted,” according to a report from Evan Horowitz at Tufts University’s Center for State Policy Analysis. “Salaries for CPCS lawyers are constrained by a statute requiring pay comparable to that of attorneys in district attorneys’ offices,” wrote Horowitz. “A bargaining agreement would have to work within that rule—or require a change in state law.” But organizers at CPCS are up to the challenge, maintaining the momentum that helped them get Question 2 across the finish line. Organizers like Werner say they don’t intend to waste any time, aiming to “move to a card count pretty quickly.” The workers at CPCS play a critical role in our for-profit legal system. While billionaires in the United States are let off the hook for their crimes with a fine and a slap on the wrist, working class people have found themselves in the crosshairs of the very system supposedly meant to protect them. Public defenders, social workers and the hundreds of others at CPCS see this humanity plain as day and serve as their advocates in a legal system stacked against the most disadvantaged. After fighting for the rights and fair treatment of their clients for decades, the time has come for these workers to do the same for themselves. T he victory of the Student List in Serbia’s parliamentary elections on October 25, 2026 marks the end of fourteen years of domination by Aleksandar Vučić and his Serbian Progressive Party. With approximately 53 percent of the vote and a projected absolute majority in parliament, the Student List achieved a result that until recently had seemed almost unimaginable. The nature of this victory, and Serbia’s political future, are difficult to understand through the conventional categories of government versus opposition, liberalism versus conservatism, or European integration versus national sovereignty. At the center of Serbia’s political transformation was a mass student movement, while behind it lay a conflict between two elites: the political elite built by Vučić’s regime over the previous decade and a half, and an academic elite that had remained relatively independent of his system of rule. The decisive factor was the latter’s ability to forge an alliance with the student movement, which had developed a distinctive form of participatory populism. This alliance made possible the political mobilisation that brought about the change of government. This populist movement did not emerge around demands for a radical transformation of Serbia’s economic or foreign policy. In many areas, it reproduced the existing political consensus, promising that similar policies could be pursued without corruption, organized crime, nepotism, and party based employment. The greatest challenge facing the new government will be to dismantle the previous regime’s clientelist mechanisms while demonstrating whether its relatively popular economic model can be maintained without relying on the same mechanisms of capital accumulation and political control. A one-party system within a multiparty democracy One of the most important features of Vučić’s political domination was the systematic weakening of the multiparty system, even as the formal institutions of parliamentary democracy remained in place. The Serbian Progressive Party sought to incorporate almost every political actor with significant electoral support. Political organizations and their leaders were offered positions within the governing coalition, often through guaranteed places on joint electoral lists, while those who refused to cooperate faced various forms of pressure, marginalisation, and political destruction. The result was an attempt to establish a kind of one-party system within a formally multiparty democracy. Almost every significant ideological position could be found within the governing coalition, from nationalist and conservative to liberal, pro-European, and socially oriented currents. Ideological conflicts were largely transferred from electoral competition to the internal relations of the governing bloc. Elections increasingly became referendums on the exercise of power and attitudes towards Vučić’s regime rather than contests between alternative political programs. The potential redirection of economic development presents a particularly complex challenge. If the new government sought to reduce dependence on real estate development and place greater emphasis on export-oriented production, it would encounter constraints arising from the existing monetary and economic model. Strengthening the competitiveness of domestic agriculture and certain industrial sectors, for example, could raise questions about the real value of the dinar. Depreciation of the domestic currency might improve the price competitiveness of exports, but it would also make imported goods more expensive and undermine purchasing power. Such a move would not automatically solve problems in production and could have serious social consequences. Preserving purchasing power was one of the Student List’s important promises. Its populist base does not expect an economic policy that asks citizens to accept declining living standards in the name of long-term development goals. Similar constraints apply to the emigration of young and educated people. Over the previous two years, the student movement strongly emphasised that young people wanted to remain in Serbia and build their futures there. Yet the emigration of educated workers is not exclusively a consequence of corruption or authoritarian government. Croatia’s experience demonstrates that even EU member states can face large-scale labor emigration, while freedom of movement within the European labor market can make migration to wealthier countries easier. The new government will therefore face a range of problems for which there are no simple technical solutions. Its response to the question of future economic development centers primarily on increasing productivity, one of the most difficult tasks of economic policy, requiring long-term structural change rather than merely replacing political personnel. Will the populist base control the new elite? The most important question following the electoral victory concerns the relationship the new government will establish with the movement that brought it to power. Over the previous two years, student plenums had been the primary source of the movement’s legitimacy. They decided on political demands, forms of mobilisation, and ultimately the move into parliamentary politics. The process of compiling the Student List further demonstrated the movement’s insistence on controlling its representatives. Candidates underwent lengthy procedures to scrutinise their biographies and professional integrity, with the aim of preventing the nomination of individuals whose past could compromise the entire movement. This approach reflected the fundamental political message that power should be entrusted to competent and uncompromised individuals. The entry of approximately 140 Student List representatives into parliament creates an entirely new situation. MPs will now possess formal political legitimacy, access to state institutions, and the ability to participate directly in decision-making. They will no longer be merely delegates of a protest movement, but holders of parliamentary mandates. The question is whether the new government will continue to recognise the authority of student plenums and other participatory forms of organization. The plenums themselves have already weakened considerably: their activity is declining, and fewer students are participating. If they manage to preserve their organizational structures and connections with elected representatives, they could function as a mechanism of social control over the new government, limiting the autonomy of professional administrators and their ability to pursue policies contrary to the expectations of the movement’s electoral base. There is also the possibility that the connection with the student movement will gradually be severed. In that case, the academic elite, which already occupies a prominent position within the Student List, could become the dominant political force in the new government. Its representatives might begin to behave like professional political and managerial elites in Western societies, justifying unpopular measures through appeals to economic necessity, fiscal responsibility, or market demands. The Student List’s MPs hold a range of political convictions. Some are closer to liberal and pro- European positions, others to conservative or nationalist ones, while some candidates are associated with socially oriented policies. As long as the main objective was to remove Vučić’s regime, these differences did not have to become politically decisive. Once the new government faces concrete questions of taxation, privatisation, public spending, labor legislation, and international relations, ideological conflicts may become unavoidable. It is easy to imagine liberal, conservative, nationalist, or left-wing groupings gradually emerging within the parliamentary majority. Such a development would mark the end of the distinctive populist unity that made electoral victory possible and could open the way to neoliberal reforms, austerity policies, or other measures that directly threaten the living standards of sections of the population. Between political normalisation and a new democracy The Student List’s victory demonstrated that even an exceptionally sophisticated party machine can be defeated through broad social mobilisation. The student movement united a large part of the population around demands to end corruption, nepotism, and party control over institutions. Its fundamental promise was one of political normalisation. The immediate consequences of this victory are already visible. From the perspective of Radnički glas (Workers’ Voice), an organization of which I am a member and which works with trade unions in education, research, culture, and other sectors, the change in political relations was felt practically overnight. Just one day after the election, we are already observing changes in the behavior of certain directors and managers, while questions are emerging about the future of numerous proceedings initiated against employees who opposed the government. A military trade union that announced several months ago that it was ceasing its activities because of political pressure has now issued a new statement, suggesting that it may resume its work. Party nepotism was an everyday experience for large numbers of employees. For many people, the Student List’s victory therefore brings a sense of liberation from immediate pressure. The possibility of speaking more freely at work, refusing a political instruction, or planning a professional future without dependence on party loyalty represents a tangible change. This helps explain the mass celebrations following the election. The victory has also opened up the possibility of transforming political representation itself. Here we encounter perhaps the most interesting, and seemingly paradoxical, similarity between the Student List and Vučić’s regime. Both, in different ways, displaced the traditional multiparty system based on competition between ideologically defined parties. Vučić incorporated different political options into his governing coalition, subordinating them to the party apparatus and its hierarchy. The Student List brought together ideologically diverse representatives around a common political objective, but built their legitimacy through mass participation rather than a traditional party organization. The absence of clearly defined party programs need not be a weakness of the new political order. Competition between political parties, in which citizens periodically choose between pre- formulated programs, is not the only possible form of democratic representation. The student movement demonstrated that enormous numbers of people who had never belonged to political parties or previously engaged in political activism could be mobilised. Their participation extended beyond protests to organizing the election campaign, protecting polling stations, and directly monitoring the electoral process. People who had previously been merely voters became active participants in political life. The question is whether this form of participation can be sustained after the electoral victory. Student plenums have already weakened, but the social energy released by the movement need not remain tied exclusively to them. It is particularly significant that many Student List candidates are professionally established individuals with no previous political ambitions. Their willingness to accept parliamentary mandates does not necessarily imply a desire to become professional politicians. Understanding political office as a temporary social responsibility could help establish a different relationship between representatives and those they represent. Trade unions could play an important role in this process. Unlike student plenums, whose activity is linked to the changing composition of the student population, trade unions have more durable organizational structures, direct connections with employees, and concrete demands arising from everyday employment relations. In the political space opening up after the fall of the previous regime, they could establish themselves as one of the organized voices from below. This would also entail a different relationship with parliamentary power. Trade unions would not have to confine themselves to traditional social dialogue, appeals to ministries, and occasional negotiations with government officials. They could establish lasting connections with Student List MPs, following the example of the cooperation they developed with student plenums during the protests, and use these relationships to articulate workers’ demands and exert pressure on the government. Implementing the promises made in the student program, including the expansion of labor rights, could provide a common framework for such action, while unions preserve their independence and their role as the organized voice of workers. Such a relationship could offer a way beyond the limitations of representative parliamentary democracy. Organized social groups could participate directly in shaping political decisions and overseeing their implementation. Parliament could then become a place where the demands of the social base are articulated, rather than an institution in which professional politicians make decisions independently between electoral cycles. The most important possibility opened up by the Student List’s victory lies in the opportunity to free political representation from its exclusive dependence on party competition and to build a more lasting connection between elected representatives and an active, organized society. The student movement has already demonstrated that mass political participation can exist outside traditional political parties. Its next challenge will be to show whether that participation can endure when representatives of the movement constitute the governing parliamentary majority. On the Eastern Mediterranean route, deaths and disappearances also rose, from 284 to 417, despite fewer arrivals in Greece. These figures reveal the limitations of measuring the success of migration policies primarily through declining arrivals. While European governments may present fewer arrivals as evidence of effective border enforcement, the rising number of deaths and disappearances shows that the dangers of maritime migration have not disappeared. Instead, these measures have reshaped migration routes and patterns of movement pushing more people towards smuggling networks and dangerous sea crossings, particularly in areas where search-and-rescue operations are inadequate. Ultimately, without addressing the root causes of why people migrate, people will continue to take whatever physical risks they need to take if it means the possibility of economic survival.

There is only one Global South phrase for the APEC agenda: debt relief

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Vijay Prashad ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-16T21:28:13+00:00

In October, I visited a steel factory in Xinyu, Jiangxi province. There was no dust in the factory. At a desk, a woman wearing an orange hard hat monitored the production of silicon-steel strips. Her attention moved between the machinery and her keyboard. “Keep the belt at the proper speed,” she said. The precision of the process depended on her knowledge as much as on the equipment before her. Here was innovation at work, sustained by the skills of a worker. That scene stayed with me as I considered the forthcoming Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit in Shenzhen. A factory requires more than a machine purchased on the world market, it requires reliable electricity, transportation, technical education, and the accumulated capabilities of an industrial workforce. These foundations take years to build. They depend on sustained investment and on public institutions able to plan beyond the immediate horizon. For countries across the Global South, building this capacity is the substance of development. The leaders gathering in Shenzhen should ask whether the international financial system allows poorer countries to make such investments. How can they train workers and build modern industries when interest payments consume the resources required for schools, energy and infrastructure? Discussions about trade and technology must confront the conditions under which production becomes possible. The promise of shared prosperity will remain unfulfilled unless APEC addresses the destruction produced by war and the suffocation produced by debt. The major countries of the Global South – from Africa, Latin America, and South Asia – are, of course, missing from the APEC participants. But amongst those who will be at the table to represent the Global South are China, Indonesia, Mexico, Papua New Guinea, Peru, and Vietnam. They bring different histories and economic structures to the table, yet they have a shared interest in preserving the capacity of our countries to develop, to strengthen public services, and to cooperate across political differences. Cooperation succeeds when it expands possibilities beyond the region of the Asia-Pacific. The theme of APEC (Building an Asia-Pacific Community to Prosper Together) establishes an important ambition for innovation and cooperation. At Shenzhen, the main agenda ideas will be for paperless trade and building cooperation for digital systems to reduce the cost for business as well as toward a Free Trade Area of the Asia-Pacific. These are worthwhile objectives, particularly when fragmentation of the world economy threatens the productive relationships built across the Pacific. The poorer nations at the table – Indonesia, Papua New Guinea, and the Philippines – will know that talk of digital infrastructure needs public investment, reliable energy, trained workers, and public institutions capable of directing technological change towards social needs. An agenda for connectivity must confront the financial conditions that prevent our societies from connecting on equitable terms. That means insisting on a serious discussion of debt. UN Trade and Development’s latest report , “A World of Debt 2026: Rising Debt Costs and Stalling Development,” describes a financial system increasingly hostile to development. Developing countries’ public debt reached $35 trillion in 2025. Their net interest payments reached $995 billion. In 51 developing countries, home to 3.7 billion people, governments spent more on interest than on either health or education. The injustice becomes clearer when the cost of borrowing is examined. UNCTAD reports average interest rates on public debt of 5.2% for developing countries, compared with 2.2% for developed economies. It estimates that borrowing at comparable rates could save developing countries around $500 billion annually. UNCTAD’s separate 2026 report on external debt sustainability reinforces the warning. Developing countries’ external debt reached $12.3 trillion in 2025, while interest costs on long-term external debt increased by almost 14%. Least developed countries devoted 18.2% of their export earnings to external debt service. Borrowing becomes a treadmill when the income earned through production is continually absorbed by obligations inherited from earlier years. Development plans remain on paper while payments leave the treasury. When interest costs overwhelm public resources because of unequal borrowing terms, the borrowing loses its developmental purposes. Without substantial debt relief for countries whose obligations have become unsustainable, there will be no possibility of the broad advance that the Global South requires. APEC cannot cancel sovereign debt by declaration, but it can raise the issue as a fundamental barrier for its main objectives – such as the importance of a digital landscape. APEC can build political support for action on debt relief and cancellation through the United Nations, the G20, and the development banks (the APEC Finance Ministers’ Process works with the Asian Development Bank, the Inter-American Development Bank, and the World Bank, but it also has project funds to support capacity building and technical cooperation). A credible agenda would begin with timely restructuring and meaningful reductions in unsustainable debt burdens. It would be very useful for APEC to join the debate around debt settlement that helps restore the capacity of poorer countries to develop, with their population’s welfare central to the calculation. In the October 2026, APEC finance ministers’ meeting, they affirmed that financial services should be accessible and affordable, particularly for individuals, households, and small businesses. This commitment to financial inclusion should be extended to the poorer nations, who cannot raise affordable funding for development. The APEC digital infrastructure statement from that meeting talked about the need for multilateral development banks to “ensure that scarce concessional and public resources catalyze, rather than replace, private investment.” But this is not enough. We need to be in discussion about debt settlement and about new finance that is not unfairly expensive. There needs to be an APEC-wide entitlement to concessional development loans or a debt-cancellation mechanism (at least one APEC state, Papua New Guinea is at high risk of debt distress, while two of them, Indonesia and the Philippines, sustain high debt by domestic borrowing with high rates of interest payments as part of their government finances). China can use its convening role to connect these issues. Shenzhen’s productive achievements provide an appropriate setting to discuss the foundations of development: infrastructure, education, industrial capacity, and sustained investment. The most valuable contribution of the summit would be to widen access to those foundations. Cooperation on technology should strengthen productive capabilities across developing economies, supported by financing arrangements that allow them to retain the gains of their own labor. The summit should be judged by whether its commitments enlarge the space for development. A declaration on innovation will mean little to a government forced to cut its education budget. A promise of connectivity will remain distant where interest payments absorb investment funds. The Global South should arrive in Shenzhen with a clear proposition: prosperity requires the resources to build it. That is an agenda worthy of the Pacific and its peoples.
