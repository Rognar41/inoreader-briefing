COINTELPRO: The FBI's Secret War on Black Liberation—and the Injustice That Never Ended

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Sharon Kyle ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-11T19:39:23+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. In 1971, eight ordinary Americans did something extraordinary. They broke into an FBI office in Media, Pennsylvania, and stole government documents exposing a secret campaign against American citizens exercising their constitutional rights. Their actions helped expose what would become one of the most notorious domestic intelligence scandals in American history: COINTELPRO, the FBI's Counterintelligence Program. But exposing government wrongdoing and obtaining justice for its victims are two entirely different things. More than four decades later, I moderated a discussion at Occidental College that brought together people who had exposed the FBI's secret operations and Black activists who had experienced the consequences of government repression firsthand. The panel included Bonnie Raines, one of the eight activists responsible for the historic FBI burglary; Betty Medsger, the former Washington Post journalist who helped bring the stolen documents to public attention; and former Black Panthers Harold Taylor and Hank Jones, whose experiences revealed how the government's pursuit of Black liberation activists continued long after COINTELPRO was officially discontinued. The event, part of the Courage and Resistance Tour, took place in 2015. Their stories raised questions that remain relevant today: What happens when government agencies use their enormous investigative powers to suppress political dissent? Who holds those agencies accountable? And what remedies are available to the people whose lives have been destroyed? Eight Ordinary Americans Take on the FBI On March 8, 1971, millions of Americans were watching Muhammad Ali fight Joe Frazier in what had been promoted as the Fight of the Century. In Media, Pennsylvania, eight antiwar activists were carrying out a very different kind of confrontation. Calling themselves the Citizens' Commission to Investigate the FBI, they entered a small FBI office and removed approximately 1,000 documents. They weren't looking for money. They were looking for evidence. For years, organizers opposing the Vietnam War suspected that the government was infiltrating their organizations, monitoring their activities, and working to undermine their effectiveness. They believed the FBI was violating the constitutional rights of Americans who were doing little more than challenging their government's policies. But they couldn't prove it. Bonnie Raines, then 29 and the mother of three young children, was among the eight. During our discussion, she explained how the group moved from conventional protest to what she described as nonviolent disruption. They had marched, circulated petitions, attended demonstrations, and traveled to Washington. Nevertheless, the Vietnam War continued, and the government's attempts to suppress opposition appeared to intensify. Their group decided that exposing FBI misconduct required something more. Bonnie visited the FBI office while pretending to be a college student interested in employment opportunities for women. Her assignment was to determine whether the office had security measures that would prevent the burglary. It did not. The activists chose the night of the Ali-Frazier fight, believing that the event would distract police and residents of the building. They escaped without detection. The FBI launched an extensive investigation but never apprehended the burglars. Their identities remained secret for more than four decades. A Journalist Receives the Evidence On March 23, 1971, Betty Medsger arrived at her Washington Post office and discovered an unusual envelope among her correspondence. It contained copies of documents from the burglarized FBI office. One of the records instructed FBI agents to create an atmosphere that would "enhance the paranoia" among political activists, encouraging them to believe government agents might be watching everywhere. Other documents described informants planted in political organizations, surveillance of college campuses, and extensive monitoring of Black communities. The documents had also been sent to other journalists and two members of Congress. But those recipients did not immediately publish the revelations. The congressional recipients turned the materials over to the FBI. At the Los Angeles Times, the documents were intercepted before reaching the intended reporter. The New York Times initially declined to publish its copy. Medsger and The Washington Post took a different approach. Despite pressure from federal officials, the newspaper published the findings, exposing practices that the FBI had concealed from the American people. The disclosures eventually helped expose COINTELPRO and contributed to congressional investigations into abuses by the nation's intelligence agencies. Medsger later documented the complete story in her book, The Burglary: The Discovery of J. Edgar Hoover's Secret FBI . What she discovered was not simply an intelligence agency collecting information. It was an agency actively interfering with the political activities of people whose views its director opposed. When Being Black Was Enough to Attract FBI Surveillance One of the most disturbing revelations concerned the surveillance of African Americans. During our panel discussion, Medsger explained that documents from the FBI office revealed surveillance directed at Black students at Swarthmore College. These students were not necessarily suspected of committing crimes. Their racial identity was sufficient to attract government attention. Other FBI records described informant networks monitoring Black communities in churches, libraries, restaurants, schools, and neighborhood gathering places. The Bureau was treating ordinary Black life as a potential security concern. That distinction matters because racial profiling is not merely an unfortunate consequence of aggressive law enforcement. It is a mechanism through which government power can reinforce an existing racial hierarchy. When an entire population is treated as inherently suspicious, the constitutional promise of equal protection becomes profoundly unequal in practice. And the FBI's campaign against the Black Panther Party demonstrated just how destructive that inequality could become. COINTELPRO and the Destruction of Black Political Organizing COINTELPRO began in 1956, initially targeting the Communist Party. Its operations eventually expanded to include other political organizations, civil rights activists, antiwar groups, and Black liberation movements. The Black Panther Party became a major target in 1967. The FBI used informants, anonymous letters, disinformation, infiltration, and efforts to provoke internal conflict. The objective was not simply to gather intelligence about possible criminal activity. It was also to disrupt organizations and undermine their political influence. Martin Luther King Jr. was subjected to extensive FBI surveillance and attempts to discredit him. The Black Panthers faced an especially aggressive campaign. Government efforts to portray the Panthers primarily as violent extremists obscured their extensive community organizing. The party established free breakfast programs for children, health initiatives, political education programs, and other community services. These activities challenged public institutions that had failed to meet the needs of Black communities. Harold Taylor explained why he had joined the Black Panther Party as a young man in South Los Angeles. He wanted to defend his community and help improve the conditions under which its members lived. His commitment was not to violence for its own sake, but to community protection and self-determination. That commitment placed him in the path of government repression. Harold Taylor: Torture and Coerced Confessions During our interview, Taylor described an encounter with Los Angeles police in the early 1970s that ended with officers firing repeatedly into the car in which he was riding. He was shot multiple times. Taylor subsequently faced criminal charges arising from the confrontation. He told our audience that a jury ultimately found he had acted in self-defense. But his ordeal did not end there. After leaving California, Taylor was arrested in New Orleans, where he described being subjected to severe physical and psychological abuse during police interrogations. He recounted beatings, sleep deprivation, denial of food and water, suffocation with plastic bags, electric shocks, and simulated drowning. The purpose, he said, was to force statements implicating other Black Panthers in criminal activity. Taylor described holding out for days before finally agreeing to make statements dictated by his interrogators. He also recounted being brought before a judge, only to be returned to the custody of officers he accused of torturing him. His account was horrifying. But equally disturbing was the role those statements would play in later efforts to prosecute former Black Panthers. The case involved the 1971 killing of San Francisco police officer John V. Young during an attack on the Ingleside Police Station. Statements obtained during the New Orleans interrogations became part of the investigation. The defendants and their supporters maintained that those statements were the product of torture and could not be trusted. The allegations of coercion helped undermine the earlier prosecutions. Yet, decades later, the same historical case would return. The San Francisco 8: When the Government Reopened the Past Following the terrorist attacks of September 11, 2001, the federal government substantially expanded its national-security apparatus. Congress enacted the USA PATRIOT Act. The Department of Homeland Security was created. Federal and local law enforcement agencies increased cooperation through counterterrorism investigations. Within this atmosphere, authorities revisited investigations involving former Black Panthers. In 2003, investigators approached Hank Jones at his home. Jones told our audience that the officers questioned him about his former political associations, including the Black Panther Party and the Weather Underground. One investigator, Jones recalled, remarked on his home and warned that he could lose everything if he failed to cooperate. Jones described the encounter as an unmistakable threat. Investigators eventually obtained legal process compelling him to appear before a grand jury. The renewed investigation would become known as the San Francisco 8 case. In January 2007, California authorities brought charges against eight men in connection with the 1971 killing of Officer Young. They were Herman Bell, Jalil Muntaqim, Harold Taylor, Hank Jones, Ray Boudreaux, Richard Brown, Richard O'Neal, and Francisco Torres. Several had been under investigation decades earlier, and the renewed case raised questions about evidence associated with the original New Orleans interrogations. Defense supporters argued that the prosecution represented a continuation of the government's historical campaign against Black liberation organizations. Prosecutors maintained that they were seeking accountability for an unsolved police killing. But the defendants faced an enormous personal burden. By 2007, many were older men who had spent decades raising families, working, and contributing to their communities. Now they faced serious criminal charges carrying the possibility of spending the remainder of their lives in prison. Their families faced renewed uncertainty. The men endured repeated court appearances, restrictions on their lives, and years of defending themselves against accusations arising from events nearly four decades earlier. Legal representation became a major undertaking involving defense attorneys, investigators, expert witnesses, and a nationwide network of supporters. Community organizations mobilized to raise awareness and provide assistance. The financial burden extended beyond courtroom expenses. The proceedings disrupted ordinary employment, family life, travel, and the stability the defendants had built over decades. And the case did not simply end with everyone being acquitted. In 2008, prosecutors dismissed the charges against Richard O'Neal. The remaining defendants became known as the San Francisco 7. In 2009, Herman Bell entered a guilty plea to a reduced charge of voluntary manslaughter, while Jalil Muntaqim entered a no-contest plea to a reduced conspiracy charge. Neither received additional prison time from the negotiated dispositions. As part of the broader resolution, prosecutors dismissed charges against Hank Jones, Harold Taylor, Ray Boudreaux, and Richard Brown, acknowledging that they lacked sufficient evidence to proceed. Francisco Torres continued fighting the remaining charges until August 2011, when his case was dismissed. For Jones and Taylor, the dismissals ended the immediate threat of conviction. But what did they restore? They did not return the years spent defending against prosecution. They did not compensate the men for the disruption of their lives or the psychological strain on their families. And they did not resolve the larger question of accountability for the original allegations of torture. The San Francisco 8 case demonstrated that legal proceedings can end without repairing the damage they have caused. The dismissal of a prosecution is not the same as restoration of the life disrupted by that prosecution. The Remedies That Never Came The exposure of COINTELPRO produced important institutional changes. Congress investigated intelligence abuses through the Church Committee. New guidelines attempted to restrict domestic intelligence operations. Oversight mechanisms were strengthened. But what happened to the people whose rights had already been violated? Some victims obtained financial settlements through civil litigation. The families and survivors of Black Panther leaders Fred Hampton and Mark Clark, killed during a 1969 Chicago police raid, reached a $1.85 million settlement in 1983. Geronimo ji Jaga Pratt, a former Black Panther who spent approximately 25 years imprisoned following a murder conviction that was eventually overturned, obtained a $4.5 million settlement in 2000. Other victims secured the dismissal of charges, access to FBI records, or favorable civil judgments. But the government never established a comprehensive national reparations process for COINTELPRO victims. There was no systematic effort to identify everyone targeted, determine the harm they suffered, compensate their losses, or review every criminal conviction potentially affected by illegal government conduct. Nor was there a comprehensive criminal reckoning for officials who directed or participated in COINTELPRO's abuses. This distinction is central to understanding the limitations of American institutional reform. Government agencies may revise their policies, adopt new procedures, and promise improved oversight without taking responsibility for the damage already inflicted. Institutional reform is not the same as justice for victims. And reform without accountability can leave the structures that permitted abuse substantially intact. The Surveillance State Did Not Disappear The 2015 Occidental College discussion opened with an ACLU presentation concerning contemporary law enforcement surveillance. The ACLU described local agencies acquiring drones, automated license plate readers, and cell-site simulators, sometimes without meaningful public debate or adequate oversight. Its representative warned that surveillance technologies purchased for narrowly defined purposes could later be used much more broadly. The organization advocated ordinances requiring public disclosure, approval by elected officials, clearly defined policies, and mechanisms for accountability. The technology available to government agencies today bears little resemblance to the paper files and telephone informants of Hoover's era. Modern surveillance can collect, aggregate, and analyze enormous quantities of information. But the fundamental constitutional questions remain. Who decides which people or communities will be monitored? What evidence is required before surveillance begins? Who supervises the agencies conducting it? What remedies exist when surveillance becomes a tool of political repression? And who pays the price when government officials abuse their authority? These are not merely questions about technology. They are questions about the distribution of power in a democracy. Exposure Is Not Justice One of the most powerful aspects of our discussion was the contrast between the experiences of those who exposed FBI misconduct and the Black activists who experienced some of its harshest consequences. Bonnie Raines and her fellow activists risked imprisonment to obtain evidence of government wrongdoing. Their courage deserves recognition. But the experiences of Harold Taylor and Hank Jones demonstrate that Black activists challenging entrenched power faced consequences extending far beyond surveillance. For some, those consequences included violence, torture, wrongful imprisonment, damaged relationships, and decades of uncertainty. Even after COINTELPRO became a national scandal, the men who would become the San Francisco 8 found themselves once again confronting the machinery of criminal prosecution. The government had changed its rules. The people who had been targeted were still living with the consequences. That contrast raises a profound question about the meaning of democracy. A constitutional guarantee is meaningful only to the extent that people can exercise it without facing unlawful retaliation. And government accountability is meaningful only when those responsible for abuses face consequences and the people harmed receive genuine remedies. Near the conclusion of our discussion, the panelists urged the audience to organize, question authority, support political prisoners, and demand more meaningful oversight. Their message was not that resistance is easy or that victories are inevitable. It was that public pressure, collective action, and independent journalism can expose wrongdoing that powerful institutions would otherwise conceal. The 1971 FBI burglary demonstrated that ordinary citizens could challenge one of the most powerful law enforcement agencies in the country. The experiences of Taylor and Jones demonstrated that exposing injustice is not enough. Until the people harmed by government repression receive meaningful remedies—and the institutions responsible are held accountable—the struggle for justice remains unfinished.

The Kural is an Indian progressive digital platform and publication that recently published a comprehensive, three-part interview with Peter McLaren, a renowned Canadian-American scholar and a leading architect of critical pedagogy. Conducted by doctoral researcher Naila Alavi, the feature interview—headlined "Who Gets to Imagine the Future?"—delves into the current state of education and social theory. Foreword Naila Alavi I first encountered Peter McLaren in the first semester of my master’s, not in person, of course, but in the way one first encounters many of the people who eventually become important to one’s intellectual life. I met him on a reading list. At the time, I was beginning to think more seriously about classrooms and the politics that inhabit them. Over the years, as my own questions moved towards education, childhood, emotion, and the ways in which institutions participate in shaping how we understand ourselves and the worlds around us, I kept finding my way back to critical pedagogy and Peter’s work. Some scholars give you answers. Others alter the questions you know how to ask. My relationship with Peter’s scholarship has been closer to the latter. Years later, The Kural brought those questions into an entirely different kind of space. The Kural began with a group of us wanting to make something together. We had spent years inside universities, writing papers, dissertations, assignments, and research that often travelled only as far as another classroom, another evaluator, or another academic conversation. We began wondering what might happen if some of that thinking were allowed another life. We wanted a space in which research could be reworked without being emptied of its complexity, where people could write seriously without always having to write academically, and where a photograph, an interview, an essay, a keyword, or an unfinished reflection could all become ways of thinking in public. There was no grand institution behind us when we began. The Kural was, and remains, something built collectively by people who believed there was value in creating such a space and were willing to do the often-invisible work required to sustain it. We have argued over words and layouts, returned drafts to one another, worried about accessibility and rigour, thought about whose voices we were making room for, and repeatedly asked ourselves what it means to take an idea produced somewhere and help it reach somewhere else. Somewhere along the way, a phrase became important to how we understood what we were trying to do. “We wanted to make thought travel”. An interview with Peter McLaren emerged naturally from those conversations. When we began thinking about whom The Kural should speak with, we wanted a conversation that would speak to the questions from which the platform itself had emerged. What happens to critical thought when the spaces that sustain it come under pressure? Why do classrooms, universities, books, curricula, histories, and even particular words become objects of such intense political struggle? What exactly is at stake when power becomes invested in determining not only what people know, but what they remember, what they fear, and what they are permitted to imagine? Peter seemed, to us, an especially important person with whom to think through these questions. I began the interview with education. I thought we were going to talk about classrooms, universities, curricula, and the contemporary assault on academic spaces. We did. But Peter kept taking the conversation somewhere larger. A question about why power fears classrooms became a conversation about memory and historical agency. A question about children opened onto the political struggle over who is permitted to shape the future. When we moved to the language of humiliation, resentment, national injury, and moral panic, Peter brought political economy into conversation with affect and showed how structures of domination must also acquire an emotional life. From there, the conversation moved through attention and distraction, through imagination and the possibility of alternatives, until we eventually arrived at algorithms, digital infrastructures, surveillance, affective publics, and what he describes as post-digital fascism. Reading his responses as they arrived, I began to realize that the conversation had acquired a movement of its own. This is why we eventually published it in three parts in The Kural . The sections followed that movement. We began with the politics of education and the question of who gets to imagine the future. We then moved into the politics of feeling, attention, and imagination. Finally, we arrived at a question that seemed to gather much of what had preceded it. Where does critical pedagogy live now? Peter’s answer to that final question is something I often return to. The classroom, he tells us, remains important, but critical pedagogy cannot remain there. It has to find life in reading groups and community organizations, in independent media and digital commons, in cultural movements, artistic collaborations, and the many other places where people come together to learn, question, remember, and imagine. He reminds us that learning to read the world critically today also means learning to read the algorithms, platforms, and infrastructures through which so much of that world now reaches us. There was something moving about arriving there because, on a vastly smaller scale, it brought us back to the impulse with which The Kural had begun. We, too, had been wondering where thought lives once it leaves the classroom. We too wanted to believe that serious intellectual work could have a public life, that thinking could happen across forms and outside established academic spaces, and that making room for such conversations was itself worthwhile. There is another reason this conversation means a great deal to us. The Kural was new. We were still learning how to become the thing we had imagined. Peter could very easily have treated an invitation from a young, independent platform as one more request in an already crowded academic life. Instead, he gave us his time. More than that, he gave the questions his attention. His responses were long, patient, generous, and serious. He never made us feel that the size of the platform determined the seriousness with which its questions deserved to be received. For that, The Kural is deeply grateful to Peter McLaren. We are grateful not only for the interview he gave us, but for the trust with which he allowed a young platform to carry his words. We are grateful for the generosity with which he stayed with our questions and for the expansiveness with which he answered them. And I am personally grateful for the strange and lovely distance travelled between encountering Peter McLaren as a name on a reading list and finding myself, years later, asking him some of the questions that his own scholarship had helped me learn how to ask. The conversation was first given a home by The Kural . Its appearance in LA Progressive now gives it another life and another readership. I find something fitting in that. A conversation about the places in which critical thought can live has itself refused to remain in one place. When we began The Kural , we wanted to make thought travel. Peter helped us do precisely that. And now, very generously, he is helping it travel further. Related: Elbows Up! Canada, Trump, and the Politics of Democratic Friendship Part I - Who Gets to Imagine the Future? PART ONE WHO GETS TO IMAGINE THE FUTURE ? 1. Across the world, authoritarian politics increasingly begins its cultural project by attacking universities, rewriting textbooks, banning words, policing teachers, and dismantling spaces where people gather to think critically. Why does power fear classrooms so deeply? What is it about collective thinking itself that becomes dangerous to right-wing projects? McLaren: When my name appeared on Professor Watchlist, a project created by Turning Point USA under the leadership of the recently assassinated Charlie Kirk, I was reminded of a lesson that authoritarian movements have understood for centuries: if you want to control a society, you must first shape how it remembers, how it thinks, and how it learns. Kirk was one of the most influential figures within the contemporary American right, a close ally of Donald Trump, and a leading advocate of a form of Christian nationalism that sought to fuse religious identity with an increasingly authoritarian political vision. His organization devoted considerable energy to targeting educators, students, and academic institutions deemed insufficiently loyal to conservative values. The purpose of Professor Watchlist was ostensibly to expose professors accused of promoting “radical” ideas. In reality, it functioned as part of a broader campaign to intimidate critical educators and portray intellectual inquiry itself as a threat. I was hardly alone. Hundreds of professors found themselves publicly targeted. Yet the existence of such a project raises an important question: if classrooms are politically insignificant, why devote so much effort to policing them? What authoritarian movements fear is not simply knowledge but the possibility that ordinary people might come to recognize themselves as makers of history rather than objects of history. The contemporary attack on universities, teachers, libraries, curricula, and public intellectuals is not accidental. Throughout history, authoritarian movements have understood something that liberal democracies often forget: education is never merely about the transmission of knowledge. It is fundamentally a struggle over consciousness itself. Power fears classrooms because classrooms contain the possibility that people might begin to read the world critically rather than simply adapt to it. Authoritarian projects depend upon naturalizing existing social arrangements. They seek to convince people that hierarchy is inevitable, that inequality is deserved, that nationalism is synonymous with patriotism, and that the present order represents the only realistic future. Critical education disrupts these assumptions. It opens spaces where students can ask dangerous questions: Who benefits from existing arrangements? Whose voices have been excluded from history? Why do certain forms of suffering persist? What alternatives might be possible? The issue is not simply knowledge but collective knowledge. A solitary individual may question authority in private, but when people gather to analyze their experiences, compare their histories, and imagine alternatives, criticism can become a social force. Authoritarian movements recognize that classrooms, universities, and cultural institutions can function as laboratories of democratic possibility. They are among the few public spaces where people learn to connect personal troubles with larger structures of power and to recognize themselves as historical agents rather than passive spectators. This is why attacks on education are so often accompanied by efforts to rewrite textbooks, ban books, police language, censor teachers, and manufacture historical amnesia. Whether in the United States, India, Brazil, Hungary, Turkey, or elsewhere, the pattern is remarkably familiar. The path toward authoritarian rule is frequently paved by efforts to subordinate education to nationalist mythology, cultural conformity, and ideological loyalty. Such measures are designed not merely to suppress information but to regulate memory itself. Memory is dangerous because it reminds us that societies have changed before and therefore can change again. Historical consciousness undermines the authoritarian fantasy that the existing order is permanent, natural, or sacred. What right-wing authoritarian projects fear most is not disagreement but the emergence of democratic publics capable of critical reflection. They fear forms of collective thinking that expose the contradictions of power, reveal the humanity of those who have been marginalized, and cultivate the capacity to imagine a world different from the one we have inherited. At its best, education teaches people that reality is not a fate to which we must submit but a field of struggle in which we can intervene. The deepest threat that critical education poses to authoritarianism is that it transforms people from objects of history into subjects of history. It teaches them that the world they inhabit was made by human beings and can therefore be remade by human beings. Every authoritarian project seeks to close the future by declaring that there is no alternative. Every critical classroom reopens the future by reminding us that history remains unfinished. For this reason, the classroom remains one of the most important political spaces in society. It is one of the few places where critical dialogue can challenge manufactured ignorance, where hope can confront cynicism, and where people can learn that democracy is not merely a method of governance but a way of living together. Authoritarianism begins by attacking the institutions that teach people how to think. Critical pedagogy begins by helping people discover that the future is still theirs to create. Authoritarian movements seek to organize fear, resentment, nostalgia, humiliation, and grievance into political forces capable of sustaining existing hierarchies. Whoever shapes how people feel about the past, the present, and the future exercises enormous influence over political life itself. The battle is not only over institutions or resources. Paul Loeb’s The Impossible Will Take a Little While: Perseverance and Hope in Troubled Times , originally published two decades ago and substantially revised for a new edition, begins with a question that is especially difficult in such a moment: how do people keep acting when they have no guarantee that their efforts will succeed? Across these accounts, Loeb returns to the ways that movements develop the capacity to act. The dramatic moments that look historic in retrospect are often preceded by years of relationship-building, political education, preparation, experimentation, and organizing. But it also understands that history will only change through our actions. Havel’s own story exemplifies this. He and his compatriots circulated a petition to free a group of political prisoners jailed by the Communist dictatorship in Czechoslovakia. People assumed that there was nothing they could do. Even many critics of the regime, like novelist Milan Kundera, mocked Havel’s efforts. Yet when the prisoners got out, they said that the solidarity of those who acted on their behalf allowed them to keep going. A few years after this campaign, the dictatorship fell, and Havel ended up being elected the country’s president. So it’s tempting, particularly in a context of attacks on justice like the present, to succumb to despair or resign ourselves to simply hoping that things will change. You write about Rosa Parks, for example, in the context of the years of organizing, political education, and relationships that preceded the Montgomery bus boycott. And what does it mean for a publishing industry that, with few exceptions, no longer publishes novels — literary fiction, in particular — remotely like it? This is, indeed, a strange time for people who care about books. There is no shortage of reports and opinion pieces , backed up by enough data, to show that America has a literary crisis and technology is eating away at our attention spans. Books still sell — the publishing industry is much more resilient than it looks — and physical bookstores proliferate, but it’s inarguable the literary novel matters much less to mass culture than it once did. Mostly gone, too, are novels like Lonesome Dove : lengthy, blood-drenched, and male -coded. Women, as TikTok has shown, can enjoy Lonesome Dove as much as men, despite a vicious gang rape later in the novel, no doubt thanks to its sharply written female characters like Lorena Wood, Clara Allen, and Elmira Johnson. The Disappearing Male Reader — and Novelist Of late, there has been much discourse about men and publishing, men and literary fiction. Are there still male readers? Is literary fiction just for women now? Is the swaggering male novelist of yore extinct for good? If so, is this good? Thornier questions arise, too, over race and sexuality. What of the white straight man? Jacob Savage, in a controversial essay for Compact , argued that white heterosexual men had been systematically driven out of the worlds of the arts, denied the prestige that they once accrued. Regardless of whether Savage is correct, there are strong data points that show men, overall, really are reading less than women — fiction especially. All of this hangs over the curious Lonesome Dove resurgence. Set in the 1870s, Lonesome Dove is about two men, Augustus McCrae and Woodrow Call, who are cattle rustlers and former Texas Rangers. Their rustling operation includes Josh Deets, an expert tracker who is black, and Pea Eye Parker. Jake Spoon, another former Texas Ranger, is on the run for accidentally killing a dentist in Arkansas; he convinces his old buddies there’s big money to be made if they can drive a herd of cattle from Texas to Montana. The novel itself, argues the writer Naomi Kanakia, is not like other mythic westerns, because the supposed good guys can’t really protect the people in their care, including Lorena Wood, a prostitute hoping to get to San Francisco. Many characters meet brutal and bloody ends. And the novel is a slow burn, with a couple hundred pages of comical ruminating and set building before the violence commences. Many contemporary editors would have cut it down drastically. “What’s interesting about Gus and Woodrow is they’re basically existential heroes. Their task — running this cattle drive — is absurd,” Kanakia writes. “This book is definitely not a work of realism. The main characters are certified badasses, able to kill many times their number of Indians and bandits guys. Even minor characters are often able to undertake mythic feats, like Pea Eye, who at one point walks a hundred miles, naked, to bring help to Gus.” The Death of the “Dad Novel” There are a few different ways, both sociologically and economically, to understand a novel like Lonesome Dove . Once, there were many more books like it, blends of the high literary and commercial that could be read, almost, as middlebrow — or midcult , as the famed critic Dwight Macdonald once derisively put it. Many of them were adapted into famous movies. Herman Wouk’s extremely popular, Pulitzer Prize–winning The Caine Mutiny might have fit the bill, as might the works of James Michener. “Dad” fiction, perhaps — books aimed at a certain kind of middle-class, aging male with a bit of disposable income and interest in losing himself in a novel for a few days or weeks. If novels like these are not totally extinct, they are not nearly as prevalent as they once were. Nor are popular new works that might have had a largely male audience in mind, like 2020s versions of American Psycho — a publishing sensation upon its release in 1991 — and Fight Club . Fittingly, both were adapted into now-iconic films. If men still sit at the top of today’s publishing conglomerates, it’s college-educated women writing, editing, and agenting most of the novels of note. It’s these same women filling book clubs, going to book events, and entering master of fine arts (MFA) programs. Men are plentiful among the ranks of the most celebrated contemporary novelists, but few of them are under the age of forty. The buzzy twenty- and thirtysomething writers — from the widely known (Sally Rooney) to the more “book” famous (Emma Cline) to the beneficiaries of media hype cycles (Honor Levy) — are almost all female. Ultimately, novels exist in a marketplace, and women are simply reading more than men. They graduate college at higher rates, and it is in college where a person is usually exposed to literature at a greater and more lasting depth. Why should a publisher take a risk on a book like Lonesome Dove when its market research tells it the audience for it might not even exist? Factor in the historical misogyny of the literary world, and a publisher can both feel virtuous and fulfill a capitalist imperative. Joyce Carol Oates once claimed on X, to great consternation, that “a friend who is a literary agent told me that he cannot even get editors to read first novels by young white male writers, no matter how good.” It’s the sort of statement that is hard to prove — and hard to dis prove. One might say, as a rejoinder, the lack of vaunted young male writers, the neo–David Foster Wallaces or even Larry McMurtrys, is evidence of a talent gap, a group not showing up like it used to. Or it could be something structural — a publishing industry, and even the broader public, losing interest in a sort of person and perspective. A more challenging question can be asked: If more books were published by men with explicitly masculine themes, would there actually be a wider male readership? It’s the old chicken-and-egg dilemma. The audience won’t emerge because the product isn’t there, and the product isn’t there because the audience is elsewhere. Men, obviously, can read and enjoy books by women, but there is the sticky biological reality that, on balance, women will probably prefer reading women, and men may prefer reading men. Boys, especially, seem to long for male teachers and role models in the classroom. As they age, and if they want to read, they might be drawn to the fiction of writers like Ernest Hemingway, Raymond Carver, and McMurtry. They might want literature that, at the bare minimum, seems masculine. The movement of literature back into the feminine realm has some historical precedent. In the nineteenth century, as Ronnie Grinberg details in Write Like a Man: Jewish Masculinity and the New York Intellectuals , “ladies were the main audience of literature,” with Jane Austen, George Eliot, Elizabeth Barrett Browning, the Brontës, and Harriet Beecher Stowe hailed as the leading practitioners of the novel, which was a form of common entertainment. Women were, at the same time, taking over the teaching profession. Men taught at the university level, but the vast majority of working men and women did not attend college. While women would remain in the teaching profession, the novel, according to Grinberg, shed some of its feminine associations in the twentieth century. Hemingway and the Lost Generation masculinized, along with a rising urban intelligentsia, the conception of the novelist. Suddenly, writing could be synonymous with a kind of machismo. There were women, too, who came to believe this. “It is not suggested that muscles write books, but there is a certain sense in which talent and experience being equal, they may be considered a bit of an advantage,” the literary critic and novelist Elizabeth Hardwick once said. “Of course the best literature by women is superior to most of the work done by men,” she added, yet “it is only the whimsical, cantankerous, the eccentric critic, or those who refuse the occasion for such distinctions, who would say that any literary work by a woman, marvelous as these may be, is on a level with the very greatest accomplishments of men.” Gambling, Porn, and Doomscrolling These sentiments would today rightly be considered retrograde. What Hardwick, who lived through much of the twentieth century and died in 2007, probably could not have foreseen was the decline of the male reader in the literary fiction space — and how that might impact what books end up in print. There’s evidence to suggest that the technological distractions of our age have corroded young men more than young women. Gambling and porn addictions, facilitated by smartphones, are overwhelmingly male vices. And if there’s dead time that might have been filled by a thick paperback, it now belongs to the phone, the infinite scroll. Other entertainments beckon, too: podcasts, immersive video games, and the betting sites Kalshi and Polymarket, which collectively gobble up idle hours. If men are reading, they are probably headed to nonfiction: books about history, finance, or politics. This market, however, is shrinking. “What has happened in the last ten years is that the overall industry has grown modestly, but fiction and nonfiction have basically flipped positions,” Sean deLone, an editor at Atria, wrote in a recent Substack piece. “In 2015, 6 of the top 25 bestselling hardcover books — this is a not-perfect but useful proxy for new books published — were fiction. In 2025, 18 of the top 25 hardcover books were fiction, meaning only 7 were nonfiction, almost an exact inversion of ten years ago.” This fiction-nonfiction inversion — the rise of one market at the probable expense of another — can likely be blamed on the collapse of male readership. “Yes, the nonfiction collapse is at its root connected to the continual, ever-devolving crisis of men. Proportionally we are gaining many more women as readers and losing our few men,” deLone argued. It would be an exaggeration to say that there are no longer any popular literary novels by men that appeal, specifically, to male sensibilities, or even to the darker side of masculinity. Europe has stars like Michel Houellebecq and David Szalay, whose Flesh won the Booker Prize last year. Tony Tulathimutte’s 2024 short story collection, Rejection , which tackles inceldom and lurid gay male sex fantasies, won critical acclaim and sold well. There very well could be more novels like these in the near future. Publishing is a trends-based industry, and the success of Rejection , at the minimum, has probably spurred on at least a few editors at large houses to acquire similar works. Will the Publishing Industry Learn From Lonesome Dove ’s Resurrection? More intriguing, perhaps, will be what the publishing world makes of Lonesome Dove ’s TikTok popularity. How much notice will it take? Many of the videos praising the novel are produced by women. This blood-soaked western has found a female audience. Ideally, this would motivate publishers to acquire more novels like Lonesome Dove in the future. New fiction tends to be much shorter — longer books cost more to print — and there’s a feeling among publishers that readers will shy away from thick books in this distractible age. Lonesome Dove ’s online success could upend that thinking or force publishers, at least, to take more risks. A young writer, a male even, working in the vein of McMurtry needn’t see his many query emails go unreturned. It’s not up to book publishing, of course, to fix men, but it wouldn’t hurt if more boys and young men, too screen-obsessed these days, wanted to read and then become writers. A novelist, like anyone else, can be toxic, but the acts of reading and writing are the very opposite — they are cleansing and enlightening and do wonders for the soul. Let the men, in deep contemplation, dream of rustling cattle all the way up to Montana. How Much Liberty Should We Limit?

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Sarah Conly ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-11T13:20:59+00:00

In the political culture of the United States, individual liberty is frequently thought of as possessing intrinsic and overriding importance. The political right and the political left in the United States have long competed to be seen as the true champions of individual freedom, and each has tried to advance its own goals under the banner of liberty. For the Right in recent decades, individual liberty has often meant the right of corporations and the wealthy to do whatever they want with their property, unconstrained by government regulation or obligations to pay taxes or collectively bargain with their workers. The Left, at its best, has stood up against state as well as employer tyranny and for the kind of economic security that enables people to live truly autonomous lives. These questions of economic coercion and exploitation aside, greater individual liberty is clearly not always an unalloyed good. The consumer bounty of twenty-first-century America has included the invasion of smartphones, social media, and live streamed audiovisual content into more and more corners of our lives — with evidence mounting that these developments are causing significant harm to our well-being and cognitive capacities . The rise of sports betting apps, which allow users to gamble on their phones from anywhere at any time of day, is a scourge on young men’s finances and their mental health. Meanwhile, merchants of offline vices like kratom, a drug linked to addiction, overdose, and liver disease, are getting free rein from the Trump administration. The corporate purveyors of addictive social media platforms, gambling apps, and dangerous supplements would no doubt argue that they and their customers should have the freedom to contract however they see fit. But we can reasonably ask in these cases and others whether imposing some restrictions on individual choice might not be to the benefit of consumers, or to society more broadly. Sarah Conly is an emerita professor of philosophy at Bowdoin College and the author of The Limits of Liberty , published by Oxford University Press last year, as well as One Child: Do We Have a Right to More ? and Against Autonomy: Justifying Coercive Paternalism . She argues that modern philosophy and American popular culture alike have misconstrued the value of autonomy. On Conly’s view, more restrictions on individual liberty are warranted than has generally been appreciated — ranging from bans on cigarettes and restrictions on restaurant portion sizes, to limitations on how much meat one can consume, to prohibiting promulgation of “dangerous falsehoods.” Jacobin editor Nick French recently interviewed Conly to discuss her argument that autonomy is only of instrumental value, her views on when individual freedom might be limited on paternalistic grounds, and the potential dangers of endorsing greater restrictions on liberty. Nick French The Limits of Liberty develops the argument that liberty (or autonomy) is of only instrumental, rather than intrinsic, value. What do you mean by this? And how does this position set you apart from other philosophical thinking about liberty? Sarah Conly When we say something has intrinsic value, we mean that it has value in itself, regardless of what it may produce. We generally think that humans have intrinsic value, for example, so that the death of a human is always a loss. That is why we believe that it is almost always wrong to kill a person, even if that person is disagreeable and unproductive. Other things, though, have value only for what they can give us. Take toothpaste — it’s valuable, but only because it helps to protect your teeth. If we had a better way of protecting teeth, all those tubes of Crest and Colgate would land in the trash. Some things, of course, have both kinds of value: my dentist has intrinsic value because he is a human being, and instrumental value because he can keep those teeth healthier. I argue that liberties have only instrumental value. That is, freedom of action of a particular kind is valuable only if it has good consequences; to be more specific, if it has more good consequences than bad consequences. Some liberties have a great deal of value — the liberty to vote, for example. Others have none at all, because they have no good consequences, or because their good consequences are outweighed by their bad consequences. Losing those liberties is no loss. This has not been a popular position in modern philosophy. Immanuel Kant is the philosopher most closely connected to the idea that liberty is a good in itself, and that our autonomy is what gives us value, but there are many others. John Locke believed in the value of liberty and greatly influenced Thomas Jefferson, who wrote in the Declaration of Independence of our unalienable right to “life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness.” John Stuart Mill wrote On Liberty to defend the value of liberty of action. And there are many contemporary libertarians who think that the protection of liberty is paramount; Robert Nozick is the best known of these. Nick French You’ve argued also that, in many instances, there’s no good reason to think that people are particularly well-equipped to make decisions that actually advance their real goals or values. In those cases, autonomy is clearly not instrumentally valuable. In your 2013 book, Against Autonomy , you support this claim by reference to the wealth of research in behavioral economics purporting to show that humans are systematically irrational decision-makers. Some of that research (like that of Dan Ariely ) has since come under serious fire. But do you think the basic picture is still supported empirically? There are also the closely related arguments of people like evolutionary biologist Daniel Lieberman , who has argued that humans evolved to have certain tendencies that are “mismatched” to modern life — our love of sugar, for instance. I wonder if you see those sorts of arguments as bolstering your position? Sarah Conly I am always shocked when I read that a respected researcher has produced shoddy work. I shouldn’t be, though; it happens in every field. Remember Cyril Burt ‘s infamous twin studies? And much more recently, there was Marc Hauser’s sloppy research into animal cognition at Harvard, which resulted in him leaving academia. Whether it is overconfidence, overambition, or a series of innocent oversights, mistakes creep in. Fortunately, most scientific discoveries are founded on reliable, replicable work, and that is true in behavioral economics too. The groundbreaking work by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman is still very well-regarded, and Kahneman, sometimes referred to as “the grandfather of behavioral economics,” received a Nobel Prize for his work. Richard Thaler received a Nobel more recently for his own contributions to behavioral economics. Behavioral economics has been a very hot field with lots of people jumping on the research bandwagon, and no doubt some of them have not done the solid work they need to before sharing their supposed results. But the field itself is strong and has yielded and will yield valuable insights into our behavior. And yes, parallel work by evolutionary biologists contributes to our understanding that we are not purely rational agents who are always motivated in a way that results in getting what is best for us. Nick French Although many readers may find your position here to be radical or counterintuitive, you argue that, in many contexts, it’s already clear we regard autonomy as having merely instrumental value. Could you unpack this claim a bit, and give one or two examples of where we treat autonomy in this way? Sarah Conly We use a lot of language that suggests that liberty is intrinsically valuable, which shows up everywhere from the Declaration of Independence, as I just mentioned, to popular country songs today. But that isn’t the way we act. People talk about freedom of speech as a precious thing, for example, but we all want restrictions on speech. We don’t want people to be able to give away state secrets, or make threats, or commit libel or slander, or engage in false advertising. We make those things illegal. Cyberbullying is becoming illegal in more and more places, precisely because we are discovering how harmful it can be. Our laws evolve when we see that protection is needed, and we generally regard that as a reasonable practical matter. Or, consider driving: We love the freedom of the road. However, we accept speed limits, drunk-driving laws, vehicle safety standards, no-passing zones, traffic lights, and the highway patrol, because we know that otherwise we can get killed. We don’t want the roads to be completely free; we don’t want some maniac driving one hundred miles an hour through a school zone to express his freedom of choice. Our standards as to what is acceptable liberty change all the time, because circumstances change, and a liberty that once might have been harmless can become dangerous. We see this in medical practice: before the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), you were free to buy any so-called drug you wanted. Then the FDA came along and said that remedies had to be tested to see that they were helpful and not harmful, because too many people were being scammed by snake oil salesmen. We see it in environmental law: Is it okay to burn your trash outside? It was once, but with larger and denser populations, individual households all burning their trash is harmful, so most municipalities don’t allow it. My position is that we could make these changes more efficiently if we just admitted that what we are doing is looking at the consequences that arise from a given liberty and judging whether it should be allowed. Nick French There seem to be two major reasons to limit liberty. The first is when leaving people to their own devices leads to collectively suboptimal outcomes. The second would be when individuals’ choices lead to negative outcomes for the individuals themselves: for instance, because of addiction, reasoning problems, systematically incomplete information, and so on — basically, when a paternalistic justification for coercion is in order. How should we think about the distinction between individual-level and social-level justifications for limiting freedom? Sarah Conly Both individual-level and social-level restrictions on liberty are important. Both kinds require the same justification: Is allowing a liberty doing so much harm that restricting it would be beneficial, even given the costs of restricting it? Many liberties, of course, affect both the individual and the society around that person, but some more greatly one or the other. Seat belt laws, for example, are primarily justified by the good they do for the individual. The death or injury of a person who didn’t wear a seat belt does have costs to others, but the primary loss is to that person. So, we have seat belt laws because sometimes individuals are shortsighted about their own safety. Social-level restrictions on liberty are familiar to us, because many have been accepted since societies first formed. Theft is illegal, for example. It might be quite beneficial to the individual thief if stealing were allowed, but it is bad for society to have that be a practice. So we try to restrict stealing by threatening punishment. Of course, many restrictions are extremely beneficial to both the individual and society. Requiring vaccinations against a highly dangerous and highly contagious disease protects both the individual and the society in which that person lives. Nick French I’m particularly interested in your arguments around coercive paternalism. Maybe we could start by talking about what types of paternalistic interventions you see as broadly justified and those you think wouldn’t generally be justified. Sarah Conly Individual-level restrictions are tricky to design. First, it is harder to know what will benefit an individual. We can be pretty certain that some social policies are desired by society — like laws against theft — but less sure of what will benefit a particular person, who may have unusual goals. Second, if we did want to enforce restrictions for the sake of the individual — paternalistic restrictions — we might find the costs of such restrictions too great. How could we make people eat their five servings of vegetables a day? We don’t want diet monitors because the costs would be silly — not just the financial costs but the psychological costs to the people being monitored. It is not worth it. It’s easier to promote the accessibility of fresh vegetables. So when it comes to individual-level restrictions, we want to restrict liberties generally recognized to be harmful, which we can restrict without excessive cost. I would make cigarettes illegal, for example. There is plenty of reason to think that people start to smoke with a lot of what are called cognitive biases, basically poor thinking (including as to how easily they will be able to quit, and how harmful smoking is likely to be to them). And for just about everyone, we know that cancer, heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and other smoking-related diseases are contrary to their long-term goals. I don’t think anyone on his deathbed says, “I’ll never see my grandchildren or enjoy another sunset, but it was worth it to smoke all those Marlboros.” And people who don’t start smoking don’t miss it. So it seems best to just prevent smoking. And this is doable, because it won’t require monitoring individual people — it requires shutting down an industry devoted to producing addictive carcinogens. Nick French To zoom in on some particular policy questions: There seems to be increasing evidence that smartphones and social media and the various activities they enable have very deleterious effects on people’s mental health and cognitive abilities. Facebook and Instagram parent company Meta recently settled a major lawsuit by most US states in part by agreeing to put restrictions on minors’ use of their platforms. But I’ve heard many people argue that perhaps similar restrictions are warranted around adults’ use as well. What do you make of that idea? How do you think we should go about considering whether, and what kind of, restrictions on adult use of social media platforms or, say, sports gambling apps are justified? Sarah Conly I don’t know enough of the facts about cell phone usage to know what would be right here, honestly. How much harm is it doing? How much do people enjoy the unrestricted use of their phones? How painful would it be to change their habits, and for how long would that pain last? Are there policies that would reduce phone usage that would be less costly than a restriction (incentives to put the phone down, for example)? How would this restriction occur? Would it allow incursions into privacy that could be dangerous? Would it just drive people crazy? Answers to all these questions need to be known before we know if a restriction on liberty is justified. Nick French In your most recent book, you argue for several coercive policy interventions for the sake of collective well-being, including: mandatory vaccinations for dangerous and highly communicable diseases; restricting how much meat people can consume for the sake of environmental sustainability; and even imposing legal liability for speech that spreads “dangerous falsehoods.” Many on the Left who may share your goals, I think, have worries about these sorts of policies. First, there is a concern about authoritarianism, what you characterize as a “slippery slope” argument. Especially given who is now in charge of the US government and the way they have wielded executive power to attack both political enemies and the idea of scientific expertise, it seems rational to be extremely wary about granting the state more power to regulate our individual choices. In other words, we might think of liberty as an important good because it is a check on tyranny and domination by other actors. How do you respond to this concern? Sarah Conly Do we want the state to abuse its power? No, we obviously don’t. But abuses can take many forms. Telling businesses they have the liberty to pollute is an abuse of power; telling commercial fishermen that they have the freedom to fish all they want in what was previously a five-hundred-thousand-square-mile protected national monument is an abuse of power; gutting environmental protections so that developers have the liberty to clear forests, drill, and build on land without regard to endangered species is an abuse of power. All of these moves allow the government to curry favor with certain groups, even though they are a huge loss for the rest of us. A release from restrictions can be as bad as a restriction. If you elect terrible people, they will find a way to do harm. And again, the policy I promote is not “Let the state do whatever it wants.” It is that liberty is valuable as a means to an end. Thus we should evaluate, carefully and thoroughly, individual liberties as to their effects and act accordingly. In some cases, liberty should absolutely be protected. In others, liberty should be restricted — including the liberty of the state to act in certain ways if that liberty is used to do harm. Nick French I broadly agree with all of that. But isn’t there a difference between saying, for instance, the state should limit the liberty of massive corporations to pollute and destroy the natural commons, and that it should have the authority to regulate what individuals put in their bodies? Sarah Conly Sure, there is a difference. In one case, we are limiting giant corporations who are hurting us, and in the other we are limiting individuals who might hurt both themselves and us. But the underlying premise is the same: sometimes a liberty hurts us in a serious way. If we want to limit you, an individual, from hurting me in a serious way, we accept that: no one objects to a law that stops you from stealing from me. If we want to limit you, an individual, from hurting yourself, we already accept that in many cases. We think it is right to require prescriptions for many medications, because the world of medicine is incredibly complicated and reading WebMD doesn’t actually make you an expert — you could kill yourself, and that is definitely not what you want. We think it is right to have safety standards for cars instead of making you do all your own research into which cars are safe and which might, in an unregulated market, essentially be heavy-duty cardboard, because you don’t have the time or expertise for that. One bad choice and, again, you might kill yourself when the cheap car you choose implodes when you are in a fender bender. I am just saying there are more cases like this than we may have realized, and being aware of them and responding to them will help us, as individuals, get where we want to be. Nick French There is also a strategic concern with these sorts of policies. Even if we are less worried about the government abusing its power, it seems likely that attempts to regulate people’s choices in some of the ways you’ve described will generate resentment, mistrust, and anti-government backlash. I think we saw this kind of reaction to some of the public health interventions during COVID-19, for instance. I know your focus is on normative issues rather than questions of political strategy, but what do you think about this idea? Might less heavy-handed policies be more effective at achieving goals like promoting environmental sustainability and public health? Sarah Conly I am quite old. Thus, I remember, for example, getting a smallpox vaccination, since this was required if you wanted to go to public school in the state of Virginia. I do not remember, or know, when this regulation was enacted, or how it was greeted at that point. But certainly, by the time I was entering school, no one was fussing about it. It was just one of those things you did. Well, I am sure I fussed about getting a shot, but whether it was required by the state was not an issue. I do remember the enactment of desegregation, however. While Brown v. Board of Education was decided in 1954, many schools in the South did not start to integrate until after the 1964 Civil Rights Act. At that point, there was more than fussing; there was, in many places, an uproar. But now, how many people are outraged by the fact of school integration? No doubt there are some, just as there are neo-Nazis and flat-earthers, but it is accepted by every reasonable person. I similarly remember the introduction of drunk-driving laws, and how that was resented. “I drive better drunk than most people drive sober” was a common refrain, and yes, there were complaints that such laws were an infringement on personal liberty. Yet now people take laws against drunk driving to be completely reasonable. The point here is that while we do sometimes resent a restriction when it is first enacted, if it is in fact beneficial, we come to accept it. We want integrated schools and safe driving. Once we learn more about such restrictions and experience them, we often begin to think them necessary to a functioning society. Politicians don’t like to do anything that will make them even temporarily unpopular, but with enough social support they can make necessary changes. We need to think about what is best for us as individuals and as a society in the long term. La France Insoumise Won’t Cede God to the Far Right

In the video , a lanky, crisply-dressed Muslim man of Malian descent walks through a cathedral to the heady sounds of organ music. Several seconds later, he is pictured shaking hands with the most important figure in the Catholic Church: Pope Leo XIV. Off camera, the two exchange gifts and a few words. Later, on French public television , Bally Bagayoko — the mayor of Saint-Denis and a member of Jean-Luc Mélenchon’s left-wing France Insoumise party — spoke of how, despite being of the Muslim faith, he shares many parts of his worldview with the pontiff. Last Friday evening, Leo had come to Saint-Denis, just north of Paris, for a prayer event for young people at the Stade de France football stadium. Bagayoko, who swept into office in this March’s municipal elections, used the opportunity to platform progressive issues, including the fight against Islamophobia, support for social mixing, and the recognition of the genocides in Palestine, Sudan, and the Democratic Republic of Congo. For a brief moment, the eyes of France’s tens of millions of Catholics (and many others around the world) were on the poverty-afflicted Parisian suburb that Bagayoko represents. For once this was not because of the usual fearmongering about high crime rates, no-go zones and the rise of the Muslim Brotherhood but rather to celebrate the city’s diversity and central role in the Catholic faith. “From a political standpoint, it’s a powerful symbol.
