The Class Divide Haunting Climate Politics

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Matt Huber ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-03T20:46:13+00:00

Socialists believe the working class is the key agent of social transformation in a capitalist society. So, perhaps the most important problem for socialists today is the fact that significant swaths of the working-class are shifting to the populist right — not just in the United States but all over the world . A new report from Joan C. Williams and Jared Abbott, The Left’s Coalition Crisis , explains how working-class views in the US diverge sharply from the views of the educated and affluent liberals and progressives that increasingly dominate the Democratic party coalition — a party that the American working class once saw as their natural home. This divergence has grown so wide — and the Democratic Party so deeply unpopular — that in many parts of the country there is a “ Democratic penalty ,” where candidates can expect losing sizable support by merely being associated with the party’s brand. As the Center for Working-Class Politics newsletter summarized it, “A key finding of this report is that college-educated liberals live in a class bubble where virtually everyone agrees with them on culture and climate issues — but working-class people don’t.” The report is specifically interested in comparing the class gap between upper-middle-class liberals — people with college degrees who vote or lean Democratic and who have household incomes in the top 25 percent of the distribution — and a specific portion of the US working class defined as “the group that has veered far right . . . middle-status voters.” This group is specified as non-college-graduate households in the middle 50 percent of the income distribution, making between roughly $40,000 and $100,000 per year. The report covers the class gap on a wide variety of issues including immigration, public safety, and abortion rights, but one of the most polarizing issues is climate change. Williams and Abbott write: Liberal college grads run 29 points more supportive of climate change initiatives than working-class respondents — the fourth-largest class gap we found (87% vs. 58%). There is a 20-point prioritization gap: working-class Americans ranked climate change as only their 27th priority (out of 30); liberal college grads ranked it 15th. Dig deeper by race, and it’s clear that only one group ranks climate change highly as an issue — white liberal college grads (ranked sixth compared to twenty-seventh for the white working class). Even black and Latino college grads don’t rank it highly (twenty-second and twenty-third out of thirty respectively). Given the scale of what’s required to address climate change, surely requiring large supermajorities to accomplish the boldest action, these numbers should raise alarm bells. Bur rather than despair, the report bluntly asserts, “Given that climate change is not a working-class priority, advocates need to link it to something that is.” And they have good ideas on how to do that. But first, we should understand what kinds of climate ideas and policies repel working-class support in the first place. Don’t Give Right-Wing Populists Ammunition One of the most frustrating aspects of climate politics today is that it is arguably the Right that mobilizes class politics most effectively on this issue. Right-wing politicians consistently argue climate action is a plot of the liberal elite to harm the lives of average working-class families with rising energy costs and lost jobs. On the other hand, liberal activists often make climate change about everything but class — “ Believe Science ” — and sometimes advocating policies like carbon taxes that would raise the price of energy (affirming precisely what the Right warns against). The report throws this problem into sharp relief. A question asking about a carbon tax that would impose an additional $40/month on consumers obtains 69 percent support from elite liberals, while working-class support is a paltry 24 percent! Meanwhile, simply reframing the tax to focus on emitters with no increase in household costs increases working-class support to 65 percent (and support from liberal college grads to 90 percent). While climate activism tends to single out the fossil fuel industry as the enemy, often targeting “Big Oil” in particular, one of the key findings is that this kind of politics falls flat with working people: “Climate action to rein in Big Oil produces the widest class gap, with support from 91% of liberal college grads versus just 50% working class support — a 41-point gap.” This might be because most working-class people understand they rely on fossil fuels for their basic everyday existence — to drive to work, to heat their homes, and more — and as we’ve seen with rising gas and electricity prices as of late, these costs impose significant burdens on already strained household budgets. A politics narrowly aimed against the fossil fuel industry will struggle to overcome the industry’s own highly misleading narrative that they are the key to delivering affordable energy to American families. Another topic beloved by affluent liberals is the commitment to the Paris Agreement — a nonbinding climate agreement signed by 195 countries in 2015. Not only does the Right paint this as a globalist plan meant to impinge on US national interests, but the treaty itself also translates into an abstract politics of degree targets and emission-cut commitments. Unsurprisingly, there is a massive class gap over whether the US should stay in the treaty: liberal college grads oppose withdrawal at a level of 95 percent, but the working class only shows 39 percent opposition. There are other topics climate activists should avoid entirely. For good or ill, the electric vehicle (EV) is cemented in the popular imagination as an overly expensive vehicle for the smug elite ; thus working-class support for government incentives to purchase EVs (not even mandates) is abysmal at 37 percent (compared to 66 percent for liberal college grads). But even left-wing climate advocates get squeamish around the idea of an EV-focused transition. One thing the report did not ask is whether there is a class gap on questions of public transit — which, in cities at least, is disproportionately relied on by the working class. Of course, New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani made expanding free public transit a cornerstone of his campaign, a policy that would have a positive climate impact as well. Meet the Working Class at the “End of the Month” The report not only shows the extent of the class gap on a number of climate questions but also demonstrates how reframing the issues can dramatically increasing working-class support for pro-climate policy. If the message shifts focus from “Big Oil” to “corporate profiteers,” working-class support increases by 12 percentage points. This is no doubt due to the simmering popular anger toward the rich in general, rather than the fossil fuel industry in particular. The most dramatic gains come, unsurprisingly, from a focus on what matters to working-class people every day: energy costs, well-paying jobs, and even pollution. A message emphasizing those issues resonated with an impressive 75 percent of working-class respondents while retaining 90 percent of affluent college-grad liberals. This finding aligns with a core slogan of the Yellow Vests movement in France, when it complained that the political class only cares about the “end of the world” when workers are trying to make it to the “end of the month.” Indeed, while climate change itself can appear gradual and abstract, it has been the consistent failure of climate advocacy to reach people at this “end of the month” level. The report also shows the working-class responds positively — increasing 17 points — to a “Build, Baby, Build” message that centers economic growth, job creation, and calls for reshoring manufacturing. It should be obvious why this framing resonates in communities destroyed by decades of free trade and deindustrialization. But this message also stands in contrast to the last two decades of climate activism that has had a skewed focus on blocking destructive fossil fuel projects like pipelines or coal-fired power. While many of these struggles are inspiring — the fight over the Dakota Access Pipeline comes to mind — this style of politics inevitably antagonizes unions and workers in these industries who we need to build and replace all that fossil fuel infrastructure with clean energy alternatives. The report shows the wider working class is more aligned with these building trades unions. One possible response is that the Democrats already tried a “Build, Baby, Build” strategy, linking climate to jobs with the Inflation Reduction Act, which led us right back to Donald Trump. But crucially, the report advises we “don’t throw out the baby with the bathwater” on that front. Not only had many IRA projects barely gotten off the ground before Trump was reelected, but studies have shown the tax-credit model of policymaking made it difficult for communities to link any economic benefits to a coherent political project of the Biden administration. (It didn’t help that Joe Biden himself was incapable of communicating those connections.) While Marxists and others on the Left are correct to point out that many college-educated progressives are themselves part of the working class — even many lawyers and doctors must work for a paycheck to survive, after all — the key to socialist political strategy is uniting the whole working-class around a majoritarian agenda. For a civilizational challenge like shifting away from fossil fuels, only broad-based working-class power is capable of achieving what planetary systems require. To build that power, we need to understand why we are failing to win many workers to our politics, and how we can change that.

Socialists Are Out-Organizing the Democratic Establishment

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Cole Sansom ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-03T19:48:59+00:00

In the weeks following the resounding electoral victory of the New York City Democratic Socialists of America (NYC-DSA) this past June, the Democratic establishment’s complaints appeared right on schedule. This time, in addition to the expected accusations of radicalism, antisemitism, and, of course, anti-Americanism, came a new line of attack: the organization is taking advantage of the Democratic Party.Former Democratic National Committee chair Jaime Harrison, best known for twice setting new election campaign spending records only to lose both times, had this to say:If you hate the Democratic Party, then please don’t run for our nomination. Don’t use our resources. Don’t rely on our volunteers. Don’t use our infrastructure. Don’t ask Democrats to invest their time, money, and energy in your campaign. Focus on building the party you actually support. Political parties aren’t perfect, but they’re built by millions of people who knock doors, make calls, organize meetings, and fight for the values they believe in. If you don’t believe in the party, then don’t ask its members to carry you across the finish line.For anyone who has experienced working with the Democratic Party, some confusion is more than justified. Which volunteers does Harrison refer to? And what infrastructure?Does he mean the few software resources that NYC-DSA campaigns pay to use (and face frequent delays and interruptions using)? Or maybe access to ActBlue, the Democratic fundraising platform? I struggle to think of any national or local Democratic organizations we could plunder for volunteers.In fact, Harrison’s descriptions of “people who knock doors, make calls, organize meetings, and fight for the values they believe in” is a far more fitting descriptor of NYC-DSA’s electoral program than anything to be found among the Democratic Party’s establishment and its elected officials and candidates.In this critique, DSA’s success running on the Democratic ballot line is commonly framed through the metaphor of “parasite and host,” recently by the Atlantic’s James Kirchik. The comparison, however, rests on the assumption of an organism that can be preyed upon.At the state level, where NYC-DSA has found most success, it is unclear what the party, whose weak efforts in 2022 arguably caused Democrats to lose the House, could offer. And Harrison’s diatribe implies an easily susceptible voter base who have been duped by hateful actors. Nowhere do these polemicists against socialism ever admit the possibility that thevoters themselves may have made a decision to support NYC-DSA candidates, based on agreeing with those candidates on the issues.This casual disregard for the general populace is what many in NYC-DSA’s Electoral Working Group consider a key point of divergence from the mainstream establishment. The project was born not through lack of access to large donor checks (although that does play a part) but instead a desire to build a political system that is in fact, genuinely small-‘d’ democratic.The core of this is that, to quote NYC-DSA cochair Grace Mausser, “volunteers [are] the centerpiece” of these campaigns. Staff provide the infrastructural support and consistent presence to build a volunteer program so that anyone from the community can walk into a campaign office and not only have their voice heard but become an active player in setting the political direction of that campaign. As a result, these campaigns do not simply reflect communities but actively construct them.This is in stark contrast to the traditional methods of Democratic campaigns. As Tascha van Auken, one of the architects of NYC-DSA’s electoral program, told Jacobin: “You can talk to people who’ve worked on run-of-the-mill, mainstream campaigns outside of DSA; the culture is really bad. People don’t feel respected.” In these campaigns, door-knocking and other voter contact is seen as irrelevant, a sideshow; television and digital advertising are considered the campaigns’ main functions.This situation benefits consultants while leaving those without the means to donate the increasingly enormous amounts required to run an ad-heavy campaign out in the lurch. As a result, working people become further removed from those that seek to represent them; increasing levels of mediation contribute to a political culture that is alienated from anyone but the very wealthy, who are able to access politicians in person, not just through a screen.This leads to the Democratic Party as it exists today, out of step and out of contact with its voter base. In this environment, NYC-DSA’s electoral project has the opportunity not simply to score some victories but to build the kind of organizing networks that will be necessary to create the just and equitable society that we envision.This is not an easy feat, as the Electoral Working Group discovered this year in coordinating across many overlapping campaigns: creating cohesive messaging, field programs, and downballot door-knocking that does not leave any candidate underrepresented is extremely difficult work.In particular, our campaign for Claire Valdez in New York’s Seventh Congressional District, required coordination across five insurgent state campaigns and a similar number of incumbents running for reelection. A typical Democratic party operation would have run these campaigns separately, with a party committee allocating resources (on a federal, state, or countywide level) and otherwise stepping back and letting staff run their operations or outsourcing to political action committees (PACs).Instead, we did the hard legwork of making decisions across those campaigns that would benefit us all, resulting in a slate that won handily across Brooklyn and Queens. (Our single loss, Conrad Blackburn in Harlem, faced extraordinary outside spending and coordination challenges early on.) While congressional candidates more easily gain media attention, NYC-DSA’s approach allows those media appearances to function as an entry point, to learn more about downballot candidates and get involved in their races.Among our campaigns, the level of skills acquired and practiced, along with the extensive spectrum of electoral work that numerous volunteers took part in, was humbling. In one assembly race, David Orkin’s, the candidate’s team filed a petitioning challenge against an opponent. The campaign staff encouraged volunteers — many of whom had never worked on an electoral campaign previously — to learn the ins and outs of electoral regulation, becoming experts in the procedures.During this process, we came to a realization: we were not somehow “hacking” the country’s electoral system. On the contrary, we were the only ones actually doing the work properly, following the rules and regulations to the letter and winning without the help of billionaires, professional consultants, or connections to the judicial or electoral institutions.NYC-DSA has never won a race simply by duping a shallow-minded electorate, as the commentariat would have you believe. Rather, we have consistently practiced the hard work of building a democratic coalition, a volunteer base, digital and physical infrastructure, and the institutional knowledge required to win elections and form a politically engaged populace. We did not steal these techniques from the Democratic Party, for there is nothing there to steal (if there ever was, it has not existed since 2008). The alliances we built, with working-class community organizations such as Desis Rising Up and Moving and CAAAV: Organizing Asian Communities, were built upon mutual trust that had to be won, not transacted.The way campaigns are run can reflect the politics they aim to produce to the world. The professionalized campaigns considered the norm by the Democratic Party elites conceive of the voting blocs of their alliance as top-down bodies, where single voices can command distinct demographic blocs; these elites are the medium through which voters are reached.I experienced this myself during a season working on a mainstream Democratic campaign. More than half a decade on, I cannot get out of my head the words of my boss, a consultant who ran field campaigns for mainstream Dems: “They’re going to do everything they can to avoid knocking doors.” With a field operation run on advanced surveillance rather than generosity, what message were they putting out to the world?NYC-DSA’s campaigns are built on repetition of our smallest unit: the conversation at the door. Our society encourages isolation and seeing others either as potential dangers or potential consumers; with every conversation, our volunteers are not only helping the campaign achieve victory but breaking down the barriers that keep us apart. Our tight volunteer infrastructure program is not only a model for the future of left-electoral campaigns but a part of the path toward winning the world we want to build.The Democratic Party’s alienation from their voters is both cause and effect of the party’s capture by elites. In its current form, it not only lacks large volunteer networks and infrastructure — it is fundamentally unable to create them. Doing so would require advancing a form of politics that would reconnect not only with the needs of working people but also with working people themselves. That would mean, essentially, becoming DSA.

Centrist Democrats Keep Making Awful Health Reform Proposals

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Matt Bruenig ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-03T16:22:33+00:00

As the start of the 2028 Democratic presidential primary nears, various policy institutions will offer up health care proposals for candidates to adopt. One such organization, the Searchlight Institute, jumped the gun a few months ago when it released Making Health Care More Available , a package of reforms headlined by a proposal to make primary care free for everyone. Overall, I find the Searchlight Institute’s proposal to be generally overstated in its significance and quite strange and cruel in its particulars. Free Primary Care for All Whenever someone goes to the doctor, the bill they receive is paid either directly by them out of pocket, by their insurer, or through some combination of the two. The precise payment mix is determined by the cost-sharing rules contained in health insurance plans, which vary from policy to policy. The Affordable Care Act established a list of ten essential health benefits (EHBs) that must be covered entirely by health insurers without any out-of-pocket cost-sharing for individuals. The Searchlight proposal would simply add primary care services as the eleventh EHB. To evaluate the significance of this proposal, we need to know what percentage of current health spending goes to primary care services that are paid for out of pocket. The short Searchlight paper does not provide a definition of primary care services. So, for my analysis, I have adopted the definitions used in the Milbank Memorial Fund’s Primary Care Scorecard . In the Milbank scorecard, author Yalda Jabbarpour provides both a narrow and a broad definition of primary care, which I replicate in the graph below. Under the narrow definition — which includes general practice, family practice, internal medicine, pediatrics, and geriatrics — primary care makes up 4.5 percent of total health spending. Under the broader definition, the number goes up to 12 percent. The vast majority of this spending (85 to 87 percent) is already paid for by insurers, not by individuals out of pocket. When you add it all up, out-of-pocket primary care spending amounts to just 0.6 percent (narrow) to 1.8 percent (broad) of all health spending. This is a drop in the bucket, and if this proposal is implemented, households will end up paying higher premiums to make up for the increased insurer payouts, making it a wash overall with some winners and losers. This is not a bad thing to do, but it is fairly trivial in the grand scheme of things. Public Option Searchlight’s second proposal is to create something called the American Health Gateway, which it calls a “nonprofit coverage option,” but which reads like what people normally call a “public option.” This public option would (1) compete in the ACA exchange market, (2) compete in the Medicare Advantage market, and (3) be available (for free?) to low-income individuals in states that have not expanded Medicaid. Below I have plotted the current distribution of health insurance in the United States and shaded as teal the population where this public option would conceivably be available. The potentially affected individuals make up around 13.2 percent of the overall population, with two-thirds of that coming from Medicare Advantage. Of course, not everyone in these groups will switch to the public option, so the percentage of people who would be directly served by it would be much smaller than that. There are a couple of really strange things about this proposal. First, if you are going to create a public option, why wouldn’t you make it available in the employer market, which is the largest market private insurers operate in? If you are going to make it available in the Medicare Advantage market, why wouldn’t you also make it available in the Medicaid Managed Care Organization (MCO) market, which is, for all relevant purposes, essentially the same thing? Second, if you are going to create a public Medicare Advantage plan, why not just create that inside the Medicare program itself? The private insurers that operate in the Medicare Advantage market are basically criminals, but seniors nonetheless like the idea of an all-in-one insurance plan that incorporates Medicare Parts A, B, and D. The obvious solution to that would be to cut out the fraudsters and have the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) offer their own Medicare Advantage–type plan, not to create yet another health insurance entity. There is something also quite comical about the Medicare Advantage aspect of the plan. When you run the clock back, what you see is Congress inviting private insurers to administer Medicare plans and then this whole thing turning into a vehicle for defrauding the government out of tremendous sums of money. Now our policy response to this is to see if the American Health Gateway can somehow convince the nation’s elderly to turn away from whatever Medicare Advantage plan Joe Montana is hocking on daytime TV in favor of the American Health Gateway, which sounds just as scammy if not more so. This is needlessly circuitous, at best. Restoring Biden-Era Exchange Subsidies, but Only for Some People Individuals who receive their insurance through an exchange receive an income-based subsidy. During the Biden administration, that subsidy was increased a bit. That increase lapsed in 2025, bringing the subsidy back down to pre–Joe Biden levels. As with most other organizations, Searchlight proposes returning to the Biden-era exchange plan subsidy levels. Unlike most other organizations, Searchlight proposes only doing so in states that have adopted Medicaid expansion. In the graph above, we can already see what share of the population is on an exchange plan. It is 4.1 percent. This proposal targets them except that it excludes those who are in one of the ten states that have not expanded Medicaid. This exclusion knocks out 41.4 percent of exchange enrollees. Once we remove these sacrificial pawns, we are left with only 2.4 percent of the population that is in an exchange plan in a covered state. Some fraction of those earn so much money that they would not receive subsidies even if we returned to the Biden-era subsidy levels. This is an extremely bizarre proposal, especially when presented alongside the public option proposal above. Taken together, what we have is: A (presumably free) public option for individuals caught in the Medicaid coverage gap due to their state not expanding Medicaid. Expanded exchange subsidies for individuals except for those living in states that did not expand Medicaid. The given rationale for the latter is that “America should not continue to subsidize states that fail to take responsibility for the health care needs of their people.” But then, the first proposal does exactly that. Beyond the logical inconsistencies, on the merits, it is hard to understand how it makes sense to punish individual exchange plan enrollees for the sins of their state government. There also is no chance that any of these holdout states expands Medicaid in response to this incentive. If you really wanted to go down this route, how about increasing the top marginal tax rate to 90 percent in states that did not expand Medicaid? That might actually get state legislators moving and put the target on the rich, not on low- and middle-income exchange plan participants. If you wanted to keep the punitive measure topically related to health care, why not also reduce or eliminate the federal subsidies for employer-sponsored insurance, Medicare, and military health care in nonexpansion states? Surely that makes more sense than a random potshot at people who don’t have access to an employer health plan. Add “Loved Ones” to Health Plan The next Searchlight proposal is to remove the current related-family rules for who can be added to a health care plan in favor of allowing plan holders to add to their policy “a loved one who is not related but is still family in their hearts.” Unless we are going to be adjudicating whether someone really is or is not someone’s “family in their hearts,” this amounts to a rule that anybody can add anyone to their health care plan. The number of “loved ones” you are allowed to add is capped at double the number of people the current version of the health plan allows, such that single plans could have the policyholder plus a “loved one” while individual-plus-spouse plans could have the policyholder, their spouse, and two “loved ones.” The proposal does not spell this out, but insofar as family plans theoretically permit an infinite number of children, it would seem individuals with those plans could add an infinite number of “loved ones.” The proposal makes clear that insurers would be allowed to raise the premium to match the extra enrollees. So, ultimately, this just amounts to the ability of some people to add some other people to their own health plan at cost, which I guess is fine, even if small-bore. The Usual Hand-Waves Finally, the Searchlight document ends with what I would call the usual underexplained hand-waves. We get a broad commitment to cut red tape, to fight fraud and abuse, and to expand health care supply via telehealth, artificial intelligence, foreign doctors, and so on. Again, fine as far as it goes, but not all that significant in the grand scheme of America’s health care dysfunction. Ultimately, the Searchlight proposal is far to the right of not just what Bernie Sanders ran on in 2020 but also what Pete Buttigieg ran on in 2020. It does not eliminate uninsurance and does little to nothing to cut down on excess administrative expenses and provider rents. It is clearly inferior to a Medicare for All–type system on all the relevant policy axes. The point of this proposal seems to have been to generate a flashy headline about free primary care for all, which it did , but that headline policy amounts to almost nothing, and the overall grab bag of proposals is internally contradictory, cruel in places, and simply not up to the task at hand.

The Border in Ceuta, Spain, Is Anything but Permissive

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Nathan Akehurst ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-03T15:23:05+00:00

European Union authorities are currently deciding on images for their new banknotes. One worthy candidate for representing today’s Europe might be bodies in the water. In the Spanish exclave of Ceuta last week, another seventy deaths were added to a cumulative total of 1,570 people killed in the central Mediterranean Sea this year and tens of thousands across the mass grave that Europe’s littorals and rivers have become. The deaths at Ceuta took place as an unprecedented fifty thousand people attempted to reach the exclave (along with nearby Melilla, one of two holdouts of Spanish and EU territory on North African soil), many by swimming. Most appear to have been Moroccans escaping poverty and limited opportunities, but sub-Saharan Africans were also represented (refugees from the Sudan war are often found on this route). Passage to Europe “It was a disaster,” says Francesca Fusaro of humanitarian NGO No Name Kitchen , whose six volunteers ran out of food and supplies almost immediately. They had already been responding to some three thousand crossing attempts throughout 2026. Now supermarket shelves were running empty, with no hope of adequate resupply for days. Images of a small community being overwhelmed were beamed around the world and seized upon in order to argue for more extreme border enforcement. But the chaos in Ceuta was the result of enforcement, not its absence. The Ceuta border was fenced off thirty years ago, as Europe shut down traditional circular migration patterns where North Africans regularly freely crossed back and forth. This immediately led to a spike in migration by those who feared permanently losing access to Europe. In the post 9/11-wave of mass securitization, border surveillance and enforcement hardened more. The EU assumed new powers, with its new border agency Frontex deploying with Spanish forces off North Africa in 2006 . Moroccan authorities’ violent clearance of migrant camps near the exclaves in 2015 did not prevent further crossings either; nor did the 2022 Melilla massacre in which dozens of people were killed by Spanish and Moroccan forces at the border. Thirty years of criminalizing migration has produced an environment where refugees and migrants rely on smugglers and more occasional chaotic mass crossings. In this case, despite talk of the EU being overwhelmed, Spanish forces in Ceuta oversaw large-scale returns the following day. But we should also ask: At what cost? How many people were unable to exercise their right to present an asylum claim before being forced back? How were suspected crossers identified, as Ceuta residents of Moroccan origin reported hiding in their homes, fearing racial profiling? Could more have been done to prevent deaths? These are searching questions, but unfortunately, they are unlikely to be asked. Border deaths are now normalized, and when the issue registers in public discourse (with the notable exception of Pope Leo’s call for more humanity in the migration debate during a recent visit to Lampedusa) it’s usually to make things worse. Last month, the EU passed hard-line new deportation laws , inaugurated to cheers of “ send them back ” led by far-right legislators. Ceuta is a test not just for Europe’s morals but for its organizing capacities. The harsh new Migration Pact, much criticized by human rights defenders, was supposed to forge European unity in such situations. Instead, the response to Ceuta has been messy , further discombobulating the bloc. Spain’s Pedro Sánchez has slammed some European leaders’ reaction as “selfish and unlawful.” When we zoom out further from Ceuta, we can see a bigger story about migration control and its relationship to geopolitical chaos. Weapons and Walls In Spain, many have accused Morocco of “ weaponizing ” migration; deliberately sponsoring the crossing as a part of a bigger power game, directed against Sánchez’s center-left government. Spanish security services claimed both in 2021 and now that Moroccan intelligence agents were embedded among those crossing. Contrary to the belief that globalization would solve conflicts by binding rivals’ interests together, the weaponization of interdependence is now more common, with states exploiting their role in complex global networks to win conflicts (the closure of the Strait of Hormuz being the most obvious current example). Around Europe, the accusation of “weaponized migration” is frequent. Turkey was accused of intentionally facilitating crossings to Greece in 2020, Morocco to Spain and Belarus to Poland in 2021, Russia to Finland in 2023, and Eastern Libya to Greece in 2025. There is evidence of such a strategy at play in all these cases; but it’s first worth asking what is being weaponized. The accusation itself has major implications for people migrating. Seeing them as the mute tools of states or smugglers forestalls a reckoning with who they are and what has compelled them to move. Worse, treating them as mere pawns helps justify violence against them. Turkish, Greek, and Belarusian actions have been used to defend EU forces shooting at refugees, imprisoning them, refusing to aid them, and suspending international law , all in the name of responding to an attack by hostile states. It is a narrative instrument for dehumanization. The height of irregular border crossings in 2015–16 only ever constituted less than 1 percentage point of the European population (the continent’s largest displaced population are Ukrainians, whose international protection was formalized and facilitated.) Europe is surrounded by much poorer nations who hold vastly higher numbers of refugees, insulating the EU from the pressures of global crises, including ones for which Europeans bear hefty responsibility, such as wars in West Asia and climate change. The EU and European states in turn bribe or coerce neighboring countries to adopt harsher migration enforcement, exporting chains of abuse around the world. European financing and diplomatic cover supports illegal deportations in Turkey, violence by border forces from Croatia to Saudi Arabia, a regime of slavery and torture for migrants in Libya , and mass strandings in deserts in Morocco and Tunisia where mostly black people are rounded up and left for dead . EU public money now even pays for patrol boats that shoot at European rescue ships. The “norm” in Moroccan-Spanish border cooperation is violence, culminating in the aforementioned Melilla massacre of 2022. European states have signaled to the world, first, that the EU’s internal political crises forestall any ability to behave rationally on migration issues and, second, that Europe depends on external states and entities, including authoritarian governments and militias, to do its dirty work. It is entirely unsurprising when states at the subordinate end of a grim transactional relationship do not always point the gun where they are told to. Madrid Under Fire None of this, of course, means ignoring how states do manipulate migration routes. Spain’s position on Morocco’s occupation of Western Sahara appears to have motivated Morocco’s permission of the last mass Ceuta crossing in 2021 and also to be a factor in this one. Morocco not only criminalizes dissent at home but is a key border control , surveillance tech, and military partner for European and NATO forces, as well as a potential key supplier of critical raw materials . Its ties to Israel and the United States — including apparently recently naming a highway after Donald Trump — have prompted some Spanish commentators to see US and/or Israeli involvement in the Ceuta incident. The evidence presented is, thus far, circumstantial at best. Undoubtedly, a US-Israel axis has exploited the incident to relentlessly troll Spain in response for its defiance; with Israel’s United Nations rep accusing Spain of settler colonialism , others demanding it cede Ceuta and Melilla to Morocco, and the White House reasserting support for the illegal Moroccan occupation of Western Sahara. The US-Israel axis is also deeply involved in the political economy of EU migration violence. A US military surveillance firm that flies spying missions over Gaza also flies for Frontex, supporting Libyan forces to intercept people fleeing. (Frontex also fields Israeli-made Heron surveillance drones.) The United States is seeking deportation deals with Libya and a brace of the same countries in which Europe also aims to set up an archipelago of gray-zone offshore detention camps . Because of its moderately heterodox foreign policy, Spain has now become a major thorn in the side of US attempts at extending its hegemony in Europe — blocking its tech companies, refusing to allow its air bases to be used to extend the US war on Iran, and being vocal in its opposition to genocide in Palestine. Its stance may also have emboldened others, such as France . A general election is upcoming in Spain, and the far right will attempt to use polarization over migration to destabilize the government. Spanish Premier Sánchez has been on a long journey since his government’s 2022 intervention at NATO, which caused the bloc to list irregular migration as a threat for the first time. The military and Guardia Civil in Ceuta may have upheld Fortress Europe by force, but a wider story is the recent large-scale regularization of Spain’s undocumented population, allowing them access to work, rights, and pathways to citizenship. The policy is a rare exception to harsher migration controls springing up across the West and has also prompted pushback. The Spanish right, backed by US conservatives’ influencer-industrial complex, argues that Sánchez’s policy encouraged the Ceuta crossing. Misleading information does seem to have played a role; people crossing were told that they could not be deported or that the border was open. However, if there was really a strong link between crossings and government migration stances, Giorgia Meloni’s Italy would not remain a leading destination for those crossing the Central Mediterranean. In Ceuta, many locals have been welcoming and done their best to help; a demonstration in support of refugees drew hundreds . But a minority have, according to some reports, attempted to form mobs to prevent people crossing. In the exclaves where Francisco Franco’s forces once gathered and prepared to storm the Spanish Republic, their mainland descendants are now encouraging activists to “defend the border.” This incident cannot be separated from a coordinated push on multiple fronts — principally a barrage of artificial-intelligence-generated slop about Muslim invasions of Spain — to destabilize the last middleweight European power with a social democratic government. Displacement Capitalism Five years ago, I wrote about a past Ceuta crossing , arguing that a rapid increase in border militarization would become states’ maladaptive “solution” to a climate-stricken world. In Morocco, years of crippling droughts have combined with state mismanagement and the long-run effects of a debt crisis and structural adjustment imposed by international financial institutions to produce a youth unemployment crisis severe enough to make tens of thousands risk a dangerous sprint into the unknown. Spain is now installing a giant floating barrier on the Ceuta coast; similar barriers built by the Trump administration in Mexico and installed earlier this year have been ripped off their moorings by floodwaters and sent slaloming down the Rio Grande Valley, forcing arterial roads to shut. Today around Ceuta, wildfires rage across North Africa. On the European side, Spanish and French cities are thick with smoke, and even temperate corners of Britain are burning. Some 270,000 Europeans have been forced from their homes — a salutary reminder that displacement is a growing feature in everyone’s lives, not only of those in lower-income countries. As real crises accelerate, with wars, environmental breakdown, political polarization, economic stagnation, and inequality, and the explosion of dangerous technologies in the hands of a few, establishment discourse continues to obsess about migration while we burn. This scapegoating strategy is only deepening. Ceuta has also sharply revealed how disarticulated the politics of such issues can be. Watching right-wingers attack Spain as a colonial force in North Africa while some on the Left call for harsher border controls has been deeply odd. Yet these contradictions aren’t about to disappear. There will be more Ceutas: more people seeking safety and escape from multiplying crises, more border regimes that make their journeys more dangerous, and more actors seeking to instrumentalize humanitarian emergencies for various political ends. A socialist politics that can digest and respond to these complexities both operationally and politically is urgent. Migration is many things: a humanitarian emergency, a social and economic opportunity, a reflection of local and global inequality and strife, and a vector for large-scale exploitation. Most governments responsible for violence at borders do not really want to stop it so much as ensure it takes place in conditions that keep migrants vulnerable and exploitable — also making racialized injustice more certain. Engaging with these multiple realities is a prerequisite for addressing them. A focus on borders, whether on opening them or closing them, is no substitute for a broader transformative politics. This involves safe and planned migration routes. But it also involves breaking out of an interminable “migration debate” about costs and benefits and into a domestic call for labor protections and high-quality public services for all, and an international call to end inequality, extractive economies, wars, and environmental breakdown. Incidents like those at Ceuta require a politics that can process both the rival moves of states and empires, and the movement of a teenage boy, arms thrashing in the warm water on a scorching-hot day, seeking a better shore.

Greece faces rapidly spreading wildfires amid firefighter shortages

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Ana Vračar ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-03T20:36:19+00:00

After days of devastating wildfires in France and Spain, Greece has become the latest European country to sound the alarm over rapidly spreading blazes. In recent days, five firefighters died as they battled the flames: this includes two aircraft crew members who were killed in a crash with another response helicopter on Sunday as they joined efforts to contain a fire northwest of Athens. Authorities have recorded over 200 forest and agricultural blazes across the country in the past week alone, according to local media . Strong winds have hampered operations, including those conducted from the air. Evangelos Tournas, Minister for Climate Crisis and Civil Protection, stated that the wind had shaped “extremely difficult conditions resulting in many cases where aircraft either cannot draw water or cannot carry out drops due to extreme turbulence.” However, extreme weather is not the only culprit for the struggles of Greek firefighters – echoing experiences from France and Spain. Recently, the Communist Party of Greece (KKE) once again criticized the New Democracy government for failing to develop a proper fire prevention plan or hire adequate staff. The KKE reiterated that the fire department suffers from “thousands of unfilled permanent positions and massive shortages of resources and essential equipment, which is why firefighters are fighting a battle under adverse conditions.” Read more: How austerity policies wrecked Greece’s ability to respond to wildfires European Union data shows Greece employed 17,400 professional firefighters in 2024, technically an increase of 1,200 from the previous year . Yet, this still represents less than 1% of the country’s total employment. This trend is not unique to Greece: while EU reports indicate firefighting numbers rose slightly over the past couple of years, the total remains below 400,000 across the bloc – accounting for only 0.19% of total employment. In contrast, still in 2024, there were 1,551,663 police officers across EU member states. In May 2026, the KKE emphasized shortages in sectors crucial for fire response are still notable. “Based on the already ‘slashed’ organizational chart drawn up by the government, there are 3,500 vacant firefighter positions; Forestry Services have been decimated, with a shortage of approximately 1,500 foresters and 10,000 forest workers,” the party wrote. Combined with aging equipment, these gaps create exhausting workloads for firefighters and compromise their ability to respond effectively, threatening their lives and the lives of the communities they are protecting. The KKE and other progressive groups are thus urging the government to adopt a fully funded national prevention strategy. These calls include regulating energy infrastructure linked to the commercialized concept of “green transition.” “The policy of so-called ‘green’ development has turned mountains into sites for wind turbines and power plants, with thousands of kilometers of cables running through forests, constituting a constant source of fire hazards,” the KKE argued . Sources suggest the current blaze in proximity of Athens originated from such a power plant . Read more: Wildfires in Europe are spreading and intensifying thanks to neoliberalism, unions warn Not even EU programs that claim to increase member states’ fire preparedness can replace adequately funded local strategies, left and progressive groups insist. In this context, the KKE suggested that the European Civil Protection Mechanism, which deploys response teams across borders in situation of crises, “legitimizes and perpetuates the lack of personnel, firefighting resources, and infrastructure in every country by shuffling meager resources from one Member State to another.” The party added that this puts “already insufficient firefighting and rescue crews – who risk their lives on the front lines – at great danger.” Similarly, MeRA25 , the progressive party led by former Finance Minister Yanis Varoufakis, shared similar concerns. They pointed out that full-time and seasonal firefighters, along with their unions, have long warned about “shortages of personnel and aerial resources, understaffed departments, aging equipment, inadequate prevention measures, exhausting work schedules, and a system that relies on self-sacrifice rather than planning.” “Firefighters deserve investment that will protect them and the citizens whom they devote their lives to protecting – they don’t deserve austerity ,” the European Federation of Public Service Unions (EPSU) stated on International Firefighters’ Day in 2024.
