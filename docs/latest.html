Lessons for YDSA from Serbia’s Mass Protests

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Reform & Revolution ／ 著者: 不明 ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-14T21:45:04+00:00

This article was written in the lead-up to the recent YDSA National Convention in Chicago to motivate the passage of “Resolution 9: For a Strategic Sanctuary Campaign on All Fronts.” The resolution has since passed and two R&R members, Kaeden Boudreaux and Tomas Jimenez, were elected as at-large members of the YDSA National Coordinating Committee on a party-building, class-struggle, mass politics platform. On June 27, Serbia’s President Aleksandar Vučić announced he would resign “within weeks.” This was in response to the largest protest wave since the movement which overthrew the dictator Slobodan Milošević in 2000. No date was set. No election was actually called. The same weekend, Vučić was already hinting he would return as the prime minister, a job he held from 2014 to 2017. Despite frequent derision as an anti-democratic strongman, the Serbian president holds very limited legal power under Serbia’s parliamentary constitution. Vučić’s real power runs through the vast patronage machine of the Serbian Progressive Party (SNS), which he formally led from 2012 to 2023 and still dominates, and which has controlled parliament for the last decade. Serbian analysts read the move as another tactical maneuver to relieve public pressure without touching SNS’s actual grip on the state. The protesters weren’t fooled. The next morning in Kraljevo they said as much, doubting Vučić’s resignation would amount to real change, and have since taken to the streets in even greater numbers. It’s worth asking why they weren’t fooled. In May 2026, the month before his announcement, Vučić traveled to Beijing . Along with some great interview clips , he received China’s Friendship Medal and signed a memorandum joining China’s Global Security Initiative, an explicit alternative to NATO and the EU security frameworks, alongside new deals for Chinese air defense systems and combat drones. Serbia has long played the great powers against each other. In this century, they’ve been dependent on Russia for energy and their claims on Kosovo, the EU for trade (especially by exporting lithium ), and now simultaneously deepening security ties with China and the USA . Managing the great powers, the “four pillars” , is one of the SNS’s most durable sources of legitimacy. This all means that the nation’s President can announce he’s resigning while all of that, the one-party patronage machine, the security deals, the great-power hedging which maintains their exports and energy, stays exactly in place. That’s the concession Serbian students have won, a real political victory, but nowhere near enough. This is the same trap every YDSA sanctuary campus is at risk of falling into, and it’s the reason our caucus Reform & Revolution is backing Resolution 9: For a Strategic Sanctuary Campaign on All Fronts . A university administration can announce a commission, a statement of values supporting immigrants, promise you personally that they’ll “look into it,” and lose nothing that actually matters, the same way Vučić can announce his resignation and SNS loses nothing that actually matters. The lesson is, don’t let your target symbolically concede, keep fighting for the substance of your program. The protests didn’t start as a fight over Vučić at all. On November 1, 2024, a concrete canopy at the Novi Sad railway station collapsed onto the platform below, killing sixteen people waiting for their train. The canopy had been “renovated” twice in two years under a Chinese-Hungarian infrastructure consortium funded by Belt and Road contracts. Later investigation revealed the records for these renovations were incomplete, contradictory, and in places missing entirely, even after months of digging. It was a perfect symbol for the decade of SNS rule defined by corruption in public contracting. The slogan that emerged, “We are all under the canopy,” was less about the train station, more a rejection of the cronyism of Serbia’s government. The early protests were characterized by mourning . Silent vigils, disorganized demonstrations at Novi Sad city hall and at universities, a culture war waged in the media, a few cabinet resignations, and virulent right-wing attacks on protesters. By December, classes were suspended at most university campuses and a number of high schools as physical blockades took hold for days and weeks on end. By January the nation had become severely polarized, with the protests spreading to every city and campus and right-wing violence intensifying, often backed physically by Serbian police and rhetorically by SNS politicians. The state’s response to scrutiny followed a familiar global pattern. When a mysterious blast of sound sent hundreds of protesters into a panicked stampede at a March 2025 rally, breaking one protestor’s leg and sending hundreds more to seek medical treatment, Serbia’s police and army fiercely denied possessing an acoustic weapon. Confronted with photos of the devices, the California -made Genasys 450 XLR and LRAD 100X sonic weapons, officials admitted purchasing them but insisted they were never used, calling them “loudspeakers” available on eBay. Serbian rights groups have since taken the matter to the European Court of Human Rights with over 4,000 testimonies. It’s the same instinct as the resignation announcement a year later, to offer a technically defensible non-answer, and hope the public moves on before anyone can check. In January, with the backing of 60 percent of the public and the public sector and education unions, protesters began to consolidate around four demands that have driven the movement since: Publication of all documents relating to the Novi Sad railway station renovation Filing criminal charges against those who attacked protesters Dismissal of charges against all arrested and detained students, activists, and citizens A 20 percent increase in the university system budget None of that required agreeing on an entire theory of Serbian politics. They name a specific document, specific set of assailants, specific set of political prisoners, and a specific budget line. That’s the difference between a demand that a target – whether an Eastern European strongman or a public university president – can be forced to answer, and a demand they can wait out. You cannot “process” a request to publish specific documents the way you can process a call for “change.” You either publish them or you visibly refuse to, and either way, the state has moved onto the students’ terrain instead of its own. R9 is built exactly on this logic. Rather than asking universities to affirm a “sanctuary campus” as a value (something outlawed in many Republican states), it names Palantir, Flock Safety, Elbit Systems, Oracle, OpenAI, and demands their contracts be cut. It names 287(g) agreements and demands they be revoked. It names a ban on ICE and DHS recruitment. Every one of those is as checkable as the renovation documents. The blockades weren’t just walkouts and statements read to a crowd. Once students occupied their departments, they opened up plenums, these are public assemblies open to any student, deliberating on the movement’s next moves. No student union or student government bureaucracy filtered the agenda. No self-appointed spokespeople negotiated on anyone’s behalf. Instead the students who occupied the building governed it through daily, sometimes hours-long meetings. This wasn’t invented from nothing, Serbian universities have a long history of plenums , drawing on socialist Yugoslavia’s own tradition of workplace self-management. The protesters are in effect reviving this form now, against a government that has spent a decade hollowing out every remaining democratic institution in the country (such as by owning or influencing over 90 percent of media ). Over time, the plenums did far more than deliberate . Working groups split off to handle finances, communications, security, and relationships with unions and sympathetic faculty. Over the course of 2025 these national structures matured enough that a decision voted on in Belgrade could be echoed, debated, and adapted in Novi Sad or Niš the same day. Occupied university buildings stopped being where students used to attend lectures and became something closer to a distributed institution, a place to deliberate, organize, and escalate as a collective. That fusion of real bottom-up legitimacy fused to a real capacity for disruption is the easiest part of this movement to admire and hardest to copy. It’s also the exact gap in a lot of American campus organizing, which too often treats democracy and action as contradictory priorities rather than the same muscle. YDSA has begun to bridge this gap with its cohort structure, where chapters with similar campaigns targeting 287(g) agreements or the same corporations share research and pressure tactics. R9 continues and formalizes these practices piloted last year, fusing our organizing capacity across the country so that university administration cannot pick off our chapter’s demands one-by-one the way Vučić is betting he can wait out this symbolic resignation. The movement itself never resolved what it actually was, and mostly refused to try. From the first plenums, students deliberately kept the protests non-partisan, framing themselves as representing “all Serbians” rather than any single political current. This makes sense given decades of the Vučić regime’s successfully discrediting opposition parties as corrupt or foreign-controlled, and it’s one that let the movement pull in liberals, conservatives, socialists, populists, and the apolitical under one roof. That same universalist framing is also why the movement was never going to be socialist, or anything else with a class program: it was built, on purpose, to be a vessel broad enough that almost no one in Serbian society could say it wasn’t theirs. The movement’s own universalist “we represent all Serbians” framing has meant it can’t exclude those to the right of the SNS government too. Journalist Vincent Bevins, reporting from inside the protests for the London Review of Books , documented far-right speakers at a Kosovo commemoration rally within the movement’s orbit. Orthodox nationalist speakers, motivated by grievances separate from corruption and education, have been able to ride the movement’s credibility into their own influence. The movement’s organizers seem uneasy about this, without a clean answer. Serbia’s organized left is very small and fragmented, and didn’t agree on how to treat any of this. Some of the country’s Trotskyist and Marxist-Leninist formations stayed on the sidelines, arguing the movement “lacks a class character,” that a protest movement backed by neoliberal opposition figures and establishment unions, without a socialist program, wasn’t a fight worth joining. Reporting and analysis from our own tradition’s international network, the CWI, has treated that abstention as a real failure. They wrote that a left that only shows up if a movement arrives pre-packaged with the right politics abandons the actual task of the left, which is to offer direction inside movements while consciousness is still forming, not after. Two other forces on the Serbian left took a different approach. The Green–Left Front (ZLF), Serbia’s largest party to the left of SNS, backed the protests’ demands from its position in the opposition and has consistently allied with them at public rallies. The Party of the Radical Left (PRL), a smaller socialist group with no parliamentary seats but real credibility inside the movement, took the more instructive approach for YDSA. It didn’t claim to have organized the blockades, defended the plenum’s self-organization, and pushed back on the state’s claims. PRL also warned – more than once – that a premature turn toward electoral politics would trade the plenums’ direct democracy for a seat at the negotiating table, the same seat every co-opted Balkan opposition party has taken before. It’s tempting, observing from the outside, to resolve that ambiguity by picking a side. To decide the movement is, or decide it’s compromised beyond use, and stop thinking about it either way. PRL’s approach shows why both are mistakes. A movement doesn’t have to already be socialist for socialists to have real, unfinished work to do inside it, arguing for its best instincts against its worst ones without ever pretending to speak for the majority. That’s the same relationship socialists have to the growing anti-ICE sentiments of the US population , “anti-ICE” is a movement we didn’t start and don’t control. A concession that doesn’t touch the actual machinery of power isn’t unique to Vučić. It’s exactly what university administrations offer every time “sanctuary campus” moves through sympathetic statements and committee hearings rather than policy, while every actual point of contact between the university and ICE stays. That’s the gap our own 2025-2026 Sanctuary Campus Campaigns have run into in many cases. Real chapter capacity has been built, often membership doubled or tripled, but broad demands let administrations offer performative wins instead of material ones, and the campaign’s energy slowly fizzled away. R9 exists to close that gap for the 2026-2027 school year, and closes it the way Novi Sad’s students did, by naming the specific things that have to change. It asks universities to: Terminate any 287(g) agreement between campus police and ICE Bar ICE agents from campus without a judicial warrant Prohibit ICE and DHS recruitment on university grounds Cut contracts and procurement ties with the corporations building the deportation and surveillance infrastructure ICE runs on: Palantir, Flock Safety, Elbit, Oracle, and OpenAI Sever investment, research, and recruitment pipelines into the military and police Every one of those is concrete. A university either has a 287(g) agreement, or it doesn’t. It either has a contract with OpenAI and Palantir, or it doesn’t. Just like the canopy documents, there’s no room for a university to “process” a way out of the answer, it either cuts the tie, visibly refuses to. Either way, the fight moves onto favorable terrain the administration doesn’t control. None of this happens through moral pressure alone. Serbia’s students didn’t win what they’ve won that way either. It took blockades that shut down normal university operations, strikes across the education system and major sectors, plenums that gave the blockades democratic legitimacy and staying power, and coordination across dozens of campuses so that no single administration or city government could simply wait out one isolated group of students. That coordination is exactly what R9’s organizing cohorts are built to provide nationally, on a realistic timeline, rather than 128 separate chapters running 128 separate campaigns. Serbian students haven’t won yet. Vučić is still in power, still hedging between Washington, Brussels, Moscow, and Beijing, and the plenums know it, which is why they’re still in the streets instead of declaring victory and going home. A parliamentary poll in June found that , while SNS still leads with 47 percent of decided voters, that the newly founded Student List is now Serbia’s clear second-largest political force, trailing SNS by only sixteen points. Although Vučić’s governing coalition has majority support combined with their ally the Socialist Party of Serbia, the fact the student protesters have unified Serbia’s opposition is a serious achievement, won by years of organizing. Power is not granted. It has to be organized, demand by demand, campus by campus, workplace by workplace, until the people who run these institutions have no room left to wait us out. YDSA delegates should adopt R9, and every chapter should be ready to join a Strategic Campaign Committee cohort next semester, to struggle for freedom as one united force as the brave students in Serbia are doing.

Veterans, Labor, and the Fight to Save the VA From Privatization

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. At a time when federal workers face mounting attacks on their jobs, collective bargaining rights, and public services, a growing coalition of veterans and labor activists is working to defend one of America's most important public institutions: the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA). That struggle was the focus of How Veterans in Labor Are Fighting Trump, VA Privatization, & Union Busting , a discussion at the 4th Annual Left Coast Forum, held October 2–4, 2026, at Cal State LA. The session featured Eric Goepel , an Army veteran, Communications Workers of America (CWA) Local 6251 member, and Southern California organizer for Common Defense, a progressive veterans organization. Steve Early , longtime labor journalist, union activist, and co-author of Courage or Complicity: How Veterans Are Responding to the Attack on Democracy , moderated the discussion. Their central argument was that veterans possess an often-overlooked source of political power: their presence within organized labor and their capacity to mobilize across traditional political divisions. Veterans in Labor Are Fighting Trump (1:07:32) Veterans Are an Untapped Force in Organized Labor Early emphasized that approximately one million former military service members belong to labor unions, yet relatively few unions systematically identify, organize, or develop these members as activists. He argued that unions have historically underestimated veterans' potential as organizers, even though military veterans have played important roles in some of America's most consequential labor struggles. From the Teamsters' reform movement to battles against corrupt union leadership, veterans have repeatedly demonstrated their willingness to challenge powerful institutions. Early suggested that unions should establish networks of veteran shop stewards, local officers, and rank-and-file members, providing political education and leadership training that connects workplace struggles with broader campaigns for social justice. Goepel agreed, describing veteran organizing as a potential "force multiplier." When veterans receive training and opportunities to organize within their existing communities, their influence can extend far beyond individual membership. With millions of workers represented by AFL-CIO unions, the organizational infrastructure already exists to make veteran activism a significant force within the labor movement. The missing ingredient, both speakers suggested, is sustained commitment. Related: ICE Killing in Maine Shows Why Vets Need Vetting—And Not Just in Politics The Fight Against VA Privatization Goepel described Common Defense's campaign, "VA Not for Sale," which challenges the growing diversion of veterans' healthcare funding into private medical systems. Over the past two decades, federal policies have increasingly expanded the use of private-sector providers through the VA's community care programs. Goepel argued that these policies threaten to weaken the public healthcare system by transferring resources away from VA facilities already struggling with staffing shortages. But the problem reaches beyond veterans. The VA is also a major training institution for America's healthcare workforce. Physicians, nurses, medical researchers, and other professionals gain valuable experience through its hospitals and affiliated medical centers. Weakening the VA, Goepel argued, therefore threatens the broader healthcare system. His proposed response involves restoring staffing, rebuilding institutional capacity, and reversing policies that encourage privatization rather than strengthening public services. The objective is not simply preserving existing programs. It is demonstrating that publicly provided healthcare can deliver high-quality services without making private profits its organizing principle. Why Veterans' Benefits Should Inform Healthcare for Everyone One of Goepel's most significant observations concerned the contradiction between America's treatment of veterans and its treatment of the broader population. Veterans have access to a network of public benefits involving healthcare, education, housing assistance, disability compensation, and employment support. Although these systems have serious shortcomings and inequalities, Goepel argued that they demonstrate something important: government can establish comprehensive programs intended to meet essential human needs. Why, he asked, should comparable protections remain unavailable to millions of other Americans? He pointed to military toxic exposure as another example. Veterans harmed by burn pits and other hazardous conditions have won expanded recognition and benefits through federal legislation. Yet civilians living near contaminated military installations may face similar environmental consequences without equivalent protections. For Goepel, defending veterans' benefits and expanding social rights are not competing objectives. They are connected struggles. Related: Not Every Veteran Candidate Is a Champion for Veterans Confronting the Military-Industrial Complex The discussion eventually moved beyond VA privatization to the enormous federal resources devoted to military spending. Participants questioned why Washington continues expanding military budgets while threatening cuts to healthcare, education, housing, and other public services. Goepel acknowledged a difficult contradiction: military contracting supports many well-paying union jobs. Workers employed by defense contractors cannot reasonably be expected to embrace reductions in weapons production without credible alternatives for their livelihoods. Early pointed to the concept of economic conversion , an approach advocated by labor reformers for decades. Rather than simply eliminating military production jobs, economic conversion would redirect industrial capacity toward civilian needs while protecting workers' wages, benefits, and employment opportunities. Factories producing military equipment, for example, could be redirected toward infrastructure, public transportation, or other socially useful production. Both speakers argued that such a transition requires government investment and planning, not abandonment of affected workers. The issue is ultimately one of national priorities: whether public resources should continue sustaining an expanding weapons industry or be redirected toward meeting human needs. Building Solidarity Beyond Veterans' Organizations The panel also examined differences between traditional veterans' service organizations and newer advocacy groups such as Common Defense. Organizations including the American Legion and Veterans of Foreign Wars have historically played important roles in securing benefits and helping veterans navigate complicated claims processes. But traditional organizations have often avoided more confrontational political advocacy. Common Defense operates through nonprofit and political structures that allow it to participate more directly in campaigns, elections, and public policy debates. Goepel argued that veterans' credibility across political and geographic divisions gives them an unusual opportunity to communicate with communities that progressive organizations sometimes struggle to reach. That credibility, however, must be used to build solidarity rather than simply advance the interests of veterans alone. Related: When Workplace Organizing Gets Tough, Here’s How to Keep Going From Defending Institutions to Building Collective Power The larger message emerging from the Left Coast Forum discussion was that defending the VA cannot be separated from defending organized labor, public healthcare, and democratic accountability. The attacks on federal workers, the privatization of essential services, and the concentration of economic power are interconnected. So must be the response. Early and Goepel offered a vision of veterans not merely as recipients of government benefits or symbols of military service, but as workers, organizers, and advocates capable of helping build a broader movement. Their challenge to organized labor was equally clear: stop overlooking the veterans already within union ranks. And their challenge to the country was more ambitious. If Americans can establish public institutions to provide healthcare, education, and economic security for those who served in uniform, why should those same commitments to human dignity remain beyond reach for everyone else?

The Problem With Homeownership

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Michael Ledger-Lomas ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-14T20:27:27+00:00

A few days from now, my adopted hometown of Vancouver in British Columbia is going to elect a new mayor and council. The low turnout in municipal elections here skews toward affluent owners of detached houses who pay the most property tax and grumble about it. Mayor Ken Sim and his ABC (“A Better City”) party, who won the 2022 election, understand their base: they froze property taxes this year after abolishing the living wage for city employees. The ABC administration also implemented a wave of layoffs and promises to repeat the trick next year if reelected. Meanwhile, underfunding has left two-thirds of the city’s community centers to crumble away . Although ABC has given property developers a free hand to put up towers in the city’s core, they have not touched the zoning regulations that prevent the construction of apartments across most of Vancouver, which interwar planners intended to be a suburban city of detached homes. Sim’s party has also stoked the dread of the transient poor that has always thrived in its more chichi neighborhoods by imposing a ban on new supportive housing and hiking the police department’s inflated budget. Although ABC’s progressive opponents have condemned these actions as cruel, many of them are no keener to challenge homeowners by permitting greater density. Vancouver is but one example of the property-value politics now widespread across Canada, in which the desires of existing homeowners take precedence over economic development or the common good. The historian Joshua Specht suggests that such politics are even more pronounced in the United States. Property Values: The Rise and Fall of the American Homeowner asks how and why municipal officials, planners, and politicians decided to take the side of homeowners against real and imagined threats to the value of their investment. It emphasizes the accidental triumph as well as the social costs of a narrow cult of private ownership that took off because it promised to maintain moral and political principles dear to Americans. Property and Ownership Specht comes to this project as a discerning analyst of market failure. Red Meat Republic , his first book, explained the rise of nationwide cattle markets that turned later nineteenth century Americans into insatiable consumers of beef. It also showed how poorly efforts to manipulate those markets often worked out: cattle ranchers lost their shirts to meatpackers, while country towns that remade themselves to handle livestock traffic failed when it migrated to better-connected locations. The foundations of the American home, Specht argues, turn out to be no less shaky. It takes Specht half of his book to get to houses. “The American story is the story of land,” he argues at some length, before presenting the cult of homeownership as the most striking of its subplots. The founding fathers dreamed of a Roman republic of independent farmers whose sturdy labor would make a patch of the new world their own and confer on them the independence that theorists insisted was crucial for participation in politics. This dream was a nightmare for indigenous peoples. Although historians now celebrate their intermittently effective resistance to settler aggression, they were displaced from their lands and eventually confined to reservations. Nevertheless, the yearning for land of one’s own certainly popularized democratic ideals among settlers. Specht tells the story of the rebel tenant farmers of New York State, who rose up in the 1840s against the old stock landowners who had tightened the screws on them. Disguised as the Native Americans they had replaced, in a loaded allusion to the Boston Tea Party, they hazed their rent collectors and disputed their leases. The moral economy of the crowd — some said mob — emboldened them to demand the right to own the land that their toils had improved. Although their ringleader, “Big Thunder,” was jailed, they prevailed, not least because New York State’s political parties sensed electoral advantage in courting them. Slavery is often regarded as the original sin that poisoned and then nearly broke up the United States, but Specht argues that these controversies came second to the primal question of who should own the soil. John Brown and his godly terrorists killed farmers at Pottawatomie Creek in Kansas because they had arrived there from slaveholder states. “Free soilers” like Brown wanted to ensure that this newly created territory belonged to independent proprietors rather than being snapped up by the South’s satanic aristocracy. After the Civil War began, the North tried to end the Southern plantocracy for good. The Emancipation Proclamation would rob it of its workforce, while the rollout of the 1862 Homestead Act in the West would establish America as an egalitarian democracy of small farmers. That vision still required wiping indigenous people off the map. Brown’s bloodstained creek bore the name of the Potawatomi people who had been marched there from Indiana before settlers pushed them still further west. General William Tecumseh Sherman of the Union Army pivoted from burning plantations to starving indigenous societies into submission by killing their buffalo and destroying their crops. A landowning democracy never took off in the defeated Confederacy. Sherman wanted to carve smallholdings out of plantations for the “respectable Negroes” who had flocked to his army. But President Andrew Johnson was a white supremacist with no desire to create a black yeomanry. Northern elites accepted the planter argument that only their large-scale estates — now worked by dependent, though nominally free, laborers — could produce enough cotton to feed northern mills. The Southern Homestead Act (1866) was the best Washington could do for black Southerners: it allocated them plots on federal lands, but these were too poor to make viable farms. In 1900, three-quarters of black farmers in the South were still tenants. Hopes of colorblind landownership nonetheless took a good while to fade. Specht points to the black homesteaders who trekked to Kansas to found Nicodemus, a boomtown that soon supported two newspapers. Landowning Democracy in a World of Finite Land Nicodemus shrank once it became clear that no railway would run near it. Western homesteaders soon learned that their towns could only survive if the farmers they hosted could get their produce to market. Yet the only way to build railways was to offer enormous land grants to railway companies. Because railways could redraw their routes at almost any time, they had an edge speculating in property, enriching themselves and stranding others. Land became a commodity rather than a firm basis for a continental polity. An agrarian republic also had to reckon with an unstable climate. The rush to homestead the West after the Civil War coincided with a wet period that encouraged the tilling and grazing of its soils. This changed when droughts and locust plagues set in. The steam-powered integration of farmers into far-flung markets also proved to be a curse after distressed Argentinians dumped their wheat in the United States and crashed prices. A landowning democracy presupposed a limitless supplies of western land. But as the historian Frederick Jackson Turner first observed, the consolidation of California and the completion of railways to the Pacific had closed the frontier. The fear grew that a grasping minority could now monopolize a finite resource at the expense of small producers. This explained social reformer Henry George’s abstruse but popular proposals to tax the value of land. It also motivated the populism of William Jennings Bryan, who urged the adoption of bimetallism to loosen up the currency and so to inflate away the debts of mortgaged farmers. Although Bryan won the Democratic presidential nomination in 1896, his populism turned out not to be that popular outside the rural South and Midwest, because it had little to say to the growing number of Americans who worked in cities. Where did America’s future lie, if not with farmers? Before Specht turns to the rise of the homeowner, he points to an important false start: the vision of Jane Addams, a wealthy woman who pursued the gospel of social reform in Chicago. She experimented with running a farm and went to Russia to commune with the novelist and ascetic Count Leo Tolstoy. Back in her adopted home of Chicago, she decided that she did not want to be an urban peasant who baked her own bread and instead helped cofound Hull House, a social hub that promoted the mixing of classes and ethnicities. Chicago was America’s future: a place that reflected the country’s heterogeneity and compensated for it by encouraging the “Americanization” of its residents. The second half of Property Values explains why this urban “social democracy” never materialized. The main cause was the state’s resolute defense and then eager promotion of private homeownership. It began as a panicked response to the mounting problems of agriculture, which became a crisis after the Great Depression. Farmers had borrowed heavily during the 1920s in order to industrialize their operations. They were then hit hard when American banks and foreign lenders sought to contract their lending and interest rates rose. The return of drought and the erosion of topsoil in the West compounded the crisis. The banks foreclosed on people and sold off farms, bringing about the first nationwide drop in home prices with the onset of the Great Depression. America’s “Resettlement” This essentially rural crisis evoked an emotional defense of homeownership from successive presidents. In 1931, Herbert Hoover observed that it “makes for happier married life” and so for “better citizenship.” He felt that “homeowners no matter how humble they may be” were the mainstays of “liberty and freedom.” Franklin Delano Roosevelt spent heavily on such phrases, passing the Emergency Farm Mortgage Act to bring relief to troubled farmers and creating the Home Owners’ Loan Corporation. In its first three years, it refinanced one million home loans on the kind of terms that are still familiar: amortization over the course of the term at fixed rates of interest. By 1936, 10 percent of all non-farm mortgages were managed by the corporation. Congress also created the Federal Housing Administration (FHA), which insured mortgages, and the Federal Savings and Loan Insurance Corporation, which insured deposits in savings and loan associations. Roosevelt’s New Dealers spoke of a “resettlement of America” that moved the site of ownership to the suburbs. Farmers would be subsidized to end overproduction of crops and the rural population would shrink. The future lay with wage earners toiling in factories and offices to pay off mortgages. Men would make the payments and women would make homes with the aid of new labor-saving gadgets. They would live outside the “skyscrapers and tenements of New York and Chicago”: homemaking required grassy yards in which adults could relax and children play. These priorities tied housing to the automobile and so to the burning of gasoline, with huge consequences for the built environment and the climate. Specht, however, is more interested in what they did to sharpen social and racial inequalities. The FHA’s decision to refuse loans to homebuyers in black or racially mixed areas ruled out a genuinely egalitarian democracy. Redlined districts subsided into slums because black homeowners could not borrow against their homes to renovate them; houses in these neighborhoods perversely became more expensive as builders had no incentive to increase supply. To the indignation of black organizations, William Levitt and other postwar suburban developers used racially restrictive covenants to obstruct non-white buyers. Residents who bought into their developments accepted the premise that black neighbors would harm their investment. When Bill and Daisy Myers bought a house in the Philadelphia suburb of Levittown in 1957, they endured a violent campaign of harassment before finally moving away after five years. Homeowner Representation Without Taxation For Specht, this ugly episode was more than an explosion of atavistic bigotry. It was a response to how markets operated: residents were likely correct to fear that racial mixing would depress prices in Levittown. Of course, this was not a result of a natural economic law — it was the result of the real estate market structurally organized by developers and financial institutions to set a higher price on ethnically homogenous neighborhoods. Even Quaker activists who supported the Myers urged the need to temper the “flow of negroes” into Levittown. Many protesters were consistent in their own minds in claiming to approve of the “nice” Myers while vitriolically opposing them as neighbors. Specht’s materialist reading of racism perhaps underplays the visceral character of the reaction to the Myerses: the people who waved Confederate flags and burned crosses on lawns worried about miscegenation as well as mortgages. A few stood against this immoral economy of the crowd. The Wechslers, who lived next door to the Myers, braved antisemitic threats for standing by them. Yet in 2000 over 90 percent of Levittown was still white. American homeowners today — even in fiercely liberal districts — continue to mobilize against renters, immigrants, or other undesirables who threaten the value of their homes. In addition to their fellow citizens, many homeowners came to view the state as a threat, forgetting that federal backing made the mortgage market viable to begin with. Specht illustrates this with a brisk telling of the rebellion against property taxes that shook late 1970s California. Investors seeking a hedge against inflation had poured money into homes. Prices rose across the nation but rocketed in Los Angeles County, where the impossibility of everyone living in a roomy detached house by the beach, or even near centers of employment, engineered an artificial scarcity of housing. Because tax assessments were computed as a fraction of the market value of homes and revised to take account of the sales price of comparable properties, bills soared accordingly. Homeowners remained more or less quiescent only because pliable and sometimes corrupt assessors were willing to keep assessments artificially low. When this element of discretion vanished, the result was a rabble-rousing campaign to pass Proposition 13, which capped the property tax rate at 1 percent of assessed value and restricted annual increases in assessed value to 2 percent. The Californian rebels resembled the Levittown racists in securing their own market position to the exclusion of other moral or social considerations. They were not wrong to resent the fact that property taxes in California were 50 percent higher than the national average. Yet the passage of Proposition 13 in 1978 did enormous damage to the funding of public goods — and of schools in particular — for decades to come. By limiting the annual increases in the assessed value of existing owners’ homes, it privileged incumbents over newcomers, shielding the former from the impact of subsequent increases in house prices. New entrants to the Californian market were stung by taxes assessed on the high prices they had just paid. An effort by one purchaser, Stephanie Nordlinger, to challenge this unfairness in the courts failed in 1992. Specht wryly notes that she still owns that home and so has long since benefited from the injustice she once contested. The revolt had big ideological ramifications nationwide, putting Americans on guard against “wasteful government.” Ronald Reagan, governor of California while the revolt gathered steam, took this resentment of taxation with him to the White House. The spread of homeowner associations as a vehicle for property development in recent decades has further entrenched selfishness as a governing principle: homeowners who pay high fees to have their associations fix their private roads and collect their trash now wonder why they must also pay property taxes to the city. The BANANA Republic The last chapter and epilogue of Property Values show how the 2008 crash pushed the treatment of homes as tradable assets to its disastrous finale. Its origins lay in the rise of mortgage-backed securities, which expanded the amount of capital available to lenders. They issued more and more loans with small down payments, high loan-to-value ratios, and teaser rates that initially kept monthly payments low before resetting. Yet bundling mortgages into securities and dividing them into different “tranches” — ostensibly safe claims were sold to investors while toxic residual risk spread throughout the financial system — disguised how risky such practices were. Foreign banks eager for a share of America’s growth were happy to purchase these securities. The sums poured into housing doubled the price of the average home, encouraging speculative buyers to flip homes for profit. Existing homeowners refinanced, extracting trillions of dollars of equity from their properties to finance renovations or just to buy consumer durables. The inability of “subprime” borrowers to keep up with their repayments finally caused the banks to initiate a wave of foreclosures. This tanked values in boomtowns like Las Vegas: buyers who now owed much more than their waning assets were worth often abandoned or vandalized their homes, perpetuating the downturn. The federal government proved keener to rescue the financial institutions that had made this market than homeowners. President Barack Obama shrank from “cramdown” legislation that would have shrunk the liabilities of individual borrowers. He also had to reckon with a fierce revival of prejudice over which Americans deserved to own homes. Many on the Right blamed the crisis on ill-considered loans to ethnic minority homeowners. The Tea Party movement arose amid ferocious opposition to government mortgage relief for struggling borrowers. Prices have rallied since the crash, not least because homeowners in the most sought-after and dynamic parts of the United States have blocked further development near them. NIMBYs (Not in My Backyard) of coastal California have hardened into BANANAs (Build Absolutely Nothing Anywhere Near Anyone), protecting home values not through naked class or racial prejudice but by posing as guardians of the environment. A tenuous but malign stability has therefore settled over the American housing market. Around two-thirds of Americans still own homes, but with the average price roughly double its 2010 level, new owners are often carrying grotesquely higher levels of debt. It is not just price pressures but a deranged climate that is hollowing out this lopsided homeowner democracy. Global warming is supercharging Californian wildfires and tropical storms in Florida, forcing states to prop up housing markets as insurers of last resort. Urban Abundance or the End of Ownership? Property Values is a model of the academic trade book. It is as fluent as it is gloomy. Each of its chapters reads like a superior kind of New Yorker essay, starting with a person or a community whose heroism or sufferings illustrate a circuitous but inexorable drift toward disaster. These horrible histories of the present often suggest that life in North America could have turned out differently — fairer, nicer, less polluting — if only we had listened to this or that visionary. But can Specht offer us a way out of the housing trap whose jaws so slowly closed around us? Not really. He respects the continued popularity of homeownership and so won’t give up on it. He shares the conviction that owners maintain their homes and so take pride in their communities more than renters do. With the frontier long closed, where to find a new “abundance” of places for Americans to own? Instead of sprawl, Specht urges “density”: filling in the costlier metropoles with higher complexes (and perhaps smaller homes) as well as expanding cheaper cities so that almost everyone can own a row house or a condo. He also offers some diffuse suggestions for cultivating “egalitarianism without exclusion” that Jane Addams would have approved, such as going for neighborhood walks or putting on potluck suppers. These are reasonable proposals but do not explain how we might overcome the entrenched suspicion of the property developers that would be required to construct enough urban condos for sale in some version of a free market. Here in Vancouver, for instance, such sentiment is just as common among renters who fear “demoviction” and professional planners who prioritize “deep urbanism” over speedy construction as it is among boomers ensconced in multimillion-dollar heritage houses. Specht’s emphasis on cities also comes as a plot twist, given how abruptly his story of ownership jumps from farms to suburbs. It is hard to see how federal or state agencies could be pushed to give up subsidizing the lives of suburban car drivers. The same holds true here in Canada, where provincial governments spend on highways but too often won’t fund the urban and intercity transit infrastructure that cities need to grow. Specht never considers whether we should abandon or heavily qualify the dream of ownership that has bent North American life out of shape — and has never been feasible for many people with low or irregular incomes. Ownership could sustain community rather than encourage selfish fear of outsiders, but it usually hasn’t; housing markets could offer dignity and security to the majority, but in the end they haven’t. There is a reason why social democrats across Europe and North America once focused on building urban nonmarket housing for rent. Access to health-giving and dignified housing without regard to income was meant to be the material foundation for the kind of ethical community dear to Jane Addams. Specht vividly illustrates the moral and political dangers of confusing property values with public values. Might it not be time to separate them once again? Western Thought Justified Animal Cruelty

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Ondine Sherman ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-14T19:13:22+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. At age seven, sitting at the dinner table with my family, I prodded my fork into a thick, squishy brown shape on my plate. My grandmother, who lived with us, had cooked her traditional Lithuanian-Jewish Litvak meal, and everyone had dug in. “ What is it? ” I asked. My beloved gran whispered back from across the table, “tongue.” I stuck out my tongue. “ This kind? ” She smiled. “ Yes, darling, but from a cow, not a little girl .” It was my first moral shock. Animals are our friends, aren’t they? Certainly, my dog Bronnie was, and all the animals in the picture books and animated films I saw as a child suggested this. Do we cut out our friends’ tongues and eat them? My second moral shock came years later when I saw images of the horror of industrialized animal agriculture and factory farms. How can grown-ups allow this? What I didn’t know then was that some of the most influential thinkers in Western history had already answered these strange paradoxes. The answers to these questions, and Western society’s broad agreement, have led to the systemic violence resulting in animal cruelty we see today, which I have spent my life trying to fight. Our toxic relationship with animals has existed for more than 2,500 years. And while it may feel like humanity has advanced and we are progressive, we have inherited an ancient school of thought that normalized animal suffering and integrated it into our social structures; it is the water we swim in today. Around 88 billion land animals were slaughtered globally in 2024. Meanwhile, people catch trillions of fish each year . These figures show a huge surge in meat consumption since reporting systems were established in 1960. Sentient Media reported that “poultry consumption has skyrocketed over the last 60 years: between 1961 and 2022, [as] the average person went from eating 2.86 kg of chicken every year to 16.96 kg—an increase of almost 600 percent.” Despite decades of animal protection advocacy, suffering keeps growing. Sentient (feeling) animals are subjected to what many consider torturous lives: impregnated, separated from their young, caged, mutilated, and killed daily. Their suffering is not only legal but also unremarkable, part of the systemized functioning of our industries, institutions, and daily meals. Animals are legally classified as property , and many governments do not consider them sentient or as having inherent moral value. They are objects under our control. Scholar Lori Gruen distinguishes between two kinds of anthropocentrism , or human-centered thinking. One is inevitable, given our human vantage point. The other kind, which she terms arrogant anthropocentrism , rests on the claim that humans are superior to all other beings. It is this belief in human superiority that justifies animal exploitation and is the focus of this article. We begin by asking, where did this idea come from? Humans have eaten animals and used them for labor for time immemorial. This was supported by several ancient influencers and thinkers, almost all of whom were European men, who sanctified their use in the Western worldview. This moral permission structure has been carved into the bones of our laws, institutions, pedagogies, and civil societies. Philosophy didn’t invent our use of animals, but it justified and normalized that use. Sociologist Pierre Bourdieu, whose work reflected views similar to those of French sociologist Émile Durkheim, believed that human life is deeply social and that we each inherit ways of thinking from the culture and society we are part of. Bourdieu said , “In each of us, in varying proportions, there is part of yesterday’s man; it is yesterday’s man who inevitably predominates in us, since the present amounts to little compared with the long past in the course of which we were formed and from which we result.” Ancient Foundations Scholars tracing the historical origins of the ideas that shaped our treatment of animals often arrive at the same starting point: the ancient Greeks. Their rationality and reasoning provided the human-animal hierarchy with its philosophical backbone. Plato imparted his teachings to Aristotle, and their ideas accumulated into a tradition that became foundational and ingrained in every aspect of life. Plato argued that rational men possessed immortal, superior souls, while enslaved people and barbarians lacked the capacity or strength for reasoning, and animals had inferior souls driven by desire and emotions. This theory shaped the rationale behind our worldview and was used to determine the moral worth of humans and nonhumans. Today, we can recognize this hierarchical thinking as the kind of overlapping inequality examined through an intersectional lens , which Merriam-Webster describes as “the complex, cumulative way in which the effects of multiple forms of discrimination (such as racism, sexism, and classism) combine, overlap, or intersect especially in the experiences of marginalized individuals or groups.” Aristotle reinforced this hierarchy. He argued for virtue ethics : Every living being has a characteristic nature and a capacity to flourish in accordance with that nature. For instance, humans have reason, and so flourishing means justice and contemplation. Based on Aristotle’s theory, rationality measures moral worth, and the world is accordingly structured in a hierarchy. Plants exist for animals, and animals exist for humans. He said , “Since nature does nothing purposeless or in vain, it is undeniably true that she has made all animals for the sake of man.” Finally, the Stoics, despite their positive legacy in promoting equality among men, primarily maintained a doctrine that elevated human fraternity while denying animals ethical consideration. Pointing to the works of Aristotle and the Stoic doctrine of oikeiōsis , which states that “human beings share a kinship with other humans but not with other species of inferior intellectual endowments,” the journal Arhe states that in the 21st century the doctrine of “human exceptionalism” is used “to justify the wholesale slaughter worldwide of non-human animals for food, clothing, medical and entertainment purposes.” Alongside the Greek philosophical tradition, monotheistic religions conferred divine authority on hierarchical systems. The word “ dominion ” in Genesis has been understood, for centuries, as a social license for humans to use animals for their benefit. Monotheistic religions also broadly denied animals a soul, placing them squarely outside the circle of moral concern. The Church Fathers cemented this into institutional doctrine. St. Augustine was one of the most influential theologians and philosophers in Christian history, and he argued that animals existed for human use . He is credited with first cementing the interpretation of dominion as part of Church doctrine through scripture. Centuries later, St. Thomas Aquinas linked Aristotle’s hierarchy directly to scripture, helping embed it in Catholic doctrine. Aquinas, however, cautioned that one must be kind to animals, not because they have inherent moral value, but because cruelty to animals makes us harsher to one another. However, dissent existed within the religious tradition, with certain voices challenging orthodoxy. For example, in the 12th and 13th centuries, the mystic St. Francis of Assisi preached reverence for all creation and urged people to treat animals as kin rather than property. He was later named the patron saint of animals and the environment. However, sadly, Francis was an exception. Pope Pius IX, in the 19th century, reaffirmed what had been an institutional doctrine for over a millennium: We have no duties toward animals. I first discovered the theological debate about animals many years ago when I read Richard Schwartz’s Judaism and Vegetarianism . Exploring current scholarship, I have come to understand that the meaning of the word “dominion” in Genesis, and even its translation, is hotly contested. Some theologians argue that humans’ God-given role was intended to be stewards, a duty to care for creation rather than a license to exploit it. However, historically, the dominion interpretation became ingrained in Western law, science, and culture. On the consequences of this view, Bible Odyssey, a biblical scholarship resource, points to “the abuses that notion has seemed to lead to in the modern period.” It adds that “Human activity is a key factor in the currently galloping rate of species extinction. The factory farming of animals and fish is a major driver of environmental degradation in North America and, increasingly, around the world.” Scientific Revolution and Enlightenment René Descartes further entrenched animals’ low societal status in scientific thought. Animals, in his view, were automata , unfeeling machines. In his framework, kicking a dog was no different from kicking a clock. This justified the expansion of vivisection practices (live experimentation on animals) because if animals cannot suffer in any morally relevant sense, it does not matter what we do to them. Descartes’s influence was not merely academic; it shaped laboratory practice and legal frameworks whose legacies remain. The Enlightenment promised liberation and reason. For animals, the news was less liberating. Baruch Spinoza both disagreed with Descartes’s claim that animals were mindless and famously rejected religious authority (and was duly excommunicated by the Amsterdam Jewish community for it). However, his views on animals were more conservative. While he accepted that animals feel, he concluded it didn’t matter. He wrote that those who cared that animals were slaughtered were led by “ womanish pity .” In his view, pity is irrational, a feeling rather than a thought, and therefore beneath serious philosophy. He concluded , “[W]e are… permitted to… use [the lower animals] at our pleasure, and treat them as is most convenient for us.” Immanuel Kant’s exclusion of animals from moral consideration has had great and lasting influence. For Kant, the capacity to represent oneself as an “I,” that is, to be a self-conscious, rational subject, is what gives a being moral worth. Animals, lacking this, are things. “All animals exist only as a means, and not for their own sakes,” he wrote in his Lectures on Ethics . “We have no direct duties to animals.” Kant also drew on the Book of Genesis to justify human authority over nature, forging a direct link between the religious tradition I explored earlier and Enlightenment philosophy. Echoing Aquinas, he argued that we should avoid cruelty to animals not for their sake but because it might lead us to treat each other badly. Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel brought this tradition to a kind of culmination. For Hegel, full consciousness —or the capacity for self-awareness, reflection, and shaping one’s place in history—marks what it means to be truly human. In his theory, animals are trapped in the present, driven by instinct, and incapable of the self-determination that generates moral standing. Humans are therefore naturally superior. (By the 19th century, this dismissal of animals having moral worth had acquired a clinical label: zoophil-psychosis , applied to women activists deemed “obsessively” sensitive to animal suffering, their concern dismissed as nervous and overly tenderhearted. The term is no longer used, but the contempt lingers in other phrases such as “crazy cat lady.”) Even as anthropocentrism is taken for granted today as simply the way things are, and eating animals is considered natural, necessary, and normal , we can trace articulations of this hierarchy and entitlement back to a handful of European men. Without the long tradition of dominant Western philosophical thought, from Plato and Aristotle through Descartes and Kant, our relationship with animals may have looked entirely different. Those Who Argued Back Despite what many scholars consider the prevailing orthodoxy of a Western anti-animal worldview, a long and rich history of heterodox voices dates back to the sixth century BCE. These thinkers held an unusual worldview and, in some cases, a diet that rejected animal products. They questioned and abandoned the view of animals as separate or lesser; rather, they argued that animals have inherent value and moral worth and deserve equality and rights. These philosophers defended animals on the grounds of sentience, kinship, and compassion. For example, Pythagoras, considered one of the first recorded ethical vegetarians, claimed that humans have no innate superiority over other animals. The Cynics argued that happiness came from living according to nature rather than convention, and Diogenes famously held up dogs as models of the simple, unselfconscious life humans should aspire to. In the Roman era, Plutarch posited that animals were rational and sentient, possessed virtues that humans lack, and deserved justice. His treatise On the Intelligence of Animals is arguably the first major philosophical work to argue that animals possess judgment, memory, and language. He wrote : “But for the sake of some little mouthful of flesh, we deprive a soul of the sun and light, and of that proportion of life and time it had been born into the world to enjoy.” Two centuries later, Porphyry went further, arguing that justice should extend to animals. In On Abstinence From Animal Food , he approvingly quoted the earlier philosopher Dicaearchus, who wrote that “together with the slaughter of animals, war and injustice were introduced.” Later, in the 16th century, French philosopher Michel de Montaigne argued that our own processes of understanding are limited , and that animals may possess forms of reason and sociality that we are simply unable to perceive. During the Enlightenment, David Hume argued that animals do have thought and reason, and a generation later, Arthur Schopenhauer formed an elaborate ethics of sameness: Compassion, he argued, is the only true foundation of morality, and it cannot stop at the species line. “The assumption that animals are without rights and the illusion that our treatment of them has no moral significance is a positively outrageous example of Western crudity and barbarity. Universal compassion is the only guarantee of morality,” Schopenhauer wrote in On the Basis of Morality . Meanwhile, much of contemporary animal advocacy draws on Jeremy Bentham’s famous utilitarian formulation : It is not “Can they reason?” but “Can they suffer?” These thinkers did not necessarily win their argument in their own time. But they kept the question alive: Do animal lives matter? Other Ways of Knowing These histories of philosophical thought were primarily written by European men and filtered through the lens of those who defined what constituted knowledge. These ideologies conveniently left out the vast diversity of human cultures that never adopted the human-animal binary in the first place, and offered a radically different vision of the human-animal relationship. Through this article, I hope to provide brief insights into the richness of these diverse traditions. Many Indigenous cosmologies are grounded in kinship with animals rather than dominion over them. Anthropologist Deborah Bird Rose described many Indigenous Australian understandings of Country as a living community of people, animals, plants, waters, and ancestral beings, where relationships of care and responsibility sustain life, and where Country is home. Rather than a human/animal division, the world is understood as full of persons, only some of whom are human, who live in partnership with each other. As the late Yolngu ceremonial elder David Gulpilil once observed : “We are brothers and sisters of the world. Doesn’t matter if you’re bird, snake, fish, kangaroo: One Red Blood .” In Mi’kmaq culture , in what is now Canada, animals are considered siblings, beings on the same spiritual and physical continuum as humans. In Haudenosaunee and Anishinaabe traditions , ecosystems are understood as societies with ethical structures and interspecies treaties, and animals are active members who directly influence how humans organize themselves. “The Euro-Christian imagination firmly divides nature from culture and associates human beings and animals according to this binary logic. Animals are assumed not to possess culture or reason or morality or language. This is not true for Indigenous knowledge systems,” states the journal Religions published by MDPI. Ecofeminist philosopher Val Plumwood observed that while Western thinking sets humans apart from nature, many Indigenous traditions conceive of animals, plants, and humans as sharing a common life force with continuity and fluidity between all life. As Potawatomi philosopher Kyle Whyte writes , “Kinship refers to qualities of the relationships we have with others—whether others are humans, plants, animals, fishes, insects, rocks, waterways, or forests.” In this worldview, the line dividing humans from animals does not exist, and what matters is the quality of relationships and their reciprocity. Together, these perspectives suggest that arrogant anthropocentrism was—and is—not inevitable. Rethinking Our Relationship With Animals The suffering of trillions of animals stems from ideas developed over centuries, embedded in law, language, and institutions, and passed on so thoroughly that most of us have never thought to question them. It is a sea of speciesism , in which animals are assigned lower or no moral worth solely based on their species membership. But a counter-tradition has always existed. And something is shifting. A growing body of scholarship in critical animal studies , multispecies justice , and ecofeminist thought is actively challenging our anthropocentric foundations. And the animal movement is growing and becoming more mainstream . For example, legal personhood cases for animals are being argued in court, and the animal agriculture industries are investing in plant-based food tech. “With this increased exposure to posthumanist critiques of the legal system and its status for and treatment of animals, an increasing number of those involved in legal education are rethinking the law’s species-based hierarchy that places humans at the apex. This flourishing interest in animal law is paralleled by growth in the field of Critical Animal Studies (CAS),” stated an article in Animal Law Review . Even our grammar is slowly changing. As Potawatomi botanist Robin Wall Kimmerer has observed , the language we use shapes how we relate to the living world. For example, many animal advocates, myself included, do not use the term “it” to refer to an animal . But much remains to be done to undo the injustices of the past. After more than 180 years of organized animal advocacy, many of the systems that exploit animals remain firmly in place. Lasting change will require developing new moral frameworks—frameworks that ensure our treatment of other animals aligns with fundamental principles of justice, equality, and rights while also respecting, revering, and caring for relationships with all beings, human and otherwise, allowing us all to flourish. Ideas can be deconstructed, and unjust systems can be rebuilt. The historical origins of the ideas that shape the human-animal relationship remind us that they are not fixed or inevitable, but rather assumptions shaped by cultural and philosophical traditions we have chosen to accept. We can discard these learned patterns that taught us to see animals as resources or property and instead rebuild a relationship based on interdependence and kinship. As a seven-year-old at the dinner table, I didn’t understand how my friends could be considered food or treated so cruelly. Now I know it was the water we were swimming in. The history traced here reminds us that this water was neither natural nor inevitable, but shaped by ideas that became embedded in our institutions and everyday lives. And because those ideas were created, we can question them, too. This article was produced by Earth | Food | Life , a project of the Independent Media Institute.

The union, which consists of the teachers, speech language pathologists, registered nurses, and therapists who work at the Western Pennsylvania School for Blind Children, ran a strong contract campaign rooted in active member involvement. On October 9, only hours after the announcement of the award of the Nobel Peace Prize to Navi Pillay, a former judge on the International Criminal Court (ICC), the Trump administration imposed its latest sanctions on the global judicial body. According to Secretary of State Marco Rubio, “we will ban transactions with this rogue court, cutting off their resources and crippling its ability to operate.” When the ICC became operational in 2002, it represented a major breakthrough for the human rights groups that had campaigned for it. The new organization had the mandate to prosecute individuals for genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity and, after 2018, the crime of aggression. Once nations ratified the ICC’s authorizing document, the Rome Statute, they took responsibility for arresting these individuals and submitting them to the Court for trial. Designed to complement, rather than to replace, national criminal justice systems, the ICC could prosecute cases only when countries were unwilling or unable to do so. A total of 125 countries ?nearly two-thirds of the world’s nations?have ratified the Rome Statute. Since its founding, the ICC (headquartered in The Hague, the Netherlands) has acted to hold individual perpetrators accountable for the world’s gravest atrocities. Its actions included opening 34 cases (some with more than one suspect), holding 17 different investigations across four continents , issuing 61 arrest warrants, and, thus far, issuing 13 convictions and 4 acquittals . From its inception, however, U.S. officials were wary of the ICC. In the United States, President Bill Clinton signed the Rome Statute, but refused to present it to the U.S. Senate for ratification, warning that it had “significant flaws,” including the inability to “protect U.S. officials.” His successor, George W. Bush , “unsigned” the treaty in 2002 and signed U.S. legislation authorizing military force to liberate any American nationals it held for crimes. During Trump’s first term as President, he denounced the ICC before the UN General Assembly and imposed economic sanctions and visa restrictions on top ICC officials for any effort to investigate the behavior of U.S. personnel in Afghanistan. Russian officials, too, soon realized that enforcement of international law threatened them. Although, initially, the Russian government signed the Rome Statute and participated constructively in the ICC, it withdrew its signature after Ukraine appealed to the ICC in 2014 and 2015 to investigate Russian war crimes in Ukraine. The ICC did initiate a preliminary inquiry that, after the full-scale Russian invasion of 2022 and Russia’s murder of Ukrainian civilians and prisoners of war in Bucha, expanded into a formal investigation. In 2023, the ICC issued arrest warrants for Russian President Vladimir Putin and Commissioner for Children’s Rights Maria Lvova-Belova for the kidnapping of thousands of Ukrainian children. Meanwhile, the assault on the ICC escalated. Although U.S. President Joseph Biden scrapped Trump’s sanctions on the Court and authorized sharing information with it during investigations of Russian atrocities in Ukraine, he and numerous officials of other nations denounced the ICC’s November 2024 war crimes arrest warrant for top Israeli officials. Netanyahu assailed what he called “the antisemitic decision” of the ICC and said that Israel refused to comply with it. With Trump’s re-election, the campaign to destroy the ICC went into high gear . In February 2025, he imposed sanctions on the ICC prosecutor. That August, Secretary of State Marco Rubio , labeling the Court “a national security threat,” added ICC judges to the sanctions list. In late 2025, a top Trump administration official told Reuters that there was “growing concern” that “the ICC will turn its attention to the president, to the vice president, to the secretary of war and others, and pursue prosecutions against them.” That was “unacceptable, and we will not allow it to happen.” The ICC, he said, had been informed that, unless it amended its founding document to ensure that it would not investigate the Republican president and his top officials, new, very punishing U.S. sanctions would be imposed on the Court. The Russian government also ramped up its attack . In December 2025, a Russian court, retaliating for the ICC’s arrest warrants for Putin and Lvova-Belova, convicted the ICC prosecutor and eight ICC judges, in absentia, of “unlawful prosecution,” sentencing them to terms of imprisonment ranging from 3-1/2 to 15 years. In July 2026, Rubio announced what the U.S. State Department called “a sweeping campaign to dismantle the threat posed by the International Criminal Court to U.S. sovereignty.” It would “feature a whole-of-government response to systematically disable the ICC’s ability to operate” and destroy the Court “brick by brick.” That August, imposing additional sanctions on Court officials , Rubio proclaimed “our unwavering mission to protect Americans from this sham of a court.” Meanwhile, U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth called on Latin American and Caribbean governments to sever their ties with the ICC. The number of withdrawals now stands at five , including Burkina Faso, Mali, and Niger. The latter three are ruled by military juntas allied with Russia. As the Nobel committee warned when announcing the Peace Prize, the “judges and institutions of international law are under political attack.” If the attack succeeds, it will produce a disaster not only for the rule of law, but for the cause of human rights.

Student Protests Are Making Their Mark on Serbia’s Election

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Lela Vujanić ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-14T17:24:21+00:00

Sitting in the trashy garden of a café frequented by students near an iconic Belgrade market, we are, naturally, discussing the elections. A somewhat hippieish father of a friend complains that the situation is terrible, that he’s never seen such polarization, and that decades-old colleagues are barely on speaking terms. He tells me he tried to lighten the atmosphere, joking “Guys, I am neutral, you can relax, you can all be yourselves with me.” His remark didn’t sit well with either camp. Nobody in Serbia these days seems to like “neutrals,” though it seems that this despised group could decide the outcome of the October 25 parliamentary elections. There is an electric mood in the country. There have already been irregularities and sporadic individual acts of violence against opponents by members of the ruling party, with more widely expected before the vote. Previous police violence against protesters and the alleged use of a sonic weapon during a March 2025 demonstration are still vivid in the collective memory. The latter has even become the subject of Lawrence Abu Hamdan’s fifteen-channel video installation currently exhibited in Venice. Few expect election day to pass peacefully. Aleksandar Vučić — competing to become prime minister, to which end he recently stepped down as president — is mainly communicating to his nominally “progressive” party’s (the Serbian Progressive Party, or SNS) own base, trying to harden its core vote and prevent defections. Student protesters, who registered as a group of citizens under the name “Student List–Students Win,” are campaigning under the slogan “Serbia or Mafia,” pointing out corruption, para-police gangs, and connections between the state and organized crime. They’re trying to capture all the neutral and still-undecided voters, those lacking information or interpretations of what’s going on, as well as past abstainers. There’s a fight for every vote, including those in the diaspora who will now have to be stubborn to get their votes counted: due to the authorities’ controversial decision, some will have to vote in the middle of the night or even fly thousands of miles to cast a ballot, if they can afford it. These elections come in the twenty-fourth month of “pumping it up,” as the students’ popular slogan calls it. The vote takes place a few days ahead of the second anniversary of the collapse of the Novi Sad railway station canopy that killed sixteen people and triggered a massive wave of student blockades and protests as well as local mobilizations through people’s assemblies, known as zborovi . In between, we have seen historic blockades and protests, assemblies and organizing , mass politicization and emancipation. Many lives were put on hold, many suffered government repression, including arrests and job losses, and many invested their labors not only in elections but in building structures meant to survive them. But now the elections are served on the table, and, as the occasion demands, we need to look at the challenger, the incumbent, and the ever-so-precarious election forecasts. The Electoral Experiment Called Student List The Student List, consisting of 250 candidates, was finally revealed to the public in mid-September, after the elections were formally called. The criteria for the list were set by student assemblies ( plenums , in Serbian) from faculties across the country; no students were to be on the list, to show their dedication not to power but to the common good; no opposition party officials, to show a break with oppositional politics as usual that yielded no results; no prior members of the ruling coalition; and proven support for the student movement and its program. There are a few household names, from a famous basketball player to an actor to a farmer who fiercely resisted the Rio Tinto lithium project. But in general, the final list features “ordinary people,” meaning that most Serbians will not recognize these names or what politics they represent. All candidates went through an unprecedented multistage process in which faculties could propose candidates and challenge one another’s nominations. Many candidates were vetoed for different (sometimes rather bizarre) reasons, among them the playwright Maja Pelević, who stays committed to the movement. “The veto process was a litmus test, ensuring that this was genuinely a shared, democratic decision of all the faculties,” she says, emphasizing that the plenums , not individual influence or name recognition, had the final say. This list is meant to represent the population in general, though its success in that regard is mixed. The gender split is 55.6 percent men and 44.4 percent women. Geographically, 75 percent of candidates come from four cities that are university centers. Most striking is the occupational structure — more than 50 percent of the candidates are from the education sector, from high school to university professors. For comparison, education accounts for less than 10 percent of Serbia’s total registered employment. Part of the reason is the support that educators gave students, meaning they also suffered government repression most. Candidates do not belong to the same ideological or political factions, and some take pride in being politically nonaligned. The impression is that this was by design. It is unknown who would take ministerial or cabinet roles in case of victory, how such a diverse group of candidates could find political and programmatic harmony in parliament, or who would prevent those elected from acting contrary to the students’ program. It is worth pausing now, for both me writing this text and for you reading it; if all of this sounds wild and experimental, that is because it is. Though there are historical reference points, like the construction of Poland’s Solidarność electoral list, the experimental and unprecedented character of the Student Lists, vetoed and voted for by anonymous assemblies representing the mass movement, is being used by opponents to discourage people from voting. We hear fearmongering that “salaries and pensions could be put in question” and that “the country could fall into chaos.” The (Un)importance of Having a Program The electoral program, developed in an equally long and thorough process of collective deliberation between plenums , labor collectives, and sectoral associations, is not fully revealed yet. The rather brief version presented as Student Plan focuses on cleaning up the state — primarily the judiciary — and the media; general claims of reforming education and health care; and providing accessible housing for youth (no luck for tenants of my age group, it seems); though fighting import monopolies and permanent employment after twelve months of temporary contract are also part of the plan. The vagueness of the program’s eleven points seems to bother no one, confirming that both students and protesting citizens are primarily focused on replacing the current government, and that the competing economic or cultural frames are postponed for after the elections. The incumbent’s offer, meanwhile, rests on the stabilitocracy of macroeconomic indicators, foreign direct investment (FDI), and economic maneuvering between foreign powers, which also includes close cooperation with Israel . In domestic policy, Vučić has, throughout the years, repeatedly positioned himself as a protector of lower-income strata. Actually, this is how he got in power in 2012 in the first place, following the waves of rampant privatization and job losses, as part of transitional “shock therapy” implemented in Serbia a decade later than in the rest of the East. But not only was Vučić no clean newcomer, but in practice the privatization and deregulation of the labor market continued, while the public sector was clientelistically tied to the ruling party and its needs. The incumbent is still playing these cards, trying to depict students and their supporters as out-of-touch with economic realities and as instruments of the West, while he himself is a patriotic protector of the nation, an economic tiger (as the SNS slogan has it) and almost an anti-imperialist smooth operator, successfully fighting external and internal dragons. While the Student List candidates are mainly talking about abstract freedom and the less abstract questions (if not first on everyone’s mind) of corruption and institutional order, some on the Student List are determined to center labor. Živojin Milovanović, candidate number 31, and a machine technician at the Electric Power of Serbia, says: “[in] agency employment, commonly known as ‘daughter companies’ in Serbia . . . workers are modern-day slaves, and I will keep repeating that. They do the hardest possible jobs, yet are paid far less than their colleagues, work on temporary and short-term contracts, and have no real voice.” As an early member of the Social Front, an alliance of students, workers, and farmers that emerged within the student movement, he promises changes to the labor code and the laws on strike action that would guarantee people’s right to organize in trade unions and ensure that they face no consequences for going on strike. “The regime shaped the laws to serve its own interests. It took control of the trade unions, turning them into its obedient servants. They did not fight for workers; instead, they suppressed every form of workers’ struggle, leaving workers without protection and without any means to change anything. The Students’ List wants to change all of this.” For proposals like these, the Party of the Radical Left (PRL) has offered the Student List “critical support,” arguing that despite the limitations of its still-incomplete program, it includes candidates and positions worth supporting. It is likewise maintaining pressure for a broader anti-capitalist agenda. Forecasting Into the Void Much has been debated about creating a referendum-like atmosphere, with Vučić versus the students as the key contenders. Credible and independent polling is scarce. In the latest election forecast from mid-September by Faktor Plus, the Vučić-led coalition list was at 48.6 percent, while the Student List, in continuous growth according to their previous polling, stood at 37.1 percent. Earlier polling by other organizations (CRTA and NSPM) showed the Student List ahead, by 44.9 to 44.4 percent. Across pollsters, about a quarter of voters are yet to make up their minds. There is a consistent expectation of high turnout, likely above the 58.8 percent recorded in 2023. While Vučić and his SNS are employing arena rallies, TV ads and appearances, and an army of bots on social media, the students are relying on messaging groups and social media, plastering the city with stickers, and, more importantly, making their presence felt in person: cycling across Serbia, organizing public rallies and events, and covering multiple city locations in a single day. Pelević, who is a member of a local, Belgrade-based popular assembly, helps students with campaigning in her municipality. “Door-to-door is essentially another form of decentralized organizing. We’re not politicians. We’re neighbors ( komšije in Serbian), we’re students, and we go out and talk to people about the problems that affect both them and us. So the way we have these conversations is completely different from when some politician comes knocking on your door, has no idea what your problems are, and pretends they’re going to solve them.” Election After-Party High expected turnout and a sizable bloc of undecideds are main factors cited as decisive in regard to the election result; but it may also be that the SNS camp will face defection. Economic slowdown, inflation and the limits of FDI as a core strategy for growth, visible in the depletion of workers’ rights and dignity as well as degradation of the environment, could break the spell of Vučić’s “protection.” The students and their candidates have exposed the regime’s corruption and the havoc in Serbia’s institutions. What still needs doing is to tie these questions to an economic agenda: first, by showing that Vučić’s political authoritarianism and economic neoliberalism work hand in hand; second, by making clear that getting rid of one without the other will not be enough. After the elections, the economic contradictions of a landlocked country whose place in the global economy rests heavily on cheap labor and lithium reserves will remain. The mass democratic opening created by the movement could also slip into parliamentary politics as usual. What happens next will depend on the movement’s ability to rebuild and sustain its power beyond the ballot box, through people like Milovanović and Pelević and the practices they represent. And ultimately, the election may be one step on a historical path that depends not only on local conditions but also on future international alliances. Somewhere between middle school and high school, I decided history wasn't for me. It seemed like an endless parade of dates, timelines, and wars, interrupted by still more wars. Without it, how can we recognize the warning signs of something we've already lived through? This year America celebrates its 250th birthday. Compared with civilizations whose histories stretch back thousands of years, we're practically adolescents. Our government surveilled civil rights leaders, including Martin Luther King Jr., while gay Americans endured generations of discrimination. Yet Americans also fought to correct injustices. The Civil Rights Act became law in 1964. Marriage equality became the law of the land in 2015. I'm not asking anyone to inherit the guilt of previous generations. I'm asking us to inherit their lessons. Facts Are Stubborn Things In 1770, John Adams, defending British soldiers accused in the Boston Massacre, reminded a courtroom that “facts are stubborn things.” More than 250 years later, those words deserve another hearing. On January 29, 2025, the president signed Ending Radical Indoctrination in K–12 Schooling. The administration says it wants patriotic education, parental rights, and an end to teaching children that they are personally responsible for historical injustices. Children shouldn't carry the guilt of previous generations. But there's a difference between assigning blame for slavery and teaching children how slavery worked, whom it harmed, and why Americans fought to end it. The order does not itself ban teaching slavery or segregation. But its definitions and funding provisions raise the question of whether teachers may feel discouraged from examining uncomfortable history honestly. On March 27, 2025, the president signed a second order: Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History. Washington helped establish a republic dedicated to liberty, and he enslaved human beings. Both are true. Why should knowing one require concealing the other? And what about the Smithsonian, where generations have gone to discover their country's story? Should the exhibits change according to what each administration finds flattering? History isn't a public relations campaign. Nor should the free press become one: its job is to examine the record, not repeat an administration's preferred version. It's optics. Pure optics. The person most responsible for the recognition of Charles Manson as a quasi-icon of the twenty-first-century radical right is James Mason. Born in 1952, Mason is a lifelong white supremacist activist who joined George Lincoln Rockwell’s American Nazi Party as a teen, then Joseph Tommasi’s more radical National Socialist Liberation Front, then became even more extreme after he took over that party’s newsletter, Siege (1980–86). He dropped off the radar for two decades following his arrests and incarceration in the 1990s but resurfaced in the 2010s after being “recovered” on the neo-Nazi online forum Iron March, and he is presently acknowledged to be the “grandfather” or “godfather” of white power accelerationism, an ideology convinced that the inevitable collapse of society and subsequent founding of a white ethnostate should be sped up through acts of terrorism. Unlike many others in the white power movement, Mason never concealed his racism or tried to make his extremism more palatable for the mainstream. Whether in his writings or interviews, he is almost obstinately open about his convictions. His 1980s publications particularly make it very plain he hates Jews, blacks, and immigrants and loves Hitler, Rockwell, and Tommasi — plus Charles Manson, “a discovery similar only to the discovery I made when I bumped into Adolf Hitler.” Throughout the 1980s, Mason promoted Manson as a “great leader/philosopher” in Siege , with far-right expert Jeffrey Kaplan going so far as to characterize the newsletter as “primarily a vehicle for the glorification of Charles Manson.” Manson and Mason Siege gained no real traction at the time, but eventually its articles were gathered and bound as SIEGE (1992), a book described by scholars of the far right as “required reading” for contemporary neo-Nazis, “a foundational text — one arguably rivalling even Mein Kampf in contemporary fascist gravitas” and “a neo-Nazi Bible,” held responsible for the spread of online “SIEGE culture” and real-world terrorist networks. Today, therefore, this culture’s celebrated predecessors are Hitler, Tommasi, Mason himself, and Manson. And SIEGE is undoubtedly the key source for understanding the idea of Manson that dominates the far right. But to understand Manson’s own ideas during the years Mason was promoting him, the key source is a far less public one, namely, the astonishing correspondence between the two men. Some years after first finding out about Manson, Mason finally reached out. He initially entered into correspondence with Lynette Fromme and Sandra Good, who were at that time still incarcerated themselves and still very close to Manson. They then made the introductions, and in 1981 Mason sent off an initial letter to Manson. Thus began a correspondence that lasted a decade, maybe longer, and that offers an extraordinary point of access into Manson’s thinking during this period, reveals the extent to which it was Manson who determined the content of Mason’s writing in Siege , and also makes clear where the two men’s respective ideas diverge. As Kathleen Belew has shown, it was broadly during this same period, between 1975 and 1995, that white power consolidated into a new social movement. It was not a resurgence of the Ku Klux Klan but a movement that managed to bring together previously distinct and even mutually exclusive right-wing and racist groups. And crucially, unlike earlier right-wing and racist violence, white power was directed against, rather than in defense of, the state. “The white power movement sought revolution and separation — the founding of a racial utopian nation,” writes Belew, who adds that many in the movement “connected ideas of a radical political future with belief in imminent apocalypse.” The Great Divide The principal catalyst here was the Vietnam War or, rather, veterans who brought the war home, because they were traumatized, because they were inappropriately demobilized and reintegrated, and because they felt betrayed by their own government. White supremacist Louis Beam, for example, who is credited with popularizing “leaderless resistance,” was a veteran who by his own account remained forever stuck in the “rice paddies and skies of Vietnam” and who recognized no difference between the enemy abroad and at home (and at home that enemy did include the state). Many in the movement experienced the 1980s as a downturn, and certainly this was true of Mason. Names known to scholars of the radical right pepper his letters, but Mason’s incessant refrain is that the movement is in dire straits. For the far right, the 1980s felt like far less fertile soil than the early twenty-first century would prove to offer, though even then, during that new century’s first decade, Kaplan could still characterize the movement as “tiny and fractious.” But Belew’s more recent retrospective gaze reveals an upward trajectory and a distinctly new, strident radicalism. “The old ways have failed miserably,” Beam wrote in his 1989 Essays of a Klansman . “Out with the conservatives and in with the radicals! Out with plans for compromise and in with plans for the sword!” At that point, Mason had been railing against conservatism in the movement for about a decade, often in radically antipasséist language influenced by Manson. When Mason first began mentioning Manson to others in the movement, it did not go over well. Though some simply dismissed it — “I’ll consider that this is your latest fantasy and that it’ll be gone in a couple of months” — others fretted: “Now you’ve got me scared,” said one, and another, “Does he plan to become a party member?” But for that very reason, according to Mason, Manson could serve as a kind of litmus test, or “threshold,” something to separate the wheat from the chaff: “Manson represents the great divide between those persons who imagine there still are choices to be made casually on the basis of Establishment mores and those who have a profound, individual sense of ‘no going back.’” Besides, there was the matter of brand recognition and impact: “Charles Manson exerts a fascination over Youth today, in the entire West.” So, undeterred by the fact that movement veterans were nervous about having a notorious murderer and madman join their ranks, Mason continued to promote Manson in Siege : “I have done what I could to inject — subtly and overtly — as many of Manson’s ideas into Movement thought as possible,” he wrote in 1986. Universal Order Indeed he had, and frankly it has not been sufficiently appreciated how extensive was Manson’s influence on Mason, a man whom scholars of the far right have characterized as “one of the most influential ideologues to rethink fascism in the face of twenty-first-century realities.” Comparing the correspondence with the newsletter, it is clear that Manson’s language came to shape Mason’s. Mason really leans into Manson’s discourse of authenticity, for example, so that increasingly enemies inside the movement come to be called “fakers” who are “role-playing” and so on, and Manson himself is identified as the exemplary “Non-Faker.” Very obviously too — and this matters in terms of tracking the movement’s new radicalism — Mason’s modernism came from Manson, a man forever obsessed with being “new” and “now” (or “nuness,” his neologism meaning “no past or place in the past and no thought of future”). “The most important word is NEW! You can’t carry the past into a NEW world,” Manson wrote to Mason. This is also why Manson chides him for any idolatry of Rockwell, or of Hitler for that matter: “If you got no dead heroes, you can be yourself more.” “Your [ sic ] dragging the past on your back,” he warns him, then counsels with the rather Baudelairean “if you’re trying to march Wagner’s music back through with a marching band dressed in 1814 garb you wake up negative thought” and “Fat sloppy people in German dress with armbands on don’t help.” Soon Mason can publish a line about “the entire Right Wing” that might as well have been written by Manson: “If they haven’t been living in 1933, they’ve been living in 1865.” Modernism was also why Manson urged Mason to drop the name National Socialist Liberation Front — “That’s preposterous and it has to go,” he quotes Manson — and adopt instead the name Universal Order. “One world. One government. One mind.” One index finger. Universal Order had a long shelf life. “As we move farther towards the Universal Order, we become more on the offensive with our world view. Wild wishes for the end times,” read a Siege Culture website launched in the 2010s. But according to Nikolas Schreck, Manson had already broken with the Universal Order in the late 1980s, and in retrospect one must wonder about the fact that when Manson recommended the name change, he did also insist that Mason swap out his emblem’s right-facing swastika over an automatic weapon for a left-facing swastika between a set of balancing scales. Kindred Spirits Though in the aftermath of the Tate–LaBianca murders no one on the Right recognized themselves in the Family, in retrospect of course one can see why Manson seemed to Mason a kindred spirit, obviously starting with the swastika, even if it did face the wrong way for a Nazi. There was also Manson’s allergy to miscegenation, including repeat references to the figure of the black rapist and the need to “save” white women and children, both well-worn white racist tropes. To the extent that it meant an imminent, apocalyptic war of the races, “Helter Skelter” must have seemed simply a traditional truism to Mason, who overlooked the fact that Manson and the Family believed that black’s victory over white was karma, and instead focused on promoting the Tate–LaBianca murders as a political attack on the degeneration of a formerly white system. But the crucial element was what Mason imagined Manson’s political project to be: a racial-socialist colony in Death Valley . . . TRIBES of White Warriors, bands of White Men with their Women and Children who have drawn together and then pulled away from the System to allow it to fall without taking them with it. Of course, Mason’s embrace of Manson may have looked mad to more traditional American Nazis in the 1980s, which were after all the heyday of Manson’s most infamous performances on national television, but politically none of this was at odds with the increasingly alienated white power movement. In other respects, however, Charles Manson did not fit well within the white power movement. Manson was never an anti-communist, and to put it mildly, he disliked the (Christian) church. His “Family” was far from nuclear, he promoted free love, and he was neither straight nor cisgender. He and the Family partook in the romanticization of the Old West, imagining themselves as cowboys and Indians and pioneers at Spahn’s and in Death Valley, but unlike white power ideologues, Manson never saw this land as “empty.” And while Manson was a fan of the founding fathers, he was sooner a supporter of Mexican revolutionary Pancho Villa than of the movement-supported Contras in Nicaragua. So even if he was happy to erase the boundaries between war and peace at home, Manson was not a supporter of American empire. Emptying the Zoo Above all, Manson did not overlap with the white power movement on the environment, so that although Mason adopted Manson’s Death Valley–based survivalism, environmental degradation had little to do with it. And this also filtered right into their most basic divergence, which Mason summed up in a 1983 issue of Siege as follows: The Manson people look upon and accept the NS Movement in America with curiosity, some admiration and some dismay. “Hate,” they say, “is not going to get it.” Trying to live in another time and place isn’t going to get it either. To our charges of a monstrous, Jewish conspiracy, they simply counter with the stated belief that people today have a death wish for themselves. We say that without unpolluted, White blood, there’ll be no civilization left. They say that without an unpolluted world, there can be no White Race . . . or any other race for that matter. Indeed, in multiple letters, Manson corrected what he thought was Mason’s understanding of Manson’s views on race or otherwise differentiated those views from Mason’s. Mason’s views are quite straightforward, informed by a white supremacist means-end utilitarianism. Manson’s views, meanwhile, remained contradictory as ever. On the one hand, he continued to believe that races are best maintained rather than mixed and, as of old, recommended that one should only have sex by race: “Only put that rod in something that looks like you,” he writes, and “I’ve only fucked white guys” (though he immediately adds he also “fucked a phony Jew when I didn’t have better sense” and in another letter that he “even fucked a cow once”). But as in the 1960s, he argued that this was as much to preserve other races as it was the white race because, historically, miscegenation had led to the disappearance of people of color. Plus he continued to hold whites in contempt above all others: “There is no white people in the truth,” he writes, and “I get more respect from black brown yellow + red more than I do my own,” or he might snark about “this white pride B.S.” He insists that, from fear of black people, whites use other whites to defend themselves — “You wouldn’t believe the things they have done to me with their fear of black people” — though often, such whites appear in his letters as “PJs” and “PCs,” meaning “phony Jews” and “phony Christians,” that is, categorized along lines of authenticity. He tells Mason that he has a friend who is Jewish, and that he feels “they have a place in this world. The real Jews do.” “Look,” he writes in 1986, “I never ask my people to hate anyone because of race.” In fact, as in the 1960s, Manson’s imagination still settled on a “separate but equal” elite order, but by the 1980s it had hypertrophied, somehow become even more maximalist and militarized. So, whereas James Mason would “send black back” — to Africa presumably — Manson countered with this vision: I would make them the ZZ like the SS only worse (better). The white SS & the Black SS and then do away with the PJs and PCs in the middle. Then I’d put Green back on the land with the air force & army & the navy [&] would empty the zoo & all wildlife back [&] make a garden & only there children could return to earth garden. This is an astonishing rainbow-fascist, eco-totalitarian dream of epic, even biblical, proportions. But it’s not King Charlie ruling a black planet, nor the James Mason–inspired whites-only world promoted by SIEGE culture. Outstripping Manson If initially Manson was too much for Mason’s veterans in the neo-Nazi movement, perhaps ultimately the movement was too much for Manson. Racist and separatist though he was, Manson just never advocated for the elimination of people of color. Aside from the fact that the most important fault lines in his thinking were determined not by race but by what was “real” — and obviously he had no problem with the idea of eliminating an entire world of “phonies” — Manson was also simply too antiwhite. That does not mean that Manson’s letters, at least during the 1980s, were not crammed with racist remarks and antisemitic tropes, because they most certainly were. And in fact, even if part of what the expression “PJs and PCs” captured was whiteness, one suspects that because whiteness was also firmly tied to “money,” the phony/real binary had by the 1980s also become entangled with antisemitism for Manson, though arguably one could read all this as a class-based critique too, because Manson often argued that the powerful used race to keep down the powerless. Finally, for the record: Manson’s interest in Adolf Hitler during the 1960s has been overstated — there exists only the most minimal evidence — but it is impossible to read the letters he wrote to James Mason and come away with anything but the certain knowledge that “AH” was one of Manson’s most prominent discursive tics during that period. Nonetheless, Manson’s views diverge from Mason, neo-Nazism, the far right, and SIEGE culture. It seems important to mark the differences, not least so as to be able to delineate the various strands that make up white radicalism in late modern American culture, and because maybe it matters that in some respects contemporary white supremacism outstrips the extremism of a man whose very face has long been a symbol of the country’s most radical racism. Officials in the Biden administration and others who had been tracking Lebanon for a very long time were extremely worried about what it would mean for Israel to open a two-front war, in view of Israel’s past record in Lebanon, which has been absolutely devastating. They were also concerned that attacking Hezbollah might bring Iran into the conflict. I chart in the book how Biden and his National Security Advisor Jake Sullivan got the Israelis to back down. They told them the intelligence that suggested Hezbollah was akin to Hamas and wanted to do something like October 7 was faulty. The ability of the United States to restrain Israel was there from the very start. Daniel Finn If we look at that opening period from October to December 2023, when there was a short-lived truce, what was the thinking of people in the Biden administration about how this was going to end? What was the ultimate goal that they believed Israel could legitimately be striving for? Akbar Shahid Ahmed One official told me about their thinking in mid-November, right on the cusp of that truce. He said, “If our goal is to bring home the Israelis (and some American citizens, too) who were captured on October 7, and to be on the way toward replacing Hamas rule with something else in Gaza, we can achieve that. We can focus on that and also rein in the bloodshed and convince Israel to adhere to the laws of war.” That would have been a vision that took Palestinian life into account, at least to a limited degree. It would certainly still have been rooted in US and Israeli primacy. But what happened instead was something very different. You saw the truce collapse in early December, after some Israelis had been released in exchange for some Palestinians, and there was a surge in humanitarian aid. Biden administration officials then very quickly hewed back to the Israeli narrative of a military-first approach. The mediating countries like Qatar and Egypt saw the collapse of the truce as a very important and chilling sign. One official said to me that Hamas was not going to take the Americans seriously for a while after that, because Israel and the United States presented the breakdown of the truce as evidence of Hamas malfeasance. One of the most disturbing aspects of this was an effort to claim, without any evidence, that Hamas had unilaterally broken the truce in order to keep Israeli hostages so it could continue sexually assaulting them. This was a line pushed out by the White House almost immediately, while Israel’s mission to the United Nations, in coordination with Sheryl Sandberg, also shone a light on claims of sexual assault on October 7. The claim was so disturbing that the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) itself put out a statement saying, “Please don’t comment on what you think might be happening with these hostages — it’s very disturbing to their families.” Hamas walked away from the abortive truce feeling quite burned. What you saw next were hollow US promises that somehow the Israeli campaign in Gaza was going to change. National Security Advisor Jake Sullivan went to Israel soon afterward and said that this was moving into a lower-intensity campaign. That is definitely not what happened. What we saw instead was US complicity in a severe assault on Gaza City, a very similar assault on Khan Yunis, and eventually the destruction of Rafah, the southernmost town in Gaza. For these officials, the truce period didn’t change their root desire to have a no-daylight policy with Israel — what they described as the “bear hug.” In some cases, notably that of Brett McGurk, they also began to link the idea of a ceasefire with the exchange of prisoners and the supply of humanitarian aid to the moon-shot notion of a regional overhaul, expanding on the Abraham Accords to include Saudi Arabia. Instead of concentrating on the sudden outbreak of conflict, McGurk wanted the United States to hew back to bigger strategic aims from before October 7, when many experts — and even Joe Biden himself — said that those aims may have fueled Palestinian desperation and backlash. He ignored the people in and out of government who were saying that you had to take this step by step: the legal aspect, the humanitarian aspect, the strategic aspect. Daniel Finn In November 2023, Biden made a public statement saying that he had no confidence in the casualty figures coming out of Gaza. A number of reporters (including you) showed at the time that, in private, the State Department considered the figures from the Gaza health ministry to be reliable. Were Biden’s comments the result of a premeditated decision by the administration to endorse this claim about the unreliability of the figures? Were there any second thoughts afterward? Akbar Shahid Ahmed I’ve asked Biden’s spokespeople, right up until the summer of 2026, if he wants to retract specifically his expression of doubt about the civilian casualty figures, as well as his claim that there were “forty beheaded babies” on October 7, which has not been validated by any kind of intelligence. He has not indicated his desire to do that — his spokespeople haven’t engaged. I did not find evidence that it was premeditated. It certainly led to a scramble, because, as I reported at the time, the State Department was consistently using these numbers without attaching caveats to them. What then happened, with the validation of the president, is that you saw efforts by hard-line pro-Israel supporters to make it impossible for the US government to use these numbers. It’s an instance for me of how the Biden administration had a contradictory policy, undercutting what they said they wanted to happen. Not long before making that comment, Biden had said that we should not confuse Hamas with the Palestinian people’s right to self-determination. But administration officials very often hewed back to their core tendencies. Biden is a lifelong Zionist with a real sense of skepticism toward Palestinians. Other figures were seeking to downplay the death toll among Palestinian civilians and then among Lebanese civilians as well. One diplomat told me that after October 7, the Biden administration was suddenly very interested in what was happening among Palestinians after a period of neglect, asking how the Gazans were living, what was happening, etc. But, as the official put it, the instinct was already to dispute whether it was as bad as the Palestinians were saying, and that was before Biden’s comment. Jake Sullivan and Brett McGurk repeatedly said to their colleagues that “the Palestinians don’t look skinny in the photos.” This was after the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC), which sets the gold standard for measuring food security, had already stated that Gaza had the highest proportion of a population experiencing acute food insecurity that they had ever tracked. Officials will sometimes present this as a case of repeating the Israeli claims in order to bulletproof them, suggesting that that they were asking for data to either back up or challenge what they were being told by Netanyahu. However, the effect was very much to say that there was a real sense of doubt at the top. This made it possible to downplay many of the most devastating incidents. Less than a month after Biden’s press conference, the United States was also promoting unproven Israeli claims that the Al-Shifa hospital in Gaza City was a Hamas command and control center. This was in spite of the fact that the intelligence US officials themselves had access to in relation to Al-Shifa did not back up those claims. Daniel Finn The period after the end of the first truce was also the period when the International Court of Justice (ICJ) held its first hearings over the case brought by South Africa, accusing Israel of violating the Genocide Convention. More or less simultaneously, this was when Israel launched a public campaign against the United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugess in the Near East (UNRWA), accusing it of collaborating with Hamas. The Biden administration and the governments of countries like Britain, Germany, and Australia responded by immediately suspending funding for UNRWA. Was there any hesitation in the US government about taking the Israeli claims at face value? Akbar Shahid Ahmed US officials were aware that Israel was going to make these claims about UNRWA. From my reporting, I have not found evidence that they knew it was going to happen on the day of the ICJ’s initial ruling, but they were aware this was coming. There were meetings between UNRWA and top US officials in the week before Israel went public with its claims. UNRWA management had felt that by firing some employees who were accused by Israel and announcing that they would do an investigation, they might satiate to a degree the Israeli desire to crack down on them. Of course, it was clear to everyone involved that UNRWA was the backbone of the humanitarian operation in Gaza. There were thousands of aid workers operating despite severe restrictions imposed by Israel, but all of them relied on UNRWA’s network of warehouses, trucks, and local contacts. Biden administration figures argued to me that giving credence to the Israeli narrative was a way to shield UNRWA by saying, “We are taking this seriously, we are suspending some funding, but that doesn’t mean we are outright denying UNRWA’s relevance or its importance.” But the effect was to trigger the domino effect you described — where US allies also suspended their funding, creating a major crunch for UNRWA as it faced the biggest need for its services in its history — and to embolden hard-line Israel supporters in Congress. There were Biden administration officials — figures like Julieta Noyes, the head of the State Department’s humanitarian aid bureau at the time — who intensely lobbied Congress not to pass new restrictions on US ties to UNRWA. But the administration had given Congress the ammunition it needed, so by April 2024, there was a ban on US funding for UNRWA that remains in place to this day. This also illustrates the limited willingness of these officials to use their leverage. People like Noyes didn’t resign from their posts — they said they were having greater influence on the inside. But many of those who stayed inside didn’t use pressure points that were available to them. For example, humanitarian experts said that Noyes could have triggered very serious investigations at the State Department and chose not to do that. Daniel Finn At one point during 2024, US government officials were publicly discouraging Israel from going into Rafah, suggesting that it was a red line for them. But when Israel went into Rafah anyway, there were no significant consequences. At the same time, there was a moment when Biden imposed restrictions on certain bomb shipments to Israel. What impact did those restrictions have? Akbar Shahid Ahmed First of all, it’s important to stress that applying basic standards of international law or US law is not meant to be optional. So many of these officials were operating in a post-9/11 US policy structure that often treats the law as optional and “leaves space for the policymaker,” as people in Washington put it. There was an incident in December 2023 where a munitions expert told Secretary of State Tony Blinken that there was no way the Israelis could use two-thousand-pound bombs in Gaza that would be discriminate and proportionate. But instead of talking about the relevant legislation, Blinken responded by saying, “You have to understand the Israelis are so traumatized — we can’t push them at this moment.” There was a serious red line over Rafah. The president said it repeatedly. Blinken himself said it to Netanyahu at the end of April: “If you treat Rafah the way you have treated the other parts of Gaza, our entire relationship with you could change.” Plans were drawn up. But the way that the Biden administration went about using its leverage was almost certain to doom their stated goals. The holdup of one shipment that you mentioned, consisting of two-thousand-pound bombs and some five-hundred-pound bombs, came out of an ad hoc process. In fact, I was told by one official whose job it was to explain and justify this policy that they heard about it from the news. When Israel leaked the news of this holdup, the Biden administration “folded like a tent,” in the words of Ben Rhodes, who served under Barack Obama. While Israel was violating Biden’s red line over Rafah, where more than 1.4 million Palestinians were sheltering, and simultaneously complaining about US pressure, the Biden administration helped them. Biden’s officials very quickly began telling people, “The Israelis do seem to be heeding what we’re saying.” They unveiled new weapons transfers in this period. The humanitarian toll in Rafah was entirely predictable and predicted. Biden himself made a commitment, saying to the Israelis, “You have to have a credible plan for dealing with the Palestinians pushed out of Rafah.” This came after a clear demonstration of how US leverage could have been used following the killing of World Central Kitchen aid workers in April 2024. Biden made demands about humanitarian aid, and Netanyahu complied, because Biden was taking a more threatening tone. You had a broader discussion about a red line and about cutting off weapons. Many US officials I spoke to, even at the highest levels, said that April 2024 was when they realized they could treat Israel like any other country and have influence over it like any other country, as one of them put it to me. This was in spite of the pressure they were facing from pro-Israel forces — they were willing to stand up in that moment. Importantly, they worked with the many other people in Congress and in the Democratic Party who were horrified about what had happened, instead of distancing themselves from the criticism. That’s when the leverage was effective. The fact that Rafah came right after that is to me extremely damning, because Biden knew the costs and knew what he could do to prevent it. There was a step-by-step choice by US officials, from Jake Sullivan to the White House Spokesperson John Kirby, to downplay the horrors of what was happening in Rafah. This was a period when thousands of people were being killed and which led directly to the current situation, where more than 1.6 million people still have nowhere to live. Daniel Finn Of course, 2024 was an election year, which had to be weighing on the thinking of everyone, including Biden himself. Before coming to the election directly, what was the response of the Biden administration and the Democratic Party leadership more broadly to the protests that were taking place in solidarity with the people of Gaza, especially the protests that were happening on university campuses? Akbar Shahid Ahmed There was very little sympathy in the Biden administration for the protesters. There were protests on college campuses but also at campaign rallies where Biden and Kamala Harris were appearing. There was a protest vote by the Uncommitted movement in the Democratic primary season. The Biden administration and the Democratic Party establishment engaged to a degree with some of the Uncommitted figures in the spring of 2024. But the broader protest movement was very often cast as being filled with Hamas sympathizers. Some officials, like Blinken or Jessica Lewis, the State Department official who oversaw weapons transfers, had protesters outside their homes, and their response was to be more stubborn. Instead of asking, “Why are people showing up at my house?” the response was, “How dare you?” It speaks to this broader hubris with the Biden administration. It is those students whose warnings that the United States was going to be complicit in one of the great disasters of the era who proved to be correct. But the administration’s choice was to argue that the protest movement was evidence of disorder and to embrace the crackdowns. As Mahmoud Khalil put it to me, this laid the groundwork for Khalil and other student protesters to be targeted by the Trump administration. It’s another instance of the Biden administration helping to undercut the values they said that they wanted to shield, because they were at the same time saying, “We respect dissent, we respect free speech.” There was also a very real sense that certain constituencies in the Democratic electoral tent simply did not matter. I can’t tell you how many times I heard Democratic Party operatives say, “Are Muslim and Arab voters really going to vote for Donald Trump, who’s proposed the Muslim ban?” Of course, there was also an element of “Let’s just get through the election.” This became dominant after Kamala Harris replaced Biden as the nominee. I was told by one Democratic congressional aide who was quite involved in trying to pressure the Biden administration over Gaza, “Let’s just win the election, get through, and see what happens.” Of course, many, many lives would be lost in that period with US support. Daniel Finn Was there serious concern in those circles that this might damage the Democratic Party in the fall elections? What was their attitude in particular to the Uncommitted movement, which was attempting to put Gaza on the agenda through the Democratic primaries in which Biden was standing? Akbar Shahid Ahmed The Uncommitted movement was a classic “work inside the tent” effort. They repeatedly said, “We are lifelong Democrats — we want to defeat Donald Trump — help us make that possible.” That was also the message from figures like Congresswoman Ilhan Omar of Minnesota. Those Democratic critics were not saying, “Trump is offering something better.” It speaks to the influence of a very dated brain trust around Biden, comprising figures like Steve Ricchetti, Mike Donilon, and Anita Dunn. They promoted the idea that if the Biden administration questioned Israel, they would be seen as weak and lose crucial donors and voter blocs. There’s sometimes a sense that this was being driven by hard-line pro-Israel supporters and donors. But it was also symbiotic with a broader Democratic Party donor class that essentially said, “Don’t rock the boat — this doesn’t matter enough.” It wasn’t just people who had Israel at the top of their minds. It was a donor class that has been deeply entrenched, going back to the Clinton era, with ideas that the Democrats must be seen as strong on the world stage and strong in military terms. The narrative was very much, “We can’t afford to be seen to be abandoning our ally Israel in the face of Hamas and Iran.” Of course, that missed the existence of growing antiwar sentiment that people like Donald Trump or J. D. Vance were actually speaking to. Trump was saying, “Look at the mess in Ukraine, look at the mess in Gaza — I’ll solve this on the world stage.” Meanwhile, the Biden administration was saying, “We’re going to continue supporting our partners.” Democrats have positioned themselves as a party of diplomacy with Iran rather than war with Iran. But in 2024, it was the Biden administration’s consistent choice to let Israel escalate vis-à-vis Iran as well as in Lebanon. This was encapsulated by statements like the one from Kamala Harris at the convention, when she said, “We want to build the most lethal fighting force in the history of the world.” Daniel Finn There was a lengthy piece in the New Yorker over the summer that included the lines of justification from some of the Biden administration officials like Tony Blinken and Brett McGurk. They claimed that they didn’t want to get into a confrontation with Israel, because they thought the Israelis were going to end their campaign in Gaza soon anyway. Then, when they began to suspect that the Israelis weren’t being entirely straight when they said it was going to be over in the next two weeks, they didn’t want to get into a confrontation with them because they thought they were on the brink of an agreed ceasefire. McGurk has been suggesting that every time there was any public tension between the US and Israeli governments, that made a ceasefire less likely. What do you make of those lines of justification based on their own reporting and research? Akbar Shahid Ahmed The New Yorker piece was very interesting to me. The New Yorker had the galleys of my book for four months by the time they published that piece, so I have to suspect that they took a lot of what was in my reporting and brought it to these officials. I think those public claims should be taken with a tremendous grain of salt. There’s a real reductionism in these post facto claims of Biden administration figures that they couldn’t have done anything differently. It eludes the fact that there were so many choices up and down the system that might have forced Israel to behave differently. I’m thinking here about lawyers who pointed out to me that in other conflicts, the United States very often asks partner countries for written assurances over how they’ll use American weapons. That doesn’t necessarily prevent them from committing war crimes or abuses or violations. But it is a line in the sand that is drawn. Importantly, it’s also something that becomes a question of liability and complicity for US officials. Are you saying at the get-go, “We really don’t want to be part of war crimes”? That choice was not made in this instance. In fact, there was a choice to tightly limit the flow of intelligence, which made it harder for humanitarian and diplomatic experts to establish what the United States knew about Israel’s intentions and actions. For instance, the Biden administration instituted a higher standard for access to intelligence for officials on this conflict than they did on Sudan, Ukraine, or anything else, saying you need a cabinet member’s approval. There was also a real effort to tie together the effort for a ceasefire to the humanitarian aid operation, which every aid expert and relief expert will tell you fuels an abnormal process that leads to the loss of life. They were receiving warnings repeatedly from their own experts saying what was possible and what routes should have been taken. I think about moments like the report in April–May 2024 that was negotiated by the administration with Chris van Hollen, their chief Democratic critic in Congress. It was a chance for the Biden administration to state frankly that the Israelis were blocking aid, as everyone said they were, and to weigh in on the legality of some of these actions. Instead, Blinken and those around him, like David Satterfield, Biden’s humanitarian envoy, chose to write a report that horrified experts. It cleared Israel of accusations of restricting aid by cherry-picking one month rather than the entire period from October 7. This allowed the flow of weapons to continue. It’s not the case that these officials were babes in the woods or that they thought the ceasefire was suddenly going to be achievable. There were so many other steps they could have taken. I want to address this claim from Brett McGurk that Hamas was being empowered and emboldened any time there was criticism of Israel. What we know from so much reporting and from my discussions with US officials, with Israelis, with the Qatari mediators, and with Hamas figures who I’ve interviewed, is that it was Netanyahu who was the stubborn one. It’s offensive to so many of us who have tracked this policy over two years to suddenly try to reframe it as if Hamas was the obstacle. The Biden administration itself repeatedly said, “We wish that Netanyahu would agree to a deal — we’re so close to a deal.” I highlight a kind of sliding-doors moment for many officials in August 2024 when they felt a deal was achievable. The figure that walked away and added new conditions in that period was Netanyahu. It was the Israeli side. There’s still an effort by those officials to rewrite the history. I interpret that in terms of them still wanting to work within the framework of how Washington does this and not wanting to question the overall US-Israel relationship. It’s easy to make Hamas the bogeyman and to say, “Give us another shot — trust us again.” Daniel Finn Going back to the question of domestic politics, when Biden made way for Kamala Harris in summer 2024, there was hope in some circles that this could be an opportunity for a change of line over Gaza. Was there a missed opportunity at that point, and if so, why? Akbar Shahid Ahmed Harris did express discomfort at certain aspects of the policy. Based on my reporting and that of others, she sometimes urged Biden to include rhetoric that was more sympathetic to Palestinians, and she expressed concern about Islamophobia. That said, Harris never demonstrated a real desire to break with Biden over Gaza, even privately, or to endorse a different policy in that way some figures in the cabinet did, when they were allowed to see Biden by Sullivan and McGurk. Was there an opportunity? Some of the figures around her, such as Phil Gordon and Ilan Goldenberg, are now attacking the way Brett McGurk is presenting the Biden-era policy and suggesting a very different US role in Israel/Palestine. I do think that some of her advisers could have offered her a different road map. There was a real debate right up until the convention, with donors and political operatives trying to get Harris to stake out a different position. Some of that was rooted in her potentially deeper empathy, and some of it was rooted in the fact that she was a lawyer. By this point, there was so much legal evidence that something was deeply wrong with the US-backed Israeli campaign. The Harris team will argue that they were in a campaign that was 98 percent Bidenland. There was deep caution. Again, there was a real sense of Democrats looking over their shoulder and wondering, “Will we be seen as weak?” Interestingly, in view of the party’s talk about equity, there was a real argument that a female candidate in particular could not be seen as insufficiently hawkish. Harris’s team chose not to take the advice for a change of line. I’ve asked them. right up until a couple of weeks before I released this book, if they wanted to make any statement of what she should have done differently — any statement of regret. There have been rumors, for instance, that Harris is willing to say they should have had a Palestinian American speaker on the stage at the Democratic National Convention. But she has not been willing to do that so far. There is some discussion about whether she might have a different policy speech in November 2026, after the midterms. But I think a lot of people will be saying, “What did you do at the time you had the power?” Don’t forget, it wasn’t just a question of what might happen if she won the election. It was also the fact that there were worsening conditions in Gaza during October and November 2024 while she was running. Israel subsequently launched a military intervention in Lebanon, and Harris didn’t put forward a different position on that, either. Daniel Finn After Harris lost the election, Biden and his officials still had the powers of their offices in the period before Trump’s inauguration. There is certainly no taboo from previous administrations against taking significant initiatives in the last days of a presidency: for example, in the dying weeks of the first Trump administration, there was a move to recognize Moroccan sovereignty over Western Sahara, which was linked to support for Israel through the Abraham Accords. Biden never reversed that decision, and it remains in place to the present day. Was there any consideration given by people in the Biden administration to making moves in relation to Gaza that might preempt what was likely to be coming under Trump and his team? Akbar Shahid Ahmed There were very significant, high-level discussions not long after the election about taking a different approach based on new intelligence that had come up the chain. Even Biden looked at this intelligence, which suggested Israeli knowledge of being part of violations of international law and pointed to US complicity in war crimes and the personal liability of US officials. These were very serious discussions that happened right at the top. The intelligence was taken seriously, and the analysis was seen as being relevant. For more than a year, you had lawyers and officials inside the US government gathering evidence and having heated discussions about whether Israel was adhering to the law. The sense from the White House and from Blinken’s team was always, “We don’t want to see a firm conclusion.” That was how many people involved described it to me. What you had were these heated discussions, including in that lame-duck period, going on for hours, where you might have a clear majority of the so-called interagency lawyers’ group say, “This is a war crime — let’s call it as such.” However, there was also a culture of — as one State Department lawyer put it to me — feeling that no one wanted to rock the boat. They had worked together previously, and they would probably work together again. Why be that person? So firm conclusions were not reached. There were some clear recommendations that Blinken in particular did receive to specifically sanction Israeli units involved in the alleged mistreatment and sexual assault of Palestinian detainees from Gaza. There was video evidence suggesting abuse. Blinken had on his desk recommendations to sanction two Israeli military units that he never acted on. Instead the Biden administration chose to be a kind of handmaiden to the incoming Trump administration. They had different reasons for doing so. Brett McGurk was actively seeking a job in the second Trump presidency, having worked for the first one. In this period, he saw a line of total continuity between what he had worked on under Trump’s first term and his approach during the Biden administration. Having worked on the Abraham Accords under Trump, he was the person who repeatedly made the idea of a grand strategic deal expanding the accords to cover Saudi Arabia the central issue, rather than aid or peace. He was the one who effectively behaved as Israel’s lawyer in internal discussions, as many people told me. For others, there’s such a fixation in Washington with projecting bipartisan comity. There’s an idea that it’s great to think that politics stops at the water’s edge. It’s a staggering approach, when you think that the Biden administration a couple of days prior had been saying that Trump was a threat to everything they held dear, and they were very scared of what was going to happen. I think about a moment where Jake Sullivan appeared with Trump’s incoming National Security Advisor at the US Institute of Peace a couple of days before the handover, and the message was very much, “Isn’t it great that we’re sitting here together? Be assured that the United States is not vulnerable.” Of course, the Trump administration shut down that institute a couple of days afterward. It was very clear that their approach to foreign policy was going to throw out these liberal values and do even more damage to a deliberative and professional process than the Biden administration had already done. What the outgoing officials chose to do was to treat these very shaky ceasefires in Lebanon and in Gaza as huge triumphs and try to take a lot of credit for them. They also took credit for the fall of Bashar al-Assad in December 2024, which they did not predict, and they tried to go out on a narrative of humungous success. They would argue, “Look, we got out those hostages at the end, and we got Palestinian prisoners released.” But of course, they also paved the way for Netanyahu to unilaterally reignite the war in Gaza in March 2025. For instance, the Biden administration vetoed internal recommendations to have a new UN Security Council resolution to try to enshrine the Gaza ceasefire. There were also moments when McGurk was joking about claims that there were violations of the ceasefire in Lebanon. The Biden administration chose not to even symbolically apply pressure and say, “We see the situation as it is, and let’s be frank about the law here.” Daniel Finn What is your impression of how the various protagonists now see their role in all of this? I’m thinking not just of people who were directly involved in the Biden administration, but also more broadly figures in the congressional leadership of the Democratic Party who had a significant role to play in this? Akbar Shahid Ahmed There’s a real fight among senior Democrats in the apparatus of the party, people who are in fundraising roles, about whether Gaza will matter. There’s a hope among many figures who have had positions of power going back to the Clinton era that this will prove to be a blip. Of course, the rise of Melat Kiros in Denver, where I’m speaking from, or Zohran Mamdani in New York suggests that there’s a serious move to make this a constant fixture in Democratic politics. There are officials from the Biden era who badly want future jobs and future influence. Blinken is on the board of the Center for American Progress, a key think tank in the Democratic Party’s evolution. Jon Finer and Jake Sullivan are very much in the public discourse. Samantha Power, who was United States Agency for International Development chief at the time, is very active in discussions about the future of humanitarian aid, as is Jack Lew, the US ambassador to Israel under Biden, and a deeply controversial figure about whom there’s a lot in my book. I think some figures do have some deep regret. Power, for instance, spoke to me on the record about concerns she had on the inside, about what she witnessed, and about how she felt the Biden administration was missing the forest for the trees in its dealings with the Israelis, with horrific humanitarian consequences for Palestinians and eventually for the Lebanese. But there’s still a culture of Washington shielding its own. Power refused to criticize specific decisions by her colleagues. McGurk, who’s out now with his new book, is being feted in the Atlantic and the New York Times and on CBS as a sage national security expert. It’s surreal to see how he’s being allowed to push the line that the Biden administration prevented war with Iran, when they were responsible for many of the Israeli escalations that crossed red lines and made a conflict easier. They were also responsible for the US military buildup and planning that enabled Trump’s war on Iran. But there’s a desire to lauder the reputations of these figures. Although some of them will express regret, like Jon Finer or some of those figures around Kamala Harris, it still avoids a direct reckoning. Finer said in an interview, “If only the lawyers had brought us firm conclusions.” But I talked earlier about the environment that was created and how, as one lawyer put it to me, “If they wanted us to find a war crime, we could have done so.” What’s bizarre to me, as I do this book tour and try to shine a light on all these figures, is the way that reputation management is still happening. Earlier this week, I received texts from two separate Biden administration figures, both quite senior, saying, “You were too harsh on this figure, and this one deserved more attention, and also, you should represent me differently in the following ways.” It’s a question of personal hubris and careerism rather than a real look at Gaza through the same lens that many of us see it, as a man-made nightmare that has made the world more dangerous for us all. It’s still much more — and especially in Washington, because of how the culture operates — a question of who can we deflect the blame to: Is it Hamas? Is it Biden as he fades from the scene? How can we obscure the patterns of decision-making? That’s what I hope Crossing The Red Line highlights: individuals do matter, but the processes that made this possible and that made Trump’s more damaging policies possible, are what really needs to be understood. フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Rochester Red Star: News from Rochester DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-14T13:59:07+00:00

by Vincent Lima In our September General Meeting, members of ROC DSA wisely rejected a proposal that would restrict their right to vote on certain key decisions. The rejected proposal appeared to be part of a broader movement to shrink the voting base of DSA. Our process for considering applications for the endorsement of candidates for elected office has been tweaked from time to time, but the main steps have remained: The candidate submits an application signed by a certain number of ROC DSA members. The application includes a questionnaire. The signature requirement signifies some baseline level of support and at least hints at the candidate’s ability to collect signatures on a petition. In reality, the chapter does not passively await applications, but the application is what formally kicks off the process. The Electoral Working Group vets the applicant. The group is open to all members. Thus, all members can engage on this level, but no member is required to do so. The EWG makes a recommendation regarding endorsement and forwards the package to the Steering Committee. A candidate forum is scheduled. Every member is welcome to listen to and interact with the candidate. This process should include deliberation. In other words, members should be able to discuss their thoughts about the candidacy in light of the forum and in light of the chapter’s broader strategic goals. The Steering Committee, which is the chapter’s elected political leadership, makes a recommendation. A ballot is sent to all members to vote Yes or No on the endorsement. Crucially, the ballot includes links to the candidate’s application, the minutes of the candidate forum, and the recommendations of the EWG and Steering Committee. The step that has been made contentious is the last. The proposal was to restrict voting on the endorsement to members who attended the deliberative meeting. This proposal to restrict the vote is made in the name of democracy. I will turn to Marx for a moment—not because he is necessary for this argument but because some advocates for limiting the franchise claim to be Marxists and argue that a universal franchise is a mockery of democracy; it is merely “bourgeois democracy.” Marx appreciated “bourgeois democracy” for extending the franchise (the right to vote) but noted accurately that not everyone has the time and inclination to engage in the democratic process. “Universal Suffrage is the equivalent for political power for the working class,” Marx wrote in the New-York Daily Tribute in 1852. In Capital, vol. 1 (1867; trans. Paul Reitter, 2024), he added (p. 235), A worker self-evidently lives [their] whole life as nothing but labor-power. Thus, by nature and by right, all [their] disposable time is labor-time that belongs to capital’s process of self-valorization. Time spent on human and intellectual growth or social functions and interactions or the free play of vital physical and mental powers, or even time spent resting on Sundays—it’s all just a waste! Lenin, in State and Revolution (1917), elaborated: “Owing to the conditions of capitalist exploitation, the modern wage slaves are so crushed by want and poverty that they ‘cannot be bothered with democracy,’ and ‘cannot be bothered with politics’; in the ordinary, peaceful course of events, the majority of the population is debarred from participation in public and political life.” Thanks to organized labor and popular movements, many of us have more time for “public and political life” than the workers described above. Still, to say, “If you can’t make the meeting, you can’t vote,” is not to take a Marxist position. The counterargument is that democracy is not just about voting; it is more about process than outcome. Indeed, it is crucially important to provide every opportunity for members to participate in the process. But voting is not a reward for participation; it is an element of participation. How do we make it possible for comrades who have to engage in social production and reproduction to participate in the democratic process? Or, in normal-people language, if you have to make a living and take care of yourself or your dependents—by going to school, caring for a child, looking after an elder, going on vacation, or whatever you will—do we tell you, “Too bad; you are canceled”? How do we include everyone? Our hybrid meetings are a great step. You can’t attend in person? Attend remotely and save the time and expense it would take to commute, or participate while keeping an eye on the baby. Our Slack debates are not great; they often consist of walls of text dominated mostly by a handful of men with time on their hands. But Slack is still an available venue for debate. Offline conversations, too, are part of member engagement. Say I pick up the phone and contact a fellow member to say, “Mary is running and I think we should back her. What do you think? OK. Don’t forget to vote when the ballot comes!” That is exactly what we should be doing to involve people in our campaigns! Showing up to meetings is but one form of involvement. I do it. Maybe you do it. But it’s not for everyone. Some members may not have the time, as discussed above. Others may just not want to do it. Maybe listening to another speech “in favor” is more than they can bear. Maybe Robert’s Rules get under their skin and all they want to do is knock on doors and talk to their neighbors about socialism. I embrace them and welcome them and insist that they have a vote. In the crosshairs of AI

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: Tempest ／ 著者: Ashley Smith ／ 公開日時: 2026-10-14T12:02:03+00:00

I n their mad rush for profit and power, the tech oligarchs have unleashed artificial intelligence (AI) into nearly every corner of life on this planet. Firms are investing billions in data centers, which house the infrastructure to process the enormous amounts of information that power AI. They are also plowing ever more money into research and development. Their hope is that the technology will overcome global capitalism’s slump , which the system has been trapped in since the Great Recession, and trigger a new epoch of expansion. States are incorporating it into their police forces for domestic surveillance as well as their militaries for cyberwarfare and high-tech weapons. Ominously, the struggle for AI supremacy is now at the center of the imperialist rivalry between the U.S. and China. The widespread, unregulated use of AI is already having devastating impacts on our environment, jobs, and rights. Some worry that it threatens our very species with extinction . In this three-part interview, Tempest Collective’s Ashley Smith asks two advanced tech workers, Josh and Yasmine, about the technology, its much hyped promise and threat, and its impact on our lives. Most importantly, they lay out how the Left and labor movement should respond and what we should demand and fight for. Part One: “Criti-hype,” AI Breakthroughs, and Betting on Profit Ashley Smith: Before we discuss the widespread panic about AI, let’s get the basics out on the table. For the layperson, what is AI? How does it work? What advances are being made toward artificial general intelligence? Is that even possible? Josh: AI is a general term that started as hype, and continues to operate that way, but it also stands for multiple things that are real. Today, it mostly refers to neural nets such as the large language and multi-media models like ChatGPT and Claude, which we most frequently encounter through chat bots and coding agents. Other types of AI, such as AlphaGo (plays go) and AlphaFold (folds proteins) use different ways to train neural nets to achieve their given goals. There are many other types of AI that fall under the moniker of GOFAI, or Good Old Fashioned AI, which use Symbolic AI and other more deterministic methods for building predictive systems. There have been many accounts of how LLMs and the transformer architectures work. I would sum these up this way. High-tech engineers set up a neural net, which is a huge matrix of random numbers (weights) arranged in a grid with inputs at one end and outputs at the other. Words are converted to streams of numbers that are then passed through the network and arrive at a prediction for the next best output in the stream. This output is compared with the actual next number in the stream, and if it is different, then it adjusts the weights so that it would be more likely to give that answer the next time. In order to get a sensible next word in a sentence, some pretty wild calculations have to be made. It is one thing to predict the next word in “A fire engine is ___” and another to answer the question: “What is the answer to this math problem that hasn’t been solved since it was asked 500 years ago….” This is why it is wrong to dismiss the technology as simply a next word predictor. There have been at least three factors that brought us to this moment: Graphics Processing Units (GPUs allowed for much faster and more efficient matrix math calculations required to train and use neural nets. Large structures and labeled data sets enabled the expansion and training of neural nets. New architectures made it possible to better set the weights. Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) is another contested term that serves many purposes. I think a starting point is to contrast it with “narrow” intelligence, where a model may be trained to perform one task, such as playing chess, and not be applicable to other tasks. AGI would be able to be applied to a wide range of tasks and do well at them. This is often measured through various benchmarks, and some of them are quite telling, such as the idea that Microsoft promoted that AGI would be here once a model created $100 billion in profits. More frequently, you hear people say that AGI will be here when a model can outperform some or all humans at most or all mental tasks. And some people insist that AGI would require models to be able to generalize beyond the data in their training data. I think most people in the field think that, for most definitions of AGI it will be possible to get there, but they differ on the amount of methodological, scaling and time that it will take. Some claim that we are already there with the latest round of models. And many claim that it will happen soon because of the exponential curves we will see with RSI. Many of the harshest critics say that we will plateau long before AGI because of the limitations of the methodologies used to create the current models, such as the lack of “world models” (the ability to learn from and model the physical world), continual learning, or lack of a symbolic structure. I am agnostic at this point. But I think we already see strong evidence of increasing capabilities with something like recursive self-improvement with the aid of humans, and this might actually lead to the development of new architectures and methods. Yasmine: I don’t have much to add to Josh’s thorough explanations here. I’d just highly encourage folks to listen to and read Dr. Alexa Hanna and Dr. Emily Bender on the AI Con . The term AI itself is not technically meaningful, and it has been a tool of mystification with a lot of utility to big tech. It obfuscates the concrete facts of what these different types of software are and the often ugly facts of how they’re made, as well as the real harms they cause. AGI is also a nebulous term. I think the claim that current AI development will culminate in the so-called singularity or AGI isn’t credible. This isn’t a conclusion I’ve independently reached, this is what most AI researchers think. 76 percent of those surveyed say AGI is not on the horizon and think that it’s unlikely to be achieved via LLMs or by means of the current scale-based approaches to training. There is also a debate as to whether it’s feasible at all. This supposed threat has taken up disproportionate space in our discussion of the harms/potential harms of the AI boom. I’m on the side that argues let’s pay attention to the actual horrors and destruction being enabled by this software or caused by its development in the here and now, rather than allow our attention to be diverted by a sci-fi story with little to no foundation in science. What has happened to trigger this? Josh: In multiple tests of new AI models, they have acted unreliably, disregarding instructions and seemingly going rogue. The most famous case of this was the Hugging Face attack described in detail by libertarian Dwarkesh Patel on his popular YouTube channel . Other industry commentators have proposed a variety of reasons, including the idea that as OpenAI and Anthropic face the opening of their books before their IPOs, they have an incentive to reduce their Research and Development spending to make their path to profitability look more plausible.
