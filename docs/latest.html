DSA Scores Election Victories Across California: From San Diego to Humboldt, membership gains fuel increased activism and wins

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: California DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-24T19:49:55+00:00

The returns are (mostly) in, and DSA-endorsed candidates for elected offices from local school boards to the U.S. Congress have been elevated throughout California. A ton of hard work went into this critical election cycle, boosted on the one hand by DSA’s swelling membership, which is now numerically the largest of any socialist organization in the country’s history, and on the other hand by the understanding that in order to stop the march of fascism we had to win big. A half dozen cities in the Golden State are now led by DSA mayors, including the largest one. DSA has become a recognized political force, drawing praise from our coalition allies and dismay from our opponents. Below are reports from ten California DSA chapters on their local election efforts. Humboldt Humboldt DSA endorsed cadre candidate G Mario Fernandez for his re-election bid for Eureka City Council. We were keen to emphasize the contrast between Mario—a DSA member and self-described anarchist—and his opponent, a Republican police officer noted for participating in violent suppression of the local pro-Palestinian student movement. We mobilized our chapter members as well as Mario's supporters among local trade unionists for a grassroots-focused ground game. Between door knocking and phone banking, we banked an estimated 1000 voter interactions. We are proud to report that Mario is currently more than 100 votes ahead at the time of writing, November 17, although results are not yet certified. Sonoma County In February 2026, Sonoma County DSA endorsed our first-ever electoral candidate, Joanna Paun, for County Board of Supervisors. This process reactivated leaders in our chapter who had figured in other electoral victories, such as the Green Party majority in Sebastopol, Omar Fateh’s Minnesota State Senate race, and door-knocking for Prop 50. It also activated a passionate, largely new membership base who believed in the power of ordinary people to challenge the capitalist class. Our opposition was well-funded by real estate, Big Ag, the Sheriff, and corporate consultant Rob Muelrath, who is behind four of the five sitting County Supervisors and numerous City Council seats. We knew it would be near-impossible to get representation for workers when the candidates for office were already beholden to corporate interests. With not a moment to waste, we mobilized our members. We organized weekly weekend door knocks that saw Joanna win 42% of the primary vote—and defeat her main opponent in their home precinct. We organized phone and text banks, made in-house video and graphic content, posted regularly on social media informing locals about the election, and continued activating members to join the campaign. We also released our first-ever Voter Guide in just our second year, in which our Electoral Committee analyzed our county’s races beyond just Joanna’s. This voter guide, combined with our presence in our communities, helped show Sonoma County residents that politics don’t start with expensive campaigns, but with them. Many of our members had never knocked doors or helped run an electoral race that actually aligned with their principles, let alone whose victory could shape the terrain they live in. For example, Joanna ran with the explicit angle of ending county cooperation with ICE, a major chapter priority we adopted in January. She garnered the support of SEIU 1021 and the North Bay Labor Council, along with over a dozen unions and progressive organizations. During our time on this campaign, our chapter forged a tighter bond with rank-and-file union members and impressed upon local leaders that we are a serious, united force in the county. After the primaries, we took a small break to celebrate our wins and mount our strength. During our primary campaign, we activated over seventy of our members and trained several who would become Field Leads. We proved that the DSA difference is indeed real—that when we unite, we can defeat opponents taking six figures from police and oligarchs with people power, union support, and buy-in among those who feel alienated from the system. We proved that a socialist agenda delivers on funds for schoolchildren, public infrastructure, affordable housing, union empowerment, and human rights for all , not just some. By late summer, we once again hit streets and doors for the general election. By then, we had become involved with our close ally, Teamsters 665, in supporting winery workers organizing a new union in an industry that hasn’t had one in over 40 years. We also organized our local No Kings IV, and our members joined numerous community initiatives to block Flock and other automated license plate readers (ALPRs). The groundwork we laid through our labor support and community connections enabled us to keep pushing in-house content that laid bare the corruption of our opponents while simultaneously showing our candidate’s vision. Our opposition scrambled. They couldn’t find an angle of attack that could stick. Ultimately, no amount of spam texts or shady mailers could overcome our members doing what they do best: talking to neighbors, friends, and family; getting active in our communities; and laying out the real stakes of the race. Money talks, but our voices were louder. As the general election drew nearer, we endorsed two more chapter members in their respective races: Sarah Seitchik Sebastian, a community organizer running for Petaluma School Board, and Caitlin Quinn, a union president running for County Board of Education Area 2. Our chapter conducted joint knocks for these candidates’ campaigns, assisted with communications, shared their literature, and proudly promoted them to our community. When all was said and done, we won. Our unity with unions and community networks overcame half a century of corporate domination in District 2. Joanna maintained a 22-point lead from the first ballot drop, and her opponent conceded within the week. Caitlin’s opponent has also conceded, Sarah is maintaining her lead, and we believe our slate will fully sweep soon. Altogether, we delivered a win not only for the socialist movement, but for the workers and families of Sonoma County—those who call this place home, who raise their children here, who have to move due to the cost of living, who wonder why their services are being cut while developers take public property, who wonder why their government is unresponsive. They won’t have to keep wondering. Now, they will have a progressive majority on the highest body in Sonoma County, allies in office to fight for their interests, and a future that belongs to them. On the state ballot measure front, we formed a tax the rich working group at our annual convention in the spring as one of the chapter’s three priority campaigns. The working group met biweekly forward to November. Along the way we collected signatures for what became Props 3 and 40 (won Prop 3, lost Prop 40) and led education and training efforts for the membership, and then with coalition partners Alameda Labor Council and Alliance of Californians for Community Empowerment (ACCE) similar events for the broader community. Later in the summer and fall we canvassed and phone banked with the labor council on the tax the rich campaign, together with our other jointly endorsed campaigns for Oakland School Board (we won one out of two seats we competed for) and Berkeley Rent Stabilization Board (five out of five, giving us an 8-1 majority). We lost Berkeley Measure Z, which would have provided seed funding for a public bank. On City Council races we went two for three, electing 23-year-old student Syrak Micael in Berkeley, reelecting George Syrop to the Hayward City Council, and running a spirited campaign for Oakland District 2 with Nate Adams that fell short. Perhaps our marquee campaign was for mayor of Richmond, where East Bay DSA has had a longstanding partnership with the Richmond Progressive Alliance. We supported two-term councilmember Claudia Jimenez in her run at the top municipal job. In winning 55-45% she became the first immigrant Latina female elected mayor of Richmond, and second DSA member, running on a strong working class-first platform of no corporate contributions, against ICE incursions and standing up to the conservative institutional elephant in the room, the Chevron refinery. Dozens of EBDSA members showed up for her canvassing and phone banking every week. Silicon Valley We are proud to report back on the incredible effort and growth our chapter demonstrated during this recent election cycle. While not every ballot measure went our way, the true victory for Silicon Valley DSA this year was our capacity to organize, mobilize, and build lasting power in our communities. Silicon Valley DSA endorsed Props 3 and 40, and No on 41 and 42. We also endorsed Redwood City Measure E (Rent Control), which lost, 46.25% Yes to 53.75% No. During the last midterm elections, our chapter simply did not have the capacity to campaign for propositions or measures in any significant way. This year, our leaders and volunteers stepped up to effectively organize our own canvasses, generate a social media campaign, and work in coalition with local organizations to reach voters directly. Our impact: Voter Contact: We made 783 voter contacts and knocked on 482 doors. Mobilization: We engaged roughly 51 unique volunteers across our campaigns, with many taking on multiple shifts. Coalition Building: We strengthened our relationships with local partners, including the Peninsula DSA, where we mobilized members to attend external canvassing opportunities. The volunteer data for those canvasses with coalition partners has not been made fully available to us at this time. We are incredibly proud of the foundation we have built this year. Our members learned new skills, our chapter developed new leaders, and we are well-positioned to continue fighting for the working class in Silicon Valley. Peninsula Peninsula DSA organized around five campaigns here in San Mateo County as well as a state level campaign. We endorsed three candidates for local city council: Ofelia Bello in East Palo Alto, Phoebe Venkat in Foster City, and Melanie Olson in South San Francisco. We also endorsed Measure E to establish stronger rent control and tenant protections in Redwood City, advocated for the Regional Transit Measure to fund and maintain public transportation, and endorsed the statewide Billionaire Tax through Prop 40. Members supported these races by gathering signatures to get the measures on the ballot, then ahead of the general election fundraising, going door knocking, dropping literature, phone banking, text banking and creating and distributing our own voter guide. Earlier, in 2025 Peninsula DSA successfully convinced the SamTrans Board to opt into SB 63, ensuring that the Regional Transit Measure would be on the ballot in 2026. We were saddened to see both Measure E and Prop 40 fail, as well as Phoebe and Melanie defeated by their corporate-backed opponents. However, we celebrated Ofelia finishing first in a crowded field and look forward to a socialist on the East Palo Alto City Council. The Regional Transit Measure also passed, keeping buses, trains and subways running throughout the Bay Area. Central Coast This general election cycle, Central Coast DSA went 3 for 3 on our endorsements: defeating Measure Q, a 7% utility users tax in Marina; defeating Measure H, which would have repealed several renter protection ordinances in Salinas; and electing our candidate for Marina City Council District 4. Our chapter ran its first cadre candidate for office, chapter co-chair Athena Rita Burciaga, and won. The membership showed up: by the end of the campaign, fifteen members were turning out to canvass, out of a chapter that now has 150 dues-paying members. Athena’s win was a comeback. On election night she was in third place with 29% of the vote, 81 votes behind. As the late ballots were counted, she climbed into first and now leads with nearly 36% of the vote, a 54-vote margin, pending certification on November 25. Many voters saw Mike Simmons, a moderate Republican, as a Democrat, and the results showed he was splitting Athena’s voters far more than those of the other Republican in the race, Brad Imamura. We overcame that by knocking on every door in Preston Park and Abrams Park, Marina’s renter neighborhoods, and the late ballots from those neighborhoods broke decisively our way. We won by running an unabashedly socialist campaign: protecting renters, building a future young people can afford, and taking on the corporate interests that have held a firm grip on Marina’s local politics for too long. On December 15, Athena Burciaga will become the first transgender person to take elected office in Monterey County. Santa Barbara DSA Santa Barbara worked very hard this election cycle in our local city races. As a smaller chapter, we decided our efforts were best used in local elections. Santa Barbara has a city council comprising six members elected through districts plus the mayor, the only citywide representative. Three city council seats were up for election as well as the mayor’s position. In June and July, a couple members of our electoral working group decided to start meeting with the most progressive candidates to discuss their interest in receiving our endorsement. This culminated in a chapter meeting where we had all the interested candidates inform us about their positions and answer questions. At that meeting we ended up endorsing two city council candidates but postponed our decision for the other two. At a special chapter meeting, we endorsed both remaining candidates, meaning we had endorsed a candidate for every district election as well as for the mayor’s seat. Following our endorsement process, we mobilized and canvassed hard. For the first few weeks we canvassed every Saturday morning. This was in partnership with the local Democratic Party, which in Santa Barbara is run by a democratic socialist; they had endorsed all the same candidates. We decided to participate in their canvassing efforts because of their access to voter information, including the PDI mobile app. Around six weeks before the election we halted our biweekly electoral working group meetings in favor of weekly door-knocking campaigns. The Santa Barbara Tenants Union did the same; they are very close allies to our chapter. We abandoned the strict rules of canvassing that the Democratic Party followed and focused on apartment complexes that had worked with the Tenants Union in the past. These seemed to be our most successful efforts, especially because of the recent passage of a rent stabilization ordinance. It’s hard to say how many doors we reached as an organization, but it is in the thousands if not tens of thousands. We averaged about three to five people every Saturday and around ten for every Wednesday canvas, including the Tenants Union members. We led canvassing events, talked to constituents about elections, posted on social media, and made a voter guide. But mainly we organized and went door to door. All of this effort resulted in all of our endorsed candidates winning their races: Devon Wardlow for City Council District 4, Ian Baucke for City Council District 5, Gabe Escobedo for City Council District 6, and Kristen Sneddon for Mayor of Santa Barbara. Sneddon was outspent by hundreds of thousands of dollars. This cycle created a six to one super majority on the Santa Barbara city council in favor of progressive politics (four of whom openly call themselves democratic socialists). For many of our members, this was their first time canvassing. We mobilized many new members, increasing our chapter’s knowledge and abilities for future campaigns. At the same time, we built new coalitions with allied organizations which we look forward to developing further post-election. We have heard many reports of non-members using and sharing it, and it has been mentioned by new members during onboarding. Phone banking efforts focused on IE DSA membership. The goal here was to activate paper members by getting them to come out for electoral work. DSA San Diego's membership initially focused on two ballot measures—one local and one statewide. While it failed at the ballot, we mobilized 100 people to reach 2,831 doors. Zuniga won his mayorship, becoming the first democratic socialist mayor in the County of San Diego!

L.A. Socialists Turn Electoral Wave into Governing Power

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: California DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-24T19:49:55+00:00

LA Mayor-elect Nithya Raman “LA was at a critical cusp in its history. Globalization had reorganized its economy, rewiring it in dramatic ways, and many people were being left behind. Yet the city had—and still has—this incredible, protean potential for better things, for progressive politics, for surprising activism.” – Mike Davis The midterm elections were truly seismic. Like many places across the country, Los Angeles made good on its protean potential from the 2026 blowout with a stronger socialist movement and a larger share of political power. DSA members won up and down the ballot. Nithya Raman has famously won the mayor’s office of the country’s second largest city in a upset victory. Marissa Roy has won the city attorney’s office, and Angela Gonzales-Torres is headed to Congress. Together, their victories put democratic socialists in two of Los Angeles’s most powerful citywide offices and send a DSA-LA member to Congress for the very first time in a race that few expected a year ago would even be a close challenge. The formerly conservative, sleepy suburb of Burbank has elected a DSA council majority, the first on the West Coast and one of only two places currently in the country. Behind those wins is DSA’s second-largest chapter. In the last year alone, the LA chapter doubled its membership to over six thousand members, launched a citywide Shake Up LA slate, and knocked more than 110,000 doors. And it did so alongside a realignment of the city’s left-labor coalition, especially that of the powerful hotel workers union UNITE HERE Local 11, which cross-endorsed most of DSA-LA’s Shake Up City Hall slate from Nithya down to Burbank city council and whose thousands of members knocked doors for her. In the midterm elections, democratic socialists won two of Los Angeles’s most powerful citywide offices and sent a socialist to Congress from the city for the very first time, in a race that few expected a year ago would even be a close challenge. National Earthquake Of course this didn’t happen in a vacuum. Nationally Democratic candidates of all stripes won control of both chambers of Congress, while Republicans lost ground in a shocking 99 percent of roughly 2,100 counties where most ballots had been counted by Wednesday morning, according to Reuters. The electorate shifted left nearly everywhere, all at once. Early voter surveys showed the multiracial and generational breadth of that rejection. The AP Voter Poll found that about 90 percent of Black voters, roughly 60 percent of Latino voters, and a whopping 70 percent of voters under 30 supported Democratic House candidates. Tufts University’s CIRCLE research center also reported Democratic majorities among young men and white youth, groups that had favored Trump in 2024. Within that national revolt, Los Angeles voters were also shifting left. Gonzales-Torres decisively defeated Democratic incumbent Jimmy Gomez, winning 62 percent of the vote while running openly as a DSA member, supporting a free Palestine and the abolition of ICE, and rejecting corporate money. Her victory gives DSA-LA its first foothold in Congress and demonstrates that a candidate can take clear positions against corporate power, state violence, and yes Palestine and the BDS movement while building a winning electoral coalition. Roy’s victory carries a historic mandate as well. With more than 695,000 votes and 68 percent support she received the largest vote total ever won in a Los Angeles city election at any time, according to her campaign . She campaigned uphill against the Democratic establishment and real estate lobby’s support of the incumbent on turning the City Attorney’s Office into the city’s largest public interest law office: pursuing abusive corporate landlords, enforcing workers’ rights, holding polluters accountable and challenging the Trump administration in court. Alongside Raman’s victory, her election gives DSA-LA its first citywide Los Angeles wins and an opportunity to put substantial public resources behind people confronting powerful interests. Raman’s mayoral victory expands that opening considerably. Her 2020 City Council victory helped establish DSA-LA as a force in city politics. Six years later, she has defeated incumbent (and one-time New Leftist) Karen Bass to become Los Angeles’s first democratic socialist (and immigrant) mayor. Her victory puts a democratic socialist at the head of the country’s second-largest city , giving the movement a new scale of power to advance housing, tenant protections and public services across Los Angeles. (A continuing ongoing tension will be between her independence as an elected and the co-governance and criticism of both the LA Chapter and in DSA’s national leadership about her level of support for the BDS movement). The chapter has also consolidated its existing beachhead in LA city council. City Councilmembers Eunisses Hernandez and Hugo Soto Martínez and LAUSD Board Member Rocío Rivas all won reelection. In the Inglewood area, Estefany Castañeda retained her seat on the Centinela Valley Union High School District board, while Yaritza Gonzalez won election to the Inglewood Unified School District board.(Ysabel Hurado, a DSA member, also retained her seat on city council). Red Burbank The chapter’s reach also expanded beyond Los Angeles. In Burbank, victories for Mike Van Gorder and Nolan Southerland created a democratic socialist council majority alongside Konstantine Anthony, while Stephen Post won in West Hollywood . Estuardo Mazariegos’s race in Los Angeles’s Ninth Council District remained unresolved as of this writing, with Mazariegos trailing by a mere 87 votes after taking on an opponent backed by major corporate and police-union spending. The scale of the work behind these campaigns matters. In its largest electoral effort to date, DSA-LA endorsed 13 candidates and mobilized more than 1,700 volunteers to fill over 4,000 canvassing shifts. Its members knocked more than 110,000 doors in chapter-led canvasses. Over the same period since the 2024 election cycle, membership grew from nearly 3,000 to more than 6,000–and has added several hundred new members since Election Day alone. DSA-LA Cochair, Sean Wakasa, at the 2026 May Day rally The Long Arc The chapter’s program places this strategy in a longer history of multiracial working-class struggle, from Justice for Janitors in the 1990s to the immigrant rights marches of 2006 and the teachers’ strike of 2019. Those movements demonstrated how organized workers could reshape Los Angeles’s politics and advance demands benefiting the wider public. For today’s socialist movement, that history underscores the importance of maintaining an independent organization capable of keeping workers mobilized, turning electoral gains into power, and holding elected representatives accountable– ( a hot button issue with the recent debate and fallout over Nithya Raman. DSA-LA’s experience shows what sustained organization can bring to that opening. The next test will be whether these victories open up more struggles and, most of all, change people’s lives. Roy’s promised enforcement agenda, a stronger socialist presence on city council and Raman’s incoming mayoral administration create openings. Those gains though as always will require organized pressure, alliances with unions and community groups, and members willing to remain involved in the difficult work of governing. They will also take sustained and coordinated pushing and accountability from movements and DSA locally. The promise of the democratic road to socialism is that working people can collectively govern the institutions that shape their lives. In Los Angeles, that promise now has more members, more organizers, and more elected Socialists in Office behind it. The work ahead is to use those gains to keep moving out to the horizon. California DSA’s “Tax the Rich Campaign”: one for two

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: California DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-24T19:49:55+00:00

Don’t throw away your pitchforks. You will have more chances to use them in the near future. With the loss of Proposition 40, the Billionaire Tax, by a slim statewide margin of 52.1–47.9%, California DSA’s 2026 “Tax the Rich” campaign went one for two. The other progressive tax on the ballot, Proposition 3, the Education and Health Care Act, won handily, 61-39%. Any attempt to spin the SEIU/United Health Workers-backed Prop 40 defeat as an indicator that California’s electorate doesn’t want to tax the rich—billionaires included—is either uninformed or deliberately misleading. So an obvious question arises: what made the difference in outcomes for the two measures? 400 million dollars from sociopathic billionaires sent a flood of paid advertising sewage across the state A couple dozen of the richest far-right Californians spent close to $400 million to defeat Prop 40—a historic record sum in a ballot measure campaign. Their effort featured scaremongering ads that were outright lies, such as “this is a tax on you” and “Sacramento politicians want to take your retirement savings”. It creatively disguised pure conjecture as expert opinion: “This will end up costing the state more than it takes in”; “All the billionaires will leave”; “Business innovation will be crushed”. Riffing on Pastor Martin Niemoller, we found that first they come for billionaires, and then they come for “you”. These deceitful messages were everywhere throughout September and October—television, radio, digital popups, social media, and in our mailboxes. They buried the purpose of the tax—to prevent three million low-income Californians from losing their health care coverage—beneath a pile of disinformation sufficient to defeat it. The firehose of spending and wall-to-wall advertising moved the needle of public opinion from close to 60% in favor in the spring to just under 50% by November 3. But what exactly did that slight majority of voters vote against? Despite the result, many were not casting a vote against taxing billionaires. Even leading anti-40 voices like Governor Gavin Newsom felt compelled to say that billionaires should be paying more taxes. But in a common dodge for the “No” side of a ballot measure, the messages were “not this one”, because it is “fatally flawed”; and plausible sounding but misleading barbs like “we need such a tax to be permanent and federal, not temporary and assessed at the state level”. And these were the ads that actually mentioned the billionaire tax; most just said “No on 40”, aware that any insertion of the title would remind voters that they hated billionaires. The billionaires’ PAC, “Building a Better California”, threw everything that $400 million would buy at the electorate, betting that with gigantic scale and volume turned up high—and the United Health Workers’ bank account unable to mount an adequate media response—enough of the media sludge would stick to the wall to defeat the measure through stoking fear and confusion. And they were right. On the wrong side Let’s be clear that the billionaires didn’t achieve this result entirely by themselves. Several nominally progressive organizations—the California Teachers Association, Building and Construction Trades Council, and Planned Parenthood—vocally opposed Prop 40, along with Governor Gavin Newsom, steering mainstream news coverage to meekly accept billionaire talking points in endlessly repeating that labor was split, “teachers” (insinuating all teachers) were against the tax, and “progressive groups” were worried that all the billionaires would leave. One thing that didn’t appear anywhere in the mainstream news stories was this angle: CTA, the BTC and Planned Parenthood leadership were opposed to the tax on billionaires. None of these organizations offered a plebiscite to its members, some of whom—like CTA affiliates United Teachers Los Angeles and United Educators of San Francisco—took matters into their own hands and came out in favor of Prop 40 in defiance of their parent organization’s stance. Likewise, the California Federation of Teachers, traditionally the more democratic and progressive of the two statewide education unions, initially neutral through a leadership vote, reversed itself and went pro-40 in September after an internal rebellion at its state council meeting. The California DSA campaign From the moment California DSA passed its resolution in January to support the Education and Health Care Act along with the Billionaire Tax, putting together a statewide tax the rich working group, democratic socialists stood virtually alone in running a united campaign for both measures. The reasons for this have been explained in earlier issues of California Red (see here and here ). Although many of our chapters worked hard to promote both and build a bridge between the two campaigns, it wasn’t until August that we were joined in common cause by powerful players like the California Federation of Labor Unions and the state Democratic Party. In that eight-month stretch we held state and local educational meetings on progressive tax policy in person and on zoom, and trainings on how to effectively hold house meetings and gain organizational endorsements for our dual campaign. We ran articles in every issue of California Red ; created flyers for the joint campaign in four languages and posted them on a one-stop shop “tax the rich” campaign page on our website, ready to download, as well as an FAQ (Frequently Asked Questions) and progressive tax bibliography. Our chapters chartered their own tax the rich working groups. We invited guest speakers to our meetings like Vanessa Williamson, author of The Price of Democracy: The Revolutionary Power of Taxation in American History . We worked with artist Jos Sances to create a beautiful campaign poster and printed thousands at a union print shop and distributed them to the chapters that wanted them. David Solnit led an art build that produced picket signs and banners. San Diego DSA printed “tax the rich” doorhangers for canvassing. After the California Federation of Labor Unions voted at its convention in early August to support both measures, we initiated a collaboration with the state federation to make October 10 “Tax the Rich Day”, with joint rallies and canvassing in eight cities. And over the final weeks before Election Day East Bay DSA staged human billboarding at busy intersections in Oakland and banner drops on freeway overpasses in Berkeley and Santa Cruz. Too late Despite the hard work of DSA members from San Diego to Sacramento, the campaigns for Prop 3 and Prop 40 grew no closer together, and things looked bad for Prop 40 until the beginning of August. At that point SEIU-UHW organizing within two of the major institutions of California progressive politics paid off. First the state Democratic Party leadership, diverging from the governor, voted narrowly to endorse the Billionaire Tax. The next day, with President Lorena Gonzalez leading the charge, the California Federation of Labor Unions at its annual convention came overwhelmingly on board. As icing on the cake, the California Nurses Association joined the newly expanding coalition the following day. But this represented the high point of the campaign. In reality, the labor federation and Democratic Party endorsements came too late to translate into effective support structures and funding. Sporadic earned media emanating from such events as enthusiastic rallies featuring Bernie Sanders and Ro Khanna and cheerleading by Nobel-prizewinning economists turned out to be no match for the record-setting spending avalanche of the other side. A progressive tax campaign needs to hold three cards to have a reasonable shot at winning the game: the right message at the right time; the largest coalition possible, which avoids internal dissension and enables a massive field campaign; and sufficient cash for at least a modicum of paid advertising to augment the ground effort and earned media against the most well-funded opposition imaginable. Prop 40 had but the first one out of three, and it wasn’t enough. With a relatively popular governor actively opposed, and putatively progressive organizations and individuals gravely restating the false messages that billionaires would not have been able to convince the public to believe on their own, the Billionaire Tax had too big a mountain to climb. Compare and contrast with Prop 3’s advantages. Built on a long-standing, extensive coalition of unions and community organizations, with the full backing of the Democratic political establishment (including Newsom, who otherwise never met a progressive tax he liked), and with a bank account supported by the biggest labor political contributor (CTA) in the state, and facing virtually no opposition spending, it offered a free lunch to the public (“not a tax increase”—technically true, just making permanent an already existing tax) along with modestly progressive tax messaging (“if it fails rich people will get a completely unnecessary tax cut”). California DSA can be justly proud of its work to bring together the two campaigns under one roof. But until all parties within organized labor fully absorb and act upon the oldest lesson of labor history—when we are united on issues of common interest for the working class we win, and when we are divided, we lose—campaigns like the Billionaire Tax will find success elusive. Keep the pitchforks handy As for those pitchforks, we can look to two possibilities. Corporate tax increases and/or tax loophole closures could be legislated, and result in enough revenue to reverse the loss of health care for millions of California residents caused by the cruel actions of Trump and the federal government. It’s within the power of incoming Governor Becerra and the legislature to do this. Such tax increases (on income, since thanks to passage of the billionaires’ deceptive Prop 42, wealth taxes are off the table for the time being) would simply require a legislative supermajority of two thirds. If the “blue wave” of November 3 should mean anything to the legislators, it’s that now is the time to act without fear of electoral retribution. The other direction to point your pitchfork is 2028. Already there are rumblings in some quarters about reviving some form of a tax on billionaires. And others are proposing a renewed shot at the split roll tax that narrowly failed in 2020, removing Prop 13 protections for commercial property. Whether early in the new year in Sacramento, or a couple years down the road, your pitchfork will come in handy. Keep its points sharp. Councilmember Van Gorder I take a lot of pride in being a loser. Surely, a first-timer like Nolan had the odds stacked against him, and I needed to focus on my own race. The Revolutionary Cocktail: Why DSA Needs Electoralism, Direct Action, and Mutual Aid to Win

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: California DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-24T19:49:55+00:00

PART TWO OF TWO [Part One appeared in the September issue of California Red . Here it is.] Now, there was an invisible through line in all three of the examples of local direct action— and that is mutual aid. When I was a ‘baby leftist,’ developing my sea legs, organizing with the PSL, mutual aid was cast aside as functionally useless anarchist praxis. It was considered band-aids for bullet holes, I was told. However, when I was cutting my teeth during the Student Intifada, organizing alongside Dutch anarchists at the University of Amsterdam, the mutual aid component to the encampments was unavoidable. In fact, it was practically baked into the whole concept! If we wanted to sustain an encampment or occupation, we simply needed supply lines that could feed and water the several hundred students, staff, and faculty demanding the university divest from Israel. This got me thinking; if we were serious about revolutionizing society, did that merely entail commandeering existing supply chains, hitting the Teamsters and Longshoremen with a coat of red paint, prefixing big chain supermarkets with “The People’s Grocery Outlet,” and calling it a day? Urban versus Rural I sat with this thought. In the midst of encampments and occupations, I was also finishing up my masters thesis on the way that geographic conditions shape organizing strategies; examining the differences and similarities of urban versus rural approaches to radical politics. A core pillar of the thesis was exposing the extractive relationship between cities and the countryside as being a microcosm of the relationships perpetuated by capitalist-colonialism, i.e., between imperial cores and their peripheries. The relations of production and distribution are inseparable from the mode of production in which they exist. That might seem abundantly apparent at a theoretical level, but the implications for local organizing are tremendous. Obviously, we want logistics and grocery workers to have strong protections and safe working conditions, where they make good money for the work they contribute to maintaining our supply chains. But simply putting these logistics networks under control of the workers won't change the fact that rural communities suffer from severe underdevelopment and precarious access to goods and services, and it certainly won't fix the “noble savage” view of urban dwellers against their rural counterparts. I’ll be the first to say that DSA won't be the organization that fixes this structural imbalance in the supply chain or be the mechanic to the relationship between urban and rural communities. That is a longterm problem that will require a serious overhaul in how we shape production and distribution both at home and abroad (good thing we are an anti-imperialist socialist organization!). However, what I want to emphasize here is that the projects and campaigns being worked on by DSA San Diego’s Ecosocialist working group and our mutual aid collective are not simply acts of kindhearted charity but deeply rooted in a theory of building sustainable community power. Seeding Food Sovereignty is an active campaign by EcoSoc that prioritizes building out community gardens throughout the city that can supplement the caloric intake of the local community. The organizational concept is that by proliferating community garden infrastructure, urban and especially suburban communities can reduce their dependence on alienated supply chains that function to obfuscate where their produce originates. Rather than an intermediary of fluorescent lighting and underpaid service workers, our gardens are sites of community building over the labor it takes to feed one another. The program functions on the old Marxian adage “from each according to their ability, to each according to their needs.” It’s no secret that gardening can be back-breaking work, but the organizers of each garden workday take each person's ability into account by offering a variety of tasks scaled by intensity. From pulling weeds and picking fruit to digging up tree stumps and building planters, there is always something for everybody. Noble charity? The event “Race the 215” advocated for public transportation support. The cyclists (shown prepping here) won the race when the bus broke down. Too often, I see criticisms of mutual aid endeavors made in the same tone with which my PSL comrades once castigated it to me: mutual aid is just noble charity which doesn't really turn the dial forward in terms of growing organizational power and is ultimately futile in the face of deeply inequitable and imperial supply chains. However, it is important to understand that the motivation behind this campaign is not to merely patch gaps in the system of distribution. Nor will mutual aid ever be able to outright replace a deeply embedded and globalized supply chain. Rather, the purpose of mutual aid such as these gardens is to cohere a community of individuals around the physical labor of building infrastructure that supports a community’s basic needs. This is an integral aspect of building a meaningful socialist project that proves we have the vision, means, and ability to build novel production and distribution structures that do not perpetuate capitalist-colonialist relations and are organized in common rather than at the direction of capital. Another similarly effective mutual aid project of the San Diego chapter is our Fix the 215 campaign. The 215 is a local bus line with the highest ridership in the city; beginning at the train station in downtown and terminating at the local state university, it takes about 50 minutes from start to finish for a total of nine miles (and that is if the bus does not break down midway through). Our demands were quite simple: dedicated bus lanes, all door boarding, and bus priority at intersections. The most recent action related to this project was Race the 215, where we partnered with our cyclist and urbanist advocacy partners to facilitate a competition between bicycle riders and the 215 bus line to see which group could finish the route faster. Needless to say, the cyclists won after the bus broke down three stops into the race. The event itself had a tinge of direct action blended into it, but the project of public transportation advocacy more broadly demonstrates a priority for reinforcing and expanding an existing working class community infrastructure that is centered around accessibility and the freedom of movement. A project such as this, however, will not be completed simply on its merits alone, and we are under no illusions that, by merely documenting significant flaws in the public transportation system and advocating for meaningful solutions, these issues will ever be fixed. We will need buy-in from the elected officials that sit on the governing authority for public transportation, the San Diego Association of Governments (SANDAG), a multi member body made up of elected officials from the City and County of San Diego as well as various periphery cities. A sustainable socialist project At the end of the day, we are in the business of politics. That means running candidates, raising money, and winning races. It is unmistakable that DSA is in the shape that it is because we have proven ourselves to be a viable alternative to decades of austerity at the ballot box. But as our comrades in any of the various groupuscules and sectariana will always remind us, the road to electoral victory is paved with opportunists and reformism. Is simply changing the flag flown at city hall to a red rosy handshake actually building a sustainable socialist project? Working people obviously lead busy lives and mundane political engagement is taxing enough, let alone organizing. Yet, through an Electoralist strategy, reforms can be fought for and won in local institutions that can alleviate some of the burden of our neoliberal hell while also demonstrating the upper ceiling of capitalist reforms (centering this concept in messaging is crucial; there will always be a box that needs breaking out of). Putting your comrade Fran or Ken in charge of the state is only the starter gun for change at the structural level. Once in office, passing policy becomes the flipside of Electoralism that can really make or break its efficacy as a grand strategy. And if the executors of the administrative bureaucracy cannot impose the will of the masses on captured political structures, then those structures prove why they must be altered or abolished. Zohran is not a good mayor because he agrees with me and is the same type of communist as I am: he is a good mayor because he actually uses his position to advance and entrench the interests of his constituents at large. Taking power where we can, then wielding it accordingly is truly the base spirit of this whole cocktail. The point of engaging in Electoralism is not about taking power for power's sake, it is in order to soften material conditions so that the most politically advanced of the masses can build a holistic movement that includes the work of direct action against defenders of the capitalist order and building the dual power infrastructures that will eventually render the state obsolete. When all three happen at the same time, that is prefiguring. This will occur most literally at the hyper-local level; i.e. organizing a residential building to lower their rent by forming a tenant union and passing a local rent control ordinance through an allied alderperson. Through participation in that struggle, those tenants will have lower rents, giving them more free time, while simultaneously demonstrating that DSA is people who get shit done– and that is better recruitment and development than any list someone could whip up. A constructive tension Electoralism also serves as a test of reach and effect. The worst outcome of a solely Direct Action and/or Mutual Aid strategy is organizing oneself into a bubble that is alienated from the masses at large. For better or for worse, ~150m Americans believe that voting is the ultimate form of political participation. If our work is resonating with communities, that should be reflected in every single primary and general election. Voting maps and district research become data points for targeted outreach strategies in support of mutual aid projects that aim to serve communities before, during, and after election day. Getting these projects started early and in election off years demonstrates to a community that DSA is not Johnny Come Latelys, but that we come from and have deep bonds to the communities we wish to represent. Another holistic aspect to Electoralism is directly related to the attitude of the administrative and elected personnel of the government apparatus who manage the relations of production under which Direct Action and Mutual Aid efforts play out. These electeds can swing favors significantly for grassroots infrastructure initiatives and allow for closer coordination with allied power brokers in negotiations. Even allied elected officials, by nature of their position to the capitalist mode of production, will naturally be restrained by the rigidity of the system, and thus must rely on their organized base to agitate for more concessions from the ruling class and deeper structural reforms. If the state is not capable of satisfying community interests, then its resources can be leveraged to build novel community-based institutions by and for the people. DSA San Diego, despite our size, is still building up our electoral program and is currently vying for the mayorship in two distinct periphery cities within the county. Each presents unique conditions in the project of building socialism and exciting opportunities for changing the dynamic between citizens and the city, easing the financial burden of existence, and creating novel infrastructure that supports working class liberation. Amazon has spent years and enormous resources trying to prevent its North American workforce from unionizing. Even where workers have successfully organized, the retail giant has used everything from legal challenges to warehouse closures to frustrate their efforts to secure a first contract. Now, for the first time, workers at an Amazon facility in North America have a collective agreement. On November 13, an independent arbitrator issued a three-year contract covering approximately eight hundred workers at Amazon’s YVR2 fulfillment center in Delta, British Columbia, a suburb of Vancouver. The workers are represented by Unifor Local 114, part of Canada’s largest private sector union. The agreement represents an important breakthrough in the struggle to organize Amazon. Unlike workers at Amazon’s Staten Island warehouse in New York, who remain without a contract, workers in British Columbia benefited from provincial labor laws that provide stronger remedies against employer interference and bargaining obstruction. Yet the victory also presents a challenge. Having secured both union recognition and a first contract through labor board intervention, Unifor must now ensure the rank-and-file organization necessary to sustain its bargaining relationship with Amazon. Legal victories are essential, but they cannot substitute for organized worker power. How Amazon’s Union Busting Backfired The road to a first contract began with an organizing campaign launched in 2023. By May 2024, Unifor had applied to the British Columbia Labour Relations Board (BCLRB) for certification at YVR2. Amazon responded with a sophisticated anti-union campaign. According to the board’s subsequent decision , the company deployed a specialized management team, subjected employees to extensive anti-union messaging, and deliberately increased hiring to dilute union support. The board found that Amazon had hired unnecessary additional workers with the purpose and effect of undermining the union’s organizing drive. It also concluded that the company’s pervasive anti-union campaign made it impossible to reliably determine employees’ wishes through a representation vote. In July 2025, the board took the extraordinary step of granting Unifor remedial certification, recognizing the union as the workers’ bargaining representative without counting the ballots from the previously conducted vote. Under the province’s Labour Relations Code, the board can certify a union when employer misconduct has made a fair representation vote impossible. The provision recognizes that traditional remedies, such as ordering employers to cease unlawful conduct or rerunning elections, may be insufficient to reverse the effects of union busting. Remedial certification effectively prevents employers from profiting from their own illegal interference. Though Amazon’s challenge to the certification decision proved unsuccessful, its resistance did not end there. In February 2026, the BCLRB ordered Amazon to provide what is likely to be over $1 million in back pay after finding that the company had unlawfully withheld wage increases from unionized employees while granting raises at other regional facilities. Yet even after these defeats, Amazon continued resisting meaningful collective bargaining. Negotiations began in late 2025, but ten bargaining sessions and subsequent mediation failed to produce an agreement. A central obstacle was Amazon’s insistence that Unifor surrender its ability to challenge the company’s productivity expectations. According to the mediator’s findings, Amazon demanded that the union permanently waive its right to contest how quickly and intensively employees were required to work. The company indicated that it would never abandon this position. Brown concluded that the parties were unlikely to reach an agreement voluntarily and recommended binding arbitration. Here, another distinctive feature of British Columbia labor law proved decisive. Section 55 of the province’s Labour Relations Code establishes a first-contract mediation and arbitration process. Where bargaining has broken down, the board can direct the parties toward a binding resolution rather than allowing an employer to indefinitely drag out negotiations. In August, the BCLRB ordered the dispute be resolved through arbitration. Amazon challenged that decision as well, unsuccessfully seeking to halt a standard labor relations process it continues to characterize as “unfair.” The resulting collective agreement demonstrates the importance of labor laws that do more than formally recognize workers’ right to unionize. While Amazon sought to thwart workers at every turn, crucial legislative protections ensured workers a defense. A Contract That Challenges Amazon’s Control The agreement provides tangible improvements for workers, including wage increases bringing the top hourly rate for regular warehouse employees to approximately C$29 by 2028, additional vacation for long-serving employees, enhanced severance protections, and guarantees concerning existing benefits. It gives the union a formal role in handling grievance procedures and access to binding arbitration to resolve disputes. But the most consequential achievement may be the union’s ability to challenge Amazon’s control over the organization and pace of work. Amazon’s business model depends on intensive surveillance and algorithmic management. Workers’ movements and productivity are continuously monitored through scanners and software, while management uses this information to evaluate performance and impose discipline. At YVR2, Amazon employs a system called Structured Productivity Performance Review, which compares individual workers’ output against that of their peers. Employees falling within the bottom 5 percent of productivity can become subject to corrective measures, potentially culminating in termination. Such systems create powerful incentives for speedup. Even without fixed production quotas, workers face pressure to continually increase their output to avoid falling behind their coworkers. The resulting competition can undermine workplace solidarity and encourage unsafe working practices. Unifor sought to replace this approach with a more transparent work-measurement system incorporating ergonomic considerations, fatigue, and the physical demands of warehouse work. The arbitrator declined to impose that alternative, concluding that insufficient evidence had been presented to establish that Amazon’s existing productivity system was unsafe. Nevertheless, he also rejected Amazon’s attempt to insulate its productivity standards from union challenges. Under the agreement, Amazon must provide advance notice of material changes to its productivity, quality, and inactive-time management systems, explain those changes, and meet with union representatives upon request. Such changes must also be safe. Combined with contractual protections against unjust discipline and access to grievance arbitration, these provisions create opportunities to challenge how Amazon’s productivity expectations are applied and enforced. The union has not won collective control over production, and Amazon retains considerable managerial authority. But the company can no longer treat every aspect of its productivity regime as an exclusively managerial concern beyond scrutiny. The contract creates an enforceable mechanism through which workers can collectively contest decisions that previously confronted them as isolated individuals. For a corporation whose labor-management strategy relies heavily on individualized monitoring, automated decision-making, and direct employer–employee communication, this represents an important breach in its system of workplace control. Winning the Contract Is Only the Beginning Amazon has made clear that it does not consider the matter settled. In a November 13 statement, the company denounced the agreement as unfairly imposed, objected to mandatory union membership and dues, and complained that workers had not voted on the contract — all of course ignoring the litany of labor law violations that precipitated the “imposed” contract. Amazon also continues to pursue legal challenges, while a separate employee application to decertify the union remains unresolved. This ongoing decertification dispute raises a potential pitfall for Unifor’s victory. Because certification and the first agreement were secured through labor board orders rather than a successful representation vote followed by a negotiated contract, the union faces the additional task of demonstrating its value to workers and building durable workplace power. This in no way diminishes the legitimacy of the legal remedies; they operated to redress employer violations exactly as they should. But legal recognition alone cannot produce an active, democratic union capable of defending its members. Unifor will need to develop strong workplace leadership, recruit and train stewards, involve members in enforcing the agreement, and demonstrate that collective representation can deliver improvements in their daily working lives. The new grievance procedure will be particularly important. Challenging unjust discipline, unsafe productivity expectations, and arbitrary management decisions can help workers experience the practical benefits of union membership while building confidence in collective action. This is especially important at Amazon, where employee turnover, workplace surveillance, and sophisticated anti-union messaging present formidable organizing challenges. Unifor’s victory has created an institutional foothold inside one of the world’s most powerful corporations. Whether that foothold becomes the basis for a wider breakthrough will depend on workers’ ability to turn contractual rights into collective strength. Amazon has lost an important battle. But its war against unionization is far from over. The next task is to ensure that this first contract becomes not simply a legal victory but the foundation of a durable, militant workplace union capable of challenging Amazon’s power from below. Rediscovering Rosa Luxemburg’s History of Ancient Rome

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Serena Unzueta ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-24T17:39:07+00:00

More than a century after her death, Rosa Luxemburg is renowned as a revolutionary socialist and leader of the 1919 Spartacist Uprising in Germany. Her writings have long been a touchstone for socialist theory. But her work as an ancient historian remains almost entirely unknown. Luxemburg’s political writings, especially her work on political economy, were full of historical references, and indeed she often stressed the importance of history to the project of political economy — and revolution itself — in her major works. These references offered a tantalizing glimpse into a rich archive of study on Luxemburg’s part, but there was a frustrating lack of further elaboration on historical topics and the study of history itself. That remained the case until 2001, when Professor Narihiko Ito discovered lost documents in Moscow of Luxemburg’s unpublished lectures and notes. One of the texts discovered is a manuscript on Greek and Roman slavery. This text, titled “Slavery,” survives because it was saved by her former student Rosi Wolfstein and biographer Paul Frölich from being destroyed after her murder in 1919. It was likely to have been a lecture she composed for her courses at the working-class night school in Berlin, where she began teaching in 1907. It has been over a decade since this text was made available in English for the first time in the first volume of Luxemburg’s complete works. However, Luxemburg’s ideas on ancient slavery have not yet received the attention they deserve. We can now see what place the role of Greco-Roman history in general and the Roman slave revolts in particular had in her understanding of Marxist theory. Ancient Roman Slavery Luxemburg opens “Slavery” by taking issue with the argument of Friedrich Engels that slavery arose in antiquity after the advent of private property. She uses examples from Greek history, and even the Inca empire in Peru, to argue that certain types of exploitation already existed in ancient societies that were communal in nature. She refers to societies of this kind as “the mark community,” a concept she takes from Georg Ludwig von Maurer’s 1854 work Introduction to the History of the Mark, Manor, Village and Town Constitution (which also greatly influenced Engels). In contrast to the early communal forms of social organization, ancient Greece and Rome developed the full alienation of the land from the people, creating a new form of exploitation and slavery. Instead of one society dominating another from the outside by exacting tribute, the dominant actor now incorporated the populations of dominated societies into its own structures and exploited their labor power for trade. For Luxemburg, two further developments arose from this shift. Firstly, trade expands from a purely external practice to an internal one as well. Secondly, “slavery and trade evolved at the same time as the aristocracy,” creating the conditions for a split between a laboring class in the countryside and an intellectual class in the cities. She believed that the Greek city-states had already maximized the cultural potential of this economic system: Greek philosophy, art, and everything that could be achieved on the basis of the slave economy of antiquity had already been perfected by Greece. Rome merely had to borrow it, appropriate it, take possession of it. From then on Rome lived, one could say, as a sponger or freeloader. For Luxemburg, Rome went further than Greece by stigmatizing all forms of labor: “Rome arrived at this conclusion: everything related to work is slave work, physical and mental. In Greece only physical labor had been regarded as slave work.” The high point of slavery in antiquity came during the late Roman Republic and Empire. It was achieved through a combination of Rome’s wars of expansion abroad and the displacement of peasant farmers from their lands at home. As Luxemburg puts it: “In the depths of Roman society radical changes began to take place around the third century BC. It began with Rome’s struggle to establish an empire on a world scale.” The peasant farmers accounted for the majority of Rome’s military forces. This resulted in the collapse of peasant agriculture, as many of these farmer-soldiers lost their lives or went into debt because they could not afford the taxes required to sustain the wars. Without their land, peasant farmers could only offer their labor power to the new, large agricultural estates that the Roman nobility had established, which were called latifundia . This was a precarious position, Luxemburg notes, as there were not enough peasants available to hire for the labor required on the estates, and the peasant farmers themselves could be called back to war at any moment. She identifies the consequences of this labor shortage: “The great proprietors thus had to bring in more slaves. And the wars provided them in great quantities.” This created new antagonisms between citizens and slaves but also led to extreme wealth inequality, as the elite used more slaves to boost their capacity for territorial expansion and production. The Roman Proletariat and the Slave Revolts Without a home or gainful employment in agriculture, peasant farmers were forced to move to the cities. Luxemburg refers to this group as the proletariat, quoting the observation of Jean Charles Léonard de Sismondi: “There was in Rome a proletariat as there is today. But whereas the proletariat today supports society, the Roman proletariat lived at the expense of society.” Ultimately, she observes, this social tendency corroded the Roman commonwealth, because the poor free citizen farmers lost their land to the rich and subsequently lived at the expense of the Roman welfare state. Part of this expense was the amount of grain needed to feed Rome’s new urban proletariat. The Roman authorities reduced the cost by moving grain production out of the Italian peninsula and importing the necessary supplies from Africa, Spain, and Sicily instead. Two revolutionary consequences arose from these conditions. First, the Roman proletariat began to demand land redistribution and rallied behind magistrates such as the Gracchi brothers who took up their cause. Second, there was a massive concentration of slaves in agricultural production who would later rise up and revolt. Luxemburg insists that slavery could still be of fundamental importance even if slaves were a minority of the population: “It is not the numbers that are definitive, but the sum total of the tendencies that result from them.” The latifundia intensified agricultural production. As well as changing the way of life for displaced peasant farmers, this also changed the nature of slavery itself, according to Luxemburg: Now of course the living conditions of the slaves had to undergo a change. They were now completely reduced to the status of naked labor power . In agriculture they were labor power that was applied for the purposes of trade. In this entire institution, or configuration, everything worked toward a single purpose: to extract the maximum possible from the slaves. For Luxemburg, “these relations inevitably led to slave revolts , particularly because the slaves had previously been free.” She goes on to list seven slave revolts that took place on the Italian peninsula and in Sicily. The Sicilian revolts are the most famous, in part due to the colorful descriptions that have been passed down from ancient accounts. It is at the bottom of this list that we find the legendary Spartacus, whose name would come to represent Luxemburg and her allies in the German workers’ movement until the time of her death. Spartacus Before the publication of “Slavery,” we did not know that Rosa Luxemburg had written about ancient history and Rome’s slave revolts, including the historical figure who became the legendary symbol of the Spartacus League. This text demonstrates that before Luxemburg called upon Spartacus to represent her current of revolutionary socialists, she was interested in the place he held within the larger context of ancient Roman slavery and the history of class struggle. At this point in the text, Luxemburg does not offer a dramatic narrative about the life of the Roman slave who Karl Marx once described, in a letter to Engels, as “the most splendid fellow in the whole of ancient history.” Such narratives were widely available in the historical works that we know Luxemburg read and studied. For example, Luxemburg read the famed German historian Theodor Mommsen closely and cites him in her work. Like many historians of his time, Mommsen had a tendency to sympathize with the ruling-class perspective on history. His account of the Spartacus uprising between 73 and 71 BCE depicts its figurehead as a “gang-leader” with an army of “half-savage herdsmen.” Mommsen came up with further colorful epithets for Spartacus and his men as he recounted the crisis they precipitated for the rulers of Rome, referring to them as “banditti” and “robbers.” He accused the rebels of inflicting “atrocities” on “defenseless civilized men,” and attributed their final defeat to internal discord, “lack of a common foe,” and a “barbarous” demand for “plunder.” For Mommsen, the only redeeming quality of Spartacus appears to be the fact that he died the death of a free man at the hands of the Roman General Marcus Licinius Crassus, whom Mommsen calls one of “the great capitalists” of Rome. The differences between Luxemburg’s account and that of Mommsen could hardly be starker. In “Slavery,” Luxemburg deals with Spartacus matter-of-factly as the final representative of the great slave revolts of ancient Roman history. She refers to “ the third Sicilian slave revolt , led by the famous Spartacus” within the broader landscape of all the documented revolts in ancient Rome, with an assessment of why they happened and why they failed. In this way, Luxemburg follows the example set by Marx, who put heroic narrative and literary detail aside when seeking to understand and explain ancient history. The ancient Spartacus uprising was born from a sequence of uprisings that came before it: “ The slave revolts were the first immense, world-historical class struggles against the exploiters . Not the free peasants, not the proletarians in Rome.” However, they did not lead to a transformation of Roman society: The slaves wanted to return to their homelands; they wanted to break loose and get away from society entirely. They were only partly successful, to the extent that they escaped, some to their homelands, some joining robber bands or becoming pirates. The slave revolts remained without consequence, because any further development of them was not based on any trend of economic development. She presents internal decay resulting from the economic logic of slavery as the main factor behind the eventual demise of the Roman Empire rather than external pressure from invading forces: “It was not only the Germans who brought about the destruction. Rome was already ripe prey for foreign conquest after such consequences had been arrived at as a result of its own development.” In her own time, Luxemburg believed that the modern proletarian revolution would succeed where the great slave revolutions of Rome had failed. In the Introduction to Political Economy , she says: In the end, this great proletariat in ancient Rome was simply maintained by the state. Capitalist commodity production, accordingly, is the first economic form in the history of humanity in which the lack of occupation and resources of a large and growing stratum of the population, and the direct and hopeless poverty of another stratum, is not simply a result of this economy but also a necessity for it, a condition of its existence. Unlike Rome’s proletariat, she argues, the modern working classes have no incentive not to unite in the struggle against capitalism. Solidarity between “wage slaves” at the national and international level is possible in order to accomplish this goal. The Spartacus League The discovery of Luxemburg’s “Slavery” text provides us with a new understanding of what “Spartacus” meant to her political works. She began teaching political economy and history in 1907. The following year, she signed her article “The Epic of Łödź” with the pen name “Spartacus.” This anticipated the formation of the Spartacus League during World War I, and it was under the banner of Spartacus that Luxemburg herself would be sacrificed after the failed uprising in January 1919. Spartacus was for Luxemburg a symbol of hope for the liberation of the international proletariat, a hope that lay not in the patrons of bread and circuses, but in the hands that reaped the wheat and baked the bread. The founding members of the Spartacus League chose an apt historical figure to represent them. But they believed that the modern proletariat possessed advantages over the slaves of antiquity that would enable it to prevail over the capitalist class. In the 1950s, the preeminent classical historian M. I. Finley, who was blacklisted during the McCarthyite Red Scare, noted that the question of ancient slavery had been “a battleground between Marxists and non-Marxists, a political issue rather than a historical phenomenon” since the time of the Communist Manifesto (if not earlier). All historians of ancient slavery, Finley argued, inherit the polemics of this history. The most prominent defender of Marx’s concept of class for the study of the ancient world was the Oxford scholar G. E. M. de Ste. Croix . In a field long dominated by male figures, it is exciting that we can now include Rosa Luxemburg’s voice in long-standing debates about ancient history. For example, we can identify two essential differences between the analysis of ancient slavery and class in de Ste. Croix’s work and that of Luxemburg in “Slavery.” The first difference is methodological. Rosa Luxemburg used the study of prehistory, comparative anthropology, and colonial ethnography in order to explain the development of slavery. De Ste. Croix, on the other hand, was hostile to the integration of prehistory and classical history. The second is theoretical. While Luxemburg identifies the cleavage between free and enslaved labor as the central class division of ancient Greek and Roman society, de Ste. Croix instead treats free peasant labor and enslaved labor as two elements of a single class. It is an important distinction that informs their respective views on the relevance of ancient history to the development of class struggle from the conditions of ancient slavery to the modern working class. The addition of Luxemburg’s voice is timely, as there is a renewed interest in Marxist theories of ancient history. Luxemburg’s “Slavery” asks us to look anew at the history of class through the history of slavery. She proposes reexamining the roots of exploitation, on the one hand, and its corrosive effects on society, on the other hand. Overall, Luxemburg offers a new and valuable perspective on the lessons of ancient history for the socialist left today. There is also the American Dream and Promise Act , which grants a pathway to citizenship for young people who were under the age of 16 when they came to the United States , many of whom received renewable two-year work permits from the Obama-era DACA (Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals) executive order. Then there’s the Dignity Act . This bill provides permanent status for Dreamers, offers a way to legalize the undocumented spouses of US citizens who currently cannot fix their status if they have unauthorized entries into the country, and also makes it possible for everyone else who is undocumented to receive work permits. This last portion of the bill has been particularly controversial, as the potential beneficiaries who are neither Dreamers nor married to US citizens would receive a renewable work permit that is good for seven years but doesn’t lead to permanent status, and must pay a yearly $1,000 fee while having 1% of their income taken from their paychecks. We need a new national conversation, and not our current one that is based on overhyped security concerns. Real change for immigrants could take place during this Congress, or the next. I Play Rocky Is Undeniably Charming

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Eileen Jones ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-24T16:47:21+00:00

I Play Rocky goes for earnest meathead sentimentality in the most shameless way, which is presumably why it’s undeniably a crowd-pleaser. It makes Song Sung Blue (2025 ) — another heartwarming underdog story of working-class American dreamers making good against all odds — look like the height of sophistication. But whereas Song Sung Blue was about delightfully “Midwest nice” characters wearing sequined outfits in a Neil Diamond tribute band who find unlikely fame, I Play Rocky is the semi-fictionalized story of how Sylvester Stallone got Rocky made. The sleeper hit of 1976, Rocky was produced on a shoestring budget, spawned a zillion-dollar Hollywood franchise, and made Sylvester Stallone an overnight star. Stallone wrote the script and starred, though he was a relatively unknown actor at the time. According to I Play Rocky , it was his incredibly stubborn insistence on playing the title role that nearly torpedoed the project again and again — and that gives this movie its name. Screenwriter Peter Gamble and director Peter Farrelly ( Green Book ) go for the obvious crowd-pleasing approach, making Stallone’s story virtually identical to Rocky Balboa’s, with almost no daylight between them, except that Stallone reads a lot. Rocky’s a boxer whereas Stallone’s an actor trying to break through by writing himself a good film role, but both are essentially lonely, bighearted laboring lugs given no chance in life, working bottom-of-the-barrel jobs and barely getting by, who chance upon one big flukey opportunity to prove their worth. This involves “going the distance,” putting everything they have on the line when all they have is their mental endurance and — especially — their endlessly tested, repeatedly wounded, ultimately exhausted bodies. Such suffering becomes the keynote of Stallone’s star persona; he’s the sad-eyed hulk getting tortured and railroaded by everybody but enduring what nearly kills him and then coming back stronger in the end. Stallone’s other hit franchise, Rambo , would only intensify the cyclical martyrdom of a muscle-bound saint who slurs his words. The Myth vs. Reality I Play Rocky pushes Stallone’s hardships to the limit, several levels beyond what they were in real life. Luckily, newcomer Anthony Ippolito has a fairly light touch with his Stallone impersonation, making it possible to stay in your seat for two hours of melodramatized working-class striving and deep-voiced mumbling. Stallone was a struggling twentysomething actor and writer when he came up with the script for Rocky , sure, but in real life, he was not a total nobody in the entertainment industry. He was actually getting traction as an actor — you can see him in a small but notable part in a mainstream Neil Simon comedy, The Prisoner of Second Avenue (1975), which came out the year before Rocky . In it, Jack Lemmon’s character, getting steadily driven mad by the challenges of life in New York City, becomes convinced Stallone’s character is a mugger stalking him and, in an irrational rage, chases him relentlessly through Central Park. Finally, Stallone collapses and hands his wallet over to Lemmon, because he’s a perfectly innocent guy now convinced that Lemmon is a mugger. Stallone is quite effective in the part. He also had one of the leads in The Lords of Flatbush (1974), an independent ensemble film full of rising talent like Henry Winkler and Armand Assante — not the negligible background role I Play Rocky suggests. Plus, he wasn’t sitting on a pile of unsold scripts before he hit it big with Rocky — Stallone had already sold one, Hell’s Kitchen (later Paradise Alley ), and he had agents who fought hard to keep him on as the lead. There was no conspiracy to make the low-budget production of Rocky so grueling and impossible that director John Avildsen (Jay Duplass) would be sure to fall behind, giving the studio an excuse to replace Stallone in the lead, as I Play Rocky depicts. Keep in mind that it was the 1970s, and studios had been giving young up-and-coming talent terrific early career opportunities since the late 1960s when it became clear that Hollywood business as usual wasn’t cutting it anymore. Stallone did run miles in beat-up sneakers during the first few days of shooting Rocky , winding up with shin splints and presumably some pretty bad blisters. But there’s no evidence he had a torn tendon and a grave doctor telling him he mustn’t walk at all for several weeks or else suffer some mysterious, unstated, forever crippling consequences. There are tidbits of cool info in Rocky , such as the experimental use of the Steadicam operated by inventor Garrett Brown. Though I Play Rocky makes it seem as if the only way to complete Rocky on its absurdly tight schedule was to shoot the entire movie using the Steadicam, which is definitely not what happened; not even close . But people making biopics never know when to stop. The movie is angled so hard toward a naively idolizing view of Stallone and his all-or-nothing gamble, you might find yourself laughing out loud at certain points. I know I did when, during the early scenes of the Rocky shoot, Stallone starts running all out, and two formerly jaded Philadelphia crew members, tough blue-collar guys, watch him with wide eyes. “Jesus, that guy is fast,” murmurs one reverently, as if he’d just witnessed the Second Coming. The best parts of the film are either the funny parts or the slightly edgy parts, which figures, given Peter Farrelly’s history as one of the Farrelly brothers responsible for crass and riotous comedies Dumb and Dumber (1994) and There’s Something About Mary (1998). Stephan James is energetically funny as Carl Weathers, the football player turned actor who struts into the audition to play Rocky’s prizefight opponent, a supreme Muhammad Ali–like boxer, as if Weathers himself were a master in the ring, which it turns out he definitely is not. The tendency of actors to lie in order to get cast becomes an amiable running gag in I Play Rocky , giving Kiki Seto as Talia Shire, who’s playing Rocky’s love Adrian Pennino, a nice comic moment when she’s supposed to do the skating scene and can’t skate. Tracy Letts is also amusing as the embittered, grousing Sandy Maddox, a composite of several United Artists executives — though colorfully awful Hollywood types make for pretty easy laughs. And Matt Dillon is quite good as Stallone’s malevolent father, Frank Sr, a man who became a hairdresser to support his family but never reconciled himself to his fate and takes his bitterness out on his son. The Understandable Nostalgia for 1970s Cinema A nostalgia for the gritty 1970s is inevitably part of the movie’s appeal, especially when little shards of the unvarnished reality of the era stab through, such as the 1975 boxing match between Muhammad Ali and Chuck Wepner that supposedly inspired the Rocky script. Stallone is shown watching the actual fight — which is riveting — on one of those small, chunky old TVs hanging over the bar in a local New York City dive. We get to see key moments such as when Wepner, a hulking white fighter shambling around the infinitely quicker and more formidable Ali, nevertheless manages to get in a surprise punch and knock Ali flat on his ass. Realizing he’s underestimated his opponent, Ali commences to batter Wepner relentlessly, but Wepner somehow stays on his feet into the fifteenth round before the fight is stopped and Ali wins on a TKO. Like a lot of movies in the recession-era 1970s , Rocky was all about working-class struggle and the blind hope that some miraculous chance would allow the protagonists to escape their bleak fates or at least redeem their thwarted lives in some sort of meaningful way. A solid number of these movies were centered on sports, whether they were comedies or dramas — see the darkly hilarious hockey movie, Slap Shot (1977) as the perfect example. These narratives often played out against an all-too-realistic backdrop of government and corporate corruption in the United States, the collapse of the industrial economy, the crumbling of infrastructure, the rising of unemployment, and the general impoverishment of the American population. I Play Rocky obviously preserves some of the lower-class misery these films emphasized, but all in the sweetening context of what we know is a success story so huge it’s like a fairy tale. The most dangerous edge of 1970s films, so many of which were remarkably grim and unsparing, has all been softened and planed away by a lot of hokum and an avoidance of the more harrowing aspects of Rocky . There’s no depiction, for example, of Rocky’s brutal beating in the ring at the hands of Apollo Creed as he loses the fight, or his raw screams for Adrian at the climactic moment of the film. This is notable because, in I Play Rocky , there’s a whole subplot featuring director John Avildsen’s old-pro wisdom about the one memorably emotional scene in a film that can make it a success even if the rest of the movie isn’t so hot, and his urgent advice to Stallone to dig deep and do the best acting of his life in such a scene. It turns out not to be the “Adrian!” scene you might be expecting. The Biopics Won’t End I Play Rocky is a bit like another recent hit biopic, Michael , which also focuses, even more worshipfully, on the early life of a hugely successful star — in that case, Michael Jackson. As with the Sylvester Stallone story, the all-purpose villain of the piece that both scars and motivates Michael Jackson is his abusive father. And both films end at the point before the star’s life becomes darkly messy and very hard to sanctify. Consider what any sequels to I Play Rocky would potentially have to deal with. Ten years after Rocky made him a star, Stallone is divorced from his wife Sasha Czack, who’s played by AnnaSophia Robb in I Play Rocky and represents his stalwart true love and devoted wife and mother of his first child. In 1985, not long after the divorce from Sasha, Stallone swiftly married model and actor Brigitte Nielsen, who played a role in Rocky IV (1985), written and directed by Stallone. The couple’s short union and ugly divorce began an extended roiling period of Stallone’s personal life that made him a favorite of the tabloid press. His tangled involvements with various models — Janice Dickinson, Angie Everhart, and Jennifer Flavin, whom he married in 1997 — kept supermarket aisle readers entertained for years. Various unsavory accusations of physical and sexual abuse against Stallone by romantic partners that allegedly occurred from the mid-1980s through 2000, including allegations by his own half sister , have kept his lawyers busy through the years. (Stallone denied, and continues to deny, all of the allegations.) As for all the inspirational bonding between Stallone and the tough but ultimately mensch-like producers of Rocky , Irwin Winkler and Robert Chartoff (played by P. J. Byrne and Toby Kebbell in I Play Rocky ), Stallone’s hatred of Winkler has long been on the record , though he claims to have loved Chartoff. At various points over the years of the franchise, Stallone has called out the elderly Winkler in the media as “remarkably untalented and parasitical,” and accused Winkler’s “moronic vulture children,” Charles and David, of “picking clean the bones” of the Rocky franchise. This feud is based on the original contract that gave the producers an equity stake in a potential franchise but deprived Stallone of one, though he continues to reap other forms of profit from Rocky movies and, of course, is very, very rich. Still, he could always be a bit richer, and it’s a fair enough point — Hollywood contracts are notoriously cutthroat. In I Play Rocky , Stallone doesn’t even read the contract before signing it, much less get a lawyer or agent to look it over. But in I Play Rocky , Winkler and Chartoff go from tough, dismissive Hollywood producers to schmaltzy, huggable slobs, and the film ends with everyone beaming over their shared success. That’s a recognizable emotional trajectory of filmmaking — lots of temporary sentimentalists in the film business giving bear hugs right before the shiv goes in — and it should be noted that both producers really did put up their houses as collateral in order to get studio funding for Rocky . But there’s no indication of the sharp dealing going on or Stallone’s vengeful fury to come. In short, I Play Rocky takes a slightly unusual approach to the biopic — focusing just on the story of Sylvester Stallone getting Rocky made and becoming a star — but otherwise it’s completely typical of the genre. That’s not a compliment, but it hardly matters; audiences love biopics regardless. It’s a shame the biopic isn’t formally classified as a subgenre of fantasy. That would clear up a lot about how these films get made. Here’s my question for historians: Has a great imperial power on the downhill slope ever gone quite this crazy before? Given the endless years of human history, it’s not often that you see something that seems truly new (leaving aside, of course, our changing techno-world from electric vehicles to the online universe). In human terms, though, while imperial powers have endlessly risen and fallen, they don’t normally fall with — to give him the credit he deserves — a Donald J. Trump at the helm or a Pete Hegseth as secretary of defense (oops, sorry, war!). And it is remarkable. The country that was once (not so very long ago) arguably the greatest imperial power ever is now being taken into the ditch of history by a uniquely nutty president and crew. Okay, so now the Democrats have impressively won sizable majorities in both the Senate and the House. This moment feels somewhat like the fishbowl ending of the movie “The Candidate,” when the victorious stunned Senator-elect, played by Robert Redford, who has just pulled off a major upset, disorientedly asks his campaign manager, “What do we do now?” Indeed: what will the Democrats do now with their majority? Can they solve real problems for everyday Americans? Governing is a lot harder than defeating a badly wounded rabid candidate like Donald Trump. Here’s an idea that would allow Democrats to solve a real problem that has been hurting many American workers as they grapple with changes in the economy. In recent years, the US labor force has been undergoing a fundamental transformation. In today’s “Uber economy,” self-employed workers, contractors, freelancers, gig workers, temps, permatemps, remote workers after COVID and so-called “independent workers” have been championed by Silicon Valley and Wall Street as the “new way to work”—the wave of the future, especially with AI changing the nature of work itself. Many US consumers have driven in an Uber taxi, but don’t realize that most drivers earn below minimum wage after accounting for vehicle expenses, gas, maintenance, and unpaid time waiting for passengers. Those drivers are so-called “independent contractors,” so the billion-dollar Uber is not responsible for providing healthcare, Social Security contributions, injured worker or unemployment compensation, not to mention sick leave or vacation pay. Related: Women Politicians Under Attack: The Growing Threat to Democracy Another type of “gig-preneur” can be found on platforms like Fiverr, an online job brokerage that connects buyers to sellers of numerous tasks and services called micro-gigs. Each gig pays as little as $5 per job (hence, its Fiverr name). One worker, after fulfilling a total of 27 orders, found that she had made a grand total of $176. “I’ve seen panhandlers get more money outside of the 7-11 ,” she complained. Other types of workers swept up in this job market degradation don’t work at gigs, they have regular jobs. Meet Chris Young, an assembly-line worker at a Nissan manufacturing plant in Tennessee. Young works alongside dozens of other employees doing the exact same job, but he doesn’t work directly for Nissan. Instead, he works as a “perma-temp” for Yates Services, a private contractor who provides a majority of Nissan’s workers. He builds the same Infiniti SUV as the Nissan workers, but he and other Yates employees receive half the salary, less job security, and way fewer safety net benefits. Some “temps” have worked at the same company for as long as 11 years—hence the new term “perma-temp.” How many “new economy” workers are there? America is a wealthy nation, but unfortunately these drastic changes to the job markets have resulted in millions of Americans not enjoying the bounty of that wealth. How many of these new economy workers are there? It’s hard to say because they are difficult to count. The Bureau of Labor Statistics counts about 12 million “independent contractors” (7.4% of 162 million workers in the US) and another 2.8 million (1.7%) on-call workers, over 9 percent of the total workforce. But the BLS uses a narrow “worker” definition, and broader definitions push total estimates much higher, from 16.5 million to over 30 million workers, depending on whether researchers count occasional side gigs or secondary jobs. Industry trackers like MBO Partners estimate that r oughly 75 million Americans (46%) engage in some form of independent work yearly, with 22% treating it as their primary full-time career, more than double the 10% who did so in 2019. Related: Why Are Democrats Shooting Themselves in the Foot on Ranked Choice Voting? New app- and Web-based technologies are making it easier than ever to hire such workers. And there is great incentive for employers to do so, especially in certain industries and occupations, to take advantage of what is known as the “independent contractor loophole.” An employer can lower labor costs dramatically by around 30%, just by evading contributions for a worker’s health benefits, Social Security, Medicare, unemployment, injured workers compensation, lunch breaks, paid sick days and vacation leave. Also, a McKinsey report found that independent contractors get paid 20-30% less than employees. So these savings provides a powerful incentive for an employer to hire as many contractors as possible. To be sure, some businesses legitimately benefit from a degree of labor flexibility, since they need to have an ability to ramp up and down in response to fluctuations in demand for their product or service. But hiring a large contractor workforce allows a company to greatly lower its labor costs by wiping out its workers’ safety net, which undermines a key component that has built the middle class and made the US economy so successful. Portable safety net provides the best of both worlds Fortunately, there is an innovative solution to this tension, and the Democrats should jump on this. It’s called a “portable safety net,” or “portable benefits.” It would allow any worker hired by multiple employers during the year to benefit from a robust safety net. Here’s how it works. Each business who hires an independent worker would contribute a certain amount for that worker, above the wage , that is pro-rated according to the number of hours the worker has worked for that employer. Those payroll deductions would be set aside in an Individual Security Account for each worker, with the funds helping that worker to pay for her/his safety net. The ISA accounts would be structured to direct the payroll deductions into existing federal and state safety net programs—Social Security, Medicare, unemployment insurance and injured workers’ compensation—as well for other safety net components, especially health care, and possibly paid sick days and vacation (the worker also would have some wages deducted, much like regularly-employed full-time workers do). Related: From “11,780 Votes” to Trump’s 2026 Election Power Grab For example, suppose Donna is a regular employee working 20 hours a week for a hairdresser, then contracts for 10 hours a week with TaskRabbit, and drives 10 shifts for Uber. She would earn 50 percent of her benefits from the hairdresser, 25 percent from TaskRabbit, and another percentage based on her wages driving for Uber. The funds from each company would be deposited into her ISA. Note that this is similar to the way Social Security and Obamacare work now, in which a retirement account or a health care account is tracked with a personal ID number for individuals who work for multiple employers. A portable safety net for all workers who need it would result in a number of beneficial effects. It would allow some scheduling flexibility for workers and businesses, as well as the health and financial security that workers and their families need. It also would shore up affordability for many individuals and families, especially after the Trump administration’s deep cuts to Medicaid, Medicare, SNAP and other programs. A portable safety net also would move us beyond all the labor abuses and lawsuits over who’s a contractor or regular employee and entitled to safety net coverage, because all workers would be covered . It would remove the perverse incentive for businesses to use cheaper contractors as a way of lowering their labor costs and undercutting their competitors, which unleashes a race to the bottom. Note that requiring all businesses in an industry to pay into their workers’ ISAs would not hurt the competitiveness of any those businesses, because all employers would be subject to the same rules. The costs for this kind of “safety net insurance plan” could be passed on by businesses to their customers, since that is the largest pool capable of absorbing these costs. Many of the customers would also be the same workers receiving benefits, so it would be a virtuous circle, “a rising tide floating all boats.” A number of countries already do something like this. I first proposed it for American workers back in 2015-16 in my book Raw Deal about the “Uber economy,” and in a policy paper for New America , and the idea sort of caught on in public policy circles . It was endorsed by 40 business, government, labor, and NGO leaders from the right and left who agree: it is a necessary foundation for the 21 st century economy. President Barack Obama endorsed portable benefits in his 2016 State of the Union address, and legislation was proposed in state legislatures in New York, Washington and New Jersey. The idea had some legislative momentum but Trump and COVID killed it. Ironically, the only employers at the time who tried the idea was Uber and Lyft for their California operations, but the Individual Security Accounts they offered were so measly as to be nearly worthless. Rather than paying into the ISA something close to the 20% of a worker’s wage that is necessary to provide an adequate safety net, Uber and Lyft offered only 2.5%. And they required minimum “hours worked” thresholds to qualify that most drivers couldn’t reach. Not surprisingly, Uber/Lyft cynically offered the portable benefits as part of their oppositional campaign, Proposition 22, to overturn a California law, AB 5, that required the companies to treat their drivers like regular employees instead of independent contractors. Related: AI Warfare Goes Unchecked So “portable safety net” was a bit ahead of its time, but none of the salient issues have gone away; in fact the need has only increased as the labor force prepares for the tsunami of AI. In the current moment, this would be a great issue for the Democrats to champion. Already there is momentum building in various states . A portable safety net for all workers is an idea whose time has come. It would help ensure that the labor force and the broader economy are placed on a fair and healthy foundation for the future. All Americans deserve to be enriched by technology and innovation, instead of made obsolete or impoverished by it. For the Democrats, this has the makings of a win-win solution. This line came from three of the most important nuclear weapons countries in the world, only one of which – the United States – had used a nuclear bomb against civilians (in Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1945). The other nuclear weapons countries (France, India, Israel, North Korea, Pakistan, and the United Kingdom) do not have the scale of the arsenals of the three that met in Shenzhen, which means that they do not have the capacity for a full nuclear war; for most of them, the nuclear weapons are a deterrent against the destruction of their state by others. The earliest treaty around the use of nuclear weapons was signed on 1 December 1959. This Antarctic Treaty banned nuclear explosions and radioactive waste disposal in Antarctica. It was followed on 5 December 1963 with the Partial Test Ban Treaty between the USSR, the United States, and the United Kingdom. This second treaty prohibited nuclear explosions in the atmosphere, in outer space, and underwater, while leaving underground testing permitted (this was eventually banned by the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test Ban Treaty of 10 September 1996, although the major nuclear powers have not signed onto it yet). Shenzhen’s nuclear summit

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Vijay Prashad ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-24T03:39:07+00:00

Lee en español aquí On November 19, President Xi Jinping of China hosted a meeting with President Vladimir Putin of Russia and President Donald Trump of the United States. They met in Shenzhen at the margins of the Asia Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) forum. The final statement that appeared from them had a very powerful line, “A nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought.” This line came from three of the most important nuclear weapons countries in the world, only one of which – the United States – has used a nuclear bomb against civilians (in Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1945). The other nuclear weapons countries (France, India, Israel, North Korea, Pakistan, and the United Kingdom) do not have the scale of the arsenals of the three that met in Shenzhen, which means that they do not have the capacity for a full nuclear war; for most of them, the nuclear weapons are a deterrent against the destruction of their state by others. The statement from these three powers, then, is indicative of the mood toward a new agreement on the danger and futility of nuclear weapons. The earliest treaty around the use of nuclear weapons was signed on December 1, 1959. This Antarctic Treaty banned nuclear explosions and radioactive waste disposal in Antarctica. It was followed on December 5, 1963 with the Partial Test Ban Treaty between the USSR, the United States, and the United Kingdom. This second treaty prohibited nuclear explosions in the atmosphere, in outer space, and underwater, while leaving underground testing permitted (this was eventually banned by the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test Ban Treaty of September 10, 1996, although the major nuclear powers have not signed onto it yet). Over the past sixty-seven years, we saw an increase in treaties to manage the diabolical presence of nuclear weapons for the first decades, and then we saw a collapse of the entire nuclear management machinery. That machinery has been dismantled piece by piece. The Shenzhen meeting is the first in very many years that revives the language of the danger of nuclear war and should be built upon by all peace-loving people. The collapse of nuclear weapons management On February 5, 2026, New Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty (New START) expired. Signed in 2010 and extended in 2021, this treaty had restricted deployed US and Russian strategic nuclear forces and provided mechanisms for verification. It is the last of the treaties that manage the nuclear weapons arsenals of the nuclear weapons states to lapse. There are now no binding numerical limits on the nuclear weapons arsenals. Reacting to this, UN Secretary-General António Guterres said , “For the first time in more than half a century, we face a world without any binding limits on the strategic nuclear arsenals.” It would be inaccurate to say that no nuclear weapons agreements remain. The Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty continues to exist, as do other nuclear treaties and obligations. The specific danger is that the two states possessing the largest arsenals (the USA and Russia) no longer operate under a binding bilateral ceiling on their strategic nuclear forces. Assurances of restraint cannot replace inspections, exchanges of information, agreed definitions, and enforceable limits. Political understandings can disappear with a change of government or a deterioration in relations. That is why the Shenzhen statement is significant, but only if it rebuilds a new treaty between these countries. The record of what has been lost is sobering: First. The Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty, signed in 1972, limited missile defense systems and helped to anchor the strategic relationship between Washington and Moscow. The United States withdrew on June 13, 2002, when US President George W. Bush decided to take advantage of temporary Russian weakness to advance the goals of US power. Whatever the limited promise made for missile defense, the abandonment of negotiated restraints encouraged adversaries to seek new means of overcoming those defenses. Military spending, a waste of social wealth, began to balloon upwards. Second. The Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty, signed in 1987, eliminated US and Soviet ground-launched missiles with ranges between 500 and 5,500 kilometers. Washington withdrew from the treaty on August 2, 2019, when President Trump decided to press further US advantages against Russia. An entire category of weapons, once removed through negotiation, returned to military planning. This withdrawal further cornered Russia and provoked the invasion of eastern Ukraine. These two examples are the culmination of years of erosion across several areas of arms control. But they are the most significant treaties to have been set aside, and therefore put the world into a new arms race, particularly a new nuclear arms race. These agreements were a product of fear and political calculation. They were needed to understand that an adversary’s insecurity could become everyone’s catastrophe. In that sense, the inspections and the restrictions operated as confidence building measures across disagreements. Their destruction has restored a dangerous dependence on threat assessments, intelligence estimates, and the judgment of leaders operating under pressure. At Shenzhen, three leaders did not announce any reductions, verification arrangements, or timetable for negotiations, but they have established a political opening through which a peace agenda could be rebuilt. China’s participation adds an important dimension, since Beijing has not been a party to most of these bilateral treaties. A durable framework must address Beijing’s security concerns as well as those of Washington and Moscow. Humanity cannot afford to dismiss an opening for negotiation because it falls short of an agreement. Europe’s absence The absence of Europe at the meeting in Shenzhen is notable. Rather than accept the deescalation that could result from the meeting in China, the European leaders gathered in Berlin for a Coalition of the Willing meeting. This name, Coalition of the Willing, comes from a platform developed by retired UK politician Keir Starmer in March 2025, with the stated purpose for European leaders to support Ukraine’s military. If in Shenzhen the three major powers spoke of their concern for nuclear war and war in general, at Berlin the Europeans spoke of increased military expenditure, weapons deliveries, and an increased pressure campaign against Russia. Coalition members reaffirmed a pledge of €70 billion in military equipment, assistance, and training for Ukraine in 2026, with at least equivalent support in 2027. They proposed stronger economic pressure on Russia and discussed a European-led multinational force, supported by the United States, to operate after a ceasefire. These commitments extended the material infrastructure of confrontation. Shenzhen focused on de-escalation through engagement, while Berlin put the matter of escalation of military commitments firmly on the table. The European leaders’ talk of military expenditure came as their own populations took to the streets to condemn the austerity agenda that would make Europe more impoverished and more insecure. There was no public mention of these protests, nor was there acknowledgment that their agenda has very low public support . Opposition to the agenda of increased military spending and increased social austerity reaches 63% in Italy, 59% in Austria, 56% in Germany, and 55% in Estonia. The statement itself is anodyne, asking for greater military spending and for Russian concessions but it does not spell out a broad plan for European security. The entire agenda relies upon Europe’s dependency on Washington for crucial military capabilities and diplomatic access. This makes the Berlin agenda utterly unsustainable. BRICS+ agenda The opening of new negotiations for nuclear weapons control carries particular significance for the BRICS+ agenda. Countries outside the principal military blocs would bear devastating consequences from a confrontation they neither initiated nor controlled. Their interest in reducing nuclear danger is direct, which is why they should press for negotiations rather than leave the issue to an exclusive conversation among the largest powers. China and Russia attended Shenzhen in their national capacities. Their participation did not give them a mandate to bargain for the entire Global South. The test is whether their diplomacy creates greater room for other countries to pursue development, trade, and independent foreign policies. Reduced confrontation could open that space; an arrangement protecting only the privileges of powerful states would narrow its promise. BRICS+ has already connected disarmament to its practical development agenda. Resources consumed by arms races are unavailable for public health, housing, ecological repair, and productive investment. Its institutions can widen access to finance while its members demand that the nuclear powers fulfill their responsibilities. The New Development Bank’s pursuit of lending in members’ national currencies illustrates one avenue for increasing countries’ economic options. At the 19th BRICS+ Summit to be held in China, the issue of the trade-off between development and war will likely be further discussed. Shenzhen’s importance lies in the possibility it reopened. Three nuclear powers acknowledged a shared danger at a moment when binding restraints have disappeared. The task now is to make their acknowledgment material: negotiations, verification, limits, and reductions. Humanity needs an international movement strong enough to insist that the next announcement concerns weapons withdrawn and agreements signed, rather than another budget for preparing the war no society can survive. On November 12, the campaigns for Ecuador’s local elections formally began. This Sunday, November 29, voters will elect prefects, mayors, city council members, and members of local boards. Historically, local elections have reflected the level of support or opposition to the current government. In this sense, right-wing president Daniel Noboa has more at stake than just a simple popular election. This is especially true when considering the last elections (which centered on four referendum questions) held on November 16, 2025. In those elections, Noboa suffered a fourfold electoral defeat, as the majority of Ecuadorians rejected his attempt to repeal the current constitution – which guarantees rights – in favor of a neoliberal one. Furthermore, Ecuadorians said no to the possibility of establishing foreign military bases. The defeat took the government by surprise, as it had been confident of a victory in a country that, in recent history, has consistently voted “yes” in referendums held by successive administrations. Read more: Ecuadorian people deal a crushing blow to neoliberalism in Noboa’s referendum And for that very reason, Noboa needs a victory at all costs. But it is important to understand that success for Noboa does not necessitate that his candidates win, but rather that his main opponent – Correísmo – loses. Noboa’s anti-Correa crusade From the very beginning, his main selling point (and the primary reason for the support that enabled him to win two presidential elections) has been to present himself as the antithesis of the supporters of Ecuador’s former president, the progressive Rafael Correa (2007–2017). As a result, Noboa has been viewed by many as a key player capable of preventing the return of Correísmo to the presidency, and it is precisely this antithetical distinction that keeps him a politically relevant figure. This has led Correa’s own party, the Citizen Revolution, to claim that the elections on November 29 will take place in an undemocratic and authoritarian environment, in which most institutions – including electoral ones – serve the interests of the executive branch. The main basis for this accusation stems from the suspension of the Citizen Revolution party in early 2025. The party’s candidates have been forced (when possible) to seek participation in the elections on other parties’ slates. Added to this is a series of allegations from the opposition regarding alleged political persecution against the main political leaders of the Citizen Revolution and their allies. Several months have already passed since Aquiles Álvarez, the then-mayor of Guayaquil, Ecuador’s largest city, was imprisoned in a maximum-security prison. The current mayor of Quito, the capital, Pabel Muñoz, who is seeking reelection, has been accused of various alleged crimes . The mayor of Cuenca was removed from office, and the prosecutor’s office is currently seeking to arrest him. Opposition leader Luisa González jailed by Noboa government One of the most emblematic cases is that of Luisa González, president of the Citizen Revolution and a two-time presidential candidate (who lost to Noboa both times). González was seeking the governorship of Manabí (a stronghold of Correísmo) but on October 22, she was arrested and has been held in prison on charges of money laundering ever since. Read more: Luisa González, former presidential candidate and opposition leader, is arrested in Ecuador And while the executive branch defends these actions by linking the Citizen Revolution to organized crime, the Correístas insist that this is political persecution, and that the upcoming election is one in which every effort has been made to ensure that their candidates fail to win elected office; therefore, they claim, the current government is seeking to ensure the victory of candidates close to it, who would run as candidates for other parties. Thus, according to this interpretation, Noboa seeks to win with his own candidates and with allied candidates from other parties. But his greatest victory would be to prevent the Citizen Revolution from achieving wins that would once again call into question his popularity, which has not recovered from its slight but steady decline over the past several months. For their part, the polls have once again shown clear inconsistencies among themselves, which has raised suspicions of alleged political manipulation of the numbers. In some polls, candidates opposed to the government hold a slight lead over many pro-government candidates or those allied with the executive branch. Other polls, however, show Noboa’s candidates in the lead. However, since these are regional elections, they will reveal different political realities and aspirations depending on the region under consideration. And while a country as complex and diverse as Ecuador may offer various paths forward, there are four provinces (Guayas, Pichincha, Manabí, and Azuay) and three cities (Quito, Guayaquil, and Cuenca) that will surely attract the most attention. They are home to a huge percentage of the population and tend to set the pace and political trends for the 2028 presidential elections.
