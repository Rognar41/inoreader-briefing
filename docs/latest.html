In Last Night’s Midterms, the Left Proved It Can Win

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Branko Marcetic ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-04T22:22:23+00:00

The Left has been so conditioned in the Trump era to look aghast and horrified at election results that it is still hard to fully believe the outcome today. Yet the historic events of last night really did happen: Donald Trump and Republicans suffered a historic rebuke from voters, handing Democrats wins up and down the ballot all across the country, flipping both chambers of Congress in their favor, and handing them the largest congressional majorities they’ve had since Barack Obama’s election. In many ways, the result is simple. Across poll after poll leading into the election and exit poll after exit poll leading out of it, the message from voters was the same: they think the economy sucks, they think prices are too high, and they’re angry at Trump and blame him squarely for this entire situation. To send this message, voters turned out in droves to hand power to a Democratic Party that these same polls show they have very little love for, sometimes viewing the Dems even more dimly than they do Trump. Democrats were expected to take the House. They were not expected to win a majority of more than 250 seats in it, as current estimates have them winning. Nor were they expected to win a Senate majority, let alone a Senate majority of at least fifty-four seats. And they were not expected to flip deep red seats across the country, to sweep Republicans from control of Texas at nearly every level of power, and to win a majority of the country’s governorships, achieving wins and trifectas in states they’ve been practically locked out of for sometimes decades. Obama referred to his 2010 midterms rout as a “shellacking.” Last night was an annihilation. This is not just good news for Democrats. For the Left, which operates within these Democratic majorities at both the state and federal levels, the results present a historic opportunity to turn its political priorities from words into tangible reality across the country, further building the power it has patiently and, for a long time, quietly been constructing from the ground up these past ten years . That includes at the federal level. Even though Trump has two more years in the White House, and Democrats are threatening to zealously turn their powers of oversight on the vast record of abuse and criminality he’s racked up during that time, there is some, extremely slim reason to believe something could actually get done. Trump phoned Politico last night to make clear he was open to working with the Democrats and didn’t “want to be just sitting there for two years vetoing everything.” The last time Trump was in the White House with a Democratic Congress, the pandemic, the United States saw the largest expansion of the welfare state in decades. Trump’s ideological malleability and the fact that he is currently looking at a legacy as a historic failure presents at least the possibility of an opening for something better to happen. It’s an opening the Left will be able to take advantage of, too. The Left will enter this new Congress with far more formidable numbers than it’s had in decades: at least seven members of the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA), creating the historic opportunity to for the first time form a distinct socialist caucus in Congress and a Squad-like progressive “ Bloc ” that could have as many as twenty members. The massive Democratic House majority means “the Bloc” will not have the expected leverage of being able to block House Democratic leader’s Hakeem Jeffries’s bid for Speaker, or to hold up legislation Freedom Caucus–style. But it will have influence. Much of the generous pandemic policies of 2020, including both Trump and, later, Joe Biden’s stimulus checks, originated with the public demands of socialist and progressive members of Congress that later found their way into legislation on the back of their clear public need and popularity. This time, the Left will be making these arguments in a world in which socialists have already transformed the way even centrist Democrats talk about politics. The party’s years-long obsession with “norms” and rescuing democracy having largely faded into the background in favor of the affordability-focused and anti-billionaire rhetoric popularized by, respectively, New York’s socialist mayor, Zohran Mamdani, and the socialist duo of Sanders and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez on their “Fighting Oligarchy” tour. Just as importantly, it’s a world in which the Left, thanks to a spate of major wins last night, has thoroughly discredited the arguments used to marginalize and dismiss its basic case. Earthquake in Michigan No left-wing victory last night was bigger than Abdul El-Sayed’s Michigan Senate win over Republican Mike Rogers by a whopping 7 points. It was the exact, hefty margin El-Sayed himself had promised he would win by back in May and defied the incessant predictions that he would end up badly underperforming. There was arguably no more important race this year than for the purple state’s Senate seat: for the Left, which sought to prove progressives could run and win outside of blue strongholds; for corporate Democrats, a segment of whom feared losing the ability to fearmonger about supposedly risky left-wing candidates; for the pro-Israel lobby, which feared that the success of a stridently pro-Palestinian message would obliterate its claim that blind fealty to Israel was “good politics”; for Trump and the MAGA movement, whose loss of the key state could be a harbinger of even worse political defeat to come. Abdul El-Sayed on election night. (Jeff Kowalsky / AFP via Getty Images) El-Sayed’s win has confirmed all of these. It represents not just a failure for MAGA, in the form of now two-time loser and career-long warmonger Mike Rogers. It’s an absolute disaster for the pro-Israel lobby and the American Israel Public Affairs Committee (AIPAC) in particular, which blew tens of millions of dollars over the course of both the primary and general to defeat El-Sayed and tried every trick in the book to defame and make voters, particularly Jewish ones, fear him. Instead, both exit polls and voting data indicates El-Sayed comfortably won Jewish voters despite this ugly, Islamophobic campaign against him. He outperformed Kamala Harris in the heavily Jewish Oakland County, the home base of his Democratic primary rival, Haley Stevens, who humiliated herself trying to kneecap him in the final days of the race. Catastrophically for the pro-Israel lobby, he did all this while maintaining an unabashed opposition to further US aid to Israel, proving unambiguously that left-wing criticisms of Israeli policy are not only broadly shared by voters in battleground states, but that they are shared by Jewish voters , too. That he managed this is especially impressive given that, unlike his Democratic colleague running for governor, El-Sayed had to overcome not only an avalanche of big money spending but also members of his own party working around the clock to sabotage him, from Stevens’s last-minute endorsement of Rogers to Rep. Hillary Scholten’s support for a write-in campaign against the Democratic nominee in the final days. Democratic defectors to the GOP had a variety of motives, but at least some of them made publicly clear they hoped El-Sayed’s defeat would nip the growing Democratic opposition to US aid to Israel in the bud — something they were willing to elect a candidate who included actual antisemites in his campaign to do, as well as to empower an administration that makes common cause with literal neo-Nazis and white supremacists. Instead, they lost and disgraced themselves for nothing. El-Sayed also did it on a progressive platform of Medicare for All and taxing the rich, two ideas that, when Bernie Sanders first popularized them in 2016, the public was told were too controversial with swing voters and risky to run on. A decade later, these ideas have such widely understood, popular support that Republicans didn’t even bother attacking them. Instead, they put all their eggs in the race-baiting basket, making El-Sayed’s Arab ethnicity and Muslim faith the overwhelming focus of their attacks in what turned out to be one of the most shockingly racist election campaigns this century — and another disgraceful failure. Wins and Losses El-Sayed’s win wasn’t the only bright spot for the Left last night. There was also Troy Jackson’s win in Maine, who succeeded where four others had failed in unseating the seemingly untouchable thirty-year Republican incumbent Susan Collins from her Senate seat. Though Jackson, an eighteen-year state legislator and former state senate president and Democratic leader, was far from your typical image of a left insurgent, he quickly consolidated socialist and progressive backing after former nominee Graham Platner’s personal scandals pushed him out of the race. Though not a socialist, Jackson’s genuine working-class background — as a fifth-generation logger who entered politics to win collective bargaining rights for his fellow woodworkers — and robust progressive record made him closer to the kind of candidates the Left hopes to see in power. In the end, Jackson was able to secure victory by neutralizing Collins’s ability to run up big scores in otherwise blue-voting areas. The other major progressive win was by James Talarico, who became the first Democrat to win a Texas seat in thirty-eight years, part of a historic blue sweep across Texas that saw Democrats end nearly three decades of GOP rule of the state. Talarico is not a socialist , despite old comments criticizing capitalism that briefly became a campaign issue, and like El-Sayed, he took pains to insist that he was a capitalist. Even so, he was backed by groups like More Perfect Union and the Progressive Change Campaign Committee and drew support from prominent socialists like Hasan Piker , who has said that the Left was “the force that backed him early on” and “allowed him to win.” Maybe more important, Talarico ran on a class-based message decrying “ billionaire mega-donors ” — one of whom, Elon Musk, lit at least $47 million on fire trying and failing to beat him — and insisting that the fundamental political divide in the United States was “top versus bottom,” a more progressive message than many Democrats ran on even in solid blue states. So his emphatic 12 point victory over the scandal-plagued Ken Paxton is a victory for the kind of populist message the Left has long advocated, even as Talarico did things like refusing to back Medicare for All and declining to campaign with Bernie Sanders when he visited the state. Maine’s Democratic senator-elect, Troy Jackson. (Troy Jackson for Maine) Speaking of Sanders, as of the time of writing, seventeen of the twenty-one candidates endorsed by the socialist senator have won their races, while only two have lost, and two are in races that are still to be called. Sanders’s endorsements were less straightforwardly left-wing than those of organizations like DSA or Justice Democrats. They included candidates like El-Sayed and Jackson, but they also included more establishment candidates like Minnesota Senator-elect Peggy Flanagan, who beat corporate-friendly rivals in their primaries. They ran the gamut from DSA candidates like New York’s Claire Valdez and Colorado’s Melat Kiros to candidates like Rebecca Cooke, the less progressive candidate in her primary but someone who flipped a Trump-voting, rural Wisconsin district that for decades was represented by a Democrat. They also included William Lawrence, the Sunrise Movement cofounder who has flipped a swing district in the swing state of Michigan despite having one of the more left-wing résumés of any Democratic candidate — something that, like El-Sayed, we were told would make him a guaranteed loser. The fact that Lawrence left DSA mid-campaign and had to distance himself from old tweets that included calls to nationalize the fossil fuel industry should not take away from the magnitude of his win. On the back of a progressive populist campaign that pushed a data center moratorium and Medicare for All , Lawrence won a purple district that used to belong to the Democrats’ patron saint of centrism, former CIA analyst Elissa Slotkin. Even some of the defeats were surprisingly good for the Left. In ruby-red Florida — where Republicans outnumber Democrats by 1.5 million and a Democrat hasn’t won statewide office since 2012 — DSA member Angie Nixon lost by less than 3 points, little different from the 2 point losing margin of the ex-Republican candidate who Democrats ran for governor. The state’s last three Democratic losing margins for Senate are 13, 16, 0.2, and 8 points. And that’s after being outspent nearly four-to-one and getting no help whatsoever from the national Democratic Party, which should be kicking itself for missing the opportunity to win another gettable Senate seat. That a DSA member would even be competitive in Florida — the heartland of right-wing Cuban extremism and the home of both Marco Rubio and now two Bolsonaro sons — would have sounded laughable five years ago, or even five months ago. That Nixon could do this well with this little help will, together with El-Sayed’s win, make it that much harder to argue that the socialist label is an inherent electoral liability. It should spark some soul-searching among the Democratic establishment and its decision to preemptively write off the race as a foregone loss. But it will no doubt also spark debate within the socialist movement, too, given Nixon’s result was built partly on distancing herself from alienating positions like defunding the police and abolishing prisons. Her strong showing, along with the success of other left-wing candidates who similarly moderated or eschewed contemporary left orthodoxy on some issues, points to the benefits of DSA candidates having the flexibility to tailor campaigns to local electorates. The Left also suffered a defeat of sorts in Nebraska, where independent Dan Osborn fell short at a second tilt at a Senate seat in a state where a Democrat hasn’t won statewide election for twenty years. Osborn, a union leader who led a successful 2021 strike against Kellogg’s over planned benefit cuts, was something of a proof of concept for the Left: that, as per research from the Center for Working-Class Politics, the way to win in such states is to run genuine working-class candidates with populist platforms outside of the Democratic Party brand. To some extent, both of Osborn’s campaigns proved this true. The last four Democratic losing margins in Nebraska Senate races were 41 points, 19 points, 24 points, and 16 points; by contrast, Osborn has fallen short by roughly the same, single-digit margin of 7 points both runs. Clearly, if pro-working-class candidates are going to flip a deep red state like Nebraska, Osborn’s campaign is a model for how to do it. But a losing margin is still a loss. Despite combining left-populist policies like “right to repair” with centrist messaging on issues like immigration — Osborn famously ran ads declaring he backed Trump’s border wall — the former union leader was unable to shake attacks that he was a secret Democrat. A particularly damaging set of “ scandals ” had Osborn on tape bashing the border wall and other Trump policies and using insulting language about his voters. On top of an avalanche of outside spending aimed against him, it proved too much to overcome. These are on top of a few other stinging defeats for the Left, including Justice Democrats–backed Justin Pearson’s razor-thin loss in Tennessee’s Ninth District, who looked like he might be able to overcome a Republican gerrymander thanks to a surge in black voter turnout. Socialists may instead be heartened by the victory of Scott Houldieson over a Trump-endorsed candidate in the Republican-held Indiana First Senate District. Houldieson, a union autoworker who cofounded and led the reform effort within the United Auto Workers that resulted in Shawn Fain’s leadership of the union, easily flipped the seat with a 14 point victory on the back of an affordability-focused campaign. Though not DSA-backed or a statewide race, it is to some extent a vindication of the kind of approach the Left believed would see success in Nebraska. The Local Game Continues Meanwhile, as usual, socialists and progressives continued their steady accrual of elected office at the state and local levels. As of the time of writing, DSA has notched 130 wins in everything from state legislatures and city councils, to mayor’s offices and ballot measures, with forty-two losses and fifty-eight races still to be decided. While Angie Nixon fell short of her Senate bid, environmentalist Ashley Herrmann became only the second socialist to win elected office in Florida and the first to be elected to its state legislature in more than a century, flipping a swing district that the Republican incumbent had won with 58 percent of the vote two years earlier. Other notable socialist wins included Robert LeVertis Bell’s election to the Kentucky state house and Mathewos Samson’s election to the Georgia General Assembly, which, together with Gabriel Sanchez’s reelection, makes him one of two socialists in the state legislature. Particularly notable are several mayoral races that were foregone conclusions after the primaries. Janeese Lewis George is now officially Washington, DC’s first socialist mayor, having won on the back of an affordable housing plan that combines “abundance”-style reforms with expanding rent stabilization. In Providence, Rhode Island, socialist David Morales has similarly cruised to an expected win and, together with a socialist-led city council, will now work to pass a robust rent control measure in the housing-crisis-stricken city — a measure the incumbent mayor he successfully primaried had vetoed. More complicated is the Los Angeles mayor’s race, where socialist City Councilor Nithya Raman is currently on track to unseat Karen Bass as Los Angeles mayor. Raman’s candidacy was the subject of some controversy , after she was endorsed by the local DSA chapter despite not fully embracing the Boycott, Divestment, and Sanctions movement against Israel and not being questioned about it, leading the DSA’s National Political Committee to nearly strip her of the endorsement, an effort that failed by one vote. If Raman does win, she’ll be the first candidate to unseat an incumbent LA mayor in twenty-one years. This is on top of a host of progressive ballot measures pushed through by majorities of voters countrywide: the defeat of an abortion ban ballot measure in Missouri, a successful ban on dark money in elections in Montana, and divestment from Israel in Providence, to name a few. Maybe most notably, ballot measures restricting the construction of data centers have so far won overwhelmingly around the country, thirty-eight to one, with eight still to be counted — a clear public vindication of a position championed and popularized by the broad Left. What’s Smart Is What’s Right For the past decade, socialists and progressives have often had good election nights even when the Democratic Party has suffered, and they have had bad nights while Democrats rejoiced. Last night may be the first one where both the Left and the centrist party establishment have seen historic wins in tandem. Over the past two years, the Left showed it was the force most willing to fight against Trump’s right-wing assault and to come up with an effective argument — affordability and fighting oligarchy — to do it. Last night finally proved left policies and rhetoric are not the electoral liability Democratic voters have been told for decades it is. For decades, Democratic voters have had to make the agonizing decision every election to choose between their heads and their hearts. The Left may have just freed them of it. Reform Candidate Says UFCW Rep Tailed Him for 300 Miles to Thwart Petition Effort

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: 不明 ／ 著者: Lisa Xu ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-04T21:46:27+00:00

In a union that has been headed by one family with no competitive elections since 1984, a group of grocery workers is organizing for change. But this week, they say, union staff who back the incumbent administration went to outrageous lengths in an attempt to make sure there’s no election this year either. On Monday afternoon, Eric Marcuz, a veteran Northern California Safeway worker, stopped at the union office to pick up blank petitions to get on the ballot in the union’s upcoming election. Marcuz is running for president on the At What Cost slate in the 23,000-member United Food and Commercial Workers (UFCW) Local 8-Golden State, one of the largest locals in the union. To get on the ballot, Marcuz and his slate-mates have to collect 443 petition signatures—2 percent of the local’s active members—in the next two weeks. If they don’t get enough signatures, no election will be held. After Marcuz picked up the petitions from the local’s Roseville office, he got in his car to drive three hours south to Fresno, to start collecting signatures with another candidate on his slate at a Save Mart store there. (The local spans a 10-hour stretch from Bakersfield up to the Oregon border). When Marcuz stopped for gas, he said he noticed that a black Dodge Ram pickup stopped as well. When he got back on the freeway, so did the truck. At the next gas stop, Marcuz spoke to the man driving the truck. It was Jeff Berns, a staff representative for Local 8-GS. His brother, Mark, is also a rep for the union—and the local’s election chair, responsible for overseeing the election. Marcuz asked Jeff Berns why he was following him. According to Marcuz, Berns said he wanted to make sure that all the members got the “accurate information” that Marcuz’s slate was going to “divide” the union. He said he was going to follow him the entire time Marcuz was out, all the way back to Marcuz’s house in Redding, several hours north. Marcuz jokingly asked him if he needed a place to stay. Berns said he didn’t—because he’d been staying a lot in the Redding area lately himself, which Marcuz said he thought was “creepy” because that’s where Marcuz lives with his family (and is not where Berns lives). Berns would live up to his promise—following Marcuz for the next 300 miles, through every stop, exit, and U-turn. He even followed him inside the Fresno Save Mart, and camped out for an hour with another union rep while Marcuz ate dinner at a supporter's house 100 miles north in Modesto. NO COMPETITIVE ELECTIONS At the gas station, Berns repeated the mantra, “Solidarity works!” That’s the motto of the incumbent president, Jacques Loveall, who is heading the Solidarity Works Team slate in this election. Loveall was appointed to lead the union in 2004, when his father, Jack Loveall, stepped down. The elder Loveall had held the top office since 1984; when he retired he was one of the highest-paid union officers in the country, earning the equivalent of $1 million today. (The Contra Costa Times reported that he had a $28,000-a-year country club membership and “two pilots on staff to chauffeur him in a private jet.”) Neither father nor son has ever faced a competitive election in the local. Back on the freeway, Marcuz called a Department of Labor agent whom he had already been in touch with. They had previously spoken about the local’s apparent attempt to cancel the election by merging with a mysterious organization called the Staffing Agencies Federated Employees, an entity which listed one of Loveall’s relatives as its president on its initial filing. The agent told him to report the intimidation to the election chair and tell him to get the rep to back off. Marcuz then called Mark Berns, the election chair, who he said told him he would talk to staff about acting in a professional manner. MORE REPS However, when Marcuz arrived at the Save Mart store in Fresno to begin collecting petition signatures, he and another slatemate, Mike Romero, said that three union reps were waiting for them, including Jeff Berns. One of the others was John Micheletti, a longtime Local 8 vice president. Far from being called off, they said, the intimidation had actually intensified. Marcuz and Romero said the reps interfered with every conversation they attempted to have with workers about signing their nomination petition. The very first worker they engaged about signing, a longtime acquaintance of Romero’s, fled once a rep entered the conversation and became confrontational. The worker did not sign the petition. “They were everywhere inside the store,” said Marcuz. At least one of the interactions was filmed on a phone. Marcuz said that Berns told him, “I’m going to make sure everyone knows who you are and not to sign that.” And additionally, “We’re going to invalidate every single signature that you get.” It is illegal for union staff and officers to campaign on union time. Berns said he was on vacation. Romero, who has worked at Save Mart for 30 years, said, “This is the most I’ve seen of union staff in six months.” He said a fourth rep turned up after Marcuz left. SEEN THIS BEFORE These tactics—following opposition candidates and invalidating signatures—have both been alleged by previous members of the local who attempted to challenge the Lovealls. A Contra Costa Times article published in 2004 reported that supporters of Loveall’s father got members to rescind their signatures to keep dissident candidate Jack Nordby off the ballot in 1997. A book written by then-Local 588 reform activist Doug Slaydon states that as many as 150 signatures were removed. (Local 588 merged with another local to form Local 8-GS in 2006—postponing a potential competitive election that year.) Nordby also alleged that he was tailed by a pickup truck while collecting ballot signatures, followed around by union reps in stores, and once, chased in his car while driving his wife and two daughters to church. The Contra Costa Times also reported allegations that Local 588 staffers staked out the home of a member of UFCW Local 1179 who had led a successful rank-and-file opposition campaign to an attempted merger of the two locals and “tailed him in union cars.” “In June 1994,” reported the Times , the member, Michael Ross-Smith, “was rear-ended in Walnut Creek by three UFCW 588 business agents following him too closely in a white 1993 Ford Thunderbird leased to the union, according to a traffic collision report from the Walnut Creek police department.” TAILED FOR SEVEN HOURS Marcuz and Romero said they quickly realized that they wouldn’t be able to collect petition signatures with this level of interference. Marcuz’s next stop was going to be to drop off petition forms with a supporter at the Turlock Safeway, over an hour to the north, but he made plans to meet the supporter at their house in Modesto instead. When Marcuz left the parking lot of the Fresno store, Romero said three vehicles followed him. Romero also realized that one of the reps had stayed behind, and he became worried that he would be followed as well. He drove around for a long time before going home, to make sure he wasn’t followed. Marcuz, meanwhile, said that Berns and Micheletti followed him for 90 miles, all the way to Modesto. They stayed parked outside the house while Marcuz ate burritos with the supporter—finally leaving after an hour, after 8 p.m. "It's crazy that they would literally go two hours out of their way to make sure nobody else can talk to the employees,” Romero said. Marcuz said he almost canceled his plans to stay alone in a hotel in Roseville overnight, worried he would be tailed there, and contemplated driving for four more hours home to Redding. In total, he estimates he was followed for seven hours, over a distance of 300 miles. SCARE TACTICS? Section 610 of the Labor-Management Reporting and Disclosure Act (LMRDA) forbids the threat or use of force or violence to restrain, coerce, or intimidate union members from exercising their right to nominate candidates or to vote in union officer elections, while Section 401(e) bars “improper interference” in the rights of union members to run for and support candidates for local union office. “It makes me think of everyone that has known there’s been a need for change in the past, and has been scared away from pursuing it,” Marcuz said. While Marcuz and his slate are standing strong, he said, “this might work. They might intimidate and scare enough people out of participating.” In the morning, Marcuz filed a police report with the Roseville Police Department alleging stalking and harassment, citing the time and distance he was followed and the behavior he encountered in the Fresno store. Local 8 did not respond to requests for comment. Petition signatures are due November 17, and ballots will be mailed November 21.

California’s election returns present a mixed picture for the candidates and ballot positions listed in the LA Progressive November 2026 Voter Guide . Xavier Becerra holds a substantial statewide lead for governor, Jane Kim leads for insurance commissioner, and many congressional and legislative candidates in the guide are ahead. But several are trailing, and statewide voters are taking a different course from Los Angeles County on some major propositions. The distinction matters most for Proposition 4, concerning public campaign financing, and Proposition 40, the billionaire wealth tax. Both have more Yes than No votes in Los Angeles County. Both are trailing statewide. This report combines the Secretary of State’s statewide and full-district returns with Los Angeles County returns for local races. Every lead described below refers to ballots counted so far. Even where the state lists all precincts as reporting, that does not establish that every eligible ballot has been counted or that results are certified. Statewide offices: Becerra leads, with Kim ahead in the insurance race Xavier Becerra has 4,992,161 votes, or 63.9%, against Steve Hilton’s 2,815,459 votes, or 36.1%, in the governor’s contest. Becerra is the candidate listed in our guide. The guide-listed candidates also lead for lieutenant governor, secretary of state, controller, treasurer, attorney general and superintendent of public instruction. The insurance commissioner contest is closer: Jane Kim leads Ben Allen, 52.0% to 48.0%, a margin of 265,576 votes. In Board of Equalization District 3, Sam Sukaton trails Mike Gipson: 707,476 votes, or 40.5%, to Gipson’s 1,038,750 votes, or 59.5%. These are the Secretary of State’s full-district figures. State propositions: public financing and wealth tax trail Proposition 4 has 3,599,779 Yes votes, or 48.0%, and 3,892,816 No votes, or 52.0%. The No side leads by 293,037 votes. LA Progressive’s guide listed Yes. Proposition 40 has 3,419,652 Yes votes, or 44.4%, against 4,280,110 No votes, or 55.6%. Its No side leads by 860,458 votes. The guide also listed Yes on this measure. Proposition 42 offers another contrast with Los Angeles County: its Yes side leads statewide with 54.3%, while the guide listed No. Proposition 38’s Yes side leads with 55.7%, also running against the guide’s No position. Other returns align with the guide. Proposition 39’s No side leads with 58.6%; Proposition 44’s No side has 81.8%. Proposition 5 is much closer, with Yes ahead by 48,425 votes, 50.3% to 49.7%. The following table uses statewide totals throughout. “Aligned” means the side currently ahead matches the guide’s listed position; it is not a declaration of passage or defeat. Congress: full-district totals change the picture in District 23 Tessa Lynn Hodge leads within Los Angeles County’s portion of Congressional District 23, but trails across the full district. The Secretary of State reports 63,681 votes for Hodge, or 48.7%, and 67,169 for Jay Obernolte, or 51.3%. Obernolte’s advantage is 3,488 votes. Angela Gonzales-Torres leads Jimmy Gomez in District 34, 58.7% to 41.3%, while Angélica María Dueñas trails Luz Maria Rivas in District 29, 39.8% to 60.2%. Salud Carbajal, Raul Ruiz and Pete Aguilar—whose contests were absent from the supplied county results—each lead in their full districts. All congressional figures below cover the entire district, including every county portion reported by the Secretary of State. State Legislature: Duncan holds a narrow lead Chris Duncan leads Tony Strickland in Senate District 36 by just 1,060 votes: 141,285 to 140,225, or 50.2% to 49.8%. His much larger percentage advantage within Los Angeles County does not reflect how close the full-district contest is. John Erickson trails Brian Goldsmith in Senate District 24, while Sarah Rascón trails Sara Hernandez in District 26. In Assembly District 51, Colin Hernandez—the candidate listed in the guide’s October 31 update—has 37.2%, behind Rick Chavez Zbur’s 62.8%. The full-district count also supplies results for several Assembly contests missing from the county page. James Ramos, Leila Namvar, Clarissa Cervantes and Corey Jackson lead; Victor Hernandez and Kevin Akin trail. State Senate State Assembly Two guide entries need clarification. The tables retain the guide entries and mark the mismatch explicitly. The Senate District 20 table uses the ballot spelling Caroline Menjivar; the guide spells her first name “Carolyn.” Juan Carrillo’s surname is also standardized to the ballot spelling. Los Angeles: Raman leads Bass, Roy leads for city attorney In the county’s 3:11 a.m. update, Nithya Raman leads Karen Bass by 16,594 votes: 377,458 to 360,864, or 51.12% to 48.88%. Perilous Future of the Republic

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. American democracy is confronting a crisis that extends far beyond the personality, policies, or political ambitions of any single president. What is at stake is the historical meaning of democracy itself: whether a republic founded upon the promise of popular sovereignty can survive the concentration of executive power, the systematic weakening of institutional restraints, the mobilization of political resentment, and the transformation of public life into an arena of permanent ideological warfare. Donald Trump represents the culmination of political tendencies that have been developing within American society for decades. His presidency did not create every contradiction now confronting the republic. Rather, it has brought those contradictions into sharper relief, exposing the vulnerabilities of institutions that many Americans had assumed to be more resilient than they are. The danger is not simply that an individual president may violate democratic norms. It is that the erosion of those norms may become normalized, that the exceptional may become routine, and that the public may gradually lose the capacity to distinguish democratic government from the increasingly personalized exercise of political power. Democracy rarely disappears in a single dramatic moment. Its institutions may remain standing while their substance is hollowed out. Elections continue, courts convene, legislatures assemble, and constitutions remain ceremonially invoked. Yet the conditions necessary for meaningful democratic participation can deteriorate beneath this institutional surface. Citizens may retain the formal right to vote while losing confidence that their voices can influence the decisions shaping their lives. The question confronting the United States is therefore not merely whether its democratic institutions can withstand another political crisis. It is whether the republic can recover the social, ethical, and political commitments that make democracy something more than a procedural arrangement. The Republic at the Edge The American republic was born amid contradictions that have never been fully resolved. Its founding declarations affirmed liberty and equality while slavery remained entrenched in its economic and political foundations. The language of universal rights coexisted with the exclusion of women, Indigenous peoples, enslaved Africans, and those without property from the privileges of citizenship. These contradictions do not invalidate the democratic aspirations expressed in the founding documents. They reveal, rather, that democratic ideals have always exceeded the political arrangements through which they were initially articulated. The struggle for democracy has consequently involved generations of movements attempting to enlarge the meaning of freedom and extend the boundaries of political membership. The abolitionist movement, the labor movement, the women's suffrage movement, the civil rights movement, and countless struggles for economic justice have challenged the tendency of political institutions to serve the interests of established power. Democracy has advanced not simply because political elites voluntarily surrendered their privileges but because ordinary people organized to demand recognition, representation, and material change. This history matters because democracy cannot be reduced to reverence for national symbols or nostalgic appeals to an imagined golden age. A democratic republic is not democratic merely because it possesses a constitution, holds elections, or celebrates its founding documents. It must continually confront the inequalities and exclusions that undermine the promises those documents contain. The contemporary crisis emerges from the widening distance between democratic rhetoric and lived experience. Economic insecurity, declining trust in public institutions, political polarization, and the increasing influence of concentrated wealth have created conditions in which authoritarian appeals can acquire considerable force. For many Americans, political institutions appear remote, unresponsive, and incapable of addressing the difficulties of everyday life. Workers struggle to secure housing, healthcare, education, and economic stability while enormous concentrations of wealth accumulate beyond their reach. Communities experience the consequences of industrial decline, environmental destruction, and the erosion of public services. Political leaders promise renewal, yet the structures generating insecurity often remain largely intact. Authoritarian movements exploit this disillusionment. They identify enemies, promise national restoration, and present concentrated leadership as the remedy for political paralysis. They offer emotionally satisfying explanations for complicated social problems while directing public anger toward vulnerable populations, immigrants, political opponents, and supposedly disloyal citizens. The resulting politics transforms legitimate grievances into instruments of domination. The problem is not that ordinary people are incapable of democratic judgment. It is that democratic judgment becomes more difficult when public discourse is saturated with disinformation, fear, scapegoating, and the deliberate destruction of a shared factual world. A republic cannot remain healthy when citizens are encouraged to regard one another as existential enemies. Related: Elbows Up! Canada, Trump, and the Politics of Democratic Friendship The Imperial Presidency The American presidency has accumulated extraordinary power over the course of the nation's history. War, national security, emergency declarations, executive orders, and the expansion of administrative authority have progressively enlarged the reach of the executive branch. These developments did not begin with Trump. They reflect a longer transformation in American political life, one in which successive administrations have expanded presidential authority while Congress has frequently struggled to exercise meaningful oversight. Trump's approach to the presidency intensifies this historical tendency by treating executive power not simply as a constitutional responsibility but as an instrument of personal authority. The distinction is fundamental. A democratic president occupies an office whose legitimacy depends upon constitutional limitations, institutional accountability, and the recognition that political authority is temporary. An authoritarian leader increasingly treats those limitations as obstacles to be overcome and public institutions as instruments to be brought under personal control. Under this conception of leadership, loyalty to the president can become more important than fidelity to the law. Independent officials may be treated as enemies. Investigations may be represented as conspiracies. Judicial decisions may be denounced whenever they frustrate executive objectives. The public service itself may be reorganized around political obedience rather than professional independence. Such developments threaten the distinction between a constitutional republic and a system in which power is exercised according to the preferences of an individual leader. The danger is compounded when the president's political movement portrays any institutional resistance as illegitimate. If courts, legislatures, journalists, civil servants, and election administrators are discredited whenever they refuse to comply with executive demands, the very mechanisms designed to restrain arbitrary power become targets of political warfare. The constitutional separation of powers is not an inconvenience imposed upon effective government. It is one of the essential safeguards against the concentration of power. The founders understood that political authority requires limits because no individual, however persuasive or popular, can be presumed immune to ambition, self-interest, or the temptation to govern without restraint. The imperial presidency emerges when those limits are progressively weakened and the executive begins to behave as though the state belongs to its occupant. From Democratic Erosion to Competitive Authoritarianism Political scientists have used the concept of competitive authoritarianism to describe systems in which elections and democratic institutions continue to exist while incumbents manipulate the political environment to make genuine competition increasingly unequal. Such systems do not necessarily abolish elections. They may instead weaken the institutions that make elections meaningful: independent courts, a free press, impartial electoral administration, legislative oversight, and the ability of opposition parties to organize without intimidation. The danger for the United States is not that every feature of such systems has already become fully established. It is that democratic erosion can occur incrementally, through the cumulative weakening of safeguards that once appeared secure. An election is not sufficient to guarantee democracy. Electoral legitimacy depends upon the conditions under which political competition takes place. Citizens must be able to obtain reliable information, criticize those in power, organize collectively, and participate without fear that the machinery of government will be weaponized against them. When political leaders delegitimize electoral outcomes without credible evidence, undermine confidence in election administration, or portray political opponents as enemies of the nation, they weaken the shared commitments upon which peaceful transfers of power depend. The events surrounding January 6, 2021, demonstrated how profoundly dangerous the rejection of electoral legitimacy can become. The attack on the United States Capitol was not merely an eruption of public disorder. It was an assault upon the constitutional process through which electoral outcomes are formally recognized. The broader lesson is that democratic institutions cannot survive indefinitely when political leaders cultivate the belief that elections are legitimate only when their own side wins. Nor can democracy thrive when the public sphere becomes so saturated with conspiracy theories that evidence itself loses its authority. A political culture in which every inconvenient fact is dismissed as fabricated, every investigation is treated as persecution, and every institutional restraint is described as treason becomes increasingly incapable of resolving conflict through democratic means. The challenge is not simply to defend existing institutions. It is to restore the public trust and political culture that allow those institutions to function. Courts must be able to interpret the law independently. Legislatures must be able to investigate executive conduct. Journalists must be free to scrutinize those who exercise power. Election officials must be able to administer elections without intimidation. Civil servants must be able to carry out lawful responsibilities without being forced to demonstrate personal allegiance to the president. These institutions are imperfect. They can reproduce class privilege, racial inequality, bureaucratic inertia, and other forms of injustice. Their independence does not make them immune from criticism or reform. Nevertheless, institutional independence remains essential to preventing the arbitrary exercise of political authority. The effort to subordinate public institutions to executive power represents a particularly dangerous development because it can transform the instruments of government into mechanisms of political retaliation. When public officials are threatened for performing their duties, when professional expertise is dismissed as ideological disloyalty, and when investigations are treated as unacceptable merely because they inconvenience those in power, the distinction between public service and personal obedience begins to collapse. This transformation has implications extending well beyond Washington. Universities, public schools, scientific institutions, cultural organizations, and independent media all depend upon some degree of intellectual freedom and institutional autonomy. They require the ability to examine evidence, challenge established assumptions and communicate conclusions without fear of political punishment. A society that systematically intimidates its intellectual and cultural institutions impoverishes its capacity for democratic self-understanding. The purpose of education is not to produce citizens who repeat the preferred language of those who govern them. It is to cultivate the capacity to question authority, evaluate evidence, recognize injustice, and participate thoughtfully in collective life. When these capacities are weakened, political obedience can be mistaken for civic responsibility. The defense of institutional independence must therefore be accompanied by a deeper commitment to democratic education. The Politics of Enemies Authoritarian politics depends upon the construction of enemies. Rather than acknowledging the complexity of social problems, authoritarian leaders offer narratives that divide the population into morally opposed camps. One group is presented as the authentic nation; the other is portrayed as corrupt, dangerous, parasitic, or fundamentally alien. The political leader then assumes the role of defender, promising to restore the nation by defeating those supposedly responsible for its decline. This strategy converts political disagreement into a struggle over national identity. Opponents are no longer people with different interpretations of the common good. They become enemies whose very presence is said to threaten the survival of the country. The rhetoric of national restoration can be particularly effective during periods of economic insecurity and social change. It offers a simplified account of suffering, directing anger toward identifiable targets rather than examining the economic and institutional arrangements that generate insecurity. Immigrants may be blamed for problems rooted in labor-market structures, inadequate housing, or failures of public policy. Minority populations may be portrayed as threats to social cohesion. Journalists and academics may be accused of undermining the nation simply because they challenge prevailing political narratives. Meanwhile, the power of corporations, the concentration of wealth, and the structural inequalities shaping everyday life may receive far less scrutiny. This is one of the central achievements of reactionary politics: it can mobilize genuine grievances while redirecting them away from the institutions and economic relationships that deserve critical examination. Democratic politics requires a different response. It must recognize the legitimate suffering of people whose lives have been destabilized by economic change, declining public services, and the erosion of community. Yet it must refuse the proposition that the suffering of one group can be remedied through the humiliation or exclusion of another. Solidarity does not require pretending that social conflicts do not exist. It requires understanding how those conflicts are produced and identifying possibilities for collective action that do not depend upon scapegoating. A democratic society must resist the temptation to confuse the politics of vengeance with the pursuit of justice. It must also be examined in relation to the economic structures that shape the possibilities of political action. Corporate wealth, financial power, lobbying, campaign financing, media ownership, and the increasing influence of private interests over public policy all affect the conditions under which democracy operates. The formal principle of political equality becomes increasingly fragile when economic inequality translates into unequal political influence. A citizen with limited resources may possess the same legal right to vote as a billionaire, yet the two do not possess comparable opportunities to shape public debate, influence legislation, or establish the boundaries of politically acceptable discussion. This is not simply a matter of individual wealth. It concerns the institutional arrangements through which economic power becomes political power. The concentration of wealth can weaken democracy even when elections remain competitive. It can restrict the range of policies considered realistic, privilege the interests of investors over those of workers, and encourage governments to treat the demands of financial markets as more urgent than the needs of ordinary citizens. The result is a contradiction at the heart of contemporary democratic capitalism. Political systems proclaim the sovereignty of the people while economic institutions frequently organize social life according to imperatives that remain beyond meaningful democratic control. The Work of Democratic Renewal Defending American democracy requires a broad commitment to institutional accountability, civic participation, and the restoration of public trust. Independent courts, a free press, fair elections, and meaningful legislative oversight remain essential. Political leaders must be held accountable when they violate the law or attempt to undermine the constitutional responsibilities of their offices. But institutional defense alone is insufficient. Democracy also requires citizens capable of distinguishing evidence from propaganda, recognizing the historical origins of political conflict, and participating in collective deliberation without surrendering their judgment to charismatic leaders. Education consequently occupies a central position in any serious project of democratic renewal. Authoritarian movements draw strength from real contradictions, unresolved injustices, and widespread disillusionment. They flourish when democratic institutions appear incapable of addressing ordinary people's needs and when political discourse becomes dominated by resentment, fear, and the search for enemies. To resist these movements effectively, democratic forces must do more than condemn their rhetoric. They must confront the conditions that make authoritarian promises attractive. They must demonstrate that democratic government can address economic insecurity, protect individual rights, strengthen public institutions, and expand opportunities for meaningful participation. They must recover the conviction that political freedom and social justice are not opposing objectives but mutually reinforcing dimensions of a genuinely democratic society. The task also requires historical honesty. American democracy has never been a completed achievement. Its history includes slavery, dispossession, exclusion, imperial ambition, and the concentration of economic power. Yet it also includes generations of people who challenged those injustices and struggled to enlarge the meaning of freedom. The democratic tradition is therefore neither an immaculate inheritance nor an irredeemable failure. It is an unfinished historical struggle whose direction depends upon the actions of those who inherit it. We must resist the temptation to regard democracy as something guaranteed by the mere survival of familiar institutions. Constitutions cannot defend themselves. Courts cannot preserve their independence without public support. Elections cannot sustain democratic legitimacy when political leaders systematically undermine the conditions of fair competition. Citizens cannot exercise meaningful freedom if they are deprived of the knowledge, resources, and collective agency necessary to participate in public life. Democracy must be continually renewed through practice. The struggle for American democracy is consequently part of a larger struggle over the future of humanity. Across the world, societies confront the concentration of wealth, ecological devastation, political polarization, technological transformation, and the growing capacity of powerful institutions to shape human life without meaningful public accountability. These challenges demand more than nostalgia for an earlier political order. They require the courage to imagine democratic possibilities that existing institutions have yet to realize. The future of the republic will depend upon whether Americans can recover the distinction between power and legitimacy, between national greatness and national domination, between political loyalty and democratic responsibility. It will depend upon whether citizens can recognize that the freedom of others is not a threat to their own, that criticism is not treason, and that the capacity to question authority is among the most important safeguards of a free society. It will depend, finally, upon whether democracy can become more than a system for selecting leaders and recover its deeper meaning as a collective commitment to human dignity, social justice, and the unfinished possibilities of historical transformation. The republic is not simply what its founders imagined, nor is it merely what its current leaders proclaim it to be. It is what successive generations make of it through their struggles, their failures, their solidarities, and their willingness to confront the contradictions of their own time. No political leader can guarantee its future. No constitution can substitute for the active participation of citizens. No appeal to national greatness can compensate for the abandonment of democratic responsibility. The struggle continues because the promise remains unfinished. The future remains unwritten. But it will not be written for us.

The 1956 Hungarian Uprising Was a Working-Class Revolt

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Adam Fabry ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-04T17:33:00+00:00

On October 23, 1956, tens of thousands of Hungarians took to the streets of Budapest. What began as a demonstration led largely by students rapidly became a popular uprising against the country’s Stalinist government. Within days, the authority of the Hungarian Communist Party had crumbled, Soviet troops had been forced to withdraw, and workers across the country were establishing their own councils to organize production and exercise political power. The Soviet leadership called it a “counterrevolution,” while Western governments celebrated it as a “revolt against communism.” Both interpretations obscured something fundamental: the Hungarian Revolution was driven by workers and ordinary people who wanted to free themselves from a system that claimed to represent their interests. The uprising was ultimately crushed by Soviet military intervention on November 4. Thousands were killed; hundreds were executed in the repression that followed; and some two hundred thousand Hungarians fled the country. Yet the revolution remains one of the twentieth century’s most important challenges to authoritarian rule in the name of socialism. For the international Left, its significance is difficult to overstate. As the Hungarian Marxist philosopher G. M. Tamás observed, Eastern Europe was precisely where socialism had supposedly been put into practice. What, then, were we to make of workers rising en masse against a self-proclaimed socialist government? The answer begins with the contradictions of the Hungarian state itself — and with the people who dared to challenge it. The Crisis of Stalinist Hungary By the mid-1950s, Hungary was a country of roughly ten million people still recovering from the devastation of World War II. Following the defeat of Nazi Germany, it had been incorporated into the Soviet sphere of influence. Its economy was reorganized around state ownership, central planning, and rapid industrialization, while political power was concentrated in the hands of a Communist Party modeled on the ruling party in Joseph Stalin’s Soviet Union. Under the leadership of Mátyás Rákosi, the party pursued an especially brutal version of this model. Heavy industry received priority over consumer goods, agriculture was subjected to forced collectivization, and living standards were sacrificed to ambitious production targets. The secret police, known as the State Protection Authority (ÁVH), became the instrument of a pervasive system of surveillance, intimidation, and repression. The government justified these policies on the grounds that they were necessary for building socialism. But workers had little control over their workplaces, peasants faced coercive agricultural policies, and ordinary citizens had virtually no say in political decisions. Although the state owned the factories, that did not mean power was in the hands of the people who worked in those factories. From a critical political-economic perspective, this exposed a deeper contradiction. The state mobilized society for rapid industrial accumulation while subordinating workers to the demands of production. The bureaucracy determined what was produced, how resources were allocated, and how much people were expected to sacrifice. The facade was socialist, but the relationship between rulers and ruled remained profoundly authoritarian. The New Course The unexpected death of Stalin in 1953 opened a small space for change. Under pressure from Moscow, Rákosi was replaced as prime minister by Imre Nagy, a reform-minded Communist who recognized that the existing economic model was unsustainable. The new government introduced economic and political reforms, known as the “New Course,” which promised to reduce the emphasis on heavy industry, improve living standards, expand consumer production, and ease political repression. The reforms generated widespread hopes. Forced labor camps were closed, restrictions on private enterprise were relaxed, and peasants received greater freedom to cultivate their land. But the success of the reform program was far from guaranteed. Rákosi retained control of the party apparatus, and powerful Stalinist factions opposed Nagy’s attempts to change course. In 1955, with Soviet support, Nagy’s opponents accused him of “right-wing deviation” and had him removed from office. His dismissal signaled that the state would not tolerate even limited reform when it threatened the authority of the party bureaucracy. The following year, Nikita Khrushchev’s denunciation of Stalin’s crimes at the Soviet Communist Party’s Twentieth Congress further undermined the regime’s legitimacy. If Moscow now acknowledged the terror and injustice of Stalin’s rule, what did that mean for the Hungarian leaders who had faithfully reproduced his methods? Students, intellectuals, writers, and increasingly workers began demanding answers. By October 1956, Hungary was like a tinderbox waiting to explode. A Revolution Takes Shape The immediate spark came on October 23. Students gathered in Budapest to demand political reform, the return of Imre Nagy to government, the withdrawal of Soviet troops, and greater national independence. Their demands also encompassed changes to workplace conditions, including opposition to the unpopular piece-rate system that tied workers’ wages to production quotas. The demonstration grew dramatically as people joined the march through the capital. By evening, a vast crowd had assembled outside parliament. Elsewhere, demonstrators pulled down a monumental statue of Stalin, an unmistakable declaration that the old order had lost its authority. The plinth upon which a large statue of Stalin had stood before October 23, 1956. Only the Stalin figure’s boots remain. (Pesti srác2 / CC BY-SA 3.0 ) The turning point came when demonstrators gathered outside the Hungarian Radio headquarters, hoping to broadcast their demands. ÁVH security forces opened fire, killing and wounding people. The violent response by the government transformed a mass demonstration into an armed uprising. Weapons were seized, police vehicles were set ablaze, and insurgents began confronting the state’s armed forces. The head of a Stalin statue sits in the middle of a Budapest street, on October 23, 1956. (Hofbauer Róbert / CC BY-SA 3.0 ) The following day, Soviet troops entered Budapest, but their intervention failed to restore order. Some Hungarian soldiers refused to fight the insurgents; others defected or handed over weapons. Nagy returned to the premiership as the authorities struggled to regain control. What had begun as a protest against political repression was becoming something much more radical. The uprising spread beyond the capital to industrial towns, provincial centers, and rural communities. Factories stopped working, local revolutionary committees appeared, and workers began organizing independently of the party and the state. Workers’ Councils This is where the conventional Cold War story becomes inadequate. The revolution cannot be understood simply as a national revolt against Soviet occupation, nor as an uprising orchestrated by anti-communist politicians. Its most consequential development was the emergence of independent working-class organization. Across Hungary, workers established revolutionary councils, elected by their colleagues and answerable to them. These bodies coordinated strikes, organized supplies, represented workers in negotiations, and increasingly asserted authority over factories and local institutions. They were not merely trade unions demanding higher wages. In many cases, they challenged the state’s right to govern workplaces and society without popular consent. Historical estimates suggest that more than two thousand workers’ councils were established during the revolution, involving more than 28,000 members. Their demands varied, but recurring themes included the withdrawal of Soviet troops, political freedoms, the release of prisoners, and genuine worker participation in economic decision-making. For a brief moment, the factory floor became a site of political power. Workers who had supposedly constituted the ruling class discovered that they could organize production and articulate political demands without the supervision of party officials. The revolution was not ideologically uniform. It brought together students, industrial workers, peasants, intellectuals, reform Communists, and supporters of different political traditions. Nor did all its participants share the same vision of Hungary’s future. But the workers’ councils gave the uprising a distinctive social character that neither the Soviet leadership nor its Western adversaries could comfortably accommodate. Beyond Budapest The working-class dimension of the revolution was particularly evident in Budapest’s industrial districts and in provincial manufacturing centers. Workers in Csepel, Újpest, and Kőbánya joined the resistance, while industrial towns such as Miskolc, Ózd, and Dunapentele (which had been renamed Sztálinváros under the Stalinist regime) became important sites of revolutionary activity. These developments matter because the revolution is often remembered primarily through images of street fighting in Budapest. Yet the uprising spread through the networks of industrial production, workplaces, and communities that underpinned the Hungarian economy. In Dunapentele, workers insisted that their town was socialist precisely because its inhabitants were workers and power belonged in their hands. They declared their intention to defend it against both fascist excesses and Soviet troops. Their statement captured the central contradiction of 1956: they were not necessarily rejecting the ideal of socialism but rejecting a political order that invoked socialism to justify its own domination. Peter Fryer was a correspondent for the Daily Worker , the official newspaper of the Communist Party of Great Britain. He witnessed the uprising and described the devastation inflicted on Budapest by Soviet forces. Working-class districts suffered particularly heavily. Fryer’s reports challenged the official Communist account of a fascist conspiracy and helped expose the reality of the intervention to readers abroad. Workers’ participation was not limited to the construction of councils or the organization of strikes. They also formed a substantial part of the armed resistance. Fighting was particularly intense in industrial neighborhoods, where insurgents used their knowledge of local streets and buildings to resist a much-better-equipped military force. Young workers were especially prominent. Many had grown up under the Communist regime, experienced its workplace discipline and political restrictions, and reached adulthood with little memory of the prewar order. The motivation behind their revolt cannot be reduced to nostalgia for the old Hungary. They were challenging the society in which they had actually lived. The distinction is crucial. The revolution did contain nationalist and anti-communist currents, and some participants sought a restoration of political arrangements from before the war. But these currents do not explain the independent organization of workers across the country, nor do they justify the Soviet claim that military intervention was necessary to defeat a fascist counterrevolution. The revolution’s social diversity should not obscure its central achievement: ordinary people began to exercise political power for themselves. The Soviet Counterattack By late October, the uprising had forced the government into a series of concessions. Nagy announced a coalition government and, under mounting pressure, moved toward political pluralism. On October 28, the government declared a ceasefire and acknowledged the revolutionary movement. The prospect of Soviet withdrawal appeared increasingly real. But the Soviet leadership faced a dilemma. Allowing Hungary to break decisively from Moscow’s control risked destabilizing the entire Soviet bloc. The danger was not merely that another government might leave the Warsaw Pact. It was that a successful revolution organized from below might inspire workers elsewhere to challenge their own rulers. The crisis also unfolded against a wider international backdrop. Western powers were unwilling to risk a direct military confrontation with the Soviet Union, while the Suez crisis was exposing the limits of Western claims to defend freedom and national sovereignty. Hungary’s fate would ultimately be decided by the balance of power between states, not by the rhetoric of either superpower. On November 1, Nagy announced Hungary’s withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact and appealed for international recognition of the country’s neutrality. Meanwhile, Moscow had already begun preparing a second military intervention. In the early hours of November 4, Soviet forces launched Operation Whirlwind, attacking Budapest and other centers of resistance. This time the assault was overwhelming. Some 58,000 Soviet troops advanced against a revolution that lacked the military resources to withstand a prolonged offensive. Hungarian army units were rapidly disarmed or neutralized, while workers and insurgents continued fighting in several areas. Nagy broadcasted a desperate appeal for help announcing that Soviet forces had attacked the capital and were attempting to overthrow the government. He subsequently sought refuge in the Yugoslav embassy, but was later arrested. In 1958, following a secret trial, he was executed alongside other leaders of the revolution, including Pál Maléter. By November 11, the main centers of armed resistance had been crushed. More than 2,500 people had died during the revolution. The workers’ councils, however, did not immediately disappear. Their continued existence forced the new authorities to confront a problem that military victory alone could not resolve: how to govern a population that had demonstrated such open hostility to the regime. Defeat and the Consolidation of Kádár’s Rule While Soviet tanks were reentering Hungary, a new Soviet-friendly government, led by János Kádár, was formed. However, the new government initially faced widespread opposition. Factories remained closed, transport services were disrupted, and workers organized strikes against the Soviet intervention. The Central Workers’ Council of Greater Budapest, established in November, became a focal point for efforts to coordinate resistance and negotiate with the authorities. The government responded with a mixture of concessions and repression. Kádár’s representatives negotiated with workers’ leaders, seeking to persuade them to resume production. But when negotiations failed to break the resistance, the authorities turned decisively toward coercion. On December 9, the headquarters of the Central Workers’ Council was raided and prominent activists were arrested. Martial law was imposed, strikes were criminalized, and imprisonment and execution became instruments of political control. By early 1957, organized resistance was increasingly difficult to sustain. Many young workers fled Hungary, while those who remained faced the prospect of prison, dismissal, or forced submission. The repression was followed by a longer process of political consolidation. Kádár rebuilt the Communist Party (now renamed the Hungarian Socialist Workers’ Party, MSZMP) and sought to win the acquiescence of a population exhausted by conflict. Over time, his government developed a more pragmatic approach to economic management and everyday life. Compared with the brutalities of the Rákosi period, Kádár’s Hungary eventually became associated with relative material security and a degree of private autonomy. But these accommodations rested on the defeat of independent political organization. Workers could enjoy certain improvements in living standards, provided they did not seek to reclaim the political power they had briefly exercised in 1956. The revolution therefore left a complicated legacy. Its defeat did not mean that Hungarian society returned unchanged to the prerevolutionary order. The authorities had learned that permanent mobilization, relentless austerity, and indiscriminate terror could undermine the stability they sought to secure. The memory of 1956 placed limits on the state’s room for maneuver, even as it tried to erase the revolution’s independent working-class character. The Legacy of 1956 Today The struggle over the meaning of 1956 did not end with the defeat of the revolution. In contemporary Hungary, its memory remains contested. Following the democratic transition in 1989–90, political commemorations have often emphasized themes of national independence and resistance to Soviet domination that primarily resonate with the emphasis of right-wing political parties on national sovereignty. Under Viktor Orbán’s sixteen-year rule, the state invested heavily in creating an institutionally revisionist memory culture in Hungary, presenting the revolution as a reactionary struggle for “national sovereignty.” However, this picture is utterly detached from the reality of what was happening on the ground in 1956. While reactionary forces sought to co-opt the revolutionary movement at the time, as often happens in mass politics, the struggle did not lose its principled socialist character. The interpretation that stresses the issue of national sovereignty obscures the revolution’s equally important challenge to domestic authoritarianism and the power of political elites. The workers’ councils should remind us that we cannot separate opposition to foreign domination from the question of who exercises power within society. The revolution was not simply a nation asserting its independence; it was also a population challenging a ruling class that claimed to represent it. The lesson extends well beyond Hungary. At a time when authoritarian movements are gaining ground across Europe and elsewhere, 1956 challenges the assumption that political alternatives must be confined to a choice between authoritarian state control and the existing capitalist order. It also offers a warning to the Left: governments cannot claim to represent working people while denying them the freedom to organize independently, challenge political authority, and determine the conditions under which they live and work. The legacy of 1956 is therefore neither simply nationalist nor merely anti-communist. It is a reminder that emancipation cannot be imposed from above, whether in the name of socialism, national security, or economic progress. The Hungarian workers who challenged their rulers demonstrated that democracy means more than choosing between competing elites. It also means the collective capacity of ordinary people to shape their own lives. This crucial class aspect is often excluded from today’s discourse: many commentators attempt to draw parallels with the war in Ukraine, which often reduces this pivotal socialist revolution to merely a struggle for national sovereignty. What 1956 Means for the Left The Hungarian Revolution was neither a vindication of liberal capitalism nor the reactionary conspiracy portrayed by Soviet propaganda. It was a heterogeneous popular uprising whose most radical expression lay in the independent organization of workers. That distinction remains politically important. If socialism means only state ownership, central planning, and rule by a party claiming exclusive authority to represent the working class, then the Hungarian workers’ revolt poses an uncomfortable question: What happens when the people supposedly represented by the state rise up against it? We cannot find the answer by dismissing the revolution as an aberration or by ignoring the political diversity of its participants. It requires taking seriously the experience of workers who challenged bureaucratic control, organized their workplaces, and demanded the right to determine their own future. The councils did not offer a complete blueprint for a socialist society. They operated under extraordinary circumstances, and their political projects were neither uniform nor free of contradictions. But they demonstrated that opposition to Stalinism could emerge from a desire for greater democracy and collective control, rather than simply a wish to restore capitalism. For the Left, the lesson is not that the revolution should be claimed uncritically by any particular tradition. It is that socialism cannot be reduced to the power of the state, and workers cannot be treated as passive beneficiaries of policies devised in their name. Emancipation requires their active participation in shaping economic and political life. In 1956, Hungarian workers confronted a regime that claimed to have abolished exploitation while denying them meaningful control over their labor and society. Their defeat exposed the violence required to preserve that contradiction. It also revealed the possibility of another kind of politics — one rooted in collective self-organization, political freedom, and the struggle against domination. The Soviet tanks crushed the revolution. They could not entirely extinguish its challenge to the meaning of socialism. For anyone on the Left still committed to a society governed democratically by those who create its wealth, that challenge remains unfinished. Our goal in putting out this explainer is not mainly to convince you to vote one way or another. In fact, this explainer is not targeted only at voters. We want all working-class people to understand what a circus so much of this is and also where our real power lies. You’ll notice not all nine of this year’s ballot questions are here. Again, we don’t aim to offer a comprehensive set of voting instructions to guide you through voting for each question. This is not a voter guide. Instead, we hope this explainer will illuminate causes, solutions, and forces at play, and we see the ballot questions that we’ve selected as being well-suited to that purpose. That doesn’t mean there aren’t questions where we have a clear stance — for example, on Question 2 to extend union rights to public defenders, we support worker organizing, regardless of any law. Our support, however, does not extend to police, an arm of the state that represses workers, and we challenge the legitimacy of the capitalist criminal-legal system. Our socialist perspective, on these questions and so much more, is distinct from that of liberals, and we want to expose misdirection and deception by capitalist parties and the ways that laws and the state serve particular economic interests. With this explainer, we seek to go beyond the narrow framing of these questions and raise far bigger ones about our capitalist society. Join us! How we think about elections We’re socialists, and our aim is socialism, which means, in the plainest terms, a seizure of political power by the working class, a democratic socialization of ownership of our economy, and an economic system in which our labor power is no longer a commodity. As said in our Principles for Socialist Electoral Work , we see U.S. elections, and those in any capitalist democracy, as largely a sham. They mislead people into thinking our hand is on the wheel when really they decide which faction of the ruling class will take it for the next few years. Even without the Electoral College, Senate, gerrymandering, and judicial review, the system is thoroughly undemocratic. The equality of participants is superficial and purely legal because the intense concentration of wealth and economic power inherent to capitalism inevitably translates into a tremendous concentration and inequality of political power, incompatible with democracy. And we refer not only to campaign spending, lobbying, and the media, but to private ownership of the means of production by the capitalist class and their devastating power — no matter who is elected — to withdraw investment as they please, throttling the economy and all of us with it. So, we’re clear-eyed about the nature of elections, and yet, still we participate. Socialists must champion struggles for reforms, not because reforms offer a way out of capitalism — they don’t — but to raise class consciousness and build a socialist party, which do. Electoral work offers opportunities not for fleeting policy or election wins but subordinate to higher, political aims: reaching and organizing the masses, dispelling illusions, and broadcasting our socialist politics. What are these questions on my ballot? Ballot questions in Massachusetts can seek to pass or repeal a law and are also required for constitutional amendments. Interest groups use them when they want to change the state constitution or cannot get the state legislature to fund, act on, or stay away from their priorities. For example, Question 6 seeks to reserve some existing sales tax revenue for conservation and is driven by conservation groups that want to force the legislature to redirect state funding to their priority. Question 8 was initiated by a Virginia-based anti-marijuana group now spending millions to try to end Massachusetts’s multibillion-dollar recreational marijuana industry. Similarly, Question 9 is driven by gun owners wanting to roll back state regulations on gun ownership to what they were before October 2024, when the governor signed a new gun law. Because state laws typically require tens of thousands of signatures to put questions on the statewide ballot and because these campaigns can sometimes necessitate millions in spending to win, statewide ballot question campaigns typically need to be well-funded. Naturally, that means many ballot questions end up being initiated by capital against the interests of labor. It should be noted too that the state legislature can stop constitutional amendments from even making it to the ballot and has the power to change or undo laws passed by ballot question. Likewise, the courts can throw out questions as well. You might know, for example, that the state legislature has battled against implementing a ballot question passed in 2024 authorizing the state auditor to audit the legislature, and that the state Supreme Court stopped rent control from appearing on your ballot this year. They have constitutional objections — our critiques are on the actual merits — but our stance is not one of demanding binding legislation by ballot question. That’s because the root cause of this crisis of democracy is the structure of the legislature itself and the classes that it represents. In a socialist world, with the working class exercising truly democratic control in their workplaces and over all of society, we could have a proportionally elected body recallable by workers, and ballot questions to circumvent a legislature wouldn’t be necessary at all. In fact, the supremacy of a legislature could be an asset to and an instrument of a working class that wielded it. But in the meantime, we know that power is not expressed by passing ballot questions. No, the power of our class comes from its indispensable role in production. “Without our brain and muscle” — without our labor — “not a single wheel can turn.” There are many things the state legislature will never willingly allow, but organized mass action can compel it. If the choice is between radical changes and the economy grinding to a halt, radical changes will indeed come. A major task for the socialist movement is to build up to that moment and then to go further still and dispense with the capitalist state and its state legislatures altogether, instituting a workers republic and putting the working class in charge of its destiny. In Central Mass & Worcester DSA, all of our efforts in the electoral arena are toward this end and for that day. We Need a Socialist Congressional Caucus

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Nick French ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-04T16:35:16+00:00

With last night’s election results , it’s official: after new members are sworn in this coming January, socialists will hold more seats in Congress than they ever have in US history. Six freshmen congress members backed by the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) — Claire Valdez and Darializa Avila Chevalier of New York, Chris Rabb of Philadelphia, Melat Kiros of Colorado, Donavan McKinney of Michigan, and Angela Gonzales-Torres of Los Angeles — are joining fellow DSA members Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and Rashida Tlaib in the House of Representatives. Counting also Sen. Bernie Sanders, long a self-described democratic socialist though not officially affiliated with DSA, the United States will have nine socialist members of Congress. The new socialist legislators, all of whom ran on the Democratic Party ballot line, can be expected (like Tlaib, AOC, and Sanders) to caucus with the Democrats. Despite achieving a meaningful beachhead in the legislature and being joined by left-populist allies like Michigan’s Abdul El-Sayed and Maine’s Troy Jackson, socialists will still be a tiny minority within the Democratic caucus. The growing left wing will have to figure out how to coordinate effectively with each other while operating within the “big tent” of the larger congressional party. That party is full of members committed to pro-corporate and militarist agendas, and avowed anti-socialists Hakeem Jeffries and Chuck Schumer are likely to be its leaders. The socialist legislators, on the other hand, have their own policy priorities and were elected in large part because of their criticisms of the party establishment: for failing to address the cost-of-living crisis, for backing Israel’s genocide in Gaza, and for mounting an ineffective opposition to Donald Trump’s attacks on workers and civil rights. To navigate the terrain of Congress effectively, including the inevitable conflicts with corporate-friendly Democrats and party leaders, socialists need their own congressional caucus. The Need for a Socialist Caucus Members of Congress on both sides of the aisle have long recognized the utility of cooperating with ideologically like-minded legislators in smaller caucuses within the larger legislative parties. The Democrats’ three main ideological caucuses are the Blue Dogs, representing the right wing of the party; the New Democrat Coalition (NDC), representing a basically socially liberal but economically moderate faction; and the Congressional Progressive Caucus (CPC), representing the party’s left-leaning members. These caucuses are venues for legislators to formulate ideas and coordinate strategy. They can serve to advance particular priorities that don’t have the support of the broader party, or at least not yet. They also signal members’ broad alignment with distinct ideological currents within the broad Democratic tent. Sanders, Tlaib, and AOC are already members of the CPC, and Sanders himself helped found the caucus (along with late DSA member Ron Dellums and others) in 1991. So it may seem like the new socialist electeds are better off simply joining the CPC and attempting to advance their priorities that way. Yet that caucus long ago ceased to be an ideologically cohesive organization, let alone one that can be expected to fight for socialist legislative goals. As Jacobin contributor Neal Meyer has written , many CPC members have refused to back left-wing priorities like Medicare for All or reducing our country’s obscene military budget. And today, many CPC members are also members of the centrist, pro-business NDC. In fact, the incumbents ousted by DSA challengers this cycle — Adriano Espaillat, Diana DeGette, Shri Thanedar, and Jimmy Gomez — were members of the CPC. What a Caucus Can Achieve So there’s a real role to be played by a new caucus that can cohere left-wing elected officials and continue efforts to build a socialist pole within the Democratic Party, expanding on the work that politicians like Sanders, AOC, and New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani have done already. In the short term, a socialist caucus may be able to make strategic interventions to advance its policy aims (and block bad policy). Here they might learn a thing or two from the House Freedom Caucus (HFC), the hard-right GOP caucus founded in 2015 that was instrumental in ousting House Speaker Kevin McCarthy and replacing him with Mike Johnson. The Freedom Caucus has often played “ legislative hardball ” against its own party to try to push policy further right. Looking at the HFC experience, scholar of the GOP Paul Heideman writes , “The conditions in which socialist legislative hardball is most likely to be successful are those in which the Democratic Party has a majority in Congress and an opposite-party president.” That is the situation in which socialists will soon find themselves. In similar circumstances, the HFC was able to “use its size and discipline to compel the House leadership to enter into fights with the administration that it otherwise would have avoided” and “succeeded in winning more power for itself in the form of committee appointments and so on.” The very large congressional majority that Democrats just won , however, means that socialists will not be able to constitute a winning vote margin, which puts limits on their leverage. Heideman has also observed that the hardball strategy has often been counterproductive. Sometimes, when Freedom Caucus members refused to vote for legislation because it was insufficiently right-wing, Republican leadership was forced to seek Democratic votes to pass the legislation instead. As a result, the legislation ended up more liberal than it would have been otherwise. The HFC has in recent years seen its numbers and influence dwindle. The HFC experience reveals the limits of the power of a small minority of legislators. Especially if and when socialist lawmakers find themselves working under a Democratic president, they will need to be creative and strategic about how they approach fights with their own party. The efforts of the Squad — the informal grouping of young legislators including AOC, Tlaib, and others who are generally regarded as the leftmost members of the House — under the Biden administration are also instructive. In 2021, the Democrats were attempting to pass both a major infrastructure spending bill, which had broad congressional support, and Build Back Better (BBB), an expansive social welfare bill that was opposed by centrist Democratic senators Kyrsten Sinema and Joe Manchin. The CPC, including the Squad, argued that Democratic leadership should hold a vote on the two bills together, to use the infrastructure bill as leverage to convince the centrists to vote for BBB. Ultimately, party leaders decided to split the votes — holding a vote on the infrastructure bill first, then BBB — and convinced most of the CPC, but not the Squad , to go along. The result was predictable: the infrastructure law passed, and BBB did not. Only the socialists and a few progressive allies held the line until the end, insisting correctly that splitting up the votes would likely doom the welfare bill. That’s exactly the kind of principled tactical intervention a socialist caucus can help make. Of course, in many cases, as with the Squad’s stand for BBB, the socialist minority will lose out to moderates, especially because they can’t rely even on the support of most of the CPC. But as socialists continue to grow their numbers in Congress, they will have more weight to throw around. In any case, the congressional left can’t avoid conflicts with the rest of the Democratic Party, as evidenced by party moderates’ continued denunciations of socialism and the concerted efforts of groups like the American Israel Public Affairs Committee and Third Way to discredit DSA and take down left-wing Democratic candidates. Building a Socialist Pole That fact points to one of the longer-term reasons socialists would do well to form a caucus. Given the ideological divisions among the Democrats and the unavoidable fights to come with politicians to their right, socialist legislators need their own congressional organization, one that can articulate and keep its members cohered around a distinctive political vision. Even when the socialist left can’t prevail in intraparty conflicts, being able to communicate what socialists stand for to the broader public is indispensable. A caucus will allow the Left to point to the legislators who have a demonstrated commitment to their priorities, making the case for electing more socialists and building up the broader institutional forces that can support them, like the labor movement and DSA. It will allow the socialist left to distinguish itself from the unreliable “progressives” of the CPC and other caucuses, helping it to consolidate support among the electorate. The value of a caucus will be all the greater if, as seems likely, socialists keep winning elections, and the conflict heightens. Over the last decade, the socialist left has hotly debated how to relate to the Democratic Party: whether to try to win control of it, or form a third party immediately, or tactically exploit the Democratic ballot line with the eventual goal of forming a party of our own. For now, it seems undeniable that, in most places, running on the Democratic line is the surest way to build up our forces in Congress as well as state and local offices. Yet it is clear that many in the party see socialists as an existential threat, and that in the long run this house divided against itself will not stand. There will be a struggle between those who are fighting for greater redistribution of wealth and the democratization of political and economic power, on the one hand, and those who bitterly oppose that on the other. That struggle might result in the triumph of one group or the other, staying within the confines of the Democratic Party or producing a splintering into two or more parties. Whatever happens, socialists in Congress can only benefit from starting to organize themselves for that battle now. Perhaps a more competent would-be despot than Donald Trump could have pulled it off. He could have consolidated his power gradually, choosing his battles against immigrants and the Left and imagined foreign adversaries wisely and making sure he didn’t alienate his existing base of support by keeping his finger on the pulse of American sentiment and staying in tune with the struggles of average people, like the high cost of living and the exhaustion with illegal foreign wars. We’ll never know. The reaction of the bulk of the American people to the cruelty and authoritarianism they’ve seen in the last two years has been abject disgust. And they have reacted positively to the pro-worker rhetoric of Bernie Sanders and the rising Left he has inspired. Candidate after candidate seemed to have finally gotten the memo to steer clear of culture war and rhetorically emphasize affordability. What it all added up to was a “blue wave” so powerful that Republicans lost in states like Texas and South Carolina and Kansas. For those who despaired at the electorate’s handing the presidency to Trump for a second time in 2024, last night was deeply heartening: the American people decisively rejected what Trump has to offer them. First Time Farce, Second Time Tragedy Donald Trump’s first turn at the presidency was more bark than bite. He did a tremendous amount of harm, but mostly the kind of harm that a hypothetical President John McCain or President Mitt Romney would have perpetrated: he lowered taxes for rich people, slashed benefits for poor people, undermined health and safety regulations, and busted unions. There were moments where he went beyond this consensus GOP agenda, usually in the direction of performative cruelty toward immigrants, but he tended to back down in the face of judicial pushback or public outrage. Often he and the constantly rotating cast of second-rate con men he surrounded himself with seemed unsure how to wield the levers of power. There were portents of what an unleashed form of Trumpist authoritarianism might look like, particularly in his desperate attempts to overturn the results of the 2020 election. Ultimately, though, this too was a flop, and a reasonable argument could be made that Trump had simply tried to do a coarser, more “populist” version of what smarter Republicans had successfully pulled off when George W. Bush stole the 2000 election from Al Gore. The second round of Trumpism has been so much worse. Reversing Karl Marx’s formula about what happens when history repeats itself, it has been “first time farce, second time tragedy.” Trump returned to power surrounded by true believers dedicated to carrying out the president’s will, and the result has been a sustained two-year assault on liberal democracy. On his first day in office, Trump signed an executive order attempting to overturn the Fourteenth Amendment’s guarantee of birthright citizenship. He bullied universities across the country into harsher crackdowns on students who protested the genocide in Gaza. He had legal permanent residents arrested and tried to have them deported for attending protests or writing op-eds. He had migrants, some of whom had entered legally and broken no laws, imprisoned in a Salvadoran gulag because of guesswork about what their tattoos might mean. And he sent thousands of Immigration and Customs Enforcement and Border Patrol agents to occupy the city of Minneapolis as a swaggering show of force to intimidate his political opposition. When those agents murdered American citizens Alex Pretti and Renée Good, in broad daylight and on camera, the administration smeared the victims as “domestic terrorists” while their bodies were still warm. Socialists, Liberal Democracy, and “Resistance Liberalism” One important thing that “resistance liberal” types got right during and after the first Trump administration was that the president’s ambitions were more rabidly authoritarian than presidents of either party in America’s recent past. Part of what left-wing and socialist critics of “resistance liberalism” got right is that Trumpism was a natural outgrowth of that recent past, and attempts to “just get back to normal” in coalition with Never Trump Republicans very badly misread the moment. Those of us who want to fight for a better, more materially egalitarian, and more authentically democratic society understand perfectly well that liberal democracy is an indispensable starting point from which we can wage that fight. It’s what gives us the room to agitate, organize, and maneuver. So democratic socialists have stood consistently against Trumpism, as we have against previous forms of authoritarianism. Even in the early months of the administration, when many establishment Democrats didn’t seem to know what had hit them, Bernie Sanders and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez were crisscrossing the country building anti-Trump enthusiasm with their Fighting Oligarchy tour, which married a defense of democracy against authoritarianism with a critique of economic inequality. This time around, many grassroots Democrats were ready to listen to that message. Enough were willing to support out-and-out socialists that a Democratic Socialists of America member is now the ( wildly popular ) mayor of the most important city in the capitalist world. And last night’s blue tidal wave more than doubled the number of socialists in Congress. Some degree of “Berniecrat”-style messaging crept into the rhetoric of Democratic candidates across the country. And Democrats in hotly contested states like Maine and Michigan openly championed substantive social democratic policies like Medicare for All. All of this represents a belated recognition that Trumpism wasn’t just a random eruption of bigotry from some toxic depth of our collective national psyche. It was a demagogic response to the material realities of a profoundly unequal and increasingly precarious society. And it’s best fought by uniting a defense of liberal democracy with a pro-worker economic message. Of course, campaign rhetoric and governance are two very different things, and the country is far from out of the woods. A profoundly dangerous authoritarian is still president — and will be for the next two years. And establishment Democrats are adept at finding creative new ways to turn even the most inspiring victories into fresh defeats. For now, though, the fact remains that the American people just handed MAGA a historically massive rebuke. That’s well worth celebrating. Fight Against Gangster Capitalism

With the Democratic Party’s landslide victory, a day of reckoning appears to have arrived for Trump and the far-right, white nationalist forces that have turned cruelty into a governing principle and hatred into a political language. Something has broken in the machinery of fear. The old fascist ideas, the politics of racial hatred, and the relentless assault on human dignity have encountered a moment of resistance. Too much suffering, too much misery, too much authoritarian violence. Too much blood in the streets, too many deaths, screams, and a deafening silence. Enough is enough, at least for the moment. The Trump regime has exposed some of the darkest impulses of political life, resurrecting the ghosts of a past that should have taught us where the glorification of state violence, racial cleansing, the crushing of dissent, and organized cruelty inevitably leads. Its legacy is written in the expansion of the carceral state, the staggering investment in prisons and punishment, the unleashing of masked agents of repression across the country, and the unapologetic embrace of a language of racial cleansing that strips entire populations of their humanity and destroys lives and civil liberties. Related: France’s Student Uprising: A Generation Revolts Against Capitalism Yet the celebrations now dominating liberal and mainstream media risk turning this electoral victory into an exercise in historical amnesia. Trump’s defeat is being hailed as a crushing repudiation of the far right, as though the political conditions that made his rise possible have suddenly disappeared. Missing from this triumphalist narrative is any serious reckoning with the Democratic Party’s own complicity in creating those conditions. From Clinton to Obama, Democratic administrations embraced neoliberal policies that deepened economic inequality, strengthened corporate power, weakened working-class institutions, and helped create the social and political landscape in which authoritarianism could flourish. Nor can the party's leadership escape responsibility for its support of Israel's genocidal war in Gaza, its exporting of horrendous military weapons, and its broader allegiance to a colonizing authoritarian order, sustained through violence and dispossession. A party so deeply implicated in the machinery of neoliberal capitalism cannot be entrusted with dismantling the very structures of power it has helped legitimate and preserve. For a fleeting moment, democracy may be able to breathe again. But breathing is not liberation, and an electoral victory is no substitute for dismantling the gangster capitalist order that breeds authoritarianism, feeds on human suffering, commodifies every aspect of life, and makes fascism an enduring possibility. The forces that produced Trump did not emerge overnight, nor will they disappear with an election. They are embedded in institutions that concentrate wealth, manufacture fear, normalize inequality, and transform human misery into a source of profit and political power. Trump may be defeated at the ballot box, but Trumpism cannot be defeated without confronting the economic, political, and cultural foundations of a system that turns greed into virtue, cruelty into policy, racism into a badge of honor, and human disposability into a governing principle. Related: When Words Become Weapons of Racial Terror Unless those foundations are dismantled, the nightmare will return, perhaps wearing a different mask. The serpent's egg, to invoke the haunting metaphor associated with Ingmar Bergman, has not been destroyed. It lies beneath the surface of seemingly democratic societies, nourished by the dark and volatile forces of a capitalist order that breeds inequality, authoritarianism, and organized cruelty. Brecht understood that fascism could not be confronted without exposing the capitalist relations of power that give rise to it. The serpent's egg remains embedded in the political, economic, and cultural machinery of gangster capitalism, waiting for the conditions under which it can hatch again. Fascism may suffer an electoral defeat, but the serpent survives, sustained by the very system that gave it life. There is another historical lesson that cannot be buried beneath the celebrations of an electoral victory: the need to recover the spirit of the Nuremberg trials. Nuremberg established a principle that must never be forgotten those who organize, authorize, and carry out state crimes cannot hide behind the authority of government or the excuse of following orders. The Trump regime has left behind a record of actions demanding criminal investigation, from the killing of people aboard boats in international waters, including attacks that legal experts have warned may constitute war crimes, to the abduction, imprisonment, and deportation of people in violation of fundamental rights and due process. The Democratic Party has a responsibility to ensure that these abuses are investigated and that those responsible, from Pete Hegseth to Stephen Miller and others who directed or enabled such policies, are held accountable wherever the evidence establishes criminal responsibility. There can be no reconciliation purchased at the expense of justice, no national healing built on organized forgetting, and no democratic renewal that grants impunity to those who turned the machinery of the state into an instrument of terror. The lesson of Nuremberg is not that history repeats itself in identical forms, but that crimes committed under the cover of state power must be exposed, judged, and punished through the rule of law. Without accountability, the promise of an electoral victory in the spirit of reviving democratic values and policies becomes another empty slogan, and the forces of authoritarian violence are given permission to return. The possibility of genuine transformation will not emerge from the exhausted leadership of a Democratic Party unwilling to break with the capitalist order it serves. It will emerge from the streets, from organized workers, from young people refusing to be written out of history, and from collective struggles that reject the politics of fear, austerity, racial hatred, and disposability. Elections may interrupt the march toward authoritarianism—a crucial pedagogical function-- but they cannot abolish the conditions-the myriad economic, political, and cultural forces-that make fascism possible. The task now is to turn this moment of resistance into a sustained struggle against the structures of power that have made democracy increasingly subordinate to wealth, militarism, and corporate domination. The future cannot be entrusted to political elites in suites, who promise to rescue democracy while preserving the economic order that is destroying it. The struggle is not simply to defeat one authoritarian leader, but to dismantle a system that makes authoritarianism possible and to build a world in which justice, equality, collective freedom, and human dignity become the foundations of democratic life.

If there’s one person who saw yesterday’s repudiation of the right coming long before everyone else did, it’s Zohran Mamdani. In one way or another, he and the movement that created him are the architects of the Democrats’ victory. Two years ago, the day after the 2024 election, the mood was grim; the future was bleak. Nobody saw the possibility for regrouping. What did Mamdani do? Just one week after Donald Trump’s victory, he went out and interviewed Trump voters all across the city. He spoke to white, black, brown, Muslim, Asian, but above all working-class men and women and, above all, in the Bronx and Queens. Everyone now thinks of Mamdani’s victory a year later, first in the primary and then in the general election, as a foregone conclusion, a New York thing that had no meaning beyond New York. It wasn’t. The reason it wasn’t is that Mamdani understood something no one else understood: Trump voters had their reasons for going for Trump, and those reasons could be explained and understood. Not the hard-bitten right-winger, mind you, but the people who put Trump over the top. That reason was economic. Mamdani saw that, and he gave it a name: affordability. And lo and behold, what did one Texas voter, who’s voted Republican all his life, say last night about how and why he voted for the Democrats for the first time? No one can afford anything anymore. Our water and air is the dirtiest and our education is horrible. Mamdani spoke to that sentiment with his use of the term “affordability.” It’s not just a strictly economic word; as this Texas voter suggests, it’s almost a spiritual term, a sense of the enshittification of life, the grinding worsening of material and therefore cultural conditions. Water is dirty; education is horrible; it’s all going to shit. That’s what Mamdani spoke to, and he said, “We can do something about that.” So many politicians now use that word “affordability” that we forget it was Mamdani who, less than two years ago, made that the word. And what every other Democratic politician across the United States, whether they’re Democratic Socialists of America or not, saw is that those Trump voters, those swing Trump voters, are gettable — if you can speak about the economic and cultural catastrophe that has been visited upon ordinary citizens. Whether we’re talking about the Latino men who shifted right toward Trump along the border in Texas or the farmers in the Midwest who’ve voted Republican their whole lives till yesterday, or the Solid South. Look at the voters who shifted from Republican to Democrat yesterday and the demographic trends. Remember those young people who voted Trump and were declared lost for a generation to the tradwives and the manosphere? They saw the largest shift left of any demographic group. Voters eighteen to twenty-nine years old saw a 32-point shift to the left. White noncollege voters, which is how this addled nation codes “white working class,” saw a 14-point shift to the left. Latino voters saw a 28-point shift to the left. It took a Muslim socialist, born in Uganda, raised in New York City, to give the American heartland a new kind of language to wage political battle against the Right. Just as it was Jewish socialists a century ago, many of them immigrants and raised in New York City, who gave America during the 1930s and 1940s, in politics and culture, in the wake of one of the first anti-immigrant backlashes the country had ever seen, a new language for itself. The Donald Trump Era in American Politics Is Ending

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Paul Heideman ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-04T14:35:42+00:00

The American people delivered an absolute rout against Donald Trump and the GOP last night. Here are five takeaways from the midterm results: 1. Trump Owns This Defeat Everything about this defeat points to a different way Donald Trump has weakened the Republican Party. In Texas and South Carolina, he pushed his preferred candidates, Ken Paxton and Darline Graham, for the Senate above the first choices of the state parties. Both were terrible candidates and terrible campaigners who made the Democrats’ job vastly easier. In Florida, he pushed an aggressive gerrymander that energized Democratic voters while failing to net any additional seats for Republicans. Nationwide, the nakedly criminal war on Iran combined with his refusal to even humor voters’ anger at the cost of living weighed down every Republican in the country. Over the last few weeks, Trump implored voters to pretend he was on the ticket. They followed his advice. 2. Billionaire Money Wasn’t Enough One reason Trump and the rest of the party felt empowered to simply ignore clear signs from the electorate was their massive financial advantage. A few dozen billionaires, including Elon Musk, Miriam Adelson, the Winklevoss brothers, and Ken Griffin, poured truly staggering amounts of money into Republican super PACs. Trump’s personal super PAC, MAGA, Inc., had over $400 million in its account. That was more money than the Democratic Senate super PAC, House super PAC, Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee, Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee, and Democratic National Committee combined . Most of the Republican financial advantage was spent on the Senate, where the GOP outspent Democrats by about 50 percent. Republicans expected to be able to use this golden hoard to simply crush Democrats in states like Texas. It wasn’t nearly enough — a fact that should be heartening to anyone dismayed about the undemocratic effects of big money in politics. 3. The Knives Are Going to Come Out for Trump Because of the previous two points, Republican politicians are going to turn on Trump quickly. The writing is on the wall for 2028. There is no reason to think that a president whose connection to reality is visibly attenuating with each passing week will be able to turn his popularity around in the next two years. Any Republican with ambitions for the future will be racing to create distance between themselves and the administration. The few Republicans who openly opposed Trump in the last few years, like Ron DeSantis , are already blaming Trump for the defeat and calling for a new direction for the party. Determining that direction will be a messy, conflictual process, with open conflict among different elements of the party. But with every passing day, there will be fewer and fewer reasons for members of the party to continue their obeisance to Trump. 4. Democrats Won the Biggest Overall Congressional Majority for Either Party Since the GOP in 2014 The size of the Democratic majority is striking. As of Wednesday morning, most projections have the Democrats winning about 245 seats in the House, and fifty-four or fifty-five seats in the Senate. This puts the Democrats in a similar position to the Republicans in 2014, when the GOP expanded on its majority in the House and finally recaptured the Senate from the Democrats. Since the Trump era began, no party has had majorities of this size in both the House and the Senate. In both the House and the Senate, this vastly decreases the leverage available to small minorities of the caucuses. In the Senate, John Fetterman will not be able to block progressive legislation the way Joe Manchin and Kyrsten Sinema were during the Biden administration. Similarly, in the House, even the expanded team of socialist legislators will not come close to providing the margin of victory for the Democratic majority. In this context, Democratic politics will likely reflect the center of gravity of the caucus in each chamber, rather than the power of a few legislators holding strategic veto points. 5. The Left Is Continuing to Rise Across the country, this was a fantastic night for the Left. Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) candidates, as expected, won their general elections in places like New York, Colorado, and Michigan. Moreover, DSA candidates won election to the state house in close races in Kentucky and Florida. Candidates like Troy Jackson and Abdul El-Sayed, who are often associated with DSA despite not being members, also won races that were widely seen as tests for the progressive wing of the Democratic Party. Angie Nixon, a DSA member who won an upset victory in her primary for Florida’s senate seat, came within a few points of winning in a state most liberals had written off as irredeemably red. Exit polls, meanwhile, showed that three times as many young voters described themselves as democratic socialists as MAGA. DSA’s project has clearly not hit its ceiling yet, and these results show that it is time to take the fight to new battlegrounds. National Park Signs

In January, park rangers removed 34 interpretive panels from a slavery exhibit at Independence National Historical Park in Philadelphia. The signs, which described the lives of the nine people George Washington enslaved at the presidential residence there, were unceremoniously taken down — in some cases leaving the dried glue in place — to comply with President Donald Trump’s Executive Order 14253 , titled “Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History.” The order calls for the removal of signs that “inappropriately disparage Americans past or living” from the country’s national parks. Apparently, telling visitors about our first president’s slaves ran afoul of that. When Trump issued the order in March 2025, Molly Blake, a social sciences librarian at the University of Minnesota, worried about the information that might be lost as a result. She began talking with colleagues at the university, including Jenny McBurney, a government publications librarian, about what they could do. Even if they couldn’t stop park rangers from tearing down the signs, maybe they could archive them for posterity. Within a few months, they would launch Save Our Signs , a nationwide effort to preserve what the government was trying to erase. Blake and McBurney couldn’t go to all 63 national parks, of course, let alone the hundreds of historic monuments and sites overseen by the National Park Service. “We knew we had to get the public involved,” Blake said, so they invited parkgoers to photograph signs to be recorded in an online archive. The response has been overwhelming. To date, members of the public have sent Save Our Signs more than 17,000 photos shot at 428 sites, from Ellis Island and the Japanese American internment camp at Manzanar to monuments dedicated to the memory of Emmett Till , whose killing helped spark the Civil Rights Movement. They’ve also sent reports and photos from parks where the signs have already been removed, which become part of the archive’s “Censorship Tracker.” “The folks that we hear from are saying things like they love our parks, they support our parks, they want to maintain the really high-quality educational materials they’re seeing there,” McBurney said. “They want us to remember our history, the good and the bad.” A Daunting Task Even for the combined forces of the American public, however, there are a lot of parks and land to cover: more than 85 million acres across 50 states. And nobody knows where the government might strike next. “They’re not hiding it,” said Alan Spears, senior director of cultural resources at the National Parks Conservation Association. “They’re also probably not putting out press releases saying that this stuff has happened. You can just show up on Monday or Tuesday of this past week, [and] the signs are gone.” Because of the uncertainty, SOS encourages parkgoers to photograph everything. “One of the signs that was removed just noted that it’s a good idea to refill your water bottle because it’s better for the environment than using plastic,” Blake said. “I wouldn’t have guessed that that would have been targeted, but it was.” Since the order was issued, park employees have removed or modified more than 100 signs at 41 National Park Service sites, from California’s Muir Woods National Monument (for mentioning climate change and the park founders’ connections to anti-immigrant policies) to Wyoming’s Grand Teton National Park (for calling attention to explorer Gustavus Cheyney Doane’s participation in a massacre of more than 150 Indigenous people). Monitoring all those signs is as daunting for the Interior Department, which includes the Park Service, as it is for SOS. The department relies on park rangers and other employees at each site, instructing them to look for signs that might not be doing an acceptable job of reminding parkgoers of this country’s “unmatched record of advancing liberty, prosperity, and human flourishing,” as Trump’s order puts it. Park staff are expected to remove or alter such signs within 120 days of the executive order. Related: This L.A. Museum Stands Up to Trump’s Whitewashing, Vowing to ‘Scrub Nothing’ Snitch Signs A confidential Park Service database, leaked in March and containing internal communications among park staffers, showed rangers wrestling with difficult questions about what exactly the order requires. Did signage about the return of California condors from near-extinction disparage hunters? one ranger asked. Another wondered whether signs about graffiti at Utah’s Arches National Park violated the directive to emphasize the park’s “natural beauty.” For SOS, the leak provided a list of parks that might be targeted for sign removals if they hadn’t been already. “Right now, we have on our website a map of parks that, based on the leaked dataset of what might be targeted, are parks that are priorities for us,” Blake said. “It was called out on this leaked spreadsheet as a place that there might be censorship happening. We really want more photos there.” One of the challenges for Save Our Signs is the ambiguity of Trump’s order as well as Secretarial Order 3431 , which Interior Secretary Doug Burgum issued to implement the executive order. Consider the orders’ ban on “inappropriate disparagement” of Americans. Is it OK for signage at a Till site to disparage the men who tortured and lynched him? Can park signs at civil rights monuments in Birmingham, Alabama, and Little Rock, Arkansas, disparage the white supremacists who opposed the Civil Rights Movement? “Inappropriate disparagement” of Americans is “a really confusing turn of phrase,” McBurney said. “Especially in the early days, nobody knew what that meant.” The secretarial order also requires parks to post notices telling visitors how to report supposed violations, an effort that has largely been a bust. The QR-code-enabled notices, prescribed in a section of Burgum’s order titled “Encouraging Public Participation,” were soon dubbed “snitch signs” by detractors, thousands of whom submitted sarcastic comments and jabs at the Trump administration. “There are some people who reported, ‘I think there needs to be more about Indigenous history here,’” Blake said. Legal Conflicts The watchdog group Public Employees for Environmental Responsibility (PEER) filed a Freedom of Information Act request last year to find out how the executive order was being implemented. The Interior Department never responded, so PEER sued to get the records in June. Tony Irish, a longtime Interior attorney who left the department last year and joined PEER in May, said, “There’s a number of terms [in the order] that, even for me, with my background in Interior programs, isn’t clear.” He wonders how these orders might run afoul of federal law, including the National Park Service Organic Act of 1916, which created the service, and the National Historic Preservation Act of 1966. “There’s other sites, particularly ones like Little Big Horn, where Congress has specifically directed the agency to provide a viewpoint from the Native American tribes that were impacted by the events there,” Irish said. It’s not just Park Service signs that are being removed and altered. Trump’s order calls for the removal of offending signs throughout all 500 million acres of Interior Department lands, comprising roughly one-fifth of the country. Park Service sites are getting most of the attention because Americans are more inclined to visit them, Irish said, but the Fish and Wildlife Service and Bureau of Land Management oversee even more acreage. PEER is suing the department for information about the order’s impact on those lands too. There have been impacts on wildlife refuges and the Bureau of Land Management, Irish said. “Part of why we want to get these records is to see just to what extent.” Insidious Alterations Little is known about the fate of many of the lost signs. When a district court judge told the Trump administration to provide an inventory of removed materials, the partial list from the Interior Department included 18 items that had been “discarded.” Few parks employees are inclined to talk about where the old signs might be, let alone which others are slated for removal, Irish said. “We’ve seen … circumstances where employees have made their personal feelings known that have led to swift reactions from the department,” he said. “So I would suspect there is some fear of being open about that.” Some of the replacement signs sanitize or dilute the history laid out in the originals in ways that aren’t immediately obvious. “These are really insidious,” said Save Our Signs’ Blake. “The differences are really subtle. In one case, a sign about Black soldiers during the Civil War, a reference to the fact that the military remained segregated until after World War II, that sentence was quietly taken out. So you could read something like that and not realize this has been censored.” This whitewashing of history at park sites reverses decades of progress at the national parks, Spears said. “We spent the last 30 years trying to raise up some of these untold stories so that we can have a better understanding of the contributions that all of us have made,” she said. Spears called the current shift “disheartening and anger-inducing.” Save Our Signs continues to document the loss as signs across the country continue to come down. Giving Community Photos a Second Life

A neighborhood organization may have years of photographs and still struggle to find a suitable picture for its next announcement. A street-fair photo is too small for print, a workshop picture has harsh lighting, and another carries last year's event date. For groups that run on volunteer time and limited budgets, taking new photographs for every newsletter or campaign is rarely practical. Thoughtful editing can make existing material useful again. Begin with the purpose of the picture Before editing anything, decide what the audience needs from the image. An event announcement should tell people what kind of gathering to expect. A report on a neighborhood project may need to show who participated and what actually happened. An archival photograph accompanying a local history piece has a different obligation: its details are part of the record. Those distinctions affect even ordinary choices. Brightening a dim meeting-room photograph may help readers see the participants. Replacing the room with an imaginary, more attractive venue could mislead them. Likewise, a tighter crop may improve a social post, but it could exclude the volunteers whose work the organization intended to recognize. It helps to keep an untouched original and write down the intended use before opening an editor. That small habit makes later decisions easier. Edit one problem at a time Natural-language editing is useful for volunteers who know what they want changed but have little experience with traditional photo software. In Pixlio's AI image editor , someone can upload a reference photo, describe the change, choose a supported model and review the result. The edited image can then become the starting point for another revision. A practical instruction could be: "Reduce the yellow cast from the indoor lighting and brighten the faces slightly. Keep the people, posters and room arrangement unchanged." It gives the editor a specific job and sets boundaries. Compare the result with the original before using it, paying particular attention to faces, printed signs and small objects that automated edits sometimes alter. There is no need to solve every issue in one prompt. A photo intended for a website banner might need a different crop after its lighting is corrected. The workspace also supports creating an image from text, useful when an organization needs an illustration or concept graphic rather than a photograph of something that happened. Choose tools that suit the actual task Pixlio is also an AI image creative studio with specialized tools for recurring jobs. Its AI Photo Restoration tool can help with scanned photographs showing scratches, fading or weak color. For a neighborhood anniversary display, that may be a reasonable way to make old pictures easier to view. Restoration should still be checked against the scan, especially where faces or historical details are unclear. The Image Text Remover is useful when an image has an outdated digital caption or date overlaid on it and the group wants to reuse the underlying photograph. Keep a copy with the original wording, and avoid treating text on real-world signs as disposable. An Image Upscaler can prepare a small image for a larger layout, though added sharpness cannot recover information that was never captured by the camera. After an initial edit, Pixlio lets users carry the selected image into a separate upscaling or other specialized process without starting again from a blank upload. Each step still deserves a fresh review. Leave room for trust Community organizations depend on people recognizing themselves and their neighborhoods in the material they publish. Ask permission when a recognizable person's image is being reused in a new context, particularly for promotional campaigns. If an image comes from an earlier event, label it accordingly rather than letting readers assume it shows the upcoming gathering. AI-generated illustrations can work well for ideas that have no documentary photograph, such as a proposed garden or an imagined future streetscape. Describe them as concepts. For images presented as evidence of a completed project or an actual turnout, ordinary accuracy is more important than visual polish. A good final check is to ask someone unfamiliar with the editing process what they believe the picture shows. If their answer differs from what took place, revisit the edit or improve the caption. A mistaken event label or altered sign is easier to correct before the picture reaches a flyer or newsletter.

Democrats take control of US Congress in major rebuke of Trump. What comes next?

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Devin B. Martinez ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-04T23:34:51+00:00

Democrats won control of the United States Congress in the country’s midterm elections on Tuesday, November 3, in a crushing rebuke of US President Donald Trump’s second term. The Republicans lost at least seven Senate seats in states that have traditionally been strongholds for the right-wing party. The party also lost over two dozen House districts to the Democrats. In the 2024 presidential election, Trump had won some of these districts by double-digit percentage points. Kansas will have a Democratic senator for the first time in nearly a century. North and South Carolina and even Texas have flipped to the Democrats in an unprecedented shift for those states. The sweeping defeat comes after $12 billion dollars was spent. mostly by billionaires on both sides of the aisle, on what analysts are calling the most expensive midterm elections in history. A condemnation of Trump and the administration’s priorities A total of 435 seats in the US House of Representatives were up for election and 35 seats of the US Senate’s 100 seats were also decided. These two chambers, the Senate and the House of Representatives, make up the US Congress. While the president himself is not up for election, various analysts have characterized these midterm elections as a referendum on US President Donald Trump. And the resounding defeat for the Republican Party is seen as a profound rejection of the administration and its priorities over the last two years. Corruption, inflation, rising fuel prices, and the Iran war are cited as some of the top concerns on voters’ minds. “The reason we lost is the war,” said Vice President JD Vance in a post online. Speaking to BreakThrough News on election night, Trita Parsi, Iranian-Swedish international relations writer and political analyst, spoke about the deep unpopularity of the Iran war as a driver of the election result. “I don’t think we have seen an election in recent memory in which the war is such a decisive issue – not a factor – but a decisive issue.” Parsi also spoke about President Trump’s claim just before elections on a morning talk show that US funding to Israel will cease by the end of 2026. This funding is another widely unpopular aspect of US policy. “The Israelis came to the conclusion over a year ago that they have lost the American public,” he said. The plan is to “hide the money,” using joint ventures inside the Pentagon budget in a way that is “outside of the scrutiny of Congress” and the American public. He argued that funding could actually increase through this model. Because support for Israel has become such a political liability, Amanda Yee, host of The China Report , explained that AIPAC announced in October that it would stop making endorsements and direct donations to candidates. “AIPAC isn’t actually stopping any of its spending, it’s just changing the way that it spends,” Yee said. She said the United Democracy Project (UDP), AIPAC’s affiliated super PAC, now operates as a “cover for AIPAC money.” UDP has already spent millions to unseat representatives critical of Israel, such as Jamaal Bowman and Cori Bush. Israel and foreign policy aren’t the only points of dissatisfaction for people in the US, however. Various domestic issues have become major political pressure points US politics, one of the biggest ones is related to AI development. AI data centers become a national flashpoint John Russell, a journalist with More Perfect Union , spoke to BreakThrough News about the increasingly unpopular development of hyper-scale data centers to power the AI boom. He said data centers are “job-killing super computers” that “explode your utility rates” if they are hooked up to the grid next to you. If they’re not hooked up to the grid, then the massive power plants they require “burn dirty fossil fuels that kill our planet.” All of these impacts sustain vast computing that is “just shoveling money up to the top of the economy, to the already fantastically rich.” “This is a uniting issue no matter who you’re voting for,” Russell said. “People can see that this is just a big club and we’re not in it. Russell argued that despite the Democratic Party claiming victory in these elections, the masses of working people are actually looking for a party that will stop “the absolute gains for the richest people on earth” and do something that actually makes life easier and more affordable for the majority. Democrats attack independent socialist candidates and the left During the BreakThrough News election coverage, Zoe Alexandra highlighted a bizarre “mailer-gate” scandal during a race in the state of Ohio that has drawn condemnation of both the Republican and Democratic parties. The Ohio Senate race, won by Democrat Sherrod Brown, involved not just Republican Jon Husted, but also independent socialist candidate Greg Levy, who received 94,509 votes. https://www.instagram.com/reel/DfTa0tNM0tE/?utm_source=ig_web_copy_link&obrf=MzRlODBiNWFlZA==&srtk=MzRlODBiNWFlZA== In this “mailer-gate” scandal, a MAGA-linked super PAC spent 2 million dollars on mailers that mimicked Greg Levy’s socialist platform. “Mailers and video ads called on voters to ‘Join the Revolution’ and support socialist candidate Greg Levy” and criticized Sherrod Brown for being anti-worker, Alexandra said. The BT journalist said the maneuver was immediately condemned by Greg Levy and his team, who called it “a dirty trick” and a form of “digital blackface.” However, Alexandra emphasized that the Democrats’ response was not to join Levy and condemn the Republicans’ dirty electoral maneuver. “Instead, the Democrats decided to turn on Greg Levy,” Alexandra said. “They went on the offensive and were very quick to say ‘Gregy Levy is working with Republicans’ and ‘Greg Levy is actually your enemy.’” She demonstrated various mailers that the Democrats paid for to disseminate these messages. Alexandra also demonstrated headlines by corporate media outlets that blame socialist Greg Levy for potentially “spoiling” the Democratic victory in the state. A Newsweek headline read, “How a Socialist Could Undermine Sherrod Brown’s Ohio Comeback.” Another headline in The Washington Post read “PAC linked to GOP firm is boosting Ohio socialist in Senate race.” The Sherrod Brown campaign went as far as taking out an attack video ad, that spread fear and suspicion about the Greg Levy campaign being influenced by the Republican Party to “split the tides” in the Ohio race. “This is where they are directing their money,” Alexandra said. “Instead of the Democrats actually looking at their own race and asking, ‘Why is it that a socialist who’s saying we should have healthcare and education and not go to war is somehow becoming more popular?’ They’re saying, ‘Let’s attack him!’” When it comes to both of the major corporate parties in the US, “they clearly are aligned on one thing which is that the left is a threat to their survival.” Various guests on the show also spoke to the “war on the left” by the Trump administration and the far right and pointed out that if Democrats control both chambers, they would theoretically be able to do something to stand against the political repression of activism and progressivism in the US, although they have shown no opposition to that trend so far. In yet another bold independent socialist campaign in the Bronx (New York’s House District 15) Andre Easton received 8.7% of the vote. He challenged Democratic incumbent Ritchie Torres, who was re-elected, but is increasingly unpopular because of his staunch pro-Israel stance and his consistant attacks on the left and progressive candidates, including Andre Easton. Easton told BreakThrough News that it is the mass movement of workers throughout the other 365 days of the year and the multiple years between elections that truly produces the change people want to see. “Your political responsibility exists beyond the ballot box,” Easton said. He added people should absolutely vote and mobilize their communities to vote. “But your political responsibility is so much more robust and so much wider and deeper than just a single bubble on a sheet.” The socialist Bronx candidate says our political duty is carried out in “our streets, in our communities, in our classrooms and workplaces.” He emphasized that his party, the Party for Socialism and Liberation, is committed to that work and will continue to do so every day after the midterm elections. Renewing their commitment to organizing and mobilizing, various activists, journalists, and progressives have highlighted the limited progress that this crushing victory for Trump represents. What does it actually mean for Democrats to control the US Congress? Now that Democrats have decisively won midterm elections, a key question that has emerged is what can people actually expect from Democratic control of the US Congress? BreakThrough News Editor-and-Chief Ben Becker pointed out that historically, the Democrats’ have failed to use their majorities to advance meaningful progressive change. “It feels good to see Republicans lose, but I don’t think we should translate that feeling into an irrational sense of hope in what the Democrats are going to be able to achieve or even want to achieve.” He highlighted the euphoria for the Democrats winning the House and the Senate after the 2006 midterms, and even more so in the 2008 presidential election. They also had a majority when Joe Biden was elected in 2020. “None of those promises were delivered,” Becker said. “Wether it was student debt relief, universal healthcare, parental leave, universal childcare, climate legislation, immigration reform, codifying roe v wade, we could go down the list.” This gridlock and “failure to deliver,” he argues, created the conditions for Trump to return with a far more extreme right-wing program in the first place. “The euphoria – if it leads to sitting back and hoping the Democrats will do well – it will actually just lead to the return of the Republicans and Trumpism or even something worse than Trumpism.”

The United Nations Security Council failed to reach an agreement on the future of the United Nations Mission for the Referendum in Western Sahara (MINURSO) following a contentious meeting on October 30, leaving the mission without a renewed mandate for the first time since its establishment in 1991. The meeting saw Russia veto a United States-sponsored draft resolution that had received 11 votes in favor, one against, and three abstentions. The proposed resolution sought to renew the mission’s mandate for another year while making significant changes to its name, structure and political framework, including making Morocco’s autonomy proposal the basis for negotiations over the territory. This session was followed closely precisely for this reason, as the proposal would have had far-reaching consequences for the people of Sahrawi in their quest for self determination. A competing Russian-sponsored draft, which proposed a straightforward one-year extension of MINURSO’s existing mandate without changing its name or structure, also failed to secure adoption. It received three votes in favor, one against, and 11 abstentions, falling short of the nine votes required for adoption. The failure of both proposals means that the mission’s mandate expired at midnight on October 31, bringing its 35-year presence in Western Sahara to an end and raising questions about the future of the UN’s role in the territory. MINURSO was established through Security Council Resolution 690 (1991), following the UN-Organization of African Unity settlement plan accepted by Morocco and the Polisario Front in 1988. Its original mandate included preparing a referendum through which the Sahrawi people would choose between independence and integration with Morocco. However, the referendum has never been held. Morocco celebrates the end of MINURSO’s mandate Morocco welcomed the failure to renew MINURSO’s mandate, describing the development as a significant step towards ending the Western Sahara question at the United Nations. In a statement issued on October 30 , the Moroccan Royal Office said the mission’s non-renewal meant its personnel, vehicles, equipment, and other presence in what Morocco refers to as its southern provinces were expected to leave in the coming days. “A 35-year chapter has now come to a close,” the statement said. Morocco further argued that the mission had outlived its original purpose and called for an end to the Security Council’s periodic consideration of the Western Sahara question, including its meetings, reports by the UN Secretary-General and briefings by his Personal Envoy. Polisario Front rejects the proposed changes The Polisario Front, which represents the Sahrawi independence movement, welcomed Russia’s opposition to the US-sponsored resolution, arguing that its adoption would have undermined the foundations of the UN-led peace process. In a communiqué issued on October 31, the movement said the deadlock resulted primarily from the US proposal, which it argued reflected the positions of Washington and Morocco while departing from the mutually-accepted framework under which MINURSO was established. The movement expressed appreciation for Russia’s veto and acknowledged the abstentions of China, Pakistan and Somalia. It also recognized the position of Algeria and other countries that have supported the Sahrawi people’s right to self-determination. The Frente POLISARIO maintained that any attempt to unilaterally alter the mission’s mandate to accommodate Morocco’s position was unacceptable. It further recalled that it had submitted an expanded proposal to the UN secretary-general on October 20, 2025, as part of its stated commitment to finding a negotiated settlement. The movement reaffirmed its willingness to cooperate with the UN secretary-general and Security Council towards what it described as a just, lasting, and definitive solution to the decolonization of Western Sahara. Sahrawi organizations warn against abandoning international responsibility The failure to renew MINURSO’s mandate has also raised concerns among Sahrawi human rights organizations about the future of international monitoring and protection in the territory. The Sahrawi Collective of Human Rights Defenders in Western Sahara (CODESA) warned that the Security Council’s failure to reach an agreement should not become an opportunity for the international community to retreat from its responsibilities towards Western Sahara. In its October 31 statement , CODESA stressed that the territory remains an unresolved decolonization question and that the Sahrawi people’s right to self-determination must be respected. The organization argued that the difficulties MINURSO has faced in implementing its original mandate should not be used to justify abandoning the mission. Instead, it calls for renewed efforts to enable the mission to carry out its responsibilities, particularly monitoring the ceasefire and reporting developments on the ground. CODESA also called for the establishment of an independent UN mechanism to monitor and report on the human rights situation in Western Sahara, citing the need to protect civilians and human rights defenders. Urging the Security Council to resume consultations, preserve MINURSO’s original mandate and avoid imposing a political settlement without the free consent of the Sahrawi people. UN maintains commitment to self-determination Following the expiry of the mission’s mandate, UN Secretary-General António Guterres reiterated that the end of MINURSO did not extinguish the Sahrawi people’s right to determine their political future. In a statement delivered through his spokesperson on November 1, Guterres said the departure of MINURSO “does not abrogate” the people’s “inalienable right to self-determination.” He further called on all parties to respect and promote the rights of the people of Western Sahara. The secretary-general maintained that the United Nations remained committed to supporting a just, lasting, and mutually-acceptable political solution that provides for the self-determination of the Sahrawi people. Although MINURSO’s operations are now winding down, the UN has indicated that its wider diplomatic efforts towards a peaceful resolution will continue.
