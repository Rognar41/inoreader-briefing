Zohran Mamdani’s Public Grocery Plan Is a Serious One

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Errol Schweizer ／ 公開日時: 2026-07-30T19:55:47+00:00

Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s pledge to open five publicly subsidized grocery stores in New York City is quickly becoming a reality. On July 27, 2026, Mamdani announced details of the plan, including a 30 percent discount to market prices of a “core basket” of healthy groceries such as fruits, vegetables, eggs, and milk; a request for proposals process to select private sector operators; and an operating subsidy that the city will use to fund the lower prices.According to estimates by the New York City Economic Development Corporation (NYC EDC), for which I am currently consulting, shoppers could save over $1,000 a year on groceries. Two locations have already been announced, including sites in the Bronx and East Harlem. City council has approved $70 million in capital funds for store build-outs, while operational funds are still being decided. The city’s annual budget is over $125 billion; the funds needed to open and run NYC Groceries are less than one-tenth of 1 percent of that budget, a literal rounding error.According to a 2025 poll, support for the public grocery idea in the city is overwhelming. A coalition including trade unions, community groups, cooperative businesses, and food security advocates has been working closely with the city to plan and implement the idea. The result is an idiosyncratic model that may point the way forward for other cities.Mamdani’s initiative is a compelling yet imperfect intervention in the food supply. Grocery prices have increased over 35 percent since 2020, with many categories such as beef, coffee, eggs, snacks, and beverages experiencing even higher inflation. Consumer prices continue to climb even though producer prices have stabilized or, in some cases like cocoa, decreased. Along with other Donald Trump–era policies and trends — like the Iran war, which has driven up food and gas prices; immigration crackdowns, which have left food rotting in the fields and increased fresh produce prices; a gutting of the regulatory sector leading to an outbreak of explosive diarrhea; draconian cutbacks to Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) benefits affecting over four million Americans; and an increase in GLP-1 usage — Americans are now buying a lot less food.According to a Bain/Nielsen IQ study, grocery unit volumes were down 1.8 percent in June, a 2 percent deterioration in a single year, adding up to billions in fewer grocery transactions. In New York alone, over 1.5 million residents face food insecurity, while low-income consumers spend upward of 25 percent of their income on groceries.The inspiration for the NYC Groceries idea comes from an unlikely place: The military commissary system, which has been around for over 150 years and fully subsidizes all groceries through public funds for service members and their families at a 23.7 percent discount. The commissary is loved by working-class shoppers and is a well-established channel of retail trade in the grocery industry, serviced by farmers, packaged food brands, and corporate wholesalers.Unlike the commissary system, the stores will be operated by a private sector expert. The stores will not be operated by the city. This could mean a local supermarket chain, a grocery cooperative, a nonprofit operator, a retail services cooperative, or a wholesaler with a retail arm. New York City, unlike most grocery markets, is not monopolized by Walmart or Kroger and has many diverse private sector operators that could run these stores. The public sector grocery stores will also be run as a public service, not as a for-profit, and the typical retail profit margins (1–3 percent) will be folded into consumer savings. They will be open to all New Yorkers, with no means testing for entry.Mamdani’s key proposal, a core basket of pantry items that will be discounted at 30 percent of market prices, also diverges from the commissary model, which discounts all prices to an average of 23.7 percent to the market. This 30 percent discount will be subsidized by the city to cover the gross margin (operating costs such as labor, utilities, general and administrative expenses, etc.), while rent and taxes will be covered by the city. Operators will have the option of selling other, noncore products at market-rate prices to cover their full overhead.The subsidies will be calculated through item-level discounts multiplied by unit volumes sold, the same way promotional discounts are calculated for grocers by their trade partners. This is a straightforward calculus for experienced operators. The subsidies will be approved through the city budget, just like funds for public transit, libraries, sanitation, and water. The stores will also not be food banks or pantries, selling donations or leftover “food waste” downstream from producers. They will offer the freshest, highest-quality products, showing that good food should be a public service.The store employees will get living wages, comprehensive benefits, and have significant labor protections. Operators will be required to ensure card check recognition, an easier and fairer way for employees to unionize should they so choose; to respect employer/management neutrality in union drives, pledging not to fight union efforts; and to not bring on anti-union consultants. The administration’s goal is to make these stores a great place to work and build a career, which is very rare in the grocery industry, which typically sees store labor as a resource to be strip-mined, streamlined, and exploited.The stores will be thoughtfully designed, laid out, and merchandised so that cash-strapped New Yorkers have a premium shopping experience. The stores will sell a mix of branded items, private labels, and locally grown products geared to the specific communities and markets they serve. Products will be familiar and mainstream, and driven by consumer demand and market trends as opposed to particular ideologies of what the working class should be eating.Mamdani’s public groceries plan could be a model and inspiration for other cities to run with. But it is not without flaws. The military commissary is publicly owned, funded, and operated; ironically, this means the Department of Defense’s public sector grocery purchasing practices are closer to communism than a socialist mayor’s. Outsourcing operations separates the risk from the responsibility, and the city will need to work closely with operators to keep the program running well and ensure operators don’t bail if the going gets tough.The upside is that selected operators will come with a ready-made supply chain, with wholesale, manufacturer, grower, and private-label contracts, operational expertise, administrative, accounting, and informational technology systems that the city will not have to invest in and build from scratch. NYC EDC has a lot of experience with the private sector, such as the NYC Ferry system, but grocery is a complicated business.The administration is facing a lawsuit from a coalition of small supermarket operators and bodega owners who oppose the idea out of fear that the city is using tax dollars to hurt their businesses. Many of these store owners already receive enormous subsidies to their operations through public supplemental food programs like SNAP and Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC). But if the city were smart, it could work with store owners near the NYC Grocery sites to increase the affordability goals. If local stores have seen their business impacted, let them receive subsidies on core basket items to lower those prices and be subjected to the same rigorous accounting, reporting, and price controls as the NYC Grocery stores.The city should also create a retail and wholesale services arm through the NYC EDC. This would ensure long term consistency in the program while building in marketing, administration, and supply chain efficiencies, such as cooperative warehousing, wholesaling and private label sourcing, including utilization of the Hunts Point Cooperative Market, regardless of who operates the stores. Such retail service organizations are very common in the grocery industry as a means for independents to stay competitive with larger chains.Mamdani’s idea to only discount a “core basket” of products is also odd, since it leaves the door open for price gouging on noncore products. Operators will want to make higher margins on noncore items and create a two-tiered pricing model that has the potential to stigmatize value-conscious shoppers. The fastest-growing grocers in the United States are deep discounters such as Aldi, Lidl, and Walmart, and are known unequivocally for low prices across the store.The administration has also passed on implementing a “Values-Based Purchasing” framework for the stores and is instead just incentivizing the operators to purchase from local suppliers. Values-Based Purchasing is modeled on the sourcing standards of more upscale chains such as Whole Foods and Erewhon, and encourages subsidized public sector supply chains to prioritize fair labor, animal welfare, environmental sustainability, economic justice, and healthy foods. Values-Based Purchasing is already required in public purchasing by New York City, and via the Good Food NY Bill passed by both New York State legislative bodies, is currently awaiting a signature by Gov. Kathy Hochul to make it state law. So this omission is bizarre and shortsighted, and will mean the city may likely end up selling products made in conditions and with ingredients that aren’t in line with the administration’s professed values.And the biggest risk is how the administration tacks progress to its goal of affordability. A simple calculation of how many discount subsidies are distributed is the easy part, but how the city estimates and communicates how five stores improve the lives of cash-strapped New Yorkers and point the way to a better and more just food system is a much broader and more compelling calculus.Despite its shortcomings, Mamdani’s public grocery sector plan is among the most compelling innovations in a food supply that is increasingly consolidated, expensive, corrupt, and lately, downright dangerous. And it aims to do one thing that much of the grocery sector, especially Mamdani’s critics, have lost the script for really well: to make good food affordable to all.And that should be the whole point.

US Mail Carriers Are Poised to Elect New Union Leadership

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Alexandra Bradbury ／ 公開日時: 2026-07-30T14:59:20+00:00

A leadership change looks likely in the National Association of Letter Carriers (NALC) this fall. Members are mad about low pay and forced overtime, dangerous heat and robberies, the rejected contract imposed last year, the backlog of tens of thousands of grievances, and the president’s secretive approach to bargaining. There’s not just one group pushing for change but a whole ecosystem of reform groups, slates, and podcasters among the five-thousand-plus delegates coming to Los Angeles for the union convention August 3–7. They’ll rub elbows and hopefully build unity at a “ Reformers Rum Party ” on Sunday night, the eve of the convention. Days before the August 5 nominations, it looks like a three-way race for president. The 200,000 members will get the chance to vote this fall — by mail ballot, of course — for ten officers, three trustees, and fifteen regional business agents. Two reform tickets have been campaigning for two years. One is the Concerned Letter Carriers (CLC) slate, headlined by current Vice President James Henry and including a number of branch (local) presidents. The other is Mike Caref , business agent for Illinois, running solo for president. Despite months of speculation about whether President Brian Renfroe would run again, he still hasn’t made any announcment. But a third slate announced July 17 includes many other incumbents, led by Eddie Davidson, current business agent for Florida, Georgia, and the Carolinas. Doomsday Clock The backdrop is ongoing existential threats to the US Postal Service (USPS). Since Trump returned to office, the postal doomsday clock has been minutes from midnight . Though Trump hasn’t acted on his threat to move the Postal Service under the Department of Commerce, his new batch of nominees for the Board of Governors aren’t even from private sector logistics but from finance — so maybe less interested in rationalizing the service than in selling it for parts. Postmaster General David Steiner, who somehow makes his predecessor Louis DeJoy look good, is going along with Trump’s extreme attacks on vote by mail. He says USPS won’t deliver ballots in states that won’t hand over voter lists to the government. Steiner has also been claiming USPS could run out of money within the year, and threatening to close post offices and cut delivery days if its finances aren’t fixed. The next-biggest union, the American Postal Workers Union (APWU), organized rallies July 28 for three reforms that would help in the short term — raise the debt limit, modify the investment strategy for retiree benefit funds (they’re all in Treasury bonds), and change how the retirement obligation is calculated. Longer term, USPS needs to adapt to fewer letters and more packages. Management’s approach has been to cut costs by degrading service. The unions argue it should instead expand revenue by adding services like banking, internet access, hunting licenses, electric vehicle charging, and checking in on seniors. In addition, retired letter carrier and longtime activist Jamie Partridge argues, the government should subsidize rural delivery, as many other countries do. Overlapping Groups A vote-no movement on the 2024 contract was organized by a coalition of forces including Caref, the CLC, the Build a Fighting NALC (BFN) caucus, and influential podcaster Corey Walton, number two on the CLC ticket. Podcasts are big with letter carriers — you can listen while you slog through the workday. One called Next Generation Carriers has interviewed activists from all the vying groups and even Renfroe himself (Labor Notes staffers have also been guests). Cohost Margo O’Neill, a steward of Branch 352 in Des Moines, is also the treasurer for BFN. Her main goals at the convention, she said, are to grow the reach of the reform movement and to build unity across the various existing groups. The Next Gen hosts have been agitating for a contract demand of “30 for 30”: a starting wage of $30 and no more than one manager per thirty carriers, to save money and alleviate micromanagement. New carriers start at about $21, in an entry-level job called “city carrier assistant” where they’re often made to work Sundays delivering Amazon packages. It can take two years to convert to a career position, and even then, everyone hired since 2013 is on second-tier Table 2 pay. In the Massachusetts chapter of BFN, “we’ve been really pushing to get people more active, talking to CCAs about their rights on the job,” said Chris Persampieri, legislative and political director of Branch 18: A lot of people had no idea that if it’s your day off, you’re not on call. You don’t have to pick up the phone. There’s no union steward on Amazon Sundays, so they’re on their own. We show up with flyers about their rights and a box of joe from Dunkin’ Donuts, because it’s Massachusetts, and that’s what we do here. Some reform activists are backing the CLC; some are backing Caref; some like certain members of the Davidson slate. The election may yield a mixed executive council of people who ran on opposing tickets, and the new president will have to “mold that into a solid team,” said Branch 3 President David Grosskopf of Buffalo, who’s running for trustee with the CLC. Contract Debacle What’s difficult to find is anyone with a good word to say about Renfroe. Last convention, he was already in hot water, facing internal charges of dereliction of duty and impaired driving of a union vehicle. Delegates voted to let him off the hook. But they also voted for more member involvement and substantive bargaining updates; Renfroe had been bargaining for nearly two years in near-complete secrecy. Soon afterward, he came out with a tentative agreement featuring a measly 1.3 percent annual raise. Members voted it down. As the union returned to the table in early 2025, Renfroe announced nationwide “Fight Like Hell” rallies — then reversed course over fears of Trump and the Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE), and rushed into binding arbitration. The arbitrator imposed a contract nearly identical to the one members had voted down, sparking more outrage. And the bad deal had taken so long to negotiate that it was more than half over, expiring in May 2026, so NALC was soon back at the table. The union has made incremental improvements in its approach this time. There was a member survey, a national day of rallies, some video updates, and a collective bargaining conference for branch and state leaders. Still, as the sixty-day mediation period expired in July, meaning the union could be moving to arbitration, members had little information. “We have no idea what economic package our union is asking for,” Grosskopf said. “We don’t know if we’re asking for $30 an hour, or $50.” Renfroe has kept even fellow officers at arm’s length from bargaining. The last straw for Mack Julion, assistant secretary-treasurer, was when the president proposed a four-day workweek of ten-hour days, meaning carriers would no longer get overtime pay after eight hours. “Ours is a physically demanding job,” Julion said. “I’m kinda known for my fancy cowboy boots; I wear those because I have a bad case of plantar fasciitis. I’ve known many letter carriers who had hip replacements and knee surgeries. “I had suggested hazard pay,” he said. “If you think a four-by-ten schedule is a higher priority than hazard pay when we’re just a couple years removed from COVID and we have letter carriers literally dying in the heat . . . We are human, and we break down.” Rather than run again with Renfroe, Julion planned to run independently for reelection. He ended up joining the Davidson slate. Democracy in Action The convention has strongly democratic features. There are no major hurdles to getting nominated. Delegates vote on every constitutional amendment or resolution submitted by any branch or state association — this year there are many . Persampieri authored an amendment for ranked-choice voting (where voters can indicate their second- and third-choice candidates and the resulting votes are tabulated in “instant runoffs”) to remove the fear of voting for a spoiler. Branch 3 proposed that the national bargaining team include five officers, not just the president. Delegates may hear internal charges brought against the officers by a San Diego carrier, Michael Rivera, over the imposition of the rejected contract last year. The officers declined to investigate the charges against themselves, but Rivera is appealing to the convention body. They will also elect delegates to the 2030 AFL-CIO convention. A “Stand-Up Slate” of younger carriers is running on themes like universal health care and the abolition of billionaires. Some resolutions were submitted calling on the union to live stream the convention, which officers decided to do. Minneapolis Branch 9 has sent resolutions on political themes, like ending the war in Iran and funding public services. BFN activist Tyler Vasseur warned first-time delegates not to be too disappointed if they don’t pass. “The idea is to force a debate on the issue,” he said. “If we get 20 to 30 percent of the vote, we can identify people who want to fight and get organized on these issues going forward.” Wins Needed Getting members into motion is the name of the game. “If we’re ever going to get the participation up in the union, people have to feel there’s a fight going on, and we have to get some wins,” Caref said. “We’ve been taking a lot of losses, and it’s really demoralizing for the carriers. “You’ve got to start with a clear vision for where you want to be, and a strategy for how you’re gonna get there. You get some little wins, you get more people involved, and you start moving in the right direction. “That’s why I always tell people, I only want to be president if you guys want me to be president,” he said, “because I’m going to give you stuff to do right away.”

Moderation Is Not a Real Political Strategy

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Matt Bruenig ／ 公開日時: 2026-07-30T14:00:34+00:00

One of Matt Yglesias’s defining political positions is that Democrats should be more moderate in order to attract more votes. This is generally invoked as a critique of the Left and so naturally attracts left-wing ire, which Yglesias seems to enjoy. One issue I’ve long had with this moderation-or-not discourse is that it’s a moving target. If we define “moderate” as something like the median of the current distribution of political views (ignoring all the complications with that concept), then there must be people who organically and earnestly hold those specific views. Yet those people are only moderates for as long as their views happen to be at the median. If the underlying distribution of views changes, which it does, then people who were formerly moderates, so defined, no longer are. But the people who advocate moderation, like Matt Yglesias, are almost never moderate in this organic sense. Yglesias’s views — e.g., those in One Billion Americans — are definitely not at the current median. These people are instead strategic moderates who advocate that politicians and other political actors determine what the current median is and then adopt those views, including in their own political advocacy. This sounds sensible until you realize that the median is constantly changing, and that minoritarian views become majoritarian (and thus moderate) views only because people advocate for them in the political sphere broadly defined. If you are constantly sampling the point-in-time median to define your views, you will have very unstable views, and you will end up having taken positions that become wildly unpopular. Yglesias on Israel Israel provides a useful case study on how this sort of strategic moderation can go wrong. After the October 7 attacks, US support for the Israeli military became a hot-button issue that divided the Left from the center-left in the Democratic Party. In the early period of that dispute, public polling was generally pro-Israel. But this completely reversed over the next couple of years ( Gallup , Pew ). This whipsawing of public opinion resulted in strategic moderates like Yglesias recommending taking positions that quickly became toxic, creating a “defund the police”–type problem for all of the politicians and political actors who followed Yglesias’s advice. To see just how bad this advice really was, I used a coding agent to download every Matt Yglesias tweet from October 7, 2023, to present that mentioned “Israel,” “Palestine,” or “Gaza.” I was hoping I could plot some kind of change over time with that data, but Yglesias appears to have deleted all of his tweets from August 2024 through May 2026. This was presumably done through a tweet deletion service that said it would delete all of his tweets but actually only deleted his last 3,200. So I shifted to a second idea, which was to have Claude analyze all 492 Israel-related tweets Yglesias made between October 7 and August 2024 (i.e., before the mass deletion) and to identify the ones in which Yglesias was arguing, directly or implicitly, that it was smart politics to be pro-Israel. You can browse the complete results of this here . Below are a few of them: The last screenshot is the one that piqued my interest in all of this because it stuck in my head from when I first saw it nearly three years ago. Narrowly, on the merits, everything about it is backward. All else equal, sending money to Israel is not good for the American poor, and, as a matter of political rhetoric, it is pretty easy to say we should be spending that money on services at home. In the years since, that particular rhetorical line seems to have become fairly common and effective. But Yglesias was not making a point about the policy merits. He was saying that Ritchie Torres was, whether sincerely or not, being a smart political moderate because he was doing good position-taking. Just two years later, three members of Congress in Torres-adjacent NYC districts were primaried by people who were, to a large extent, running against the pro-Israel positions of the incumbents. Torres did not face a similar sort of challenge this go-around. He may have won even if he did. But it’s hard to believe today that his past pro-Israel position-taking is a political asset that is helping his impoverished constituents. The same is true for other politicians, not just Torres, who shrewdly followed the polls in 2023 and now have a record on Israel that puts them at odds with the majority of Americans and the vast majority of Democrats. Earnest Politics Are Unavoidable The problems with this sort of strategic moderation are not just that you can get burned by a rapid change of public opinion. There is also something more conceptually troubling about it, which is that it is, in a Kantian sense, not universalizable. If everyone were to try to deduce what the median is and then adopt that view, then there would be no median. To have a median, you need people to earnestly engage in political opinion formation and political advocacy that generates a distribution of political views. Yet this sort of earnest political engagement, which is what makes strategic moderation possible at all, is regarded as naive and reckless by strategic moderates. These days, Yglesias has, in the spirit of strategic moderation, said that Democrats should actually bash Israel. That is, after all, what the current median requires. But if everyone took his advice in 2023, then the median would have never moved to the current anti-Israel position. To the extent that Yglesias tries to deal with these paradoxes, it’s by imagining that it’s possible to simultaneously be moving public opinion in your direction without that bleeding over at all into the electoral process. But these two things are obviously intertwined. There is no secret place where you can spread your ideas away from the ears of voters, and winning politicians’ support is part of the process of moving public opinion. Yglesias’s favored example on this point actually proves the opposite. On Twitter and elsewhere, Yglesias likes to point out that gay marriage advocates did not press Barack Obama to run as a gay marriage proponent because they knew it was not yet popular enough at the time, and Obama had given reassurances that he was actually lying about his views on gay marriage. Yet these same gay marriage advocates had, in the 1990s and 2000s, pursued state-level lawsuits aimed at legalizing gay marriage as part of a long-haul strategy of changing public opinion. Some of these suits succeeded, but they also generated public backlash in the form of state-level constitutional amendments banning same-sex marriage. It was in this back-and-forth that a durable public consensus in favor of gay marriage was forged. Free riding off of earnest politics by having others do it and then, as if floating above it all, putting your finger on the median and going “there” can be very psychologically satisfying. It makes you feel like you are one of the only serious people in politics. But what it really does is turn yourself into a non-factor, someone whose political views twist in the wind based on how persuasive other political actors — the ones who don’t engage in strategic moderation — end up being.

by Jean Allen As I write, the 12th of June, everyone around the world is celebrating. All our struggles, all our toil, all the sadness and stress and anxiety you live your life in, all of it has been made worth it. Your wealth has trickled up. The world has its first trillionaire! It’s Elon Musk, the great South African genius. Don’t you feel great? Doesn’t this make you feel happy? Elon Musk has become a trillionaire because his company, SpaceX, went public in an initial public offering set to be one of the largest in history. Why is that happening? Among talk that we are in an AI bubble, among serious questions about the profitability of any element of SpaceX besides Starlink , SpaceX has launched itself into a stock market whose regulations have been drastically changed recently. Shorter financial reporting requirements, combined with a new requirement that all ‘indexed’ funds (aka, wealth being institutionally managed which operates by fixed indexes of stocks) have to treat new offerings as if they were existing companies, is leading to a tremendous amount of wealth flowing into Elon Musk’s pockets from our retirement funds. This is a tremendous climb for a man born to a South African emerald empire, that he now alone has more wealth than all of South Africa and Romania combined. And isn’t that great? I think it’s good, personally. For a long time Americans have lived under the delusion that we live in the best of all possible worlds. We believed we lived in a place where wealth was distributed perhaps slightly unfairly, but which generally went according to merit. The rise of the tech billionaire seemed to be a sign of this, with these odd geniuses who seemed able to connect the world. Elon Musk has certainly been one such genius, having used his money from 2010 on inserting himself in media he liked, buying favorable appearances with Iron Man, Lisa Simpson, Rick & Morty, and more. After his daughter came out as trans and in opposition to his (already far-right) politics, he decided to become more explicitly involved in politics, buying Twitter and turning it into a vector for far-right beliefs the world over and using it to, among other things, help instigate the Belfast race riots . Elon Musk is a hateful mediocrity who blares out his lack of curiosity or taste on a daily basis. His money comes from various means of defrauding the public. And he is the world’s first trillionaire. All big capital is like this. There is this idea that the wealth of a capitalist is a just reward for all the great decisions they make, that the money a capitalist has is quantitatively the same as the money we have. This is false. For you and me, money is something we need to buy necessities, services, perhaps the occasional major purchase or luxury. And as wealth gets larger it stays the same. Apartments become condos become homes become mansions. But at a certain point wealth stops being just a sum of currency, the ability to purchase yet better necessities or yet more luxuries. At a certain point currency becomes capital, becomes the ability to independently plan the economy. In my hometown there are two schools. The public school was built a century ago on a hill, with a classic red-brick veneer. It was built on the behalf of a wealthy widow (Mrs.Russel Sage), who I was taught to appreciate as a great philanthropist in the elementary school she founded. The private school was built in the 00s, with a hyper-modern aesthetic, and was built when a wealthy widow (Mrs. Courtney Sale Ross) decided she wanted her daughters to get a liberal arts education out on Long Island. That is what wealth does to political economy. Instead of a school system designed by our democratic representatives, wealth allows planning to be done according to nearly any whim a rich person might have. And building private schools with non-unionized workforces according to your personal whims is often one of the best kind of whims a rich person might have. You might be in a city that’s been trying to establish public transit for decades, and Elon Musk can individually decide to build a nonsensical Hot Wheels tunnel for individual cars designed by one of his other subsidiaries . Or you might be Musk’s colleague who does international health planning according to your desire to reduce the African population . “Elon Musk is possibly the greatest living inventor!” – Lisa Simpson I think we should put this whole class on a performance improvement plan (PIP). I don’t see what society is really getting out of such a substantial investment in such a narrow set of people. I think it is morally and ethically wrong for such a small number of men to have so much decision-making power while so many of us have so little. I think that this imbalance makes everyone involved worse people. You can see it most obviously in Elon Musk. Do you think Elon Musk is happy? It doesn’t seem so to me. He seems like the last in a long line of pathetic rich men I’ve met, men who never faced pushback and so never had to mature. He is the world’s richest man, and he claims to be on the leaderboards of Diablo IV, a game known for being a relentless time suck that only real gamers (read losers) would spend so much time in. He is the world’s richest man and it is nearly impossible to watch an interview with him for not flinching. He is desperately emotionally needy for something the world’s richest man can never get – approval. No one is his equal and so he can not be truly respected and so he desperately seeks the approval of our demented old patriarch. Put him on a PIP for all our sakes. The Saudi-Emirati Rivalry Is Fueling a Series of Wars

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Jean Montagne ／ 公開日時: 2026-07-30T13:01:58+00:00

The feud between the rulers of Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates (UAE) has ramifications well beyond these two capital-rich, autocratic states in the Arabian Peninsula. It directly affects the long-suffering peoples of Sudan, South Sudan, Yemen, and Somalia who are among the poorest and most conflict-burdened populations of the world. Long simmering under the surface, the Saudi-Emirati rivalry blew up on December 30, 2025, when the Saudis bombed two Emirati ships in the port of Mukalla in Yemen. This was a response to the takeover of the country’s easternmost governorates by the UAE-supported Yemeni separatist Southern Transitional Council (STC) in previous weeks. The Saudi bombing prompted the evacuation of all Emirati personnel and equipment from Yemen and the rapid defeat of the STC throughout the country. Yemen remains the most visible site of the worsening Saudi-Emirati relationship, but there are many others both in foreign and domestic issues. History of Rivalry In the days before the United States and Iran agreed to a nemorandum of understanding that has now disintegrated, the UAE joined Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and other Gulf states in appealing to Donald Trump to end the war. But Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) monarchs are unlikely to forget that the UAE was the only regional state that had earlier encouraged Israel and the US to pursue attacks on Iran and even actively participated in them while others were struggling to end the conflict. As the GCC rulers had long predicted, their territories were the main targets of Iranian attacks. In coming years, joint action and cooperation between Saudi Arabia and the UAE will continue when circumstances demand it, but their rivalry is now a fundamental feature of regional politics and will worsen, regardless of a meaningless exchange of friendly messages between two officials on July 21. Decades of pretense that the GCC states are a cohesive group of like-minded autocracies are misleading. Going back to the nineteenth century, rival families ruling little more than villages were riven by intrafamily disputes, with their leaders killing each other to control the then-scarce resources of the region. This sequence was interrupted when Great Britain fossilized the relationships between them as imperial overlord. Saudi Arabia was, of course, the exception, but it is barely a century since the Al Saud finally took over Hejaz and established the country along its current borders. Had it not been for British domination of the coastal statelets, there is little doubt that, with the exception of Oman and Yemen, the entire peninsula would now be part of Saudi Arabia. Lip Service Relations between Saudi Arabia and the UAE have been tense more often than not. The long-lasting conflict over the Buraimi Oasis was only solved in 1974 when the kingdom recognized the UAE diplomatically. Particularly since the beginning of the twenty-first century, under the leadership of Mohammed bin Zayed (MBZ), the country has used its massive hydrocarbon export income to increase its power and challenge Saudi dominance of the GCC states and beyond. As early as 2009, the Emirati leadership considered Saudi Arabia a serious security threat, as revealed in a US Wikileaks cable from that year: While the UAE pays lip service to the idea of GCC unity, the reality is that Abu Dhabi is deeply skeptical of multilateral approaches particularly on military matter [sic]. And while publicly expressing close ties with Riyadh, the UAE privately regards the Kingdom as its second greatest security threat after Iran (Israel is not on the list). After King Salman’s advent to the Saudi throne in 2015, his son Mohammed bin Salman (MBS) soon ascended to very senior positions. To become crown prince, he needed support from Washington, which favored the then-incumbent, his cousin Mohammed bin Naif, who had been a close collaborator of US intelligence agencies for decades. The UAE’s influential ambassador Yousef al Otaiba made the change possible by persuading the US authorities to support MBS. For a couple of years, observers generally described the relationship between MBZ and MBS as one of mentor and mentee. The high point of their cooperation, which was also to be the root of their future divergence, was the blockade of Qatar between 2017 and 2021. Initiated by the Emiratis, it was at first supported by MBS. However, the Saudi-initiated reconciliation with Qatar was imposed on a reluctant UAE by 2021. By now, the relationship had already significantly deteriorated as a result of this and other issues. Yemen was the first space where their rivalry was visible as early as the summer of 2017, when UAE officials did not hesitate, in private at least, to insult the Saudi leadership. Both Saudi Arabia and the UAE aspire to middle-ranking world power status with international influence. To achieve its targets, Saudi Arabia must develop many sectors in which the UAE is decades ahead, including tourism, entertainment, sports, hospitality, and luxury infrastructure. Domestic Strategies The development strategies of the two states are similar if not identical, based on the export of hydrocarbons, a close alliance with the United States and other Global North states, and neoliberal economic policies. Both are designing their economies for a post-oil future on the basis of advanced technologies, tourism, and grandiose projects. Politically, they are both absolute monarchies unwilling to accept any form of challenge, or even any hint of alternative political views, repressing the mildest forms of dissent. In the long run, the size and financial clout of the Saudi regime are advantages it possesses. The population balance favors the kingdom, three times that of the UAE. Moreover, if we only include nationals of the two countries, disregarding migrant workers, the UAE has barely 10 percent of Saudi Arabia’s population. The UAE has a much higher per capita income for its nationals and is way ahead of the kingdom in its development model: for example, the Saudis only introduced tourism visas in 2019. The UAE’s strategic geographic position, about halfway between East Asia and Europe, is a major asset for trade and manufacturing. Disagreements and forms of competition are growing. Taking early advantage of the GCC Free Trade Agreement, the UAE established numerous productive and other enterprises in a series of “free zones,” mostly in Dubai. In July 2021, the Saudi regime ended free trade for outputs from free zones and imposed import tariffs on these products, targeting the UAE, which is most affected. The same year, it decreed that all major international companies operating in the kingdom must have their HQ there. This prompted more than 660 companies to move to Riyadh, although its living conditions are not yet quite as relaxed as those in Dubai. Various incentives have been provided to support this move. Although the UAE has a long head start, the Saudis are catching up, largely thanks to two factors: improved standards of entertainment and comfort in the kingdom, alongside the size and scope of potential profits for the companies concerned, which far outstrip anything the UAE can offer. This month, the Financial Times reported systematic delays in financial transfers between the two countries. Another difference is the UAE’s focus on military interventionism. Since 1990, its forces have been involved in US-led operations in Afghanistan, Somalia, Kosovo, Libya, and Iraq and received the moniker of “little Sparta” from senior US military staff. By contrast, the Saudi military are widely reputed to be little more than a “parade” army. This has been one reason that few Saudi ground troops have been active in the fighting in Yemen, though the UAE suffered significant losses there. International Relations Saudi and Emirati policies toward Russia and China are similar, ignoring the US-led sanctions and embargoes in favor of deepening economic relations. The Saudis pioneered a prisoner exchange between Ukraine and Russia in 2022. They also hosted an international Ukraine conference in August 2023 as well as meetings involving Ukraine, Russia, and the United States in March 2025. However, the UAE has mediated more prisoner exchanges, and the US-sponsored Ukraine-Russia negotiations in late 2025 took place in Abu Dhabi, suggesting that on this front, it is unclear who has Washington’s favor. In Yemen, the two states were pursuing incompatible policies as early as 2017, and the gap steadily widened until the open breach six months ago. The UAE supports Southern separatists, while the Saudis are in favor of retaining a unified state. A major point of divergence emerged in Sudan when war broke out in 2023 between the Sudanese Armed Forces under Abdel Fattah al-Burhan and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) led by Hemedti (officially Mohammed Hamdan Dagalo), which have been responsible for horrific massacres. Saudi Arabia supports the “official” armed forces, while the Emiratis continue actively arming and financing the RSF despite worldwide outrage at their atrocities in Darfur. In 2025, the UAE supported the formation of a RSF parallel government, while the Saudis established a coordinating council with the Burhan government. Following the eruption of the rivalry in Yemen in December 2025, disagreements on Sudan flared up publicly once again. Tensions are also noticeable around the Red Sea. Saudi Arabia has facilities in Djibouti, whose government expelled the UAE in 2017. The Emiratis then set up a new base in Berbera in the unrecognized state of Somaliland, with additional facilities in Eritrea. Following Israel’s recognition of Somaliland, the UAE is now building military facilities inland to facilitate Israeli and US activities in the area. Meanwhile, the Saudis are deepening their relations with Somalia. They have kept the UAE out of the Red Sea Council, a regional body created in 2020, although it must be said that this remains little more than a paper organization at time of writing. A Deep Split Differences over oil exports and production first became prominent in 2021, when the Saudis compromised with the Emirati position. However, the UAE went on to leave OPEC on May 1 this year, a move that will deeply affect finances in Saudi Arabia. When the long-term impact of constraints on the Strait of Hormuz are solved, oil prices will drop thanks to a glut and the increase in renewable alternatives. This context explains why rivalry, even explicit hostility, dominates the relationship between the Saudi Kingdom and the UAE, with their two leaders hardly on speaking terms. As early as late 2022, MBS was said to have told reporters that the UAE “stabbed us in the back,” for which he threatened to retaliate. Although it is highly unlikely that this situation will lead to lasting armed confrontation between the two states, there is little doubt that the rivalry will intensify and manifest in other situations. There is now a deep split between the UAE and Israel on the one hand and a larger number of states that include Saudi Arabia, other GCC members, and Egypt, alongside Turkey and Pakistan. This is likely to be a feature of regional politics for years to come. Yemen may remain the most visible litmus test of their relationship, though the Red Sea and surrounding states are also likely to become prominent. As the RSF prepares more atrocities in Sudan, now in Kordofan, the UAE shows no sign of reducing its support. Conflict continues in Yemen, with the Emirati leadership undermining efforts to solve the crisis. The main victims of this rivalry are the people of these countries.

Things Keep Getting Worse for Brazil’s Bolsonaro Dynasty

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Olavo Passos de Souza ／ 公開日時: 2026-07-29T19:13:51+00:00

As Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva campaigns for a fourth term in office ahead of this October’s general election, the Brazilian right is struggling to keep up with the country’s popular left-wing leader. The right-wing political vacuum created by the conviction of former President Jair Bolsonaro left many questioning who would take on the Bolsonarist cause — still the overwhelming force among Brazilian conservatives. In the end, Flávio Bolsonaro, the former president’s eldest son, and himself a veteran politician, took on the mantle and is currently Lula’s main opponent for the leadership of Brazil. However, a series of financial and personal scandals that have developed over the last few months, ranging from alleged movie bribes to quarrels with his influential stepmother, have rocked the younger Bolsonaro’s candidacy, with some arguing his bid is now in free fall. Dynastic Politics In 2025, as the likelihood grew that Jair Bolsonaro would be convicted on charges of conspiracy against Brazilian democracy, the question of who would replace him began to emerge. Much like Lula on the opposite side of the spectrum, Bolsonaro had come to dominate Brazil’s right-wing camp. The Liberal Party (PL), adopted by Bolsonaro during his presidency and soon molded into his populist quasi-fascistic image, became the mainstream right-wing party of Brazil. It eclipsed the neoliberal (and confusingly named) Social Democratic Party, shifting Brazilian politics away from the center and toward the far right. Today the Liberal Party is among the largest in Brazil, with the most senators and the second-highest number of deputies, not to mention thousands of mayors and local representatives. Its candidates preach an evangelical, authoritarian, and reactionary program that blurs the line between state and religion as well as between private and public interests. In these circles, Bolsonaro is seen more as a father figure than a conventional political leader. Yet for many months, the favorite to replace Bolsonaro was not a member of the Liberal Party. Instead, the expected choice was the Governor of São Paulo, Tarcísio de Freitas, a figure who managed to position himself as the more moderate and palatable option. This not due to any actual moderation on his part — Freitas is a noted right-wing populist, with his own fair share of controversies — but simply by dint of being unconnected to the countless scandals that plagued Bolsonaro and his main political supporters. By painting himself as a less scandalous and more pragmatic kind of right-winger, Tarcísio recalled the abortive US presidential campaign of Ron DeSantis, the Florida politician who tried to present himself as the more reasonable Republican alternative to Donald Trump. His Father’s Son In the end, and to the surprise of many — Tarcísio included — Flávio Bolsonaro, Jair’s oldest son and a senator in his own right, announced his candidacy under the Liberal Party banner after reportedly consulting with his imprisoned father. The announcement placed Flávio in the position of successor, or more accurately, replacement (as his father’s influence is certain to loom large over his hypothetical administration). It also immediately deflated the hopes of Tarcísio, who promptly announced his reelection campaign for the São Paulo governorship instead. More than a party decision, Bolsonaro’s choice in favor of his own son demonstrates that the former president wishes above all to keep the leadership of the Brazilian right in his grasp. Nevertheless, Flávio Bolsonaro is not a carbon copy of his father. While his father has a long track record of brutishness and throwing insults that made him loved by some and hated by many, Flávio has a reputation for politeness and pragmatism. He has been known to work well even with rivals from Lula’s Workers’ Party (PT) if it meant passing legislation. He is no less right wing than his father and defends many of the reactionary claims about the historic military dictatorship and the 2022 election, which Bolsonaro supporters falsely maintain was stolen by Lula. Yet despite being an ardent defender of his father’s legacy, Flávio is known as the more “respectable” of the three Bolsonaro brothers, and seems intent on presenting his own persona. His campaign had already suffered from Lula’s bump in popularity earlier this year. Now it has faced some massive setbacks, all connected to his own family. Embarrassing Relatives Flávio’s well-groomed, calculated persona starkly contrasts with that of his younger brother, Eduardo. Currently residing in the United States, Eduardo takes much more after his father in demeanor, being known for his online rants and slur-filled tirades against those who oppose the family. The former president’s chief spokesperson and speechwriter, Eduardo is a gifted propagandist who has spent the last few years on the conservative lecture circuit preaching about the supposed persecution of his family, the dictatorial tendencies of the Brazilian judiciary, and the dangers of cultural Marxism. A figure with close ties to US right-wing media, Eduardo Bolsonaro took leave of his position as federal deputy to live in the United States in early 2025. He has led the anti-Brazilian lobby in US politics from this vantage point ever since, working hard to sabotage the Brazilian government through any means. In July 2025, he took partial credit for Trump’s decision to impose tariffs on Brazil, a move justified with reference to the treatment of Jair Bolsonaro. However, Eduardo’s lobbying generated a tremendous backlash inside Brazil. Not only did it create a “rally-around-the-flag” effect, bolstering Lula’s polling at a time where the president was facing his lowest ebb in terms of popularity, it brought wide condemnation upon the Bolsonaro family from across the Brazilian political spectrum. Eduardo has since had his mandate as a federal deputy revoked for his lengthy absence. In June this year, he was convicted of attempting to obstruct justice and the trial of his father with his lobbying. Eduardo was quick to brand the Brazilian authorities a “Gestapo” and ask the US government for political asylum. This was quickly granted, in contrast with the way the Trump administration treats genuine refugees, and he now possesses a green card. Eduardo’s antics have done much to hurt his brother’s standing as a presidential candidate. His involvement in the tariffs imposed upon Brazil have significantly tarnished the Bolsonaro name, and with the United States announcing an additional 25 percent tariff on all Brazilian goods in July, the issue remains fresh in the minds of all Brazilians. Making Movies Meanwhile, Flávio Bolsonaro has also been caught up in a bizarre banking scandal. Leaked messages between Flávio and Brazilian banker Daniel Vorcaro showed him asking for almost $25 million to help fund his movie project Dark Horse . The film is a Brazilian-American production, starring American actor Jim Caviezel — previously best known for playing Jesus in The Passion of the Christ — as Jair Bolsonaro. It has not yet been released, but there has already been much talk among the Brazilian public about its casting choices and presumed propagandistic quality. Vorcaro, head of the Master Bank, is currently under investigation for a large and extremely public banking fraud scandal. The origins of the money transferred for the funding of the film — a transfer initially denied by all parties — have been put under heavy scrutiny, not to mention the propriety of a serving senator receiving such money. The leaked messages have revealed deep ties between Flávio and Vorcaro, with the two having a long history of communication. As the investigation into the latter continues, there may be further revelations. The Master Bank is a very popular institution, particularly among working-class people in Brazil, and the case has attracted great public attention, with Vorcaro quickly becoming a vilified figure. Bolsonaro’s messages, asking Vorcaro to cover expenses and addressing him as “brother,” have not helped his image with the public. The family has made a habit of campaigning on anti-corruption platforms, despite its own proliferation of corruption scandals, and Flávio’s presidential campaign is no different, throwing accusations of gross corruption at Lula and the PT. The Master Bank scandal has made it impossible for the younger Bolsonaro to properly campaign on such a platform without inviting charges of hypocrisy. However, that hasn’t stopped him from trying. In a recent rally, Flávio appeared with a shirt displaying the phrase “Pix is from Bolsonaro, Master is from Lula,” attempting to take credit for a very popular direct cashless payment system used in Brazil while placing the blame for the Master scandal with Lula. It seems unlikely that such rhetoric will have any effect, but there is little else Flávio can do to distance himself from this ongoing scandal. Family Quarrel On top of all this, Flávio has recently had a very public falling out with his stepmother, Michelle Bolsonaro, in a family quarrel that threatens to divide the Bolsonarist electoral base. Michelle rose to prominence during her time as First Lady. She is an influential conservative voice among Brazilian women and serves as president of the Liberal Party’s women’s wing, coordinating the campaign of many conservative female candidates. Michelle has long been known to harbor greater political aspirations. She is not only popular with the female electorate, whom she targets specifically in campaigns, but also has strong ties to the powerful evangelical movement in Brazil. Following Jair Bolsonaro’s arrest and conviction, she was also seen as a possible candidate to replace him before the more experienced Flávio was chosen. There have been many reports over the past few years of conflict between Michelle and her stepsons, with the latter claiming she was inexperienced and meddling with fields outside of her scope. Recently this fight has intensified over a possible political alliance which Michelle criticized as being “inconsistent with the interests of the Brazilian Right.” Previously these fights unfolded behind closed doors, but Michelle has recently brought them to the forefront by releasing a twenty-seven-minute-long video on June 25 where she gives her account of the disagreements with Flávio Bolsonaro and the reasons for her clash with the brothers. In the course of the video, she directed many passive-aggressive attacks at the candidate of her party. Presenting the video as being for all the “good women” of Brazil, Michelle positioned herself as a defender of conservative women nationally, praising their “delicate strength.” The language she used stressed her loyalty to the leader (Bolsonaro), with Michelle arguing that she always operated under the direct instructions and with the full endorsement of her husband. She claimed that it was Flávio and other members of the Liberal Party who were going against the interests of the Brazilian right, and urged Flávio to stop talking about her, suggesting that the online gossip was affecting the well-being of her daughter. With the highly publicized and analyzed video, Michelle has created a very public rift between herself (and by extension her own following) and Flávio Bolsonaro. Michelle´s influence among evangelical circles, evident rhetoric skills, direct connection to Bolsonaro, and (so far) clean record make her attacks on Flávio a devastating blow to his campaign. This could be evidence of her own desire to eventually head the Bolsonarist movement herself, as well as to distance herself from what might very well become an electoral sinking ship. Hanging by a Thread Despite being surrounded by scandals, Flávio Bolsonaro continues to head the Liberal Party´s bid for the presidency. There are no other candidates that come even close to his or Lula’s polling figures (all other candidates are below 10 percent, while Flávio and Lula float around the 40 percent mark). This means that the Brazilian right’s only hope of recovering the presidency lies with Flávio’s flailing campaign. Following Brazilian electoral law, national conventions are taking place over the last week of July and the first week of August. It is in these conventions, held separately by the branches of a party from each Brazilian state, where the pre-candidates are usually confirmed by their party delegates. It is noteworthy that Flávio has arrived at such a moment without already having a running mate already selected, something that nearly every other major candidate has taken care of. This may be a sign that noteworthy figures of the Brazilian right and center are attempting to distance themselves from his campaign. Nothing will be official until the conclusion of these separate conventions in early August, but Bolsonaro’s presidential hopes are hanging by a thread. While it would be unprecedented to remove him from the ticket, far crazier things have happened in Brazilian politics. With the Master Bank investigation set to run and run, he may yet become the third member of the Bolsonaro family to face the legal consequences of their actions.
