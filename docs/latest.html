The Big Beautiful Boycott: Starbucks Workers’ Fight for a First Contract

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: The Call ／ 著者: Ian M. ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-10T00:39:44+00:00

After five years of strikes, marches on the boss, petitions, and legal action, Starbucks Workers United (SBWU) called for a boycott in August. Beyond individual coffee drinkers, the union is asking universities, other unions, candidates, and political organizations to sign on to the boycott campaign. Boycotts have a long history in the labor movement but can be risky. They rely on external support, and if they fail to make a dent in the company’s bottom line, they risk undercutting the momentum of the movement. Luckily for SBWU, unions are more popular than ever, and we’ve already seen that plenty of people are ready to throw down to support their baristas. Since 2021, Starbucks workers have been organizing at an impressive rate. Anyone who pays any attention to the labor movement has heard about the rank-and-file energy that has led over 12,000 workers at almost 700 stores to organize unions. This is a movement built on bottom-up militancy. Through a pandemic, two presidencies, four CEOs, and intense company offensives, Starbucks workers have soldiered on. Carried by the energy of the Bernie campaign, Black Lives Matter Movement, and Covid strike wave, the union has continued to grow rapidly. And at key inflection points, it has risen to the political moment, beginning with the labor-friendly Biden administration. Its National Labor Relations Board fast-tracked elections and frequently issued fines and injunctions against the company, helped, but it was reverberations of these consciousness-raising mass movements that actually inspired the workers. According to Eric Blanc’s We Are The Union , in 2022, more than 25% of leaders of new union drives cited BLM, Bernie Sanders, LGBTQ rights, or climate as the primary inspiration for their work. And in October 2023, soon after Israel launched its genocidal campaign in Gaza, SBWU became one of the first unions in the US to call for a ceasefire. Immediately, the company filed a lawsuit, alleging that SBWU was harming its image. The result was an organic consumer boycott so widespread that, in conjunction with a 2024 holiday-season strike at more than 300 stores and several student-led pressure campaigns that cut university contracts with Starbucks, it brought the company to the bargaining table for the first time. Initially, workers secured 33 tentative agreements on a number of issues, but with Trump and his counter-revolution coming to power, the writing was on the wall. The company would walk away in 2025. The 2025 Strike In 2025, while new stores were still organizing, the company was unyielding. That summer, SBWU set about building a strike big enough to make a real dent. Workers started organizing both internally and externally. While stores elected strike captains, the union worked to build a strong network of supporters who could bolster picket lines and take solidarity actions. Reaching out to both individuals and groups, SBWU and DSA came to work closely. DSA, through its National Labor Commission (NLC), already had a strong system of Solidarity Captains. These members were point people in their chapters, with the know-how and the support to activate their comrades in support of workers’ struggles. We on the NLC worked to connect DSA solidarity captains to their local SBWU strike captains and built our own muscles in anticipation of a strike. SBWU often chooses to launch national strikes on Red Cup Day, when Starbucks unveils its limited-edition holiday-themed cup. Every November, it proves to be the company’s highest sales day of the year. Having a concrete timeline was unusual and helpful for DSA. We were able to prepare captains ahead of time with all the information they needed, as well as a variety of support tactics chapters could use. Using DSA’s Labor Solidarity Fund, the Labor Commission ensured that chapters had banners, megaphones, tents, and anything else they might need to support the picket lines. We even worked with a socialist chef to create a strike kitchen menu, enabling over a dozen DSA and YDSA chapters to bring good, hot food to the lines . In Pennsylvania and Nevada, DSA and YDSA chapters mobilized to support mass picketing outside Starbucks’ regional distribution facilities, disrupting shipments of beans and other key supplies across the Northeast and Southwest. For the six-week duration of the strike, over 100 DSA chapters were mobilized. Whether supporting picket lines or talking to baristas and customers at nonunion stores, our nationwide strength was on display. While the company claimed there was no effect on its bottom line, the union’s estimates suggested differently. New stores were inspired to organize. The movement grew. But the company did not fold. A few months later, SBWU returned to the bargaining table after submitting a new, lower wage proposal. Since then, the two sides have been bargaining off and on. The Boycott Now Since August 25th, for the first time, workers themselves are calling on customers to avoid all corporately owned Starbucks locations and products. This means stand-alone brick and mortar stores (excluding those in Target stores, airports, etc.), as well as Starbucks products in grocery stores. Though it’s not yet clear exactly how the boycott will fit in a larger strategy to win a contract, it does build on SBWU’s strengths. To get the word out, Starbucks workers have continued to skillfully use social media, which was critical in the movement’s initial growth. The boycott also functions as an effective structure test for the union: it can test its relationships with other organizations and with elected officials. The first stage of the boycott campaign included reaching out to unions and progressive organizations, including DSA, to pass resolutions in support and ban Starbucks products from their events, encouraging others to join. City councils in Atlanta , St. Louis , and Philadelphia have passed boycott resolutions, and Austin DSA members are now using their positions on the city council to fight for their own. Politicians like NYC DSA’s Claire Valdez have spoken publicly supporting the boycott and for the council to change procurement rules to prevent the city from buying Starbucks products. Undoubtedly, building the boycott will require some action in the real world. While the union isn’t taking many of these steps yet, DSAers should be prepared to organize informational events and rallies alongside baristas in order to spread the word. In some places, we already have a good model. Louisville DSA organized a solidarity rally with SBWU members in early September. According to organizer Galen Z.S., nearly 40 union siblings and DSA members came out in 100+ degree heat to occupy a strip of grass outside a Starbucks. Since that store was having its election a week later, the rally was an opportunity to demonstrate solidarity in the face of intense union-busting. (Though in the end, intimidation and threats led to an 8-7 loss.) Rallies like this one are an example of the multifaceted strategy necessary to take down Starbucks. Worker action on the shop floor can occur alongside local advocacy groups, faith communities, and allies in other unions. When CEO Brian Niccol sees an unbroken wall of social opposition to drinking Starbucks coffee and 12,000+ union baristas acting in concert on the shop floor, the power built will be formidable. What Will It Take? Starbucks is a massive company. While SBWU represents more than 700 stores, there are over 10,000 corporately owned Starbucks locations in the United States. There are many thousands more workers to organize and elections to win, but a contract could be won before then. Whether it’s necessary to settle for a less lucrative first contract to temper company opposition or win a stronger contract to show workers that their organizing was worth it is still an open question . Either outcome depends on the ability to keep applying pressure through frequent shop floor actions, effective strikes, and a mass boycott movement. All this must contend with high turnover: at many unionized stores, it’s not uncommon to see an entirely new set of workers just a year after an election. Socialists have much to learn from this organizing drive, and an important role to play if it is going to succeed. While Starbucks may not be the “commanding heights of industry,” SBWU is a real breakthrough in organizing the service sector, a large and growing portion of the U.S. economy. And Starbucks organizing drives have had a “spillover effect,” inspiring workers in other nearby workplaces to organize themselves. According to a study from Colorado State, nearly 20% of the nationwide increase in union elections since 2021 can be attributed to Starbucks organizing drive “spillovers,” with counties that saw Starbucks elections showing a 175% increase in union elections. And while union baristas don’t always stick around for long, they will take their class struggle experience with them for the rest of their lives. An Effective AI Pushback Has to Go Beyond the Local

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Aziz Huq ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-09T22:01:55+00:00

Last week’s election returns embodied a broad, national, and bipartisan reaction against data centers. In forty-six of the forty-seven local referenda held across some thirteen states, voters rejected new data center construction. Public opposition reflects local concerns about water usage, grid sustainability, and noise pollution. This opposition is also bubbling up at a moment when most of the public distrust s artificial intelligence (AI) in general, when two-thirds of Americans hold negative views of AI firms and their close ties to the Trump administration, and when fears about AI rank high among Americans’ leading worries. This confluence of AI-adjacent fears is no accident. Each anxious spark leaps from a different facet of a single Silicon Valley strategy for hyperscaling AI. If this strategy works, it would induce a dramatic decline in labor’s income share of income. If it fails, it would saddle the state with crushing mountains of debt. Voters, to be sure, likely perceive this dilemma only weakly. Yet their vivid fears create a chance for the Left to articulate a comprehensive diagnosis of hyperscale AI’s manifold problems — one that not only affirms the public’s fears but also articulates a coherent response. And just as the Silicon Valley strategy runs on a logic of scale, so too an effectual pushback must escalate beyond the local. Heads, AI Wins. Tails, You Lose. Generative AI is being developed by a small handful of firms. They are taking on massive amounts of debt to build computing power on which costly new tools can be offered at scale. Given the size of these investments, the firms are commercially viable only if they sell their wares at scale. This means either tapping hidden wellsprings of productivity growth ( unlikely ) or being used as a low-marginal-cost substitute for white-collar labor (more likely). The ensuing debt-fueled displacement of white-collar labor, if successful, will induce a massive shift of income from labor to capital, as even the AI firms concede . The ruination of ecosystems and acceleration of the climate crisis are mere side effects. There is strong historical evidence of technology’s corrosive effect on the labor share: recent data from Europe suggests that AI, no less than other recent tech leaps, causes losses to labor and corresponding gains to capital. Worse, because most democracies tax capital at lower rates than labor, that shift will constrict the state, diminishing its fiscal capacity to cushion job losses and declining wages. In effect, hyperscale AI creates a distributional crisis and robs the state of tools to address it. Of course, this strategy might not work. AI might not be transformative as its boosters claim. Markets may lose their nerve before profits can be reaped. But if the AI strategy isn’t successful, the leading firms now dominant in public equity markets (and hence our pensions and 401[k]s), will almost certainly demand a “rescue” akin to the 2008–9 federal financial-crisis interventions. As of August, hyperscale AI firms had accrued more than $500 million in debt from private equity, banks, and institutional investors such as Blackrock and Pimco. If that debt sours, the repercussions of the crisis, as in 2008–9, would likely force the government’s hand. If that happens, a massive transfer of risk to public balance sheets will follow. This is capitalism as a “heads I win, tails you lose” proposition. Whether or not it founders, hyperscale AI capitalism will cleave open immense lost opportunities for society at large. On one estimate , US AI investments will add up to some $10.3 trillion between 2025 and 2032, or an average of 3.63 percent of US gross domestic product per year. This is $10 trillion that will not and cannot be spend on maternal care, pre-K education, cancer treatment, environmental mitigation, or any other social programs and planetary reparative work we desperately need. It is, by any measure, a staggering divergence of investment away from areas of urgent moral priority. No wonder, then, that the American public has such profound anxieties. The data center build-outs are simply the most apparent of the many economic, political, fiscal, and environmental crises that hyperscale AI is inducing. Building Toward a National Response What then might be an effectual response? Except in Harwood, North Dakota, voters have pushed back at a local level by denying construction permission, or in Perry, Ohio, demanding that firms post a bond as insurance against the risk of fiscal implosion. Localities’ responses are circumscribed, though, by their limited powers and their small geographic reach. Cities and counties can do little about the labor market effects or fiscal risk. Many environmental costs associated with new demands on the electric grid are out of their reach. Some cities such as Austin and San Antonio own and operate their own electrical utilities, giving them a lever that doesn’t exist elsewhere in the country. But these are exceptions. Solely local responses are usually tightly constrained. Since the effects of hyperscale AI capitalism emerge at the national level, it would ideally be the federal government that drives a comprehensive response. Pessimism about the prospect of federal action is wise for now. After Tuesday’s election, Trump reiterated his belief that data centers are “gold . . . only foolish leadership will say NO.” His December 2025 executive order , barring states from regulating AI, remains in force, although the legal theories upon which it rests are flawed. Even if a Democratic-controlled House and Senate can overcome his veto, any resulting AI legislation will likely be weak thanks to the need to assemble supermajorities to ensure bills’ passage. The difficulty of federal action, however, does not mean that progressives should give up addressing hyperscale AI capitalism. They still have ways to use both their control at the state level and at the regional level to highlight its costsand offer the beginnings of a commensurate response — indeed, one that might be fully nationalized in two years. In January, Democrats will have “trifectas” in twenty-two states and hold the governor’s office in seven more (Kansas and Alaska are still not called as I write). This is a first platform for action. To date, states’ responses have been reactive and purely negative: New York’s and Oregon’s governors paused data center construction, while laws imposing moratoria have been introduced but not passed in sixteen states. Advocates of successful local ballot initiatives have indicated that they plan to turn to bans in their states. Yet a pause is not a policy. It does nothing to communicate the complex and interconnected harms of hyperscale AI capitalism. So it leaves the field open for AI’s advocates to advance their tall tales of illimitable gain. Nor does it prevent the building of data centers elsewhere in the nation and so does nothing to rein in its fiscal and labor costs. An alternate approach would be for states to force tech firms to not just account for the local effects of data centers but also recognize and take responsibility for all the social costs of their hyperscale strategy. It would seek to channel economic development into less costly, more decent forms. Bans, however, are a badly formed tool to this subtle end. As a start, states could recognize their own complicity in hyperscale AI. In forty states, data centers benefit from exemptions from the sales and use taxes that would ordinarily accrue on building materials, machinery, and equipment. The nominal justification for such exemptions is the jobs new builds purportedly bring. But a data center at best tends to create only two or three hundred jobs, with no long-term effect on wages. Where a landlord like Equinix, Data Realty, or CyrusOne rents computing power to remote clients, even that meager gain goes unrealized. States’ and localities’ ensuing revenue loss is hard to measure , because only some of the states with these tax exemptions report their fiscal effect. Texas, however, lost some $1 billion in FY2025; in 2024, Illinois lost $370 million; and Arizona lost $19 million. A simple first step is terminating all these tax exemptions on the ground that the local benefits from data centers have been illusory, following Maine’s example. That might also prompt states to reconsider more generally their blind pursuit of corporate investments through tax relief. Having withdrawn their reckless subsidies, states should follow Perry, Ohio’s lead by forcing tech firms to bear the social costs of their speculative investments. New construction, for example, might be conditioned on bonds tied to the environmental and state-level labor market effects of their business models. Firms would first be required to report not only on expected environmental harms (including from increased energy demand) but also address whether a data center’s expected uses will displace human labor or otherwise have wage-suppressive effects (as a leading study has already found). It would then have to post bonds sufficient to pay for state investments to buffer those effects. The law might impel an AI-funded guarantee fund to sustain state unemployment payouts and retraining costs generated by AI. States could also require that data center investments be backed by sufficient nondebt financing to mitigate any risk of catastrophic fiscal events. And even if data centers are not fully banned, states might simply target specific uses as verboten — say, the large-scale automation of American jobs. Firms that flout this would see their centers shut down. At the same time, states need not act alone. An underappreciated part of the American constitutional order is groups of states’ latent authority to enter into binding “ compacts .” These are treaty-like agreements, often created without Congress’s approval, to coordinate regulation between a pool of states. Existing compacts address drivers’ licenses, insurance coverage, and medical practice. They allow states to set uniform standards and realize regulation at scale, even if the national government is AWOL. Imagine Texas, Virginia, California, Pennsylvania, and New York forging an interstate compact on AI and data centers. No firm would be permitted to operate data centers in any state unless they agreed to meet certain environmental standards and avoid use cases that threatened serious job loss. The compacting states could require a common set of disclosures — not only about the data centers themselves but also about the fiscal and labor market risks associated with AI more generally, and perhaps about other AI-related harms. Unlike simple bans, this arrangement is more likely to be politically self-sustaining. Firms would, in effect, have to document on an ongoing basis the knot of harms that flow from hyperscale AI — hence supplying the grounds for their own regulation. And they could not simply blame states for quashing economic growth. A race to the bottom as firms tried to pit states against each other would be more difficult. It is true that hyperscale AI capitalists are likely to flee from compacting states. But notice that the creation of a robust compact would limit their flight options, at least within the United States. To see this, consider the political costs to a red state governor who welcomes fleeing data centers that decline to comply with environmental and labor market protections. Recall that some 89 percent of Americans oppose data center siting near them. Isn’t it likely that the governor will meet the same fate as other erstwhile champions of AI, such as outgoing Texas Governor Greg Abbott? Compacting to clarify AI’s harms makes it politically more difficult for noncompacting states to embrace the industry. No doubt, these solutions are incomplete. But they offer a pathway for dealing with not just the local but also the national harms of hyperscale AI in a politically durable way. They not only signal clearly that voters’ concerns have been heard but also help build virtuous circles of disclosure, action, and revenue so the perils of hyperscale AI can be enduringly addressed — a groundwork that offers a foundation for national action when the time is right. Metro DC DSA Condemns Councilmember Christina Henderson’s Attempted Power Grab and Disregard for DC Home Rule Date: November 8, 2026 Media Contact: For all press inquiries, please contact media@mdcdsa.org . Washington, DC: Metro DC Democratic Socialists of America condemns At-Large Councilmember Christina Henderson’s cynical attempt to manipulate the District’s Home Rule laws for her own political advantage, undermining the democratic process and distracting from the urgent challenges facing working-class Washingtonians. Details: In 2020, Henderson switched her party affiliation from Democrat to independent to run for an At-Large Council seat. Now, following Councilmember Robert White’s resignation, Henderson attempted to reverse that decision, potentially depriving the DC Democratic Party of its authority to appoint an interim replacement and shifting that power to the DC Council. When the DC Democratic Party moved to exercise its appointment authority, selecting Jamal Holtz before Henderson’s registration change took effect, Henderson raised the prospect of legal action. Only after her maneuver failed did she reverse course and re-register as an independent. Analysis: Home Rule is not a political tool to be invoked when convenient and discarded when inconvenient. It is the foundation of the District’s limited right to govern itself, a right generations of Washingtonians have fought to secure against congressional interference. Henderson’s actions have all the appearances of a naked political power grab, seemingly designed to strengthen her position for a potential challenge to the Council chairmanship. Whatever her ambitions, Washingtonians deserve better than elected officials exploiting party registration rules to advance their political careers. This spectacle is particularly offensive at a moment when DC residents face an escalating affordability crisis. Working families are struggling with rising rents, the cost of groceries, and underfunded public services. Instead of addressing these urgent needs, Henderson has manufactured a political controversy over who gets to fill a temporary vacancy on the Council. The timing could hardly be worse. As the District enters a new political era, with renewed threats to our autonomy and an expanded National Guard presence in our streets, our elected officials should be demonstrating the seriousness and resolve necessary to defend DC residents against federal interference. Instead, Henderson’s political maneuvering makes our local government appear unserious, irresponsible, and consumed by personal ambition. Bottom Line: The irony of Henderson invoking Home Rule to defend her actions should not be lost on anyone. The District’s right to self-government belongs to its residents, not to politicians seeking to manipulate the rules for personal advantage. The DC Democratic Party’s authority to appoint an interim replacement is established under District law, and voters will have the opportunity to elect their next At-Large Councilmember in a special election. Metro DC DSA supports respecting the established appointment process while reaffirming our commitment to full DC statehood, which would eliminate the undemocratic congressional restrictions on our local government that helped create this controversy. Councilmember Henderson should abandon these political games and return her attention to the people she was elected to serve. Washingtonians need leaders fighting for affordable housing, strong public schools, fully funded public services, and genuine democratic self-determination, not politicians more interested in their next promotion than the needs of their constituents. Our city deserves a government that takes itself seriously, respects the will of its voters, and puts working people first. End the Private Health Insurance Tax: Pass Medicare for All

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Tim Higginbotham ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-09T18:18:50+00:00

Every other Monday, I arrive at work bleary-eyed, still finishing my coffee, and settle into the workweek by reviewing my latest pay stub. I’m looking for miscounted hours, abnormal deductions — the sorts of mistakes that never happen but could. This morning was different. I spent the weekend fixating on the sorry state of health insurance in this country, roused in large part by Democrats’ comprehensive midterm victory, including wins by supporters of Medicare for All like Troy Jackson and Abdul El-Sayed. I arrived at my desk alert and reviewed my pay stub, focusing specifically on health care contributions. When we look at our pay stubs, employer and employee health insurance contributions combine into a number we numbly accept as we focus on our take-home pay. In my case, the biweekly total is $1,267. That adds up to about $33,000 over the year, higher than the average employer-sponsored family plan cost of $28,303 . Single-coverage plans average about $9,000, while workers buying on the marketplace pay upward of $7,500 annually ($12,000 in my home state of Alaska). These figures represent real, consequential lost wages. And they get higher every year. Health Insurance Premiums Are a Tax I have a stable union job with my local municipal government. My health insurance leaves a lot to be desired, often burdening my family with out-of-pocket costs (a reality brought home just last week during an emergency room visit for our daughter, who thankfully is fine). Still, it is better than what most American workers have. I am meant to feel lucky for it. Look at the alternatives: I could have a lesser plan with higher deductibles and out-of-pocket limits. I could have no insurance at all, like roughly 10 percent of Americans. Or scarier still, I could be forced to pay taxes into a single-payer system that removes health care from the workplace and covers everyone equally. But staring at a pay stub leaves us to wonder: What are health insurance premiums if not a tax? They’re compulsory; we can’t opt out. And even if we could, we would never expose ourselves to such catastrophic risk. The deduction is listed separately from federal taxes on the pay stub, but what makes it different? The answer is who gets the money. Our health care premiums are nothing other than a compulsory tax paid to private insurance companies instead of the federal government. When included with our other taxes, they make the United States one of the highest-taxed countries in the world — higher even than the Nordic states . The United States spends roughly 18 percent of its GDP on health care, double that of peer nations with universal health care systems. The oft-stated benefit of our system is that it saves us from taxes, but in fact it taxes us at a premium and sends the excess to insurers as profit. Worse, it burdens us with impossible out-of-pocket costs at our times of greatest need. Our paychecks suffer from this waste. Rising premiums help keep our wages stuck as employers weigh insurance and wages against the same bottom line. Every dollar diverted to surging premiums is a dollar stolen from our wages and benefits. All the while, workers shoulder ever-increasing deductibles and contribution shares. Your Paycheck Under Medicare for All You have heard, of course, that single-payer health insurance — Medicare for All — will cost trillions of dollars. It will raise your taxes. It will take away your hard-won and much-beloved employer-sponsored insurance. It’s tempting to argue the obvious: yes, Medicare for All will raise your taxes, but the increase in your taxes will be significantly lower than your current premiums and out-of-pocket costs, which will disappear. Looking at my pay stub, however, I don’t even want to concede that it will raise taxes. Again, payroll insurance contributions are a compulsory, unavoidable tax on your wages . Under Bernie Sanders’s Medicare for All proposal, my employer (a budget-strapped municipal government) would pay $260 per paycheck through a 7.5 percent payroll tax, and I would pay $94 via a 4 percent income tax after standard deductions. The combined total of $354 represents $913 in savings — $23,738 over a year. Medicare for All will reconfigure your taxes, sure. But it won’t raise them. It will lower them. The $24,000 newly freed from my paycheck would land on the bargaining table in negotiations over my union’s next contract. Some of this will go directly to wage increases. Some will go to benefits. Some will stay with the municipal government. For all future negotiations, though, we will not have to tackle the albatross of rising insurance premiums. What Is Health Insurance For? Health care is expensive, costing trillions per year no matter who pays. Currently, it’s paid for in a convoluted, wasteful scheme: private insurers act as payers using funds taken from our wages, while the government covers its share in Medicare and Medicaid. The rest is paid out-of-pocket by patients in times of need. The effect is to chain us to our jobs, suppress our wages, and burden our negotiations. We all know this is not what health insurance is for. Health insurance is meant to minimize risk, minimize costs, and mitigate catastrophes. This can only be accomplished with a single, government payer. A true tax should fund a public good to benefit society, not drain your wages to benefit profiteers. With an overwhelming mandate and the policy still popular , Democrats ought to run a Medicare for All supporter in 2028 and make it their first priority in the new Congress. The policy will save up to a trillion dollars annually in overall spending. You can look at your pay stub to see where those savings will land. RECAP: 2026 Member Mobilization Institute ONAWAY, MI – UAW members from across the country gathered at Black Lake for the 2026 Member Mobilization Institute, a week of learning new skills, sharing ideas, and exploring how we can strengthen our union together. Workshops, discussions, and hands-on practice challenged participants to consider what it takes to build lasting power and to prepare for the opportunities and challenges ahead, including May Day 2028. Learning continued beyond the classroom as delegates embraced opportunities to share their own worker stories by creating art with block printing, bandanas, signs, buttons, photography, and zines. Creativity became a powerful organizing tool, helping participants communicate their message, connect with others, and find their entry point in the movement. From inspiring art exhibits to a memorable evening that blended live music and labor history, delegates experienced firsthand how culture helps strengthen our collective action. Impeachment Could Become A Powerful Tool

Some Democrats are threatening to impeach Donald Trump again in January when they have the majority in the House and Senate But enough Republicans will remain to block conviction since they will constitute more than one third of the Senate. Perhaps the third time would be the charm, since some Republican politicians are distancing themselves from him. But a successful impeachment probably needs to be led by Republicans. If initiated by Democrats, many Republicans would argue that the impeachment is just another partisan attack. However Democrats should not abandon all thoughts of impeachment. Rather than impeaching Donald Trump, however, they should impeach his more outrageous subordinates beginning with Pete Hegseth, Todd Blanche, Russell Vought, and Robert F. Kennedy Jr. Regrettably, Stephen Miller--- Trump’s new chief of staff--- is not considered a “civil officer” of the U.S. and therefore probably cannot be impeached. Representative Thomas Massie (R-Kentucky) may introduce a resolution to impeach Hegseth this week, and it may well pass the House even before Democrats have taken over because many Republicans can’t stand him. A president’s associates, as we will see, are more likely to be convicted by the required two-thirds Senate majority than he is. My Ph.D. dissertation examined the role of the king or queen in six constitutional monarchies. The history of Great Britain, the Netherlands, Belgium, Norway, Sweden, and Denmark indicates that removing a leader’s subordinates can be a powerful way to control what that hereditary leader is doing. Related: One Sided View of Preposterous Lawsuit At PBS The old idea that “the king can do no wrong” persists in these countries despite the fact that they are democracies. These apparently conflicting facts are reconciled by requiring that royal decisions must have the “countersignature” of a minister who can be held accountable and ousted by the parliament. These countries thereby enjoy a strict separation of two roles. The monarch is ceremonial head of state. The prime minister is head of government. The monarch is theoretically all-powerful, but actual decisions are made by the cabinet under the leadership of the prime minister. The American presidency combines the two roles--- head of state and head of government--- in a single official. But like constitutional monarchs, the president, acting individually, has little actual power. Presidential decisions will have no real consequences unless they are carried out by subordinates--- cabinet members and the other people employed by their various departments. The analogy with the European monarchies, like all analogies, is not an exact one. In the United States, cabinet members are not picked by Congress and cannot be members of Congress. But thanks to the impeachment power set forth in the Constitution, cabinet members can be removed by Congress. And the Senate can block replacements put forward by the president. Our federal Constitution has no provisions for recalling the president. To the extent that American presidents are politically immune from removal by impeachment, they are therefore much like the unelected European monarchs. But the same limitation does not apply to impeachment of that president’s advisors and staff. Related: A Major Political Question: To Whom Did God Give Land? It will be easier to convince two-thirds of the Senate to convict mere presidential advisors than to convict the president. Impeachment supporters from the president’s party can argue that their loyalty to the president is firm but that the advisor has been giving the president bad advice. It would therefore be harder for a president to threaten to “primary” senators who, professing support for him, simply vote to remove the advisor. Removing an advisor would give the president both a motive and a face-saving way to reverse policies that the removed official had been administering. Although Harry Truman’s desk sign that “the buck stops here” would remain true, the advisor could be the scapegoat for problems that really were the president’s responsibility. And critics of the policy should be happy to see it reversed, even if the ultimately guilty president remains in office. Impeaching presidential advisors could become an important part of American government despite its repeated failure to remove presidents. It would strengthen the hand of Congress, today a weakened body despite the intent of those who wrote the Constitution to make it the strongest branch of government. It is not impossible for a president’s party to turn on him, as Richard Nixon found out. But the future importance of impeachment may lie mainly in its use to remove presidential advisors and, hopefully, reverse policies with which they have been associated.

California UFCW Leaders Really Don’t Want Union Elections

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Lisa Xu ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-09T16:09:28+00:00

In a union that has been headed by one family with no competitive elections since 1984, a group of grocery workers is organizing for change. But last week, they say, union staff who back the incumbent administration went to outrageous lengths in an attempt to make sure there’s no election this year either. On the afternoon of Monday, November 2, Eric Marcuz, a veteran Northern California Safeway worker, stopped at the union office to pick up blank petitions to get on the ballot in the union’s upcoming election. Marcuz is running for president on the At What Cost slate in the 23,000-member United Food and Commercial Workers (UFCW) Local 8–Golden State (8-GS), one of the largest locals in the union. To get on the ballot, Marcuz and his slate-mates have to collect 443 petition signatures — 2 percent of the local’s active members — by mid-November. If they don’t get enough signatures, no election will be held. After Marcuz picked up the petitions from the local’s Roseville office, he got in his car to drive three hours south to Fresno, to start collecting signatures with another candidate on his slate at a Save Mart store there. (The local spans a ten-hour stretch from Bakersfield up to the Oregon border.) When Marcuz stopped for gas, he said he noticed that a black Dodge Ram pickup stopped as well. When he got back on the freeway, so did the truck. At the next gas stop, Marcuz spoke to the man driving the truck. It was Jeff Berns, a staff representative for Local 8-GS. His brother, Mark, is also on staff at the union — and the local’s election chair, responsible for overseeing the election. Marcuz asked Jeff Berns why he was following him. According to Marcuz, Berns said he wanted to make sure that all the members got the “accurate information” that Marcuz’s slate was going to “divide” the union. He said he was going to follow him the entire time Marcuz was out, all the way back to Marcuz’s house in Redding, several hours north. Marcuz jokingly asked him if he needed a place to stay. Berns said he didn’t — because he’d been staying a lot in the Redding area lately himself, which Marcuz said he thought was “creepy” because that’s where Marcuz lives with his family (and is not where Berns lives). Berns would live up to his promise — following Marcuz for the next three hundred miles, through every stop, exit, and U-turn. He even followed him inside the Fresno Save Mart and camped out for an hour with another union rep while Marcuz ate dinner at a supporter’s house one hundred miles north in Modesto. No Competitive Elections At the gas station, Berns repeated the mantra, “Solidarity works!” That’s the motto of the incumbent president, Jacques Loveall, who is heading the Solidarity Works Team slate in this election. Loveall was appointed to lead the union in 2004, when his father, Jack Loveall, stepped down. The elder Loveall had held the top office since 1984; when he retired he was one of the highest-paid union officers in the country, earning the equivalent of $1 million today. (The Contra Costa Times reported that he had a $28,000-a-year country club membership and “two pilots on staff to chauffeur him in a private jet.”) Neither father nor son has ever faced a competitive election in the local. Back on the freeway, Marcuz called a Department of Labor agent whom he had already been in touch with. They had previously spoken about the local’s apparent attempt to cancel the election by merging with a mysterious organization called the Staffing Agencies Federated Employees, an entity that listed one of Loveall’s relatives as its president on its initial filing. The agent told him to report the intimidation to the election chair and tell him to get the rep to back off. Marcuz then called Mark Berns, the election chair, who he said told him he would talk to staff about acting in a professional manner. More Reps However, when Marcuz arrived at the Save Mart store in Fresno to begin collecting petition signatures, he and another slatemate, Mike Romero, said that three union reps were waiting for them, including Jeff Berns. One of the others was John Micheletti, a longtime Local 8 vice president. Far from being called off, they said, the intimidation had actually intensified. Marcuz and Romero said the reps interfered with every conversation they attempted to have with workers about signing their nomination petition. The very first worker they engaged about signing, a longtime acquaintance of Romero’s, fled once a rep entered the conversation and became confrontational. The worker did not sign the petition. “They were everywhere inside the store,” said Marcuz. At least one of the interactions was filmed on a phone. Marcuz said that Berns told him, “I’m going to make sure everyone knows who you are and not to sign that.” And additionally, “We’re going to invalidate every single signature that you get.” It is illegal for union staff and officers to campaign on union time. Berns said he was on vacation. Romero, who has worked at Save Mart for thirty years, said, “This is the most I’ve seen of union staff in six months.” He said a fourth rep turned up after Marcuz left. Seen This Before These tactics — following opposition candidates and invalidating signatures — have both been alleged by previous members of the local who attempted to challenge the Lovealls. A Contra Costa Times article published in 2004 reported that supporters of Loveall’s father got members to rescind their signatures to keep dissident candidate Jack Nordby off the ballot in 1997. A book written by then Local 588 reform activist Doug Slaydon states that as many as 150 signatures were removed. (Local 588 merged with another local to form Local 8-GS in 2006 — postponing a potential competitive election that year.) Nordby also alleged that he was tailed by a pickup truck while collecting ballot signatures, followed around by union reps in stores, and once chased in his car while driving his wife and two daughters to church. The Contra Costa Times also reported allegations that Local 588 staffers staked out the home of a member of UFCW Local 1179 who had led a successful rank-and-file opposition campaign to an attempted merger of the two locals and “tailed him in union cars.” “In June 1994,” reported the Times , the member, Michael Ross-Smith, “was rear-ended in Walnut Creek by three UFCW 588 business agents following him too closely in a white 1993 Ford Thunderbird leased to the union, according to a traffic collision report from the Walnut Creek police department.” Tailed for Seven Hours Marcuz and Romero said they quickly realized that they wouldn’t be able to collect petition signatures with this level of interference. Marcuz’s next stop was going to be to drop off petition forms with a supporter at the Turlock Safeway, over an hour to the north, but he made plans to meet the supporter at their house in Modesto instead. When Marcuz left the parking lot of the Fresno store, Romero said three vehicles followed him. Romero also realized that one of the reps had stayed behind, and he became worried that he would be followed as well. He drove around for a long time before going home, to make sure he wasn’t followed. Marcuz, meanwhile, said that Berns and Micheletti followed him for ninety miles, all the way to Modesto. They stayed parked outside the house while Marcuz ate burritos with the supporter — finally leaving after an hour, after 8 p.m. “It’s crazy that they would literally go two hours out of their way to make sure nobody else can talk to the employees,” Romero said. Marcuz said he almost canceled his plans to stay alone in a hotel in Roseville overnight, worried he would be tailed there, and contemplated driving for four more hours home to Redding. In total, he estimates he was followed for seven hours, over a distance of three hundred miles. Scare Tactics Section 610 of the Labor-Management Reporting and Disclosure Act (LMRDA) forbids the threat or use of force or violence to restrain, coerce, or intimidate union members from exercising their right to nominate candidates or to vote in union officer elections, while Section 401(e) bars “improper interference” in the rights of union members to run for and support candidates for local union office. “It makes me think of everyone that has known there’s been a need for change in the past and has been scared away from pursuing it,” Marcuz said. While Marcuz and his slate are standing strong, he said, “this might work. They might intimidate and scare enough people out of participating.” In the morning, Marcuz filed a police report with the Roseville Police Department alleging stalking and harassment, citing the time and distance he was followed and the behavior he encountered in the Fresno store. Local 8 did not respond to requests for comment. Petition signatures are due November 17, and ballots will be mailed November 21. Young people are taking to the streets across France, Italy, Belgium, India, Serbia, and Albania, refusing to surrender their futures to a capitalist system that has abandoned them or to a politics that Naomi Klein and Astra Taylor have described as “end times fascism.” These protests are fueled by mounting anger over deteriorating schools, economic insecurity, unaffordable housing, political corruption, and a ruling class increasingly indifferent to the needs and voices of the young. As far right governments in Europe, the United States, and Latin America cancel out a future plagued by endless wars, climate catastrophe, staggering inequality, deepening poverty, and deteriorating public institutions, young people are rethinking the language of politics, resistance, and hope. A 21-year-old student protesting in Milan captured this spirit of resistance in an interview with Reuters , declaring: “It’s vital to take part in people’s spontaneous movements born from an explosion of rage that made collective action possible.” She added, “I think the worst damage this ruling class has done to my generation is stealing even the ability to imagine a future, to dream.” Rather than address these grievances, governments have increasingly responded with curfews, protest bans , and brutal police repression, treating the democratic aspirations of young people as threats to be crushed rather than demands for justice to be heard. These uprisings are driven by more than economic grievances. They express a growing refusal to accept the moral and political cruelty of a system that has declared the young disposable. At their core is a recognition that neoliberal capitalism has robbed an entire generation of the promise of a better future and that austerity is class warfare disguised as economic necessity. Carlotta Magoga , writing in European Democracy Hub, is right in stating that, “From Serbia to Bangladesh and Senegal, young people are becoming a driving force for a more democratic world. … In search of alternative routes, they are developing new forms of civic engagement — through artistic protest, online campaigns and storytelling — and rewriting the rules of political participation.” Through their courageous presence in the streets, they are fashioning a form of activism “as a way to rethink how democracy works.” For example, in France, students have challenged overcrowded classrooms, teacher shortages, and a university admissions system that reinforces class inequality. In Italy, students have joined workers in protests over low wages, unaffordable housing , deteriorating public services, and increased military spending. In Genoa, port workers and students have also protested the use of ports for arms shipments . In Serbia, where opposition to government corruption has been central, students have demanded investigations into public contracts, government spending, and the political control of public institutions. These movements do not all embrace an explicitly anti-capitalist agenda, but their demands expose conflicts over who controls public resources, whose interests governments serve, and how economic and political power is organized and maintained. Capitalism, in one of its more disingenuous moments, celebrates youth rhetorically as the future but denies youth the resources to have a future. What governments fear about these revolts is that students are moving beyond demands to make capitalism more humane. Protests across Europe and beyond are challenging gangster capitalism as a political order marked by corruption, neoliberal austerity, authoritarianism, militarism, racism, and the concentration of wealth and political power in the hands of a financial elite. At the heart of this confrontation is a struggle over the moral and political values that will govern society and determine the fate of future generations. Two radically opposed moral visions are confronting one another on the world stage. One is rooted in solidarity, justice, and democratic possibility; the other in greed, racial hatred, organized brutality, and authoritarian domination. What is at stake is the future of democracy and whether coming generations will inherit a world governed by human dignity or one surrendered to the cruelty of the powerful. To more and more people, the promises of sex-positive liberal feminism feel empty. The sexual ideas that have dominated feminism in the last two decades — including that sexual liberation always serves women’s interests, that consent is the only relevant sexual ethic, and that consuming porn and buying sex are benign practices like any other — are beginning to ring hollow. In this context, an emergent theoretical strand with the pointed, self-chosen name “reactionary feminism” has drawn considerable attention. It calls into question the liberal assertion that every form of sexual morality is anti-feminist — a view the Left has absorbed in recent decades. Although they work to maintain an appearance of ideological ambivalence, reactionary feminists are aligned with the post-liberal right , and they are finding success in attracting to that project women who are disenchanted with feminism’s wholesale embrace of sexual openness for its own sake. Their prescriptions are often at odds with what many feminists would consider emancipatory, but they are identifying real flaws in liberal feminism, and the Left should take notice. At the forefront of this new conservative feminism are British authors Mary Harrington and Louise Perry. Both have written popular books on how postwar sexual liberation in the West has failed women. In her 2022 book The Case Against the Sexual Revolution , Perry argues that shifting cultural norms around casual sex have led to a hookup culture that frustrates many women — even if they endorse these norms politically. In Perry’s telling, this is no wonder: as rigid sexual morality has receded, a liberal feminist paradigm has taken its place, one that de-exceptionalizes sex and portrays it as inconsequential. With contraception and a sexual culture in which casual sex doesn’t require emotional commitments, why would we need to have special guardrails around sex? For straight women on the dating market, this often manifests as feeling simultaneously sexually pursued and emotionally neglected. Many liberal women are responding to this dilemma with heteropessimism, or a bleak outlook on male-female pairings altogether. While reactionary feminism indulges its own form of gender fatalism (I return to this below), Perry is suggesting that these disappointed women look elsewhere: the problem is not heterosexuality but a culture of sexual casualness pursued erroneously in the name of progress. By making implicit liberal assumptions about casual sex and social progress explicit, Perry taps into intuitions most people share: for instance, that the expectation of emotional commitment after having sex is not too much to ask, or that sex can be a site of exceptional vulnerability and risk. Her willingness to say these things aloud has earned her an audience eager to hear something more nuanced than slogans about sexual autonomy. Frustration With Liberal Sexual Culture In academic feminism, sex is often presented as a banal activity, like going on a walk together. What happens in the course of sex is equivalent to deciding to go down this path or climb up this hill — with both people able to say at any time that they would like to turn around and go back home. This neutralized view of sex might appear emancipatory, promising as it does to overturn the sexual scripts and social stigmas that hold up the status quo. But unfortunately, it doesn’t align with actual experience. The vulnerability and boundary crossing involved in sex can have far-reaching consequences if someone takes advantage of them. For most people, the stakes of sex going well or poorly are thus much higher than those of going on a walk, even when people consider it casual. The broad Left’s consensus on this view of sex poses a dilemma for many women. If I’m expecting emotional engagement from a man after a hookup, does that secretly make me “trad” and a conservative? If monogamy fosters moralism and possessiveness in sexuality, does that not make nonmonogamy far more feminist? The success of Perry’s book, which came out when #MeToo was still relatively fresh, and there was cultural space to consider the potential downsides of sexual liberalism, suggested that many women had been privately entertaining these questions for years. Perry shined a light on the gap between political endorsement of goals like sexual freedom and conflicted attitudes about what that actually looks like in real life. There is no doubt that being “sexually liberated” — expressed, for instance, by engaging in nonmonogamy and kink or BDSM — has accrued social status and moral value among progressive feminists. Countless self-help books and articles about ethical nonmonogamy have come out in recent years. It seems simply cool to be chill enough to have an open relationship and not to really want someone to commit only to you. The alternative of wanting a committed romantic partner is, by contrast, conservative and uncool. Last year, writer Chanté Joseph went viral with a Vogue article that aptly captured this dynamic and asked, “Is having a boyfriend embarrassing now?” One of the women Joseph quotes says that having a boyfriend feels “Republican.” A hard blow for romantics. At the same time, it has also become cool to enjoy more physically violent sex. We can see this most evidently in the mainstreaming of “kinky sex” in popular culture since Fifty Shades of Grey . Data backs this up. If you’re a woman under twenty-four, the chance that your partner choked you during your last sexual encounter is about one in three, according to a representative survey by Indiana University researcher Debby Herbenick. Among millennial women (age twenty-five to thirty-nine), the figure was only 11 percent, and for women above the age of forty, it was 3 percent. This is an immense shift in sexual behavior across a single generation. In interviews with psychologist Nicola Gavey, young women who experienced such “rough sex” said they felt pressured by current sexual norms to make themselves want it in order not to seem boring or prudish. The reality of these parallel cultural developments is what makes Perry’s critique of liberal sexual culture more complicated than closed-minded conservative moralism. Marxist Rhetoric Reactionary feminists are not hesitant to offer women well-established conservative solutions for sexual liberalism’s discontents: refrain from casual sex, don’t get drunk with strange men around, get married. But they wrap these solutions in new leftist rhetoric. For many liberal and leftist feminists, marriage is a historically sexist institution aimed at controlling women and property. For Mary Harrington, by contrast, marriage enables “radical solidarity between the sexes.” It acts “as the smallest possible unit of resistance to overwhelming economic, cultural and political pressure to be lone atoms in a market,” as she writes in her 2023 book Feminism Against Progress . Rather than striving to be a girlboss or buying yourself flowers, Harrington proposes an attractive vision of monogamous marriage as radical solidarity. Who wants to be a lone atom in the market? In her book, Harrington argues that the belief in progress has led feminism astray: toward individual freedom, away from solidarity and care for others. For Harrington, this has to do with extending the concepts of freedom and trade from the economy to human relationships. But this “logic of individual profit” goes against our human nature as social animals. Harrington therefore wants to defend the relational interests of humans against “the market’s power to liquefy, to commodify and to alienate.” The concepts of commodification and alienation are laden with the Marxist critique that capitalism is not the right way to organize work and production. From a Marxist perspective, commodification and alienation are wrong because they thwart what is most distinctive about being human: free conscious activity (you could call that human nature). In the reactionary feminist view, things are not as clear. The cases of commodification they focus on are surrogacy, prostitution, and paying another woman to take care of one’s children. Reactionary feminists are quick to use leftist rhetoric in their critiques, but they remain vague about why surrogacy and prostitution are wrong. Is it because no one should divert women’s bodies from their “proper” use — reproductive sex within heterosexual marriage? Or is it because of the exceptional nature of exploitation involved in renting a woman’s womb or circumventing the mutuality of sexual attraction with money? We can find indications for both lines of argument in Harrington and Perry. Struggle of Interests For Perry, it is no wonder that men and women have had very different experiences in this liberated sexual culture. She argues that men and women have fundamentally opposing interests in the “sexual marketplace,” as she calls it, a contention that revolves around divergent proclivities and vulnerabilities rooted in biological reality. Women may be choosier about mates, for example, because women are uniquely vulnerable to becoming pregnant and bearing children after a sexual encounter, whereas men’s reproductive imperatives may motivate them toward sexual novelty and variety. For Perry, drawing on a concept that the Marxist radical feminist Shulamith Firestone first introduced, women thus constitute a “sex class,” which is locked in conflict with an opposing class, even while heterosexual men and women are oriented toward each other. If women and men simply have different interests by nature, then the possible political solutions seem reassuringly simple. One can become a revolutionary to overthrow nature (Firestone wanted to outsource pregnancy to artificial wombs) or become fatalistic about sexual relationships between men and women. While reactionary feminists tend to push back against heteropessimism, they are guilty of a version of it themselves. To them, women and men can never fully overcome their fundamental struggle of interests. We must all instead make peace with them and tame them through institutions like marriage, which they argue contains the excesses of an unchecked sexual marketplace. The problem with proclaiming an eternal struggle of interests between women and men is that it obscures the forces of socialization on gender relations and sexual violence. It takes women’s submission and men’s aggression to be natural facts. For Perry in particular, each notable difference in men and women’s behavior ultimately comes down to how women and men are simply wired differently and always have been. Although she is a critical thinker in other regards, Perry is captivated by evolutionary psychology and very keen to incorporate every “just-so” story that emanates from the field. Feminists have often disputed and criticized this science for naturalizing what is inherently social: gender roles. In the status quo, then, Perry might be right that women and men have different interests in the “sexual marketplace.” But when we take gender roles into account — and resist being satisfied by speculative stories that neatly explain how each gender stereotype has a deep root in our prehistory — we can begin to see that it needn’t be this way forever. Conservative Materialism With their Marxist rhetoric, reactionary feminists pick up, in a new and more explicit way, a well-established materialist thread in conservative argument: washing machines did more for the advancement of women than equal rights ever could. Conservative crusader Phyllis Schlafly declared as much in the 1970s. If we step back from the obvious political function of Schlafly’s assertion — cementing the view that homemaking is women’s true nature — we can see a materialist argument. Schlafly claims that what changed women’s lives and work most profoundly was not equal rights but electricity, mass food production, and household appliances. Harrington argues in a similar albeit more sophisticated vein that “the story of feminism is one of economic transitions — and particularly of the Industrial Revolution.” What enabled feminism’s fight for women’s equal rights was that women went from being producers in the agrarian household to wage workers and consumers. Harrington holds that as capitalism made work “more centralised, more mechanised and less agrarian, the resulting split between ‘work’ and ‘home’ drove women increasingly into a domestic-only role.” In the reactionary feminist view, then, the core of feminist politics is less a question of moral progress than of material conditions. This sounds eerily similar to a Marxist feminist perspective. And it also seems at odds with propagating women’s and men’s eternal struggle of interests. But Harrington and Perry see the biologistic opposition of interests as part of the material conditions of women’s lives. In doing so, they muddle the concerns of those radical feminists who are hyperfocused on women’s biology, with those of Marxist feminists, who look at economic and structural reasons for women’s situation. Reactionary feminists use ideological ambivalence as a strategy in their political project. As Harrington told The Atlantic about her book: “There are a great many conservatives who haven’t noticed quite how much Marxism I’ve smuggled in.” But she is also candid about the mission of reactionary feminists to build conservative and, if need be, “illiberal” institutions. Although they use Marxist rhetoric, their concept of women’s interests lacks a clear class analysis. The rapidly rising number of women who are choked during sex, often enough against their will, is just one telling example of a sexual culture in which women still experience a lot of harm. Adherents of sex-positive feminism have little to say about this — for fear of moralism — and understandably so. A substantive feminist vision of sexuality and of what the good life for a woman is could fill this void. Instead, conservative women have mostly filled it with their gender fatalism. Many on the Left mistakenly think of all feminist cultural critiques as moralism that distracts from the real concern: economic interests. As the Russian revolutionary Alexandra Kollontai argued more than a century ago, even under socialism, people still need morality to guide gender relations and sexuality. The Dollar’s Power Never Came From Oil

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Matías Vernengo ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-09T13:36:20+00:00

The war in Iran and the disruption of shipping through the Strait of Hormuz have renewed an old debate about the foundations of American global power. Oil has been central to the geopolitical order dominated by the United States since World War II. The dollar is the principal international currency, oil is overwhelmingly priced in dollars, and American military power has been essential to controlling access to petroleum, particularly in the Middle East. The question raised by some analysts is whether the erosion of this arrangement, accelerated by the transition to cleaner sources of energy, signals the end of American hegemony and of the privileged position of the dollar. There are good reasons to take this possibility seriously. China has become the leading producer of solar panels, batteries, electric vehicles, and many of the technologies essential to the transition away from fossil fuels. The transition to new energy technologies could therefore profoundly reshape the distribution of global economic power at a time when American military superiority is being questioned. But there is a problem with moving directly from the decline of petroleum to the decline of the dollar. International monetary hegemony does not depend primarily on which commodities are traded, and only secondarily on which country possesses technological superiority in the industries of the future. It rests on a broader structure of fiscal, financial, productive, and military power that has historically developed over centuries. Understanding that distinction requires looking beyond oil to the historical relationship between money and empire. The Hydrocarbon Dollar Thesis An influential interpretation of international economic power emphasizes the relationship between energy technologies and geopolitical hegemony. Britain’s rise coincided with the coal-powered Industrial Revolution, while American dominance was associated with the age of petroleum, particularly as oil became essential to modern warfare and transportation. In this interpretation, China’s predominance in renewable energy technologies raises the possibility of another hegemonic transition. Daniel Bessner has suggested that China’s predominance in renewable energy technologies may herald that transition. Helen Thompson, in her book Disorder: Hard Times in the 21st Century , provides a sophisticated historical version of this argument. She shows how successive energy regimes shaped imperial rivalry, international finance, and the geopolitical conflicts of the twentieth century. For her, oil altered the material foundations of military and geopolitical power, creating a structural disadvantage for Britain and an advantage for the United States. Britain had become the leading industrial and naval power during the age of coal, a resource it possessed in abundance. The transition to oil, initially for naval propulsion and subsequently for motorized transportation and aviation, changed the strategic equation. In 1911, Winston Churchill, as First Lord of the Admiralty, decided that oil should replace coal in the Royal Navy. Oil-powered ships offered military advantages, but the change created a serious strategic vulnerability. The energy transition forced Britain to import a resource it previously did not need. In addition, the United States possessed an enormous advantage in oil production. Its rapidly expanding oil industry gave it the capacity to supply both its own economy and the Allied war effort. Thompson notes that approximately 80 percent of the oil used by the Allies in World War I came from the United States. During the interwar period, British governments tried to establish secure supplies through their Middle Eastern concessions. Nevertheless, their reliance on western hemisphere oil persisted. During World War II, Britain again needed oil supplied by American companies, paid for in dollars. Thompson’s point is not simply that oil was a superior energy technology. It is that the geographical distribution of petroleum reserves fundamentally altered the capacity of competing states to sustain modern warfare. More importantly, Thompson’s contrast between Britain’s ascent during the age of coal and America’s ascent during the age of oil suggests that, in the next renewable energy age, based on minerals, batteries, and electrification, China might become the key player. There is much to this argument. Countries that depend heavily on imported oil are vulnerable to changes in international oil prices and the availability of foreign exchange. The expansion of renewable energy can reduce these vulnerabilities. It may also weaken the strategic importance of oil-producing regions and the influence of countries that dominate them. But reducing the external dependence associated with energy imports is not the same thing as escaping the international monetary hierarchy. The ascent of the United States implied that the infrastructure of international trade and finance was organized around the dollar — most clearly after the Bretton Woods Agreement in 1944. Many internationally traded commodities, including oil, were priced in dollars. By the 1970s, after the collapse of the Bretton Woods system and the two oil shocks, oil-exporting countries earned large dollar surpluses and recycled their revenues through American banks and Treasury bonds. The petrodollar system thus helped sustain the international demand for the dollar. A conventional fear is that international investors might lose confidence in the dollar and shift their holdings into other assets. If the international demand for dollars is partly sustained by the hydrocarbon economy, then a transition to renewables could reduce that demand and thereby weaken dollar hegemony. In that sense, petrodollars might become eventually battery-renminbis. However, pricing oil in another currency would not necessarily undermine the international role of the dollar. Foreign exchange markets make it possible to acquire currencies for particular transactions without fundamentally changing the denomination of international debts, the composition of reserves, or the structure of international credit. The critical question is therefore not simply what currency is used to purchase oil, even if that matters. It is why the rest of the world continues to need dollars, and why the United States does not face a comparable need for foreign currencies. Money and the Fiscal-Military State Another explanation, developed recently by Ho-fung Hung in Greenback Empire , places geopolitical and military power at the center of international monetary relations. The postwar international monetary order was intrinsically connected to the overwhelming predominance of the United States following World War II. First its wartime allies, and subsequently even its former enemies, Germany and Japan, became dependent on American military protection.The United States also exercised considerable control over access to strategic commodities, particularly petroleum, and maintained close security relationships with major oil-producing countries, even as they sought greater autonomy through the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC). Even geopolitical adversaries remained, to varying degrees, dependent on the dollar-centered international financial system. The widespread use of dollars and accumulation of American financial assets cannot be separated from these political and strategic relationships. The resulting system is self-reinforcing. American military superiority supports the international role of the dollar, while dollar hegemony allows the United States to sustain military expenditure and international commitments on a scale that would be considerably more difficult for other countries. Nevertheless, a fuller explanation requires placing these relationships in a longer historical perspective. The fiscal-military state was central to the development of modern capitalism and the rise of successive hegemonic powers. Public debt, central banking, and military expenditure did not merely support economic development after the fact. They were essential institutions in the creation of modern capitalist economies. The Bank of England, founded in 1694, was established principally to finance the British state during its wars against France. Public debt became the foundation of an expanding financial market. The central bank helped transform government obligations into liquid financial assets, supporting both private finance and state expenditure. The development of the British fiscal-military state, to use John Brewer’s apt concept, allowed the government to finance its navy, sustain prolonged wars, protect commercial routes, and promote the international expansion of British capitalism. This process helped establish the foundations of sterling’s international predominance. It was not simply that British industrial and naval power made sterling an international currency. The institutions associated with public finance and monetary development helped create that industrial and naval power in the first place. The same relationship would later emerge in the United States. Government expenditure, public credit, and the creation of financial institutions supported national economic integration and industrial development. Alexander Hamilton understood the significance of public debt and banking institutions for the construction of a national economy. The federal fiscal and monetary apparatus developed further during the Civil War, while the creation of the Federal Reserve in 1913 helped establish the financial infrastructure required for the dollar’s international expansion. The relationship between fiscal-military and monetary power is circular. The fiscal capacity anchored in taxation, public debt, and central banking makes possible government expenditure on military procurement and research that promotes technological development and military power. These capabilities, together with geopolitical alliances and financial institutions, help sustain the international use of the hegemonic currency as the unit of account in trade and debt contracts, and as the main reserve asset. Monetary predominance then reinforces the original advantage. The hegemonic state can borrow and settle its external obligations in its own currency without having to accumulate foreign exchange as other countries do. It also acquires leverage over the external financial constraints faced by other states through its control over international liquidity and financial infrastructure. Rather than deriving simply from commodity pricing, market confidence, or network effects, monetary hegemony is thus both a product of fiscal-military power and a central means of reproducing it. How Britain Actually Lost Monetary Hegemony The transition from British to American hegemony provides a particularly revealing historical example of how currency competition works. It is tempting to associate Britain’s decline with the transition from coal to petroleum, just as it is tempting to interpret America’s current difficulties as a consequence of its attachment to fossil fuels. Energy certainly mattered. Coal was fundamental to British industrialization and naval power, while petroleum became increasingly important for modern transportation and warfare. But the crucial transformation in the international monetary hierarchy occurred through a different mechanism. During the nineteenth century, Britain was the world’s principal financial center. London supplied international credit, financed trade, and exported capital on an enormous scale. Sterling became the leading currency of international transactions. As Jay Sexton shows in Debtor Diplomacy , the United States, despite its growing productive capacity, remained dependent on British finance. American railroads, infrastructure, and industrial expansion benefited from access to London’s capital markets, which British financial diplomacy helped facilitate. By the beginning of the twentieth century, however, the United States had become the world’s largest industrial economy. Its productive predominance was not yet matched by equivalent international monetary power. World War I transformed this relationship. Britain entered the war as the leading international creditor but became increasingly dependent on American productive resources and finance. Maintaining its military commitments required massive purchases from the United States, financed through the liquidation of foreign assets and increasingly through American credit. By 1917, Britain could no longer sustain its war effort without substantial American financial assistance. The United States, which had previously depended on British finance, emerged from the war as the principal international creditor. John Maynard Keynes, who worked at the British Treasury dealing with the interallied web of debts, observed that “[i]n another year’s time we shall have forfeited the claim we had staked out in the New World and in exchange this country will be mortgaged to America.” The transformation of monetary power followed. The creation of the Federal Reserve supported the development of dollar-denominated trade finance. As suggested in Charles Kindleberger’s classic account of the interwar period, The World in Depression, 1929 – 1939 , the economic collapse and the geopolitical turbulence that led to two world wars were intrinsically connected to the global crisis of British hegemony and the absence of a clear hegemonic power. World War II and the establishment of the Bretton Woods system institutionalized American monetary predominance. Sterling continued to play an important international role, particularly within the sterling area, but Britain had become subordinate to the United States. As David James Gill shows in The Long Shadow of Default , American financial assistance through Lend-Lease, the 1946 Anglo-American Loan, and Marshall Plan aid had cushioned Britain’s decline. At Suez, Washington demonstrated that it could also use its financial predominance to impose constraints. The Suez Crisis of 1956 dramatically demonstrated the limits of American support. When Britain and France, together with Israel, intervened militarily against Egypt following the nationalization of the Suez Canal, the Eisenhower administration opposed their operation. Britain faced a financial crisis as sterling came under speculative pressure and its dollar reserves declined. The United States had the capacity to provide the financial assistance Britain required, but withheld support until the British government agreed to withdraw. The significance of Suez was not simply that Egypt could disrupt an important petroleum route, as Iran is doing now in the Strait of Hormuz. It was that Britain could no longer conduct an independent imperial military operation when the United States opposed it, because Washington could impose an external financial constraint that London could not escape. Suez did not create Britain’s monetary subordination. It made visible the hierarchy that had emerged through the two world wars. An equivalent moment for the United States would require another power capable of imposing a comparable external constraint on Washington. Disrupting international petroleum supplies is not by itself sufficient, in particular because the United States is not dependent on imports of energy commodities. The Global Fiat Dollar Standard and the Rise of China The history of international monetary standards reinforces the argument. The transition from Dutch to British hegemony coincided with the gradual displacement of silver by gold as the principal international monetary standard. The transition from British to American hegemony eventually produced a more radical transformation, namely, the demonetization of gold itself. The gold standard collapsed during the interwar crisis of the 1930s, although gold remained central to the international monetary arrangements established at Bretton Woods. Under that system, foreign monetary authorities could convert dollars into gold at a fixed price. When President Richard Nixon suspended dollar convertibility in August 1971, many observers expected American monetary predominance to decline. The United States could no longer guarantee the metallic backing of its international liabilities. Yet the dollar did not lose its position as the principal international currency. On the contrary, the eventual consolidation of the global fiat flexible dollar standard eliminated the requirement that the United States settle foreign official dollar claims in gold. The international monetary system remained organized around American liabilities but without the same metallic constraint. The aggressive reassertion of American monetary power in the early 1980s was central to the restoration of American hegemony, as Maria da Conceição Tavares argued at the time. Susan Strange similarly challenged the conventional view that American structural power was declining. The Volcker shock demonstrated the international consequences of American monetary power. The sharp increase in American interest rates helped produce the debt crisis of the developing world, forcing countries throughout Latin America and elsewhere into painful external adjustment. The aggressive reassertion of American monetary power in the early 1980s was central to the restoration of American hegemony. This history presents a serious problem for interpretations centered on the material backing of money or the pricing of particular commodities. The disappearance of gold convertibility did not end dollar predominance. The petrodollar arrangements of the 1970s reinforced an already existing monetary hierarchy. They did not create it. Together with the expansion of offshore dollar markets and private international banking, they helped consolidate what Peter Gowan called the Dollar-Wall Street Regime. Neither the deindustrialization of the United States nor the rise of Japan, the Asian Tigers, and China necessarily implies the end of American hegemony or of the international position of the dollar. The Dollar-Wall Street Regime survives even if the United States has been challenged in the productive arena. The distinction between productive and monetary predominance is essential for understanding the current international order. China has become a leading industrial power and an increasingly important source of advanced technology. In renewable energy, China clearly challenges the productive predominance long enjoyed by the United States. But China does not possess an equivalent position in international finance. The dollar remains the principal currency of international credit, foreign exchange trading, and official reserves. China itself continues to hold substantial dollar reserves, and much of its international lending and trade remains connected to the dollar system. The United States does not face a symmetrical dependence on the renminbi. This asymmetry is the foundation of American monetary privilege. The United States can finance international commitments and external liabilities in its own currency. Most other countries, including China, must continue to consider their access to the principal international currency. More importantly, the dollar’s position allows Washington to influence the external financial constraints faced by other countries. American sanctions can restrict access to international payment systems and financial markets. Conversely, the Federal Reserve can provide emergency dollar liquidity to selected foreign central banks, alleviating pressures that might otherwise produce financial crises. This ability to impose or relax external constraints is not simply an incidental benefit of the dollar’s international role. It is one of the central mechanisms through which monetary hierarchy sustains geopolitical power. China’s growing technological capabilities could certainly alter this balance over time. Dependence on Chinese strategic minerals, equipment, and manufacturing capacity already creates vulnerabilities for the United States. But dependence on particular imported commodities or technologies is different from financial dependence on another state’s currency. For a transformation comparable to the British-American transition, the United States would have to become dependent on Chinese-controlled resources or finance under conditions in which China could determine the terms of access. The emergence of substantial international borrowing, credit, and settlement in renminbi could form part of such a transformation, but nothing of the sort is in the cards in the near future. The Resilience of the Dollar None of this means that American monetary hegemony is permanent. China’s advances in batteries, electric vehicles, and renewable energy could erode American productive predominance, at least in some sectors. The increasing use of financial sanctions and other coercive measures may also encourage countries to develop alternatives to the dollar. But greater currency competition is not the same as a reversal of the underlying international financial hierarchy. Britain’s loss of monetary hegemony was ultimately tied not only to its dependence on American production but also to its reliance on dollar financing during the two world wars. Suez demonstrated that Washington could use that dependence to constrain British policy. The United States, by contrast, does not need to borrow in renminbi to sustain its international commitments, and China cannot yet impose a comparable external financial constraint on Washington. China’s challenge to American productive predominance is real, but no significant decline in the dollar’s international market share has occurred. Even if such a decline were to take place, it would not by itself establish a new monetary hegemon. Indeed, as America’s productive lead and the legitimacy of its international leadership erode, its reliance on sanctions, financial exclusion, and military pressure may increase. The problem may therefore be less the imminent disappearance of American monetary hegemony than its increasingly coercive character. The transition away from fossil fuels is essential for addressing climate change and could substantially reduce the external vulnerabilities of oil-importing countries. But it will not automatically create an alternative international monetary order. To paraphrase John Connally’s famous dictum, the dollar remains America’s currency and the rest of the world’s problem. Paraguay is undergoing one of the most significant political and financial crises in its recent history. The Central Bank of Paraguay’s intervention in Ueno Bank —following the detection of serious irregularities in its operations and solvency issues—triggered a crisis that quickly expanded beyond the banking sector. Today, it has direct consequences for the government of Santiago Peña and has triggered an impeachment process that calls into question the very future of the president himself. To understand what is happening, it is necessary to explain a few key points. Ueno Bank experienced exponential growth in recent years, coinciding with the start of Santiago Peña’s administration. It grew to have nearly three million customers—more than 50 percent of the country’s adult population—and received approximately $800 million in deposits from the public sector. Among these funds are those linked to the Social Security Institute (IPS), which administers the retirement and health contributions of thousands of workers. Related: Latin America’s Far-Right Turn: Authoritarianism, Austerity and the New Oligarchy One of the problems is that the president himself maintained business ties with the group associated with the bank until March 2025. Added to this is the fact that Carlos Carvallo, who had served as president of Financiera Ueno, subsequently went on to head the Central Bank, the agency responsible for supervising and regulating the financial system. During this administration, provisions designed to prevent this type of conflict of interest—known as “revolving door” regulations—were also amended. The bank’s intervention, the freezing of deposits, and investigations into loans granted to companies linked to its owners ultimately exposed a web of relationships between private interests and public institutions. However, beyond the numbers and financial transactions, there are thousands of people who today cannot access their savings ; workers facing uncertainty about their wages; and retirees whose financial resources are at risk. Financial inclusion, which was touted as one of the great achievements of the economic model, ultimately turned into a massive scam against the population. Santiago Peña’s ties to the business group, the decisions made by the executive branch, and the responsibilities of the oversight agencies are among the issues that gave rise to the impeachment request. The opposition spearheaded the process in Congress, and on 5 November, the Chamber of Deputies approved the charges against the president . Peña submitted his resignation, but both chambers rejected this attempt to step down, and the Senate proceeded with the process. At the time of this writing, the president is set to present his defense in the impeachment trial. However, to understand this crisis, one must also look to the National Republican Association (ANR), the Colorado Party , – deeply entrenched in the Paraguayan state apparatus and linked to Alfredo Stroessner’s 35-year dictatorship – and particularly to Horacio Cartes (HC). Paraguay has a political peculiarity that is fundamental to understanding what is happening. The Colorado Party has governed virtually uninterrupted since 1947; the only interruption in that long cycle was the administration of Fernando Lugo (2008–2012), who came to power leading an alliance that managed to defeat the Colorado Party at the polls after more than six decades. However, his administration came to an abrupt end in June 2012 through a parliamentary coup that removed him from office in just 48 hours via a controversial impeachment trial. For social movements and sectors of the popular base, this event was not just another episode in history but a political operation orchestrated by sectors of the Paraguayan right, with Horacio Cartes as one of its main actors and with alleged complicity and support from external interests, including those of the U.S. Embassy. Currently, power within the ANR is fiercely contested by internal factions, but the Cartes faction (HC) has become the ruling party’s main power base. Cartes was not only president of the Republic from 2013 to 2018 but subsequently assumed the presidency of the Colorado Party itself and maintained enormous influence over the decisions of Santiago Peña’s government. Peña rose to the presidency with Cartes’ political backing. His candidacy and subsequent administration were built under the leadership of the Honor Colorado movement. For this reason, what we are seeing today involves a major political contradiction: the very same faction that built and sustained this administration has now decided to support the impeachment proceedings against the president. Cartes’ position shifted as the crisis unfolded. Initially, his inner circle rejected the possibility of impeachment and sought to pin the blame on Central Bank officials. However, on 5 November, Cartes publicly announced that the ANR would support Peña’s impeachment. This move cannot be interpreted simply as an institutional decision by the Colorado Party; it also reflects a struggle for political control, for the continuity of the government’s agenda, and for the preservation of a power structure that the movement itself helped build. Against this backdrop, social movements and the popular sector are not limiting their criticism to demanding individual accountability and rejecting any attempt at impunity; they are also warning that this crisis is yet another example of the failure of the liberal economic model, which for years insisted that the market could regulate itself, that the state should reduce its intervention, and that privatization and deregulation were the path to development. They promised that, if we let capital operate freely, wealth would eventually reach the entire population, that economic growth would translate into well-being, and that everyone would benefit from this supposed trickle-down effect. In Paraguay today, the consequences are felt by every worker, as a result of financial narco-capitalism. This is a model that unscrupulously manages public resources, concentrates wealth, and fosters ties between political power and private interests. It is a system in which profits remain in the hands of certain sectors, while the consequences of their operations ultimately fall on the population. Therefore, what is happening on this side of the southern part of our America is not isolated from what is happening in other parts of the world; it is part of a dynamic in which big capital dictates political decisions, appropriates the commons, and turns basic rights and needs into business opportunities. The settings change, the actors change, but the logic remains the same. However, the evident failure of the economic policy of financial capitalism also opens up an opportunity for the organized popular movement: to envision a different future and build a political and economic alternative that breaks with this logic—to pave the way toward a world in which the economy serves life rather than the accumulation of wealth by a few, and in which people can decide on their resources, their present, and their destiny. The necessity to avoid Türkiye and go through Israel was not about commerce but entirely, for the United States, about the politics of normalization for Israel. The general view is now that the countries of West Asia need to come together to seek their security through negotiations. India’s Mediterranean engagement combines commercial expansion with strategic calculation.
