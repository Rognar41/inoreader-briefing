OPINION: Where Does MA’s Movement for Affordable Housing Go From Here?

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Boston DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-09T20:01:21+00:00

[[{“value”:” Dense housing in Massachusetts, all rented to tenants, all suffering from rent hikes (Working Mass) Released By: Central Massachusetts & Worcester DSA Program Committee WORCESTER – Massachusetts has some of the most rent-burdened tenants in a country where the crisis is ubiquitous . Every year in Massachusetts, thousands of working-class people are pushed onto the streets, three-quarters of which are families with children , while their landlords continue to raise rents higher and higher. This state of affairs has forced many to leave Massachusetts altogether, but the reality for those most threatened by rent gouging is that leaving their homes, jobs, and lives behind to look elsewhere simply isn’t an option. Even for those of us with a little extra in our paychecks, rent is increasingly eating away at our ability to get by. We should not be surprised that the overwhelming majority of state residents report that the cost of housing is their top concern , and that rent control enjoys considerable support . History Rent control has advanced and receded in Massachusetts for over 100 years as fighting tenant movements have ebbed and flowed. The story begins with a housing crisis caused by soldiers returning from the first World War, at a time when the Russian Revolution — which redistributed the great plots of feudal lords amongst those who worked the land — inspired working-class unrest and revolt across Europe and around the world. Although the Massachusetts legislature passed a ban on “unreasonable” rents in 1919, then, as now, landlords and government officials were concerned less with the letter of the law than with capitalists’ ability to maximize profits. The law was never enforced, and was taken off the books several years later. Rent control made a comeback in Mass as an emergency measure during the Second World War, and, after being deconstructed once again in the 1950s, became the chief demand of a massive tenants’ movement in the late ‘60s. Finally, at the height of this movement, the state legislature caved, passing a law that allowed individual communities to control rent in 1970. By then, organizations like the Cambridge Tenants Organizing Committee had long been directly attacking capitalism and the institution of landlordship itself. As the rage of tenant struggle cooled and the movement on the streets dissipated, this policy, too, only lasted a handful of years. In Boston and Cambridge, tenants successfully petitioned the state to continue to allow rent regulation on the basis of “home rule” , meaning local options. But as the Cold War ended, the Soviet enemy of American capitalism was finally destroyed, and the neoliberal consensus was cemented at home and exported worldwide more than ever, the home rule deal wouldn’t last. In the late ‘90s, a concerted effort led by landlord organizations Massachusetts Small Property Owners Association and MassLandlords began to push for the total eradication of all rent control. In the cities, they found little traction with tenants who wouldn’t be able to survive the rent spikes landlords drooled after. One landlord described with great frustration the power that tenants — the hard-working people whose paychecks he looted once a month, every month — still have today: “ In Cambridge, tenants outnumber landlords by three to one. It was like three wolves and a chicken deciding what’s going to be for dinner .” Realizing that local campaigns were a losing strategy, opponents of rent control took to a statewide question, gathering enough signatures to relitigate the issue on the ballot in 1994. Margins were incredibly slim, but ultimately, the influence of suburban property values won out. On New Year’s Day, 1995, rent control was ended in every city across the Commonwealth . In recent years, certain organizations, particularly a coalition of nonprofit organizations in Homes for All Massachusetts , have attempted to revive lost tenant protections through a protracted, hitherto unsuccessful lobbying campaign. Some politicians have vocalized support for a local option in individual cities, with yearly rental increase caps as high as 10%, while opposing a statewide solution — more a public relations exercise than a genuine attempt to relieve rent-burdened tenants. All of these efforts, ultimately, culminated in the fiasco of this year. The 2026 Ballot Initiative Last year, Homes For All Massachusetts (H4A) announced that it had managed to gather enough signatures necessary to get a rent stabilization proposal on the ballot for the November 2026 election. The initiative would have limited annual rent increases for residential units to the Consumer Price Index (CPI) or 5%, whichever lower, with large carveouts and exemptions. This modest proposal was the center of a media storm driven by seething reactions of landlord organizations like Housing For Massachusetts . Politicians like Maura Healey — who publicly brands herself as a champion of affordable housing , yet openly states full-throated opposition to rent control on the basis that it would scare corporate developers — sided with their donors over a crushing supermajority of MA voters who indicated they would “definitely or probably” vote in favor of the measure in one Spring 2026 poll . Tenants inspired by the ballot initiative, including many DSA members tirelessly committed to working-class struggle , campaigned for it intensely. Many allies understandably expressed betrayal , however, once H4A entered backroom deal-making with property investors to withdraw the question from the ballot. The compromise was a yet weaker proposal for a 10% (or 5% plus CPI, whichever lower) cap that municipalities must opt into, with a limit to the number of apartments the policy could apply to. The scope of the original initiative’s effect was never certain, and it would have never eliminated tenants’ existing rent burden as wages stagnate and living costs skyrocket. Even so, the compromise represented an enormous step backward, and would have implemented nothing itself. State officials sat quietly on the sidelines, as if to wait for the people in charge — the landlord and business groups H4A was negotiating with — to give them their marching orders. In the end, the Massachusetts Supreme Court struck down the initiative entirely. Ironically, it was the proposal’s exemptions for religious institutions — one of many carveouts written to blunt resistance from moneyed interests — that provided the courts with the legal basis to kill the initiative . Central Mass & Worcester DSA’s Alternative H4A has led with solutions they think are most likely to be accepted by the rich and powerful. This is no accident: progressive nonprofits and non-governmental organizations take up worthwhile goals, but often rely on a web of grant funding from foundations and donors that place constraints on their activity. Push the political horizon of a campaign beyond what funding sources find acceptable, and risk the collapse of the entire organization. This forms the basis of many problems, chief among them: a lack of meaningful democratic accountability to the working-class constituencies these agencies set out to help. We propose a different approach: we believe that by leading with our most radical and class-partisan demands, the working class can best position itself to build an organized, politically conscious movement to coerce concessions through struggle. We want to build that working-class movement — a democratic one, no less, that does not sideline tenants in backroom dealings with their enemy, but welcomes them to join DSA , determine our organization’s strategy, and challenge the rigged system head-on. Central Mass & Worcester DSA has proposed a policy question which will appear on the ballot in the sixth Worcester State House district that instructs the district’s representative to support a measure using a tax on vacant properties and wealth and inheritances over $5 million to fund a robust, union-constructed, universal social housing program that “eliminates the role of landlords,” caps rent at 10% of household income, and places housing under tenants’ democratic management. The State House, which itself is filled with profiteers on the human need for shelter, will not consider this proposal for even a moment. But, if landlords and developers are increasingly confronted with a working-class movement that threatens their profits and their role as the dominant class in society, the reader might find that they will become far more open to the comparatively moderate rent control measures they reject now — in fact, they’ll beg the workers of the Commonwealth to take it. This piece was written and released with the democratic approval of the Program Committee of the Central Massachusetts & Worcester DSA chapter, which organizes the working class across the central region of the Commonwealth. The Amazonopticon and the Antiunion Arsenal

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: Boston DSA ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-09T20:01:21+00:00

[[{“value”:” By: Matt Wolfinger With Flock and Axon cameras on every corner, commuting to work for the average American means becoming an unwilling participant in a nationwide web of mass surveillance. Even clocking at work won’t protect them from the all-seeing AI – workplace surveillance systems can track them with even more precision, collecting data on the speed of their work, who they talk to and most importantly, if they’re planning to unionize. Labor organizing in the United States is growing more popular. A 2025 poll from Gallup found that nearly 70 percent of Americans approve of labor unions, while recent polling shows labor unions have reached a near-historic high of 72%. Despite their popularity, only 10 percent of workers in the U.S. are union members. This isn’t for lack of trying, as workers across the country fight tooth and nail to unionize their workplace. But innovations in AI and surveillance technology have made it easier than ever to keep tabs on workers and curtail any union activity — no more so than innovator and supervillain corporation Amazon. New Frontiers in Exploitation Workers at e-commerce behemoth Amazon are no stranger to union drives. Numerous warehouses have attempted to unionize but only one – JFK8 in Staten Island, New York – has won its union election. More than four years later, Amazon still refuses to come to the bargaining table. The independent Amazon Labor Union at JFK8, now affiliated with the International Brotherhood of Teamsters (IBT), faced fierce opposition from a company infamous for its brutal union-busting campaigns. There’s a seemingly inhuman ability for Amazon to get ahead of union efforts, before most employees are even aware of them, because their methods are inhuman: company-wide mass surveillance tracking workers both on and off the clock. In 2020, Business Insider uncovered Amazon’s use of algorithm-driven “heat maps” to track which Whole Foods locations were at the highest risk of unionizing, tracking “risk-factors” like local unemployment rates, average wages and the racial diversity of employees. Amazon makes use of “bossware” tech like cameras, handheld scanners and biometric trackers to monitor each worker’s location, how fast they’re working, and the lengths of their bathroom breaks. The data collected on each employee gets fed into an algorithm tasked with optimizing efficiency. Internal documents obtained by Vice in 2022 reveal exactly how the algorithm functions. The aforementioned surveillance tech tallies each employee’s daily “time off task” (TOT). Talk to your coworker on the clock? That’s time off task. Walk to the wrong floor? Also time off task. Take a bathroom break longer than 10 minutes? Unsurprisingly, that’s more time off task. Those 10 minutes include the walk to the bathroom, even if it’s on the other side of the warehouse, placing pregnant and disabled workers at a clear disadvantage . Document filed by Amazon to the NLRB concerning warehouse JFK8 in Staten Island, showing an example template for one employee’s “time off task” data. (Obtained by Vice, 2022 ) Whoever accumulates the most TOT that day is crowned the “top offender” and called into an office to be grilled by their manager. If a worker can’t recall what they were doing during a stretch in time, that’s more TOT. Accumulate more than 120 minutes of TOT in one day, or 30 minutes over 3 days in a single year and the algorithm fires you on the spot, no human oversight needed. The algorithm will auto-trigger warnings and terminations if employees fall behind on their quotas, which are also determined by the algorithm. Bossware lets employers automate the hiring process, generate schedules, conduct layoffs and write performance reviews. Amazon has touted its advancements in AI, and its implementation stretches far beyond its fulfillment centers. Amazon delivery vans come fashioned with AI-powered cameras that track the van’s speed, location, and the driver’s biometric data, even as they contract out to third-party vendors who legally operate as separate employers (Delivery Service Partner (DSP)) even though routes and hours are set by Amazon. Small facial expressions, like a driver yawning or looking in the side mirror get flagged and shared with their supervisor. Although done under the pretense of increased safety, drivers under surveillance report feeling pressured to drive more recklessly to keep up with their quotas. The company endangers its communities. A 2019 investigation from ProPublica found more than 60 DSP-involved accidents occurred over a 4-year span, ten resulting in deaths. Amazon argued that since those drivers are subcontractors, they can’t be held liable for their actions. The Antiunion Arsenal, Assembled In 2018, Amazon debuted their new “Just Walk Out” technology at convenience stores, later expanding to Amazon Fresh grocery stores. The company claimed that customers could enter the store, select their items, then walk out the door without stopping at check out – the advanced AI system would simply track what items they left with and bill them later. This tech, if implemented, would reduce the number of employees required to run each location, an obvious test run for Amazon’s hundreds of Whole Foods locations. Reporting from The Information uncovered that this system was largely overseen by underpaid workers in India, who manually reviewed over 70 percent of transactions. A similar system is used in Amazon’s warehouses. When the AI-powered cameras that track the stowing of packages fail, the footage is sent to thousands of workers in Costa Rica and India to review. They’re also required to report any rulebreaking, training the AI to identify these behaviors in the process. Those workers told the Bureau of Investigative Journalism that their quotas are so grueling they try to blink as little as possible, afraid they’ll miss a minute detail or fall behind pace and be punished – watched just as closely as the workers they’re paid to surveil. Amazon workers who know they’re being watched often feel pressured to work faster, leading to increased rates of burnout and workplace injury, according to a study from the University of Illinois Chicago’s Center for Urban Economic Development. Nearly 70 percent of those surveyed report taking unpaid time off to recover from constant pain and exhaustion. Even if workers aren’t under constant surveillance, just knowing it’s possible is enough to instill an overwhelming sense of fear and paranoia. Employees worried that the slightest misstep will cost them their jobs (in the midst of a cost of living crisis ) may feel more inclined to keep any prounion opinions to themselves. Throw in steep quotas and your employees have no time to think of anything else, like how a union could improve their working conditions. Not even off the clock conversations go unseen. Vice obtained documents in late 2020 revealing Amazon had been using a social media surveillance team to infiltrate dozens of private Facebook groups and scan websites, subreddits and Twitter threads to collect intel on employee complaints and “planning for any strike or protest against Amazon.” The largest amount of Amazon’s antiunion spending comes in the form of “union avoidance” attorneys and consultants, who can earn upwards of $10,000 per day to persuade employees against unionizing. A recent report from LaborLab and the Economic Policy Institute found that U.S. employers spend around $1.7 billion each year on union avoidance consultants and law firms. Multibillion-dollar companies like Amazon have more than enough money to burn, spending over $26 million on consultants last year. The amount is likely higher, as companies are only required to disclose spending on consultants who directly interact with employees. “Amazon’s MO is to lean on consultants pretty hard,” Teke Wiggin, strategic coordinator at LaborLab and a Ph.D. candidate at Northwestern University researching union-busting told Working Mass. They employ consultants and an in-house “employee relations team” of union busters to collect intel and plant antiunion propaganda in their conversations with workers. Wiggin said: This all has to be done very subtly, because soliciting grievances, for example, explicitly is illegal. So it’s all done indirectly. It makes workers feel listened to, but they’re also gauging the union support of all these individual employees. The attorneys, explained Wiggin, help formulate talking points – cherry picking lines from a union’s constitution that sound unfavorable out of context for consultants to parrot at workers on-site. Wiggin is the author of Weaponizing the Workplace , a study detailing Amazon’s use of “algorithmic management,” automating human relations tasks through algorithms, to disrupt the company’s first ever union election in Bessemer, Alabama. The 2021 union drive in Bessemer ultimately failed, with roughly 70 percent of workers voting no. Some who began as prounion but switched sides later expressed regret to Wiggin, saying they’d been misled or intimidated by Amazon. The tactics used in Bessemer became the blueprint for Amazon’s unionbusting efforts moving forward, building what Wiggin calls the “antiunion arsenal.” Companies like Amazon make employees reliant on in-house apps that automate human resource management. Employees are required to use the company’s A to Z app daily to keep track of their schedule, request time off and view pay stubs. Management exploits this reliance to send employees multiple text blasts per day with the classic antiunion lies: unions are scams, they impede any real progress, employees will lose their benefits, and they’ll drive a wedge between you and the architects of your suffering. Part of one message sent to JFK8 employees in Staten Island, NY through Amazon’s A to Z app ahead of their election. (Obtained by Vice ) They’ve found a way to circumvent the need for illegal in-person “captive audience” meetings (though Amazon holds those, too) to smother employees with antiunion propaganda at every hour of the day. Bessemer employees reported going to bed thinking they had the next day off, then waking up to find they’d be scheduled for a 7am shift while sleeping. Employees are punished for not refreshing the app 24/7, allowing Amazon to build a case of infractions as pretense for layoffs. Leading up to the election, Amazon pulled out all the stops to bust the union – literally. Union organizers would speak to employees at the intersection outside the Bessemer warehouse, making use of the down time at red lights to build a connection and inform them of their right to organize. To retaliate, Amazon asked the county to shorten the duration of red lights at the intersection, making it impossible for anyone to get a word in. This led to at least one car crash that narrowly missed an organizer canvassing outside. Preemptive Moves and Pinkertons, Back Again Another tool in the antiunion arsenal is “algorithmic slack-cutting,” where companies improve working conditions ahead of union elections to win back disgruntled employees. Ahead of Bessemer’s election, management relaxed enforcement of TOT and decreased the volume of packages brought through the warehouse to give workers the impression their demands were being heard. This led some who were prounion to switch sides, believing it was no longer necessary. After the vote failed, Amazon reinstated those policies. For employees already working in dehumanizing conditions, the slightest improvement can feel like a massive change. “Amazon has more room than traditional employers to rapidly improve working conditions,” said Wiggin, “and because they can rapidly improve working conditions through algorithmic slack-cutting, they have more capacity to sway voters in their direction.” Amazon has already hired the Pinkertons (yes, those Pinkertons) to collect intel on workers deemed “labor organizing threats.” Although times have changed, their methods have stayed the same – infiltrate the rank-and-file and gather intel the employer can use to their advantage. Even when workers win their elections, antiunion law firms will stall negotiations for as long as possible by constantly filing appeals on behalf of employers. More than four years after the Staten Island warehouse won their union election, the NLRB only recently ordered Amazon to finally start negotiating. Amazon plans to appeal that decision as well, holding it up in the courts and pushing negotiations back even further. Workers at Amazon warehouse DCB8 in Bellingham, Massachusetts have been attempting to unionize through the American Postal Workers Union (APWU) for two years. Since then, Amazon has rolled out the same union-busting strategies they used to crush the union in Bessemer. Bellingham workers announced their intention to unionize in May 2024 by wearing shirts on the floor featuring Amazon’s smile logo and “Union” in bright orange text. Within two hours, management had plastered antiunion messaging in every break room, bathroom and on every TV. The messaging started out as general antiunion talking points, but eventually named the APWU directly. Amazon’s pervasive antiunion strategy has made it difficult for DCB8 organizers to convince some workers to join the cause. Laurie Masteron, a former DCB8 worker and lead organizer, shared on APWU’s podcast : It has a huge impact, because when you have a conversation with someone who is new or has been there for a very short span of time, they parrot exactly what they see in here. It’s having that same conversation every single day, banging your head against the wall in hopes of breaking through that. Management closely surveilled those wearing union shirts to write them up for minor infractions, like forgetting to wear their ring scanner. “It’s a way of intimidating fellow employees into not wearing their shirts and not standing loud and proud,” Masterson explained. Masterson was fired by Amazon in December after returning from time off she’d taken to recover from a shoulder injury sustained on-site. The company cited moving her hand to unclog a jam on the conveyor belt (which caused the injury) as a fireable offense, a rule Masterson had never heard about. The APWU and AFL-CIO have filed a lawsuit through the NLRB over the firing, one of many Unfair Labor Practice (ULP_ lawsuits brought against Amazon on DCB8’s behalf. The APWU did not respond to multiple requests for an interview with a representative or DCB8 organizer. Borrowing Tactics from the Hotel Industry In June, Business Insider revealed Amazon is testing a new program called “Right Station Link,” taking the form of wearable trackers that “automatically capture check-in and labor-hours data for ‘indirect’ support roles, such as equipment maintenance, safety coordination, and floor management, that have historically been harder to track.” The goal is to increase efficiency and reduce idle time. Unlike the handheld scanners used to track stationary employees, support workers are constantly moving and manually check in throughout their shift. Right Station Link would automate that process. Amazon plans to implement the program in every North American warehouse by the 2026 holiday season. A spokesperson for Amazon told Business Insider that the system is similar to those used by hotel workers to log their progress and receive assignments. Although unintentional, it’s a very revealing comparison given the history hospitality workers have with this technology. Striking Marriott workers in Philadelphia shared in 2018 that these devices, which assigned each cleaner to specific rooms instead of letting them clean each one in order, led to less efficient and more exhausting routes as cleaners jumped randomly from room to room. This also increased the volume of complaints from guests, who assumed their rooms had been skipped. In 2023, California hotel owners responded to strikes by mass-hiring scabs through the Instawork hiring app. When the temporary workers who unknowingly signed up to cross the picket line decided to join it instead, Instawork automatically slashed their hours and wages to retaliate. Beyond opinions on unions, bossware gives employers tools to influence and mobilize their workers for their own political gain. Uber and Lyft flooded employers and riders with in-app reminders to vote “Yes” on Proposition 22 in California, permanently classifying all gig workers as independent contractors, the same loophole barring Amazon’s DSP drivers from unionizing or receiving benefits. Managers at Amazon’s warehouses have been butting heads with an algorithm progressively given more decision making power, and reducing the ability for human managers to override it, even when the AI makes decisions based on incorrect or out-of-date information. When the algorithm couldn’t account for managers needing more workers for specific sections of the warehouse, they’d manually reassign them then modify the timesheets to accommodate it. Amazon has since reduced the ability for managers to override the algorithm, despite its many flaws. Long-term, letting AI take over responsibilities and make decisions in the workplace lessens our ability to think for ourselves and become experts in the field. If we become over-reliant on AI, we’ll grow rusty and “deskilling” will erode our ability to perform tasks, understand the nature of our work and actively problem solve when issues arise. Workers are provided with little transparency on what data employers collect on them and what it’s used for. There’s an overreliance on AI, a technology infamous for hallucinating false information, to remove humans from the decision-making process. The powers-that-be at Amazon would rather double down and punish those who dare to exercise their own judgment. These advancements in tech have put the working class in a chokehold, unable to work without engaging with the tools employers exploit to exert their own influence. Clyde, a union activist who spoke to Wiggin, put it in Marxist terms: New forms of production enable data and attentional captivity [of workers]. If [workers] weren’t as attentive to their monitors and scanners as they are, their workplace would be a lot less efficient and union drives would be a lot easier . . . . The means of production are the means of communication. As long as the managerial class has access to unregulated AI and algorithmic management tools they pose an existential threat to the working class, whether eliminating jobs through automation or helping suppress unions. The world survived and thrived long before AI, and we can do so again. Placing guardrails on AI in the workplace and providing transparency on how algorithms work, (removing them from the “black box” ) is a good first step to protect the privacy of workers. Long-term, bossware should be excised from the labor market completely, giving unions a fighting chance against the oligarchs who’ve gone all in on technology designed to isolate, exploit and destroy them. Matt Wolfinger is a data journalist, Northeastern University graduate, and a contributing writer to Working Mass . The Beatles Belong to Us

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Alex Niven ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-09T16:55:25+00:00

As we enter the late 2020s, the legacy of the Beatles — history’s biggest band, by any measure — is facing a final reckoning. Paul McCartney and Ringo Starr, the group’s last surviving members, are now eighty-four and eighty-six, respectively. This should remind us that what might be called the “Beatles generation” is reaching the end of its natural lifespan, its icons departing the stage to become musty archival heritage. All things, as the late George Harrison once acknowledged, must pass.Partly as a result, the second half of the decade looks set to be a heyday of fabology (that is, critical appraisal of the Fab Four). But many of the retrospectives, gratingly, will seek to claim the Beatles for the cause of cultural liberalism. The last few years have seen a rash of books by “centrist dad” media figures, from Ian Leslie’s John & Paul: A Love Story in Songs to Stuart Maconie’s With a Little Help From Their Friends (the titles sum up the soggy, middlebrow bias of these texts). Even more ominously, a blockbuster Beatles biopic is now in production. Directed by Sam Mendes, a filmmaker who churns out vaguely progressive-nationalist fare in the vein of 1917, Britannia, and the James Bond movies Skyfall and Spectre, “The Beatles — A Four-Film Cinematic Event” will likely trigger a British Invasion for the TikTok era when it drops in April 2028.The battle for the soul of the Beatles, and their sociopolitical significance, is well and truly on then. On the one hand, there is something understandable, even appropriate, about attempts to locate the band at the cuddlier end of capitalism. The first mass-marketed boy group, the Beatles undoubtedly began as front men for the consumerist strain in postwar social democracy. A bolder thesis is that they saved American capitalism from itself at a crucial moment in the mid-1960s, pacifying a populace that had been destabilized by the civil rights movement, the Vietnam War, and John F. Kennedy’s assassination, and helping to downgrade a radical zeitgeist into a harmless hippie bacchanal that ultimately served the socioeconomic status quo rather well.This, more or less, was the view of the critic Ian MacDonald, whose 1994 book Revolution in the Head: The Beatles’ Records and the Sixties is often judged to be the definitive study of the band. In MacDonald’s telling, the counterculture the Beatles embodied “divided America against itself more violently than at any time since the Civil War and came within a whisker of sweeping the French government out of office” — however, “its actual achievements were meagre: a few curriculum changes and some minor additions to civil rights legislation.” Writing in the ashes of the Ronald Reagan and Margaret Thatcher era, MacDonald judged the Beatles phenomenon (if not the band itself) to be a dress rehearsal for the neoliberal turn. For him, the deification of “individual self-determination” in the Beatles narrative and the wider 1960s scene was a sort of cultural Mont Pèlerin moment.A glance at the record sheet throws up plenty of support for this theory, from the proto-monetarism of “Taxman,” Harrison’s Toryish whine of 1966, to the left-baiting grouches of “Revolution,” John Lennon’s counterrevolutionary take on 1968, culminating in the more recent missteps of the elder Beatles, such as McCartney’s acceptance of a knighthood in 1997 and his performances at Queen Elizabeth II’s jubilee concerts in 2002 and 2012. However, the idea that the Beatles were in thrall to the capitalist establishment and godfathers of modern individualism has real limits.To understand the Beatles’ political side, we must first examine their tribal roots. While the Irish ancestry of the band members is well-known, surprisingly little has been made of the fact that both Lennon and McCartney were descendants of migrants from Ulster, one of many violent historic epicenters of British colonialism. This was hardly irrelevant. While McCartney responded to the 1972 Bloody Sunday massacre in Derry by composing the strident “Give Ireland Back to the Irish,” Lennon (who had once claimed the Beatles were “all Irish”) offered vigorous support for the Provisional Irish Republican Army in the same period.In fact, while McCartney’s politics were basically those of a natural, if unaligned, socialist (support for civil rights, vegetarianism, refusal of private schooling for his children), Lennon’s were often militantly leftist. Despite much easy sneering about the millionaire who dreams of a world without possessions in “Imagine,” Lennon was authentic in his engagement with the radical left. His political journey in these years is yet to be fully mapped, but the dialogue he maintained with New Left Review editors Tariq Ali and Robin Blackburn in the early ’70s and the products of this relationship — the ultraleft “Power to the People,” and attendance at a 1972 Troops Out of Ireland demonstration with a “For the IRA, Against British Imperialism” banner — are well attested. Remarkable in themselves, these biographical morsels highlight a more essential aspect of the Lennon and McCartney worldview. As Irish-coded natives of Liverpool — a culturally working-class northern city where “Scouse not English” is a common rallying cry — the Beatles were fundamentally at odds with the traditionalist core of bourgeois England. With a history of famine, slavery, and industrial hardship in mind, they were disinclined to view Anglo-Saxon capitalism as a static, essentially benign entity (as does the average English liberal). Through many twists and turns, this was the grounding of the band’s basic allegiance to iconoclastic, historically un-English values like modernism, egalitarianism, and political rebelliousness. Whether this deeper cultural identity is useful as a framework for understanding the nuances of the music itself is a more expansive question. While we have to be careful not to reduce aesthetics to pure ideology (after all, musicians are not political theorists), there is surely a case to be made that the power and resonance of the best Lennon and McCartney songs derives from their central themes of mutuality, cooperation, collectivism, and love — in other words, the cornerstones of socialism. But that debate is for another arena. The Really, Really Short Twenty-First Century

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: David Broder ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-09T16:55:25+00:00

A few months ago, my partner and I met a liberal, fortysomething history professor who told us that for his next book he’d been reading up on late-1990s alter-globalization movements. As the millennium neared, global capitalism seemingly stood unchallenged. Social democrats and ex-communists praised the free market, and groupings like the World Trade Organization (WTO) integrated Russian and Chinese leaders. Yet this, he had discovered, wasn’t the whole story. It turned out that each of these elite conferences had been confronted by mass protests calling for an alternative global community. This rainbow coalition hadn’t detailed exactly what it wanted. But it had, he said, a world-changing ambition hard to find on today’s Left. It was odd to hear him discuss this period like a lost Atlantis. When I moved into an East London squat in the late 2000s, it wasn’t even clearly in the past; its activists, its media, and its meetups were still with us, shaping the mass opposition to the ongoing war in Iraq. People still derided the practice of “summit hopping” from city to city to protest the gatherings of statesmen; some activists ironically identified with the term. These had, for many, been inspiring mobilizations. When the G8 met in Genoa in 2001, the repression culminated in the police shooting dead the young activist Carlo Giuliani. When we protested the G20 in London in 2009, a police officer killed Ian Tomlinson, a newspaper seller wandering through the demonstration, and then the authorities lied about it. Upon the quarter-century anniversaries of similar summit protests, many accounts have emphasized their uncontrollable energy, with no single strategic leadership. While trade unions did join in, organized labor, political parties, and national governments were hardly center stage. Rather, at these protests and at countersummits like the World Social Forum, there were a variety of forces and personally chosen affinity groups: farmers, indigenous collectives, NGOs, tax-justice campaigns, and masked-up activists dressed in white overalls. If a word like “socialism” had for decades meant different things to different people, even such a vague future ambition had slipped from view. For some, this offered a way of moving on from obsolete categories. This same desire at least partly explains the notoriety of the 2000 book Empire , penned by Duke professor of literature Michael Hardt and Italian political philosopher Antonio Negri. Drawing on post-Marxist tools, Empire cast itself as a way of reading a postmodern reality. It spoke of a planet ruled not by the rival projects of the strongest capitalist states but by a more pervasive, many-layered combination of powers. Faced with capital’s apparent triumph and the dissolution of old left-wing parties, it promised a hopeful counternarrative of polyphonic resistance to capitalist coordination. Everywhere and Nowhere Empire print runs repeatedly sold out in the book’s early years. Many observers of this commercial success were suspicious of its reception in mainstream outlets like The New York Times . Left-wing reviewers sniffily mentioned that thepaper had proclaimed the book the “Next Big Idea” and attributed this attention to Negri’s “radical chic” image (at the time, he was jailed in Rome on hyped-up charges of inciting terrorism decades earlier). Marxist critics of the book often recognized the buzz around it within the alter-globalization movement and then sought to gently dissuade readers from Hardt and Negri’s analysis. Ironically, writing for The New York Times , Emily Eakin adopted a more materialist outlook on Empire than most who name-checked her piece. Her story centered on Harvard University Press’s publishing success, which responded to “the need in fields like English, history and philosophy for a major new theory.” Hardt and Negri were “hot” because they offered a fresh account of why disciplines like literary theory were useful for interpreting globalization. Covering everything from the Roman Empire to Karl Marx, Baruch Spinoza, and Niccolò Machiavelli, explained Eakin, here was a tome with “the formal trappings of a master theory in the old European tradition.” This was not the only reason for interest. Faced with the epochal turn to the market in the Eastern Bloc and China, even many anti-Stalinist Marxists harked back to earlier, pre-Comintern ideas. What some found compelling about Hardt and Negri’s system was its promise of novelty and engagement with a new, post-1989 world. It claimed to transcend “the first major wave of Marxist theoretical analyses of imperialism” by Vladimir Lenin and Rosa Luxemburg, “clustered around the period of World War I.” Hardt and Negri argued that while Marxist-Leninist anti-imperialism had sought national liberation — that is, inclusion in sovereignty and modernity — this model was now exhausted by its defeats. But what was Empire? It was a new global sovereign, replacing the old imperialist rivalries between nation-states. It had emerged as a disciplining response to the capitalist crisis of the 1970s and the spontaneous “proletarian internationalism” of working-class insurgency, both Eastern and Western. Rather than just extending US imperialism’s geographic reach, this produced an “international disciplinary order” in which the First, Second, and Third Worlds were now interwoven. Its “hybrid constitution” combined international organizations, states, multinationals, NGOs, and the media. While institutions like the G8 and the WTO helped coordinate this globalized economy, this form of power was deterritorial and pervasive: there was no “outside” space to break away into anymore. If the United States was near the top of the “imperial pyramid,” there were also signs of collaboration among once hostile states. In particular, the 1990s were a high-water mark for Washington acting in the name of the international community. The United Nations — including the Soviet leadership — gave its backing to George H. W. Bush’s Gulf War in 1991, beating back Saddam Hussein’s invasion of Kuwait. Genocides in Rwanda and Srebrenica exposed the UN’s limits as a peacekeeping force. Yet there were other signs of political convergence. In 1997, Russia was welcomed as the Group of Seven’s eighth member (thereby creating the G8), and early in Vladimir Putin’s rule, Western leaders saw him as an ally in the war on terror. We’re Coloring In Maps Again Yet Empire was more a work that expounded a philosophical system against past Marxisms than one materially analyzing developments in capitalism. As Samir Amin pointed out, in soft-pedaling the US state’s pivotal role, it showed little interest in what either Democratic or Republican administrations said, for instance, about cornering access to global resources. Faced with the fall of Soviet-style communism and Third World nationalist projects, Hardt and Negri didn’t really examine the new states there either. Yes, they said that “socialist modernization” in China and the USSR ended up preparing a skilled workforce for capital. But what role did these former socialist states play in Empire? The book made only passing reference to their post-1991 histories. Today the book’s claimed novelty seems ironic. Donald Trump, Marco Rubio, and others openly boast of their plans to use the US military to control the resources of Venezuela and Iran — and moan that Europe isn’t helping more. US leaders do not justify this in the name of international peace but at most point out that China wants control of oil and waterways too. In such fields as artificial intelligence, rival nation-states play a key role in defending their own tech firms and regulating others’. For sure, Hardt and Negri’s system has its get-out clauses: the text is so vague and poetic and changes subject so often that it can easily retreat into inscrutable abstractions about contradiction and hybrid structures. Yet this doesn’t make it all that useful for explaining what is happening in the actually existing twenty-first century. The issue here is not that Trump’s aberrant behavior has upset predictions. Maybe the recent fracas over Greenland really does owe to his childlike desire to recolor a big chunk of the North Atlantic map. But more than that, it’s worth understanding that the globalization of which Hardt and Negri spoke pivoted on the US state specifically; that the authors were correct to see globalization as a response to the profitability crises of the 1970s; and that it was utopian to imagine that fear of proletarian internationalism was producing a unitary imperial order. To understand this, we could look to an excellent recent book by Benjamin Bürbaumer, which, though not claiming the same scope as Empire , illustrates many of its limits. Bürbaumer’s Chine/États-Unis, le capitalisme contre la mondialisation develops a materialist account of both Washington’s and Beijing’s roles in the age of capitalist globalization. We again start from the understanding that both US and Chinese leaders turned to globalization as a response to crisis and as a strategy for pushing back against working-class economic demands. Unlike Hardt and Negri, Bürbaumer recognizes that these strategies reflect the interests of particular fractions of capital (in the US case, transnationally oriented businesses) and of the states themselves. He thus addresses one of the most fundamental questions of Marxist analysis: How do the ruling classes act in order to protect and promote capital accumulation? From the 1970s, Bürbaumer explains, US leaders defending transnational capital had an interest in China’s and the Eastern Bloc’s integration into globalization in a subordinate role: as a massive supply of cheaper labor, which also helped to discipline American workers. For a rising pro-market faction in China’s Communist Party, this offered a way of accelerating national development, allowing technology transfer, providing a pressure valve for internal conflicts, and producing new middle-class groups that would support further liberalization. Yet this also produced new tensions, as an ascendant Chinese capitalism met the limits of its subordinate position. Since the 2008 crisis, this conflict has intensified in everything from tech patents to the dominance of the dollar. Now the protection of rival trade routes is matched with a buildup of military hardware. The success of Bürbaumer’s approach is that, rather than relying on telling us that the system has countless different parts, it is willing to tell us what they are. Going beyond the recognition that everything is somehow interrelated, it allows us to see what power looks like today and helps us imagine what might be done to avoid its worst outcomes. Age of Empire It’s not just that states are directing investment, building infrastructure, and even preparing for war. They also remain key sites of struggle, unreplaced by genuinely global movements. Already in the high phase of the alter-globalization protests, many actors understood that the expansion of capitalism into new spaces helped to undermine national-level legislation (on labor, on environmental standards) and democratic control over them. Far from a reactionary throwback, these areas remain pivotal conquests of the workers’ movement and the focus of many present-day struggles. While historic workers’ parties have wilted, their past gains have not disappeared entirely. As Empire was being written, in 1994, historian Eric Hobsbawm published The Age of Extremes . It framed the run from World War I to the collapse of the Soviet Union as a “short” twentieth century: a period of disasters and of both hopeful and dreadful struggles to build a new world. Its end did not produce an irenic new order but killed off an antagonist that had long forced liberal capitalism to reform. Of Hobsbawm’s main works, this was the most marked by the failure of the communist movement with which his life was intertwined. Yet he concluded by raising apt questions about how the hollowing out of democracy and dismantling of national-level social protections still threatened liberal capitalism. Empire instead remained coy about the institutional legacy of twentieth-century reformism, while refusing to frankly assess the results of the 1970s autonomist left in which Negri had been active. To provide a framework to do better than the struggles of the past, Empire counted on a willpower that would spring from life and labor itself. The authors name-checked past anti-colonial wars and antifascist movements to illustrate the unending course of popular revolt, even if dismissing their choices and outcomes. The militants of historical communist parties appeared in the book as “sad, ascetic agent[s],” beholden only to “duty and discipline,” unlike the “irrepressible lightness and joy” with which Negri credited himself. Likewise doomed were all who pinned their desires on national liberation: “The day when the Palestinians are institutionalized, I will no longer be at their side,” declared Jean Genet, quoted in lieu of analysis. With “the construction of the nation-state,” Hardt and Negri explained, “all the oppressive functions of modern sovereignty inevitably blossom in full force.” Singing the praises of mobilization within and against Empire, Hardt and Negri’s treatise never engaged with the demands or expectations of the subaltern. The industrial working class appears only as a dying actor; any defense of identity and locality — and, it would seem, also rights won within the state — is cast as backward-looking. Any strategy that seeks an “outside” from capitalist globalization rather than fights within it, we read, “obscures and even negates the real alternatives and the potentials for liberation that exist within Empire.” What does this mean in practice? The authors find it hard to explain. In further works such as Multitude and Commonwealth , Hardt and Negri settle for suggesting that Empire itself prompts myriad forms of resistance and refusal that — perhaps without yet realizing it — may end up marching along parallel paths. Appearing after Hobsbawm’s Age of Extremes , Empire saw the defeat of the short twentieth century’s illusions as the removal of statist projects that had only gotten in the way. Given the many failings of the socialist and communist parties and their recent epochal defeat, Empire had a captive audience who needed little convincing that the project of reforming the state had hit a dead end. Yet the twenty-first century that Empire heralded, marking the surpassing of imperialism in favor of unified capitalist empire, would itself run aground almost immediately, lurching from crisis to international conflict. The next big idea on campus in 2000 was an artifact of the Left’s defeats and the promised globalization that wouldn’t live up to the billing.

Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan Now Have Their Own NATO

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Annelle Sheline ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-09T16:55:25+00:00

On August 7, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, and Turkey announced a mutual defense pact called the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement. Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan compared the arrangement to NATO, specifically citing Article 5’s collective defense clause. President Donald Trump lauded the pact as a “BIG, BOLD, IMPORTANT FIRST STEP” toward ensuring countries in the Middle East “will finally be able to defend themselves.” On this point, he’s largely right. What he failed to mention is that this shift reflects the growing realization that the United States and Israel are the most significant drivers of instability in the Middle East. Middle Eastern leaders were once able to ignore this, largely because they believed the US security umbrella would insulate them from the repercussions of American militarism. But Israel’s attack on Qatar last year, followed by the destruction of the ongoing war with Iran, finally punctured this fantasy. The Mecca Agreement represents a manifestation of regional leaders responding to this newly apparent reality and grappling with a post-American Middle East. The pact is clearly intended to reestablish deterrence against Iran, yet it is also meant to deter Israel. From Turkey’s perspective, Israel presents a more likely aggressor, with figures from Benjamin Netanyahu to Naftali Bennett describing Turkey as a threat comparable to Iran. The pact is thus a practical reaction to a fraying regional order. The end of American empire in the Middle East means the end of a series of fantasies it was propping up: that Gulf states could spend endlessly on vanity projects; that Israel’s occupation of Palestine could be swept under the rug; that Israeli military adventurism could continue unchecked. The question now facing Middle Eastern leaders is what happens when all those myths collapse at once. The Mirage of American Security For years, as Washington waged wars on Afghanistan and Iraq and tried to strangle Iran and Syria with sanctions, the Gulf states had been willing to overlook American bellicosity because they felt protected from its consequences. Their wealth appeared to insulate the members of the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) from the region’s chaos, not least because they purchased massive quantities of prestige American military hardware, the physical manifestation of the US security guarantee. It was under this illusion of stability that Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman (MBS) dreamed up his grandiose plans for Neom, the Line, and the other megaprojects of Saudi Vision 2030. Now, simultaneous to the crumbling of American-sponsored security, MBS has been forced to acknowledge that the scope of his vision exceeded the kingdom’s fiscal capacity. A recent exposé by The Wall Street Journal revealed that construction on the various megaprojects has largely ceased, while the Saudi state is paying $16 billion merely to cancel construction contracts. Outsiders may speculate on the crown prince’s political future, given that he staked his image on transforming the kingdom by 2030. Yet under his tenure, the kingdom has indeed undergone massive societal shifts. The government altered the terms of the social contract: previously Saudi men received government salaries generous enough to care for their female relatives, who were legally considered minors. Over the past decade, the state has encouraged women to join public life — female employment rose from approximately 17 percent in 2016 to 31 percent in 2026 — while seeking to promote jobs in the private sector for all Saudis, rather than continued reliance on the state’s largesse. This transformation has had a more profound effect on the citizenry than would a gleaming futuristic city, although Saudis can reasonably ask why their leaders wasted billions on vanity projects that now sit abandoned in the desert. Shorn of the illusion of American security, will Saudi leadership also now embrace more responsible stewardship of the kingdom’s resources? They may have to: the Saudi economy is struggling, as the Strait of Hormuz remains largely closed and Ansar Allah threatens Saudi tankers trying to exit the Bab el-Mandeb Strait en route to Asia. Saudi ships can proceed north through the Suez Canal and take the much longer journey around Africa, which can add between $500,000 to $2.5 million per trip. In the second quarter of 2026, Saudi Arabia’s GDP experienced its sharpest fall since the second quarter of 2020 at the height of the COVID-19 pandemic. The Saudi economy has traditionally experienced boom-and-bust cycles, due to its dependence on fossil fuel markets; Vision 2030 was supposed to help lessen those shocks. Unfortunately, although more than half of Saudi GDP is now derived from nonoil sectors, tourism and entertainment are just as vulnerable to Iranian missiles as Saudi oil exports. An Islamic NATO? Saudi fiscal constraints raise another question: What is Riyadh bringing to the Mecca Agreement, if not massive wealth? Saudi Arabia appears intent on leveraging its religious soft power by naming the pact the Mecca Agreement and carrying out the signing in Islam’s holiest city at the Al-Safa Palace overlooking the Kaaba. Commentators have already dubbed it “the Islamic NATO.” Turkey and Pakistan provide more tangible assets. Turkey offers both its status as a member of NATO and as a significant military power in its own right. Turkey is eleventh in the world for weapons exports, while both Pakistan and Saudi Arabia import massive amounts of defense articles annually. Pakistan theoretically brings nuclear deterrence, although the text of the agreement does not establish an explicit nuclear umbrella. As Yemeni projectiles continue to strike Saudi tankers in the Red Sea without an apparent response from Ankara or Islamabad, it is unclear how exactly the commitment to mutual defense will be put into practice. Still, in the absence of a better alternative, other Gulf states may join the pact. On the day the agreement was announced, former Qatari Prime Minister Sheikh Hamad Bin Jassim Al-Thani called for it to become a regional security alliance and said, “May the rest of the Gulf Co-operation Council [GCC] countries take the initiative to join.” Although Qatar might sign on, Oman is less likely to, as it has long maintained a posture of neutrality. Kuwait and Bahrain have traditionally adhered to Saudi preferences, although more recently have drifted into the orbit of the United Arab Emirates (UAE). The UAE is one GCC member who almost certainly will not join. Although the declaration of the Mecca Agreement was certainly sped up by the Iran war, its development was spurred by the Emirati-Israeli partnership that the Abraham Accords formalized in 2020. The Emirati and Saudi rivalry has grown more intense in recent years, flaring up in Yemen in December when Saudi Arabia fired on Emirati ships and forced the UAE to leave the country, after having engaged in joint military operations against Ansar Allah since 2015. Abu Dhabi views its partnership with Israel as part of a broader strategy: in February, just days before attacking Iran alongside the United States, Netanyahu announced the “hexagon of alliances” between Israel, the UAE, India, Greece, and the Republic of Cyprus. This constellation of countries is clearly at odds with Pakistan and Turkey. Although the “hexagon” ostensibly remains closely tied to the US, it also envisions a region and a world that relies less on the United States. The announcement of the Mecca Agreement appears to finally bury any lingering speculation that Saudi Arabia might normalize with Israel. From the Saudi perspective, all of Washington’s previous talk about the “India Middle East Corridor” (IMEC) and the “Middle East Security Alliance” (MESA), while framed as promoting regional security and independence, was actually intended to integrate Israel into the region without addressing the festering conflict at the heart of the Middle East: Israel’s ongoing occupation of Palestine. American policy toward the Middle East has long been guided by Israeli preferences. Washington’s role in the so-called peace process was always primarily intended to safeguard Israel and prioritize its concerns. The end of the era of American domination, which artificially inflated the clout of Israel as well as the small but wealthy Gulf states, may finally allow for a rebalancing of regional power that reflects the realities on the ground. Should the Left Not Celebrate the Decline of American Empire? Some may see the end of American military hegemony in the Middle East as inherently destabilizing and a harbinger of an increasingly unstable and unpredictable world. Although the post–World War II world order has been hardly peaceful, at least it successfully prevented what many view as the most lethal of all human activities: war between great powers. To those bemoaning the end of the relative Pax that Americana has preserved, the Mecca Agreement appears alarming. The foreign policy establishment reacted swiftly. Steve Cook of the Council on Foreign Relations wrote “The U.S.-led Middle East Is Over. The Mecca Agreement Proves It.” Neoconservative Robert Kagan penned “ Iran Unleashed ” for The Atlantic , predicting, “Instead of an American-Israeli order, this will be an alignment less implacably hostile to Iran, less friendly to Israel, and more independent of the United States.” The wailing from Washington reflects the widespread belief that without the US, the region will descend into chaos. The assumption that the world needs America to act as the global hegemon presumes that no other country is capable of ordering their own international affairs in a manner that provides security and stability. The paternalism inherent in that mindset betrays the underlying imperial logic: “We have to control these people for their own good; they are better off as a result of our control.” Although Washington has sought to neuter or dismantle international institutions, from the United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East to the International Criminal Court to the United Nations itself, many countries remain committed to the UN system and to international law. While recognizing the urgent need to reform the UN Security Council and other global bodies, the international system could become more influential in the absence of American primacy. The climate crisis is only one of many global problems that requires more effective international cooperation. It is unlikely that the Mecca Agreement or other regional alignments will bring peace to the Middle East. The end of the era of US hegemony appears likely to spur many such regional realignments, which may provoke future conflict. The possibility that western and southern Asia will split between an Israeli-Emirati-Indian bloc and a Turkish-Saudi-Pakistani bloc could presage war. And yet as support from the United States declines, the moral hazard that has spurred Israeli and Saudi aggression could give way to a more grounded assessment of these nations’ capabilities. For the region, the era of American domination has been anything but peaceful. Instead, it has been marked by deepening autocracy, neoliberalism, and austerity, and greater militarization at best, brutal war at worst. Therefore, while some may fear the end of the US unipolar moment, for many in the Middle East, the end of US dominance may offer at least the possibility of something better. The Mecca Agreement demonstrates that other countries are in fact capable of implementing new institutional arrangements that can, theoretically, provide order even in the absence of the United States. Unfortunately, however, America’s influence has largely primed the region for more violence rather than less.

West Indian Cricketers Refused to Grovel

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Meagan Day ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-09T16:55:25+00:00

The summer of 1976 was Britain’s hottest in 350 years. The West Indies cricket team was there all season long, playing England in city after city in the oppressive heat. There was a lot riding on its performance. To the cricketers and their fans back home in the Caribbean, the team’s mission went beyond athletics. Though the West Indies had produced some legendary international cricketers and won stirring victories, its players were often condescendingly referred to as “calypso cricketers,” meaning they were entertaining but not particularly serious. Now they intended to prove themselves a capable force in the sport. Under the leadership of new captain Clive Lloyd of Guyana, the “Windies” had been building skill and discipline and reimagining their approach to the game. Driving this project of reinvention was the pervasive feeling that the team had come to represent something bigger than itself. West Indies cricket, writes historian Hilary Beckles, “was born, raised, and socialized within the fiery cauldron of colonial oppression and social protest. In its mature form it is essentially an ideological and politicized species and knows no world better than that of liberation struggle.” To players and supporters in the mid-1970s, West Indian cricket symbolized self-determination. It was a heady time. In 1960, only three West Indian nations had achieved independence from European colonial powers. By the early 1980s, that number was sixteen. Throughout the 1960s and ’70s, the islands were not only throwing off the yoke of European rule but were also in some cases, as in Michael Manley’s Jamaica, resisting the directives of global capitalism. The Caribbean was experiencing cultural upheaval as well: the emergence of new philosophies, new identities, and new music like reggae that were enveloped in an aura of pride and protest. Because cricket was one of the few formal activities the West Indies undertook together on the international stage, the sport emerged as a primary vehicle for asserting West Indian independence. Nowhere was that more on display than in England in 1976. As West Indian cricketer Michael Holding of Jamaica recalls in an interview in Stevan Riley’s 2010 documentary Fire in Babylon , “We wanted to be able to show Englishmen, ‘You brought the game to us, and now we’re better than you.’” The apparent arrogance of the English team added fuel to the fire. In the lead-up to the West Indian team’s arrival on British shores, cricketer Tony Greig gave an interview that set the tone for the season. Greig said that the press was “building these West Indians up, because I’m not really sure they’re as good as everyone thinks they are.” When they’re good, they’re good, he said. “But if they’re down, they grovel, and I intend, with the help of [my teammates], to make them grovel.” To West Indians, the word “grovel” called up histories — distant, recent, and ongoing — of racial and national humiliation. To make matters worse, Greig hailed from apartheid South Africa. “Here was this guy,” remembers captain Clive Lloyd, “apartheid still going strong, and he’s gonna make these black guys grovel.” Legendary Antiguan cricketer Viv Richards remembers Lloyd telling the team, “We don’t need to say much. Our man on the television just said it all for us. We know what we’ve gotta do.” A Medium of Expression The early history of West Indian cricket is artfully rendered in the Marxist intellectual C. L. R. James’s semi-autobiographical 1963 book, Beyond a Boundary . In the West Indies, James wrote, “the cricket field was a stage on which selected individuals played representative roles which were charged with social significance.” West Indians played for the love of the game, but the meaning of cricket didn’t stop there. James played club cricket in Trinidad in the 1920s. At the time, there was clear racial segregation in West Indian cricket. White players, lighter-skinned players, and darker-skinned black players generally had their own teams. James’s private schooling allowed him to be something of an exception, and he ended up in the Maple Cricket Club, “the club of the brown-skinned middle class,” despite his darker complexion. Over the years, James developed a fierce admiration for Shannon, a club for dark-skinned black cricketers. “The Shannon Club played with a spirit and relentlessness, they were supported by the crowd with a jealous enthusiasm which even then showed the social passions which were using cricket as a medium of expression,” James wrote. When Shannon played well, the explanation “was not mere skill. They played as if they knew that their club represented the great mass of black people in the island.” The dynamic was much the same for the West Indian international team after it appointed Frank Worrell as its first black captain for a full series in 1960 (following a hard-fought campaign in which James himself participated) and especially after decolonization sped up in the 1960s. The team came to represent millions of ordinary West Indians, descended from slaves and indentured servants, either newly freed from colonial rule or still living under it, and usually exploited and impoverished either way. In the late 1960s, Caribbeans were moved not only by their own independence struggles but also by anti-colonial movements in Africa and the radical politics of black people in the United States. West Indian cricketers were particularly inspired by the Olympics Black Power salute of 1968. “We had been born in colonial times. We grew up in independent times,” Colin Croft of Guyana told Riley. Come the ’70s, “We started thinking like West Indians and not like Englishmen who were living in the West Indies.” Viv Richards, now regarded as one of the greatest batters of all time, was full of fervor. He called Bob Marley his “battlefield music” and wore Rastafarian colors on the field. Of the 1970s, he recalls, “That was the time when I think the heat was on for you to start getting up and standing up because of some of the things you felt were happening worldwide.” He added, “My bat would have been my sword at that time.” Defeat Down Under The notion of cricket as an expression of anti-colonial politics had been developing for decades, but it was institutionalized when Clive Lloyd became captain of the West Indian team in 1974. Lloyd was explicit with West Indian cricketers that they were playing to represent and embolden black Caribbean people — people abused by slavery and colonization, whose freedom was still constrained by racism and poverty. The team looked up to Lloyd and took his words to heart. Lloyd’s team was subject to its first major test in Australia in 1975. Australia was a formidable opponent, having pushed the envelope in “fast bowling,” pitching the cricket ball at incredibly high speeds. Fast bowling not only makes the ball harder to hit but increases the rate and severity of injury, particularly in the days before modern protective gear. The goal wasn’t to harm the batter, but neither was fear of injury throwing batters off their game an unwanted side effect. Not only were the Australian players fearsome; the crowd was intimidating, too. Cheering on Australian fast bowler Dennis Lillee, they chanted “Lillee, Lillee, kill, kill, kill!” In addition to the bloodthirsty chants, the all-white crowd hurled racial insults at the West Indies cricket team, which was composed of mostly Afro-Caribbean and a few Indo-Caribbean play-ers. They remember hearing taunts of “black bastard” and “go back to the tree where you came from.” The physical battery they sustained from the Australian fast bowlers and the verbal abuse they took from the crowds messed with the West Indian cricketers’ heads, and they played badly. Of six test matches against Australia, they lost five. The press called it a “pathetic performance.” The West Indies team re-turned home humiliated but determined to repair its reputation. To accomplish that, the team decided to commit to fast bowling like the Australians. Lloyd traversed the West Indies looking for club players who could bowl hard and fast — or learn to. One recruit was Jamaica’s Michael Holding, for whom the strategy was above all psychological. “Once you have the capa-bility of hurting a person with that ball,” he told Riley, “that person is not thinking about how to play the ball. He’s thinking about self-preservation.” The team debuted its new aggressive fast bowling style against India in March 1976. India stopped batting in protest during the fourth test match amid injuries caused by the West Indies’ fast bowling. Next up was England. Declaration of War England was cricket’s cradle, and the United Kingdom was the dominant colonial power in the Caribbean. For players and fans who associated the sport with West Indian independence, the symbolic stakes of defeating England in the summer of 1976 couldn’t have been higher. British decolonization in the West Indies was a live issue, a storyline still unfolding. Caribbeans were watching referendum results and cricket matches back to back. For example, several of the team’s players were from Antigua, including Viv Richards and formidable fast bowler Andy Roberts. In February 1976, between the Australian and Indian competitions, Antigua held a general election in which immediate independence from the United Kingdom was a central issue; opponents of independence prevailed. Defeating England was socially important for another reason. Between 1948 and 1970, nearly half a million people immigrated from the Caribbean to Britain as part of the Windrush generation. A large West Indian community now resided there, often facing not only miserable living and working conditions but also intense racism and xenophobia. The West Indian cricket team felt it had to play to represent Caribbeans back home and on British soil. The players were even worried about the racist abuse West Indians would face at work the next day if they lost. Then there was the business of groveling. Tony Greig maintained that he never meant to invoke the caricature of the servile colonial subject (his own father asked him later if he owned a dictionary), but that’s how the comment was interpreted. In Viv Richards: The Authorised Biography , Trevor McDonald wrote, “For the South African captain of an English team to publicly threaten to make the West Indies grovel was probably the closest any cricketer ever came to making a formal declaration of war.” David Tossell wrote in his book Grovel! that “The West Indies’ anger was felt on two levels — the implicit, if unintentional, racial insult and the slur on the team as cricketers.” For his part, “Lloyd was fed up with the West Indians being characterised as happy-go-lucky players who wilted when the going got tough.” He wanted to retire the “calypso cricketers” stereotype once and for all. The team brought all of this passion, resentment, and determination to the oval. Gordon Greenidge of Barbados had lived in England in his youth and had experienced prejudice there. “My anger came out in the way I played,” he told Riley. “I felt that to forcefully go at what I was doing, to attack, was a way of letting out that anger. It wouldn’t be right to take it out on a fellow human being, though you felt like that at times, but I’m sure gonna take it out on five-and-a-half ounces.” The English crowds were more restrained than those in Australia. More important, there was a huge Caribbean presence flying national flags, cheering on the West Indies team, and taunting Greig about his “grovel” comment whenever he hit the field. This time, it was the West Indians doing the fast bowling. The new strategy didn’t land well with everyone. The cricket press complained of dangerous ball speeds — despite the fact that the West Indies team had been inspired by the Australians, whose fast bowling hadn’t generated nearly as much outrage. “The world more or less por-trayed the West Indies team as brutal,” Greenidge told Riley, “bringing the game into disrepute. But the adrenaline that’s gonna be pumping, the tension that would have mounted from that ill-fated comment, you were gonna release that ball at a serious pace.” Viv Richards stood by the team’s strategy, which delivered major returns as the season progressed. “A lot of folks felt that we were spoiling the game, aiming to kill,” he told Riley. “No, man. Aggression meets aggression. That’s how I look at life. You fight, I’m gonna fight.” “We had a mission,” he added. “We believed in ourselves, and we believed that we were just as good as anyone. Equal, for that matter.” “Past History and Future Hopes” Between the late 1970s and the early 1990s, the West Indies cricket team was the undisputed best in the world. They beat England in the blazing hot summer of 1976 and then went on to beat England six more times in a row. The team won the first two Cricket World Cups, in 1975 and 1979, and did not lose a test series between 1980 and 1995. The press dubbed its four fast bowlers, who could each bowl a ball over ninety miles per hour, the Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse. The fate of West Indian cricket and West Indian social progress diverged in the 1980s. The team dominated the sport, while the Caribbean itself began to suffer economically under the weight of neoliberal global financial policy. For example, Michael Manley’s democratic socialist government in Jamaica, crucified by the International Monetary Fund and targeted by the CIA, met defeat in 1980, and internal strife and invasion dislodged a revolutionary government in Grenada just three years later. Still, it mattered in dark times that the Windies towered over the rest, that they had bested their former masters at a game of their devising. C. L. R. James wrote that while the English were bred into knowledge of their own society’s great achievements and long traditions, twentieth-century Caribbeans had none that they were aware of. It was cricket, he observed, that filled the vacuum. “What do they know of cricket who only cricket know?” James asked. Their knowledge ran deeper than that of those for whom cricket is a diversion, no matter how vigorously pursued. The situation has changed. The West Indies is no longer the best at cricket, nor is cricket so central to Caribbean identity. But as the summer of 1976 showed, there was a time when West Indians brought to cricket matches “the whole past history and future hopes of the islands” — a time when to experience cricket was to profoundly experience oneself.

It happens every time now. The United States jumps headfirst into a Middle Eastern quagmire, and, like clockwork, voices start rising, blaming it all on Israel. Bush administration, and the joint US-Israeli onslaught on Iran that began on February 28, 2026, led by the second Trump administration.The best-known “blame it on Israel” controversy remains the one touched off by John Mearsheimer and Stephen Walt. It began with an article of theirs published in the London Review of Books in 2006 before being developed the year after into a hefty book of close to five hundred pages, The Israel Lobby and U.S. Foreign Policy. The two authors began this work at a time when the Bush administration’s fiasco in Iraq had become so blatant that the US Congress had to intervene and appoint a bipartisan commission — the Iraq Study Group, also known as the Baker–Hamilton Commission — tasked with designing a way out of what had evolved into a damaging morass.This fact alone raises a question: Why hadn’t the two authors reacted in the same way to the previous devastating war waged on Iraq by the United States, the original 1991 onslaught led by the George H. Prior to the 1991 onslaught, Saddam Hussein’s Iraq had come out of its eight-year war against Iran with a swollen army and a huge debt. It invaded the oil-rich Emirate of Kuwait in August 1990 in an attempt to solve its economic problem and fund the preservation and development of its armed forces. Israel obviously had a huge interest in the destruction of that Iraqi state, and it would have been easy to build a compelling argument showing how both the Israeli government and the Israel lobby had intensively and decisively intervened on the side of the pro-war section of the US establishment.It’s no great mystery why Mearsheimer and Walt omitted this history: they both supported that war, which they likely saw as serving the United States’ national interest — a concept that refers to a state’s interest in a traditional “realist” manner, with no regard to the specific social interests this state represents. The two academics’ conservative brand of realism thus construes the world as divided among powers whose legitimate interests stem from their geopolitical position and their drive toward achieving regional or global hegemony.As realists, Mearsheimer and Walt were very much in tune with the senior Bush administration’s own realism, which converged with the view of Washington’s Arab allies that, in the absence of a credible alternative suiting American hegemony, the overthrow of Saddam Hussein’s regime was to be avoided. This is why the 1991 onslaught stopped short of marching on Baghdad, a position that led to bitter recrimination from some neocons and their Iraqi allies, who saw in it a betrayal of their aspiration for regime change. Trump would later heap scorn on the regime change ambition once it had failed, fully endorsing the so-called realist approach.The contrast between the 1991 war and the 2003 invasion was blatant — not only because the neocon-stuffed Bush administration pursued regime change, much to the annoyance of the Arab oil monarchies, which rightly feared that the real beneficiary would be their nemesis Iran, but also in the war’s rationale from the standpoint of the US national interest as Mearsheimer and Walt perceived it.They found the decision to overthrow Saddam Hussein “difficult to fathom.” Hussein was, they granted, “a brutal tyrant with worrisome ambitions — including a desire to obtain WMD — but his own incompetence had put these dangerous objectives out of reach. Ibrahim Traoré, president of Burkina Faso since October 2022, polarizes opinion, to say the least. Some hail him as the hope for a new Pan-Africanism, the long-overdue end of French imperialism in West Africa, and resource sovereignty. Others point to the authoritarian traits of the regime and the repression of trade unionists, civil society activists, and journalists. The scope of Traoré’s political projects, and the pace at which they are being implemented, is striking. This has generated enthusiasm, particularly among a younger generation that vehemently rejects French neocolonial dominance and is deeply frustrated by a gerontocratic political system unable or unwilling to deliver reform. The question remains, though, whether a military government can be the alternative, and whether the ends justify the means. Coups to Fight Terrorism Traoré and his Patriotic Movement for Safeguard and Restoration (MPSR 2) seized power through a coup d’état on September 30, 2022. He overthrew the previous military government of Lieutenant Colonel Paul-Henri Sandaogo Damiba, which had itself come to power only in January 2022, also through a coup. Damiba’s junta called itself MPSR. Neither Damiba nor Traoré came to power with a worked-out political or ideological agenda. Both justified their coups by pointing to the previous government’s failure to cope with the security crisis. Attacks by jihadist groups had increased sharply since the late 2010s, resulting in several thousand deaths a year, the closure of schools and medical facilities, and the internal displacement of more than two million people. Given that his only agenda was the “fight against terror,” no one would have pre-dicted at the end of 2022 that Traoré would carry out political reforms, some of them far-reaching, in the space of just three years. In January 2023, he called on France to withdraw its remainingtroops, four hundred special forces soldiers who were supposed to be fighting the jihadist groups. The withdrawal of French troops was popular across the region, and in Burkina Faso in particular. Radical social movement organizations had been mobilizing against French neocolonial dominance for many years. At the end of the 2010s, however, such mobilization spread well beyond the circles of radical activists. This broadening had to do with the rapidly deteriorating security situation and the perception that the well-equipped French special forces were unable — or unwilling — to push back the jihadist groups. “It’s not for our beautiful eyes that France is here,” one activist remarked in December 2020. Traoré has taken up that anti-French sentiment, which had been building since 2019, and used it to generate support for his government. A New Geopolitical Bloc Along with the rapid change in relations with France since 2022, there has been a shift in relations with neighboring Benin and Côte d’Ivoire as well as with the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS). ECOWAS suspended the memberships of Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger after military coups in those countries. After the coup in Niger in July 2023, ECOWAS and France put pressure on Niger’s junta to reinstate the previous president, Mohamed Bazoum. Benin, Côte d’Ivoire, and Senegal signaled their willingness to send troops to Niger. The military governments in Mali and Burkina Faso had obvious reason to fear a similar intervention. They declared their solidarity with Niger and their intention to support it militarily if necessary. Unsurprisingly, Burkina Faso, Mali, and Niger announced their withdrawal from ECOWAS in January 2024. Their exit was formally confirmed one year later. The adoption of the Liptako-Gourma Charter by the three countries in September 2023 laid the foundations for the Alliance of Sahel States (AES), established in July 2024 . Created as a mutual defense pact after the crisis of July–August 2023, it was primarily a response to external pressure, though it has since developed well beyond those origins. A joint TV channel, AES TV, and an investment and development bank were set up in 2025. Defense and security policy remain at the center of the alliance. In December 2025, the five-thousand-strong AES Unified Force was launched. The AES does not represent a new Pan-African movement so much as a new geopolitical bloc reflecting recent regional and global shifts — “like a marriage of reason ,” as AES TV’s director, Salif Sanogo, put it. More Economic Sovereignty, Fewer Civil Liberties? The Traoré government has also implemented a number of reforms in domestic and economic policy. A new mining law adopted in July 2024 promotes local processing of minerals, formalizes artisanal mining, and grants the state the right to buy shares in mining projects. The mandatory state stake in a mine rose from 10 to 15 percent. In August 2024, the government acquired two gold mines (out of twelve in the country, all operated by multinational mining companies) for $90 million. It transferred them to the state-owned Société de Participation Minière du Burkina Faso (SOPAMIB). Prominent projects aimed at boosting the local economy include two tomato processing plants, largely financed by the state and inaugurated by Traoré in September and December 2024. Strengthening domestic value creation and increasing state participation in the mining sector have long been demands of social movements and radical civil society groups. Ironically, those demands are now being met by a government that has proven deeply repressive toward the same movements and activists. The military government has been able to implement its projects in part because there is effectively no institutional room for opposition, and because the scope for criticism by the media, social movements, and organized civil society is sharply limited. The civilian government of Roch Marc Christian Kaboré, ousted by Damiba’s coup in January 2022, had already restricted freedoms of assembly, expression, and the press during Kaboré’s first term as president (2015–19), citing the terrorist threat. Party activity has been suspended since October 2022, and in February 2026 political parties and groups were formally dissolved . The Burkinabè Journalists’ Association had already been dissolved in March 2025, and its president and vice president, along with a number of other journalists, were detained . Some of them later appeared in a video wearing military uniforms, having been conscripted to fight terrorism. Control over the media is tight. The country’s most important independent radio station, Radio Oméga, was suspended in August–September 2023 after it broadcast an interview with a representative of a civil society organization on the coup in Niger. It was suspended again for three months in August 2025 after referring to the government as a junta — a term that the authorities deemed “inappropriate” as well as “prejudicial and offensive .” Social media plays a key — and problematic — role. While the space for independent coverage is limited, social media is used heavily to generate support for Traoré and his government. He has become a star in this field within a very short time. Many of the images and videos circulating on social media are AI-generated: praise for Traoré from Beyoncé, Justin Bieber, and Rihanna, for example, or a message from the pope. It is virtually impossible to trace how these videos were made. The hype on social media has combined with a lack of alternative sources of information and a strong desire, especially among the younger generation, for real change in political and eco-nomic structures — above all, an end to neocolonial and imperialist exploitation and domination. The result is Traoré’s image as a Pan-Africanist revolutionary. Sankara and Traoré References to Thomas Sankara, with whom Traoré is frequently compared, form part of this image. Sankara, who served as president from 1983 to 1987, was also an ambitious young military officer who came to power through a coup. An icon of Pan-Africanism, he is often called “ Africa’s Ch e.” In terms of political and ideological content, the comparison between the two men holds only to a limited extent . But it has a real impact on public discourse, and Traoré knows how to use allusions to Sankara to gain legitimacy. In 2023, Traoré’s government declared the anniversary of Sankara’s assassination, October 15, a national holiday for the first time. It honored Sankara with the title “hero of the nation” and renamed one of Ouagadougou’s central roads, Boulevard Général Charles de Gaulle, as Boulevard Thomas Sankara. In fact, Sankara had already been rehabilitated and designated a national hero in 2000 , under Blaise Compaoré, the man who overthrew him. Traoré opened the Thomas Sankara Memorial in Ouagadougou in 2025, although planning for the memorial and the commission granted to the Burkinabè-German architect Francis Kéré date back to 2017, during Kaboré’s first term. Polarization The conditions for movements and activists have changed significantly in Burkina Faso in recent years. Human rights organizations, youth groups, and labor unions active in the country for decades had been struggling with the security crisis since the late 2010s, which made large-scale activity across the country difficult. Restrictions on civil rights under the state of emergency further limited their scope for action. Under the 2023 policy of “ general mobilization ,” the transitional government empowered itself to requisition people and goods for the “fight against terror.” It has used the decree to conscript journalists and representatives of civil society organizations and trade unions into counterterrorism operations across the country. While the organizations that mobilized the popular insurrection of 2014 have been pushed into the background, a number of new groups and social media activists have emerged in recent years. They are less concerned with their own political and ideological agendas than with supporting the current government. The most visible are the “Wayiyan,” groups of mostly young men in cities who gather at roundabouts and other central locations to observe the “ smooth running of the transition.” According to political scientist Rahmane Idrissa , they have threatened to attack anyone organizing a ceremony to mark the anniversary of the 2014 insurrection. It is hard to see why a progressive popular revolution — as Traoré proclaimed it on April 2, 2025 — would need to restrain movements and media that have long cam-paigned for policies such as resource sovereignty. Traoré cultivates his image as a revolutionary, as Sankara once did, and the association with Sankara is central to that image. Sankara’s politics are often described as a form of pragmatic socialism. Traoré’s outlook is rather more pragmatic than socialist. This approach may be strategically astute, as it appeals not only to the younger generation but also to powerful religious and traditional elites. Sankara’s experience showed how difficult it is to overcome those elites. He tried anyway. Traoré’s conditions are, of course, quite different from those under which Sankara operated. Sankara did not face the threat from jihadist groups that now dominates the situation in Burkina Faso. But the main problem with the comparison is that the link to Sankara serves as a source of legitimacy for Traoré, giving rise to a personality cult that hinders rather than promotes critical debate on political strategy and vision.

Confronting the ecological crisis

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: Tempest ／ 著者: Paul Fleckenstein ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-09T20:21:05+00:00

T he ecological crisis is upon us. It touches everywhere on the planet. Its character is complex, manifesting in floods, fires, disease, eradication of forests and fisheries. Interacting factors worsen globally connected catastrophes. Ian Angus’s Metabolic Rifts: Capitalism Assault on the Earth System makes an important contribution to understanding and guiding a socialist response to these compounding ecological crises. Angus is an ecological Marxist scholar and founding member of the Global Ecosocialist Network . Metabolic Rifts connects modern science, history, and Marxism’s metabolic rift theory. Angus says that our ecosocialist politics must be based on a concrete scientific understanding of how our world has evolved, how it is changing today, and where those changes may take us. The way we build socialism, and the kind of socialism that can be built, will be profoundly shaped by the state of the planet we must build it on. If our programs and activity don’t have a scientific basis, all our efforts to change the world will be in vain. (5) The book accessibly explains metabolic rift theory, the key insight of Marxist ecology. For Karl Marx, Frederick Engels, and subsequent Marxists in this tradition, the Earth’s metabolism is not a metaphor but a material reality. Interacting social, biological, chemical, and geological processes make life possible. Metabolism Marx used the concept of metabolism in three related ways. First, there is universal metabolism, Earth’s life-support system. This refers to the biological, chemical, and geological cycles on which all life depends. Social metabolism is the way human society relates to the rest of nature. Social orders change over time, and class society—and then capitalism marks a significant historical shift. Finally, metabolic rift is capitalism’s tendency to disrupt Earth’s cycles. The key factor driving this disruption is the subordination of human needs to capitalism’s drive for profit and accumulation. This process breaks the metabolic connection between humans and the rest of nature. Capitalism cannot change the natural laws governing life itself. Drawing on soil science, Marx first analyzed the rift between town and country. In England, working-class urbanization and the separation of agricultural production and people meant that the food and human wastes previously returned to the soil flowed as pollution into rivers. This process broke the nutrient cycle and undermined soil fertility. The metabolism of the earth includes material and energy cycles operating on time frames ranging from hours to hundreds of millions of years. These include the water cycle, the carbon cycle, and the nitrogen cycle. With the attention rightly given to global heating and disruption of the carbon cycle, which is mostly caused by fossil fuels, other disruptions, like with the nitrogen cycle, are less appreciated, although no less important. A handful of healthy soil contains hundreds of millions of microorganisms. Soils are the most species-rich habitat on Earth. Healthy soil is necessary for fertility and absorbing carbon, and for nutritious food. Angus traces the history of soil destruction to the town-country rift, which then led to imperial struggles to secure nitrogen-rich guano deposits to restore fertility. Ultimately, there was the development of industrial production and its complex of cash crops and fossil-fuel-derived nitrogen fertilizer. Farmers grossly over-apply nitrogen fertilizer, wasting two-thirds of it, polluting water and air, and disrupting a key Earth System metabolic cycle. Capital versus the Earth System The main part of Angus’s book is titled “Capital versus the Earth System.” It traces the ecological revolutions, including photosynthesis and land-based organisms, through which life and the rest of the planet historically co-evolved. This evolution created the conditions for more life and, ultimately, the stable equilibrium of the Holocene epoch over the last 10,000 years. Historically, relatively small changes in temperature or oxygen levels supported large changes in the biosphere. This insight is important for understanding ecological tipping points, when incremental change exceeds a threshold and may cause major changes in the Earth System. Holocene chart in Metabolic Rifts (108). Angus summarizes the distinctive anti-ecologic logic of capitalism as being characterized by “generalized production of commodities: profit maximization; competition among multiple capitalist; short-termism, and unending accumulation” (115). These are inter-related dynamics that, among other outcomes, lead to uncontrolled growth. Ecologically, this means an ever-increasing linear flow of material and energy through the system that is in direct conflict with the universal metabolism. Angus writes, The growth imperative is not, as liberal environmentalists often argue, just a mistaken policy or a false ideology. It is a fundamental structural feature of capitalism. Uncontrolled growth is an inevitable result of the profit system, of capitalism’s inherent drive to accumulate ever more capital.” (122) The Anthropocene epoch Angus has been an important proponent of the theory that we have entered into a new Anthropocene epoch. On this view, the Earth has transitioned from a period of temperature and ecological stability known as the Holocene and is now in the capitalist-driven and unstable period of the Anthropocene, a qualitatively new geological epoch where human activity has fundamentally reshaped the Earth. The reference point for this thesis is the post-World War II period known at the Great Acceleration. This marks an unprecedented era of fossil fuel enabled economic growth. The charts below show the general trends. The Great Acceleration: Socio-Economic Trends Since 1750. Image credit: A Planetary Health Check . The Great Acceleration: Earth Systems Trends Since 1750. Image credit: A Planetary Health Check . In an Earth Systems framework, the result of capitalist production is that key planetary boundaries for maintaining a stable biosphere are being exceeded. The ecological crisis is much more than about global heating and the need for green energy at scale. Exceeding boundaries also risks creating new tipping points, producing even worse outcomes. In one global heating scenario, for example, after a certain point of warming, a cascade of factors could set in to produce Hot House Earth, an environment inhospitable to human society and many existing forms of life. Rough Guide to Planetary Boundaries. Image credit: A Rough Guide to Planetary Boundaries . Scientists share with socialists and millions of oppressed and exploited workers a sense that we need a radical break with the status quo to resolve escalating crises and deteriorating living conditions. Angus quotes from the 2018 landmark earth science article “Trajectories of the Earth System in the Anthropocene,” published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences: “Incremental linear changes to the present socioeconomic system are not enough to stabilize the Earth System. Widespread, rapid, and fundamental transformations will likely be required to reduce the risk of crossing the threshold and locking in the Hothouse Earth pathway: these include changes in behavior, technology and innovation, governance, and values.” (215) Ecosocialist strategy While revolution must be the goal of ecosocialists, this doesn’t mean we have no options for immediate political struggle. Ecosocialists need to help elaborate necessary non-incremental and revolutionary social and ecological changes. But we must also contribute politically and practically to immediate fights for reforms. Angus makes the case that our collective goal can’t be to govern nature, as capitalists and ecomodernists urge, but to democratically govern our dynamic interaction with the Earth. For Metabolic Rifts, a guiding goal is expanding the commons—the democratically regulated and accessible provisioning of our basic needs. Housing, education, healthcare, and food shouldn’t be commodified but freely available to all. The original expropriation of direct producers from the land was integral to early capitalist development. Solving the general metabolic rift requires overcoming the social rift between workers and their communities from the means of livelihood. The long process of original expropriation, of evicting peasants from the country and forcing them into cities, was a long, extremely violent, and contested process in Europe. Ian Angus’s earlier book The War Against the Commons: Dispossession and Resistance in the Making of Capitalism chronicles this history and points to the role of popular resistance and the need to reestablish a conception of the commons. Other goals—from closing fossil fuel corporations and expropriating agribusiness, to banning single-use plastic and other chemicals that nature cannot absorb and recycle, to paying international ecological debts and abolishing the U.S. military empire—would be part of the revolutionary transformation to ecosoicalism. Immediate reforms are challenging—and will not immediately affect the global crisis. But we must engage in struggles that people can learn from and that can have real-world impact. The expansion of the global commons can only come about through the mass struggle of the global working class, indigenous communities, and colonized peoples. From halting fossil fuel infrastructure, closing data centers, taking back public water supplies, and demanding public housing and transit to the struggle for a free Palestine and end to genocide, everything has an ecological dimension. Our challenge is to bring the broad scope of ecological breakdown and the need for democratic social and ecological renewal into all our struggles. Metabolic Rifts helps us do this. US votes against UN resolution promoting maps reflecting Africa’s true size

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Nicholas Mwangi ／ 公開日時: 2026-09-09T04:39:03+00:00

The United Nations General Assembly has backed an African-led initiative calling for a more accurate representation of the world’s continents, particularly Africa, in maps used in education, public communication, and other areas. On September 4, the General Assembly adopted the resolution titled “Correct the Map: Rebalancing Global Cartographic Representation and Promoting Equitable Representation of the World’s Regions, Particularly Africa.” The resolution was sponsored by Togo on behalf of the African Group and passed by an overwhelming 164 votes to one, with six countries, Estonia, Georgia, Lithuania, Moldova, Serbia, and Ukraine, abstaining. The United States was the only country to vote against it. Speaking before the vote, Yaryna Ferencevych, deputy US representative to the UN Economic and Social Council, dismissed the initiative as an “ideological project” rather than a meaningful priority for the United Nations. The vote represents an important moment in a campaign arguing that the maps through which generations have learned about the world have not always provided an accurate picture of the relative size of continents. Introducing the resolution, Togo’s minister for foreign Affairs, Robert Dussey, argued that the issue was not simply about replacing one map with another. It was about looking at how humanity has been taught to see the world. “Maps shape our understanding of the world,” Dussey told the General Assembly. “They guide education, nourish the imagination and influence collective perceptions.” For the African states behind the initiative, the question was therefore not just technical. The way the world is represented, he argued, influences how people understand the relative importance, size and place of different regions and peoples. What the resolution asks for Beyond encouraging member states and international organizations to adopt more accurate cartographic representations, he pronounced that the purpose is strictly cartographic, scientific, and educational. “What we are doing here is promoting a more accurate representation of the relative sizes of the world’s regions. I would like to reassure everyone that what we are discussing is fundamentally a matter of science.” Stressing that the text invites UN entities, regional organizations, academia, think tanks, and technology companies “to cooperate in order to build national capacities, support research and innovation,” with particular attention to developing countries and African countries in map-making, geography, and geospatial technology. Adding that the resolution “fully respects existing mandates,” creates no new financial obligations, and does not establish any new institutional mechanism at the UN. Education is nonetheless central to it, the text encourages a more critical approach to how maps are taught in school curricula, including an explanation of projection methods, their characteristics, their limits, and their appropriate uses. Why this matters for Africa The debate centers largely on the widespread use of the Mercator projection , developed by Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator in 1569. The Mercator projection was designed primarily for maritime navigation. Its ability to preserve directions made it particularly useful to sailors navigating long distances. But that advantage comes with significant distortions in the relative size of landmasses. The further a territory is located from the Equator, the larger it appears on the map. Greenland, for example, can appear roughly comparable in size to Africa, even though Africa is around 14 times larger. Africa covers approximately 30.4 million square kilometers and is the world’s second-largest continent. The result is a world map in which countries and regions in northern latitudes appear disproportionately large, while Africa and other areas closer to the Equator appear relatively diminished. Togo’s foreign minister tied the resolution directly to several ongoing African-led initiatives: the Decade for African Roots and the African Diaspora (2021–2031), the Second International Decade for People of African Descent (2025–2034), and the final declaration of the Ninth Pan-African Congress. Correcting “disproportionate cartographic representations” of the continent, he said, is part of ensuring Africa is “appropriately recognized on the international sphere” and of “restoring the dignity of the continent and its diasporas” by correcting “historic distortions that we have inherited from our past legacies.” Adding, “By adopting this resolution, we’re not just correcting a map, we are contributing to correcting our perception,” he said, adding that knowledge “should elevate,” should “bring us closer, not divide us,” and should reflect the world “in all of its diversity, its truth, and its dignity.” He rejected the idea that this was a symbolic gesture, “Correcting the map is not simply an aesthetic or symbolic issue, but rather, it is a demand for scientific accuracy and educational accuracy.” Each region, he argued, matters and each people “deserves to be seen as they are” with Africa, “like all other regions,” belonging in humanity’s “universal consciousness,” recognized “in its full reality, its contributions, and its future” rather than “diminished by distortions from the past.” The overwhelming support for the resolution has been met with broad and positive reception across Africa and in many parts of the world.
