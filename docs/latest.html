Pension Is Wall Street's Casino

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Ellen Brown/Web of Debt ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-06T23:13:55+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. “Most billionaires don’t park their money in making more businesses. They park their money in the stock market. That is not meaningfully investing in opportunity, it’s just investing in other billionaires.” So said Abdul El-Sayed , U.S. Senator-elect from Michigan. He said that he would rather “have an economy that makes a thousand millionaires than one billionaire,” and that money that would otherwise go into the S&P 500 should go instead to “good schools, good infrastructure, health care, child care.” It’s a laudable sentiment, but those thousand millionaires would probably still be parking their money in investments designed to make more money without producing anything. When an investor buys Nvidia shares on the stock market, the money goes to whoever sold the shares, not to Nvidia. A company gets new capital only when it issues new stock. The profits from stock market trades go either to the buyer or the seller, depending on which way the penny falls. And it’s not just the billionaires and millionaires playing those financial markets. Our household savings, corporate funds and pension funds are gambling there as well. State and local pension funds hold $6.2 trillion , more than a fifth of the GDP of the U.S. economy. That massive pool of money is not building housing, infrastructure or local businesses. It is parked in Wall Street’s casino, bidding up the price of existing assets, while asset managers collect their fees whether the pension funds win or lose. And many pundits are predicting an imminent market crash, in which the funds will lose big. Worse, the returns to the pension funds are no better than they could get from a simple index fund without incurring outside management fees, and sometimes they are worse. Meanwhile, the nation’s only state-owned bank, the Bank of North Dakota, earned a 17.2% return on its capital in 2025 while lending into its state’s own economy, and paid $258 million of its profits back to the state. North Dakota’s money is safe, earning, and invested in local productivity. Related: AI Abundance: Meaning Beyond Work The House Always Wins “Everything feels like a casino,” said UK financial commentator Anna Bocca in a March 2026 video titled “ How Financialisation Rewired the Economy .” “ Financialization ” is the process by which financial markets and institutions have gained growing influence over economic policy and investing, shifting the focus from making physical goods and services to using money to make more money. The “house” in today’s financial casino is the asset manager collecting our pension money – Bocca names BlackRock, State Street, and Vanguard – and the house wins even when the pensioners lose. Consider New York City. Its five pension funds held $326.3 billion as of June 30, 2026. The city pays about $10 billion a year into them, even as it struggles to close a projected $5.4 billion budget deficit. What does the city get for its money? In 2015, then-Comptroller Scott Stringer examined ten years of results from the asset managers running the city’s pension funds. Managers of public stocks and bonds had beaten their benchmarks (the returns the same money would have earned in a standard market index such as the S&P 500) by $2.063 billion. But that was before deducting the managers’ fees. The fees consumed more than 95% of the gain, leaving the funds only an estimated $40 million ahead. Managers of the funds’ private investments (stakes in assets not traded on public exchanges, such as private equity and real estate funds) fell $2.6 billion short of their benchmarks after fees. Overall, the outside managers fell more than $2.5 billion dollars short of benchmark returns over the ten year period. The pension funds would have done better in plain index funds. “Right now, heads or tails, Wall Street wins,” Stringer said . Stringer’s study was cited by Scott Baker, managing editor at OpEdNews and a Public Banking Institute expert on pension funds, in a November 2025 article titled “ The Case for Selling off the New York City Pension Funds .” Baker asks, “Why do we need pension funds at all?” More on his answer after a look at pension fund financing as it has evolved over the last half-century. Related: The Digital Dollar That Was Too Democratic to Survive How Our Pensions Got into the Casino The evolution of pension fund financing was traced in a July 2025 report from the American Federation of Teachers (AFT) and other sponsors titled “ From Public Pensions to Private Fortunes: How Working People’s Retirements Line Billionaire Pockets .” The authors observed that before 1980, state and local pension funds were often treated as a “pay as you go” system: a portion of current employee premiums was used to pay benefits to retirees, similar to Social Security. Then a new “actuarial” accounting approach began treating any shortfall between a fund’s assets and its future obligations as an “unfunded” liability. The pressure to close that apparent gap “drove an unprecedented accumulation of assets” – money-producing investments such as stocks, bonds, real estate and stakes in private equity. Between 1980 and 2007, state and local pension assets grew from $183 billion to $3.2 trillion, from 6% to 23% of GDP. By 2024, they stood at $6.2 trillion (an equivalent percentage of GDP, which had also grown). Tom Sgouros, a public finance consultant, former senior policy advisor to the Rhode Island General Treasurer and fellow at Brown University’s Policy Lab, has questioned the need for this accumulation of financial assets at the expense of productive investment. In a 2022 report for the National Conference on Public Employee Retirement Systems , he pointed out that a public pension plan, unlike a private one, “need not worry about the disappearance of its sponsoring employer,” in this case the city. Cities can go bankrupt, but they will always be there. A new government will just replace the old one. Until the 1960s, according to a November 2024 housing report co-authored by Sgouros for Americans for Financial Reform (AFR) and Georgetown University, it was “unremarkable” for pension funds to invest in state and local government bonds. Today, however, it is rare. Most municipal bonds are tax-exempt and pay lower interest rates than corporate bonds; and pension funds, which pay no taxes, get no benefit from the tax break. The older arrangement, which Sgouros calls “fiscal mutualism,” meant that public workers’ savings financed public needs: their pension funds lent to their own cities and states for schools and other projects, and were paid back with interest. This mutually beneficial arrangement unraveled when pension funds began chasing higher yields in corporate bonds, forcing local governments to borrow on the open market at higher rates and fees. Meanwhile, most private sector workers have lost their guaranteed pensions altogether. According to a March 2026 Congressional Research Service report , only 14% of private sector workers had access in 2025 to a “defined benefit” pension (one that guarantees a set monthly payment for life, usually based on salary and years of service), compared to 70% with access to a “defined contribution” plan such as a 401(k), whose value rises and falls with the markets. Related: AI Abundance and the Future of Freedom “Patsies for the Private Equity Industry” According to Reason Foundation data , in 2001 public pension funds held 57.7% of their assets in publicly-traded stocks, 31.4% in bonds, and 9% in “alternatives” — private equity, hedge funds, real estate and commodities. By fiscal year 2024, 34% of pension fund investments were in the alternatives, which carry significantly more costs and risks even than the stock market. Private equity (PE) is particularly risky and controversial. It relies heavily on borrowed money, and investors must commit their money for ten years or more. The management fees are also far higher – typically 2% of asset values plus 20% of profits – and according to the AFT report, “PE managers frequently manipulate reported asset values.” Correcting for these factors, the report concludes, private equity’s “yield advantage is little more than a mirage, facilitated by leverage used for buyouts.” Yet public pension funds are the biggest backers of the private equity industry. According to the AFT report, public pensions “make up almost a third of all investors to private equity funds and contribute an astonishing 67 percent of their capital.” PE firms are notorious for the leveraged buyout, described by the AFR housing report as “using a company’s own assets to finance its takeover by private investors and then looting them after: selling off real estate, closing pension plans, abruptly shuttering less-profitable divisions and major layoffs.” Employment at companies bought by private equity shrinks significantly, and a leveraged buyout can result in bankruptcy. The report points to the devastating collapse of two private equity-owned hospital chains, Steward Health Care and Prospect Medical Holdings. What do the pension funds get for backing PE firms? In a 2020 study titled “ An Inconvenient Fact: Private Equity Returns & The Billionaire Factory ,” Oxford professor Ludovic Phalippou found that private equity funds “have returned about the same as public equity indices since at least 2006.” Yet the performance fees on funds raised from 2006 to 2015 were estimated at $230 billion. In an interview, Phalippou called it a “wealth transfer from several hundred million pension scheme members to a few thousand people working in private equity,” one that “might be one of the largest in the history of modern finance.” In an April 2024 podcast , financial journalist Bethany McLean countered the fiction that pension fund managers are sophisticated investors, stating, “ They’re just the patsies for the private equity industry .” Related: Fed Regime Change: From Big Bank Bailouts to Local Productivity Bailing Out the Casino Privatization, says Bocca, has “shifted all financial risk and reward away from the state and onto the individual pensioner,” while funneling trillions of dollars into the hands of global asset managers. BlackRock alone now manages a record $15.3 trillion . Bocca describes the pursuit of shareholder profit over company productivity as “cost cutting instead of long-term innovation, debt loading instead of reinvestment, outsourcing instead of stable employment, stock buybacks instead of wage growth.” When a private equity firm loads a company with debt and it collapses, “it’s often the workers, residents, or taxpayers who end up bearing the consequences.” Asset managers’ fees grow with the size of the pool, not necessarily with its performance; and when markets crash, the losses are absorbed by the pensioners. A case in point is Blackstone, considered the world’s largest asset manager and the largest landlord in the United States. Blackstone is funded by CalPERS and many other public pension funds. In 2021, it bought 5,600 affordable housing units in San Diego and raised rents by 43% to 65% in two years. “Among the most troubling aspects of the housing crisis,” wrote the authors of the AFR housing report, “is the extent to which workers’ own capital is being used to make it worse.” The pension fund that is supposed to secure a teacher’s retirement may be financing the landlord raising her rent. The asset managers’ fastest growing product is private credit — loans made directly to companies by investment funds rather than banks. According to the AFT report, private credit loans “are not regulated and not covered by securities laws or even rated,” and private credit “also relies heavily on public pensions.” It is now seeing rising defaults on its loans, which are often to companies owned by PE firms. Yet an August 2025 executive order directed regulators to ease the way for private equity and other alternative assets to be included in 401(k) plans. When the whole casino is in trouble, the public again bails it out. When markets froze in March 2020, the Federal Reserve hired BlackRock to manage its new corporate debt-buying programs. The flood of central bank money lifted asset prices, and those who owned the most assets gained the most. According to Oxfam, billionaires’ wealth rose more in the first 24 months of Covid-19 than in the previous 23 years combined. “Investors keep the upside,” says Bocca. “Society absorbs the downside.” Today, Moneywise reports , the U.S. has a record 989 billionaires, and the 400 wealthiest Americans are worth a record $8 trillion , up from $6.6 trillion a year earlier. Related: Rethinking America’s Financial Plumbing: The Wealth Concentration Engine A Better Way: Pay As You Go, and a Public Bank Scott Baker’s proposal for New York City is to get out of the money-making-money business altogether. The city could guarantee retirees their current benefits plus annual cost-of-living increases, and pay them as Social Security does, from current revenues. It could lay off the outside investment managers and sell off the fund assets gradually over about five years. Part of the proceeds could pay down city debt, immediately raising the city’s credit rating and lowering borrowing costs. The rest could capitalize a public bank. Baker envisions a bank with about $50 billion in deposits, lending to local small businesses and individuals that large commercial banks pass over. The stellar U.S. model is the Bank of North Dakota (BND), founded in 1919. By law , state funds are deposited in the BND, which uses them to back loans into the state, mostly in partnership with local banks. As noted above, BND earned net income of $231.8 million in 2025 – a 17.2% return – and paid $210 million into the state’s general fund and another $48.3 million to other state funds. There is no house taking a cut. Momentum for public banks is building around the country, as detailed at a Public Banking Institute conference in Philadelphia on October 22-24. In Washington State and New Mexico , enthusiastic support for public bank bills followed presentations earlier this year by BND president Don Morgan on the mechanics and benefits of the BND. City-owned banks are also in the works. On October 21, the Los Angeles City Council voted 11 to 3 to identify $460,000 for a feasibility study for a city-owned bank; and on November 3, two other California cities voted on public bank initiatives. San Francisco’s Proposition B would create a municipal finance corporation as the first step toward a city-owned public bank, and Berkeley voters will decide on a citizen-initiated parcel tax that would raise $58 million over six years to capitalize a public bank. Neither vote had been called as of November 5; California can take up to 30 days to finalize votes. In Philadelphia, where the City Council voted in 2022 to create a Public Financial Authority as a first step toward a public bank, advocates are pushing “ Project Beloved Community ,” a ten-year plan that includes a city public bank and proposes investing city pension funds in local housing, clean energy and community-owned businesses. In New York City, Mayor Mamdani and Comptroller Levine have begun moving city deposits to community lenders , a first step while advocates press Albany to pass the New York Public Banking Act . And at the national level, The National Infrastructure Bank Act , HR 5356, now has 65 co-sponsors. The money is already ours — our pension contributions, our tax dollars and our public deposits. Rather than handing it to a casino where the house always wins and the pensioners absorb the losses, we can put it to work building homes, businesses and infrastructure in our own communities, and keep the profits for ourselves. Remembering Dolores Huerta (1930-2026)

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: DSA Feed - Global ／ 著者: DSA Religion and Socialism Working Group ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-06T21:25:04+00:00

Dolores Huerta , who died on October 28 at the age of 96, was an honorary chair of DSA from 2012-2016. But her involvement with our organization went back as far as being a speaker at a 1975 convention of one of DSA’s predecessor organizations. My wife, Dolores Delgado-Campbell, and I met her even earlier,when we were co-chairs of the United Farm Workers (UFW) lettuce boycott committee in Sacramento. We entered the fray in 1972 when the corrupt leadership of the Teamsters Union signed sweetheart contracts with grape growers, committing one of the worst examples of union betrayal in U.S. history. These contracts effectively ended the grape strike of mostly Mexican American and Filipino workers that many churches and synagogues had supported. Working with Dolores and the UFW for the next decade shaped our own political and career paths and broadened our political perspectives. We paid a political cost, too. In 1973, because of her involvement in the lettuce boycott, Dolores Campbell was not rehired (i.e., de facto fired) from her position teaching Women ’s History and Mexican American History at American River College in Sacramento. After a seven-year struggle and assistance from her union, she regained the position and became a sought after professor. In a satisfying footnote to history, more than 50 years after her association with Huerta and la causa made her persona non grata, she brought Huerta to a celebration of women’s history at the college in 2012. The auditorium was filled with students, faculty and staff trying to get into the room. Dolores Huerta was a relentless bundle of spirit and energy, and it was almost impossible to say “No” to her, in large part because she always worked longer and harder than anyone else. Dolores was also a persistent supporter of DSA’s own Latino Commission (1983- 2004). She is best known for her co-founding of the United Farm Workers union with Cesar Chavez and many others including former DSA honorary chair Eliseo Medina, Philip Vera Cruz, and Larry Itleong. Her influence has been profound. The creation of the UFW changed the nature of labor organizing in the Southwest and contributed significantly to the growth of Latino politics in the United States.Her frequent public engagements at colleges, universities, and high schools, where she brought a Latina feminist perspective to labor, civil rights , and immigration issues highlights her long history and personal commitment to movement building. That commitment to community mobilization against corporate power produced a backlash against her. Texas even banned textbooks on U.S. history because they included her story. On the other hand, when I contributed to a popular history book for middle-school students, The Americans: A History of the United States (1970 & 1979), we included a recording Dolores made in 1968. After leaving UFW leadership in 1994, Dolores traveled the country organizing and encouraging women to step up and take leadership roles in unions , politics, and more. She grew to become one of the leading feminist leaders in the nation focusing particularly on empowering young women and Latinas. Dolores was raised in the Catholic faith, and her lifetime of work demonstrated her commitment to the ideals of the struggle for social justice.Her thinking evolved constantly. Earlier in her life, she was against abortion ; later she was a champion of reproductive justice. Her intensive work, recognized when President Barack Obama presented her with the Presidential Medal of Freedom, illustrated how the fight for union rights and wages also included the fight for women’s leadership in unions, immigrant justice , and reproductive freedom. In the last year of her life she broke a decades-long silence about being assaulted as a young woman by Cesar Chavez and spoke of the misogyny within the farmworker movement, the labor movement, and society in general. We consider ourselves blessed to have had many opportunities to learn from Dolores and to have worked alongside her in campaigns. For more information about her lifetime of union and social justice work, read her story at In Memory of Dolores Huerta . Author Duane Campbell, Dolores Delgado-Campbell, and Dolores Huerta in 2014 . Israel’s Shaky Democracy Continues to Crumble

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Jared Hillel ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-06T18:19:29+00:00

In July 1950, speaking at the annual Zionist Organization of America convention, US Vice President Alben W. Barkley asserted that Israel was “an oasis of liberty in the desert of despotism,” articulating a precursor to the oft-repeated adage “Israel is the only democracy in the Middle East.” While the expression cannot be traced to a singular person, the sentiment that Israel — a nation with free elections and civil liberties — stands alone amid a sea of authoritarian regimes has been cultivated by Western institutions and Israeli leaders since the state was founded. From the outset, Israel’s earliest laws legalizing persecution against Palestinians showed the incompatibility between Jewish supremacy and democracy. But the subjugation of one group is rarely self-contained, and throughout history, illiberal nations have almost always turned against a portion of their citizens, often as violence against the outgroup reaches its peak. In the midst of the bloodiest three years in the country’s history, and particularly during the election campaign, this tendency began to play out. Israel has disenfranchised voters , at times even contradicting its own discriminatory laws, in efforts to marginalize Jews perceived as a threat to Benjamin Netanyahu and his increasingly violent political agenda. Barriers to the Ballot In the past three years, emigration from Israel has reached record heights. According to a study published by Tel Aviv University, 270,000 citizens have left the country in the past three years. Between 2013 and 2015, the corresponding period in the previous decade, the number sat at 183,000. Like much of the American Jewish diaspora , those who have left in recent years have become deeply critical of Benjamin Netanyahu. While Israel guarantees every citizen over the age of eighteen the right to vote, it has never permitted absentee ballots. With the exception of diplomats , emissaries, and Israel Defense Forces (IDF) soldiers occupying territory in Gaza, Lebanon, and Syria, voters must be in the country on Election Day. With this election largely seen as one of the most consequential in the nation’s history, a survey taken before the election estimated that up to 67,000 expats could be returning home to vote. On October 27, Likud won twenty-seven seats, and Gad Eisenkot’s Yashar party won twenty-one. Netanyahu’s bloc took fifty-three seats, against forty-eight for the Jewish opposition bloc and nineteen for the Arab-led parties. No bloc won the sixty-one seats needed for a majority. The results have left the country facing uncertain coalition negotiations. Leading up to the vote, Likud tried to stop Israelis outside the country from coming back. In September, Likud petitioned the Central Election Committee, demanding it block Fly & Vote, an initiative by the nonpartisan American-Israel Democracy (AID) Coalition, which sought to help Israelis abroad return and cast a vote. Offering logistical help, including eligibility checks and passport renewals, the organization also coordinated six group flights and helped search for commercial flights. While participants were still required to pay their airfare, Likud argued the initiative violated financing laws and constituted election bribery. While the petition was ultimately dismissed, the AID Coalition agreed to not subsidize tickets, organize any additional group flights, or provide transportation and accommodation. This summer, a second spat broke out when hundreds of Israelis living abroad discovered they had been stripped of their voting rights after being classified by the Interior Ministry as nonresidents. The designation, known as Code 51, excluded citizens from the voter registry altogether. Petitioning the Supreme Court, the expats argued that they were not notified of their status change, with several only realizing when checking their electoral eligibility. The courts dismissed the case on procedural grounds, stating the claim was filed too late. While there is no definitive proof linking Likud to the Code 51 scandal, it is clear that the party has repeatedly tried to curtail the expat vote. Israeli newspaper Haaretz reported that informal discussions were held between senior Transport Ministry officials about the possibility of restricting designated landing slots at airports, making it more difficult to fly into the country. Similar to the proposed judicial reforms of 2023, where Netanyahu sought to weaken the power of the Supreme Court, this democratic backsliding has prompted furor among the Israeli left. But as is often the case with liberal Zionists, the far greater repression affecting Palestinians remains largely ignored. Defending the Ethnocracy The AID Coalition describes itself as a group “strengthening, and preserving the democratic foundations of the State of Israel.” While a valiant objective, a more honest statement would be about preserving an ethnocracy. Israeli Professor Oren Yiftachel describes this form of governance as one that “promotes the expansion of the dominant group in contested territory and its domination of power structures while maintaining a democratic facade.” This dynamic has been present since the state’s founding. While the 150,000 Palestinians who remained in their homeland after the Nakba were declared citizens and granted the right to vote from the beginning, the community also faced systematic discrimination from the outset. From 1948 until 1966, Palestinian citizens of Israel were placed under military rule , which included measures that restricted their movement, prohibited political organizing, and imposed arbitrary curfews. Simultaneously, the government confiscated hundreds of thousands of dunams of land, uprooting farmers from their livelihood and forcing many to turn to low-wage labor to survive. In 1967, a year after lifting the military administration, Israel would turn their attention toward Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza after occupying both territories in the Six-Day War. Assuming control of around one million Palestinians, residents of the newly occupied territory were placed under military rule. This time citizenship and the right to vote were withheld from them, since such incorporation would threaten the Jewish demographic majority. As Jewish settlers moved into the occupied territories, aided by generous Israeli government subsidies, an apartheid regime took shape, as authorities applied separate laws, courts, and democratic rights to Palestinians and Israelis living in neighboring areas. Although Israel disengaged from Gaza in 2005, it lay siege to the Strip two years later, imposing a crushing blockade. After the October 7 attacks, it returned yet again as the occupying power, this time unleashing a genocide that has killed more than 74,000 Palestinians. In the West Bank, the apartheid regime continues, as roughly 500,000 settlers, excluding those in East Jerusalem, live with full rights outside of Israel’s internationally recognized border, beside Palestinians who cannot vote, travel freely, or even build on their ancestral land. Amid the dire situation in the West Bank and Gaza, the plight of Palestinians living inside Israel is largely forgotten. Yet these 2.1 million residents continue to live as second-class citizens. In 2018, the Israeli Knesset passed the quasi-constitutional Nation-State Law , stipulating that self-determination is unique to the Jewish people, without guaranteeing equality for its other citizens. Furthermore, Palestinian citizens of Israel continue to face entrenched discrimination in urban planning, economic development, and educational opportunities. The Authoritarian Turn In 2024, the Varieties of Democracy Institute downgraded Israel from a liberal to an electoral democracy. The demotion followed Netanyahu and his coalition’s attempt to overhaul the judiciary, their accusations the Supreme Court was “left-leaning,” and deterioration in protections against torture. This classification did not take into account the active atrocities that Israel was perpetrating in Gaza and the West Bank at the time. Yet since this reclassification, Netanyahu has shed more of the democratic facade he has long sought to uphold. More than five hundred humanitarian workers have been killed since the war in Gaza began. In December 2025, Israel attempted to bar thirty-seven nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) from operating in the West Bank and Gaza. The Supreme Court froze the ban, allowing the organizations to continue operating pending a final ruling. More recently, numerous foreign journalists have been turned away at the border during the election campaign, with others facing arduous interrogations and detention before being allowed entry. Perhaps most revealing is the government’s willingness to break long-standing supremacist laws, which uphold Jews as a privileged class in Israel. Starting in 2025, several Jewish activists were detained and deported for engaging in protective presence, a form of nonviolent solidarity work in which civilians deter violent settlers from harassing Palestinian communities. Earlier this year, a Jewish American academic on a three-year residency visa was denied entry after being accused of being a “left-wing anarchist.” After the backlash prompted by the film NAZA , in which IDF soldiers admitted to knowingly killing innocent civilians in Gaza, Netanyahu announced plans to introduce a bill that would strip those who defame the IDF of their Israeli citizenship. Last month, it was reported that two Jews were denied citizenship for having previously partaken in anti-occupation activism in the West Bank. This was the first known case of the state violating the Law of Return, which stipulates that every Jew is entitled to immigrate to Israel. Many saw Gad Eisenkot as the last chance to halt the country’s authoritarian drift. The election has left open the possibility that he could still lead the government, but he has no clear path to power. While he might reverse the emerging trend of discriminating against Jews and Israelis deemed insufficiently patriotic, none of his proposed policies address the core issues that make Israel an ethnostate. The election has not changed the reality for Palestinians, with or without Israeli citizenship, or the refusal of every major party to address these injustices. What Eisenkot may offer is merely a return to the illusion of democracy. Medi-Cal Crisis Deepens

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: Mark Kreidler ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-06T18:14:36+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. I met Rena Salamacha last summer, when the worst of H.R. 1’s cuts to federal Medicaid funding weren’t yet finalized, only feared. Salamacha, CEO of Mee Memorial Healthcare System in Monterey County, knew then that the system’s medical center in King City, serving rural and mostly low income patients, was already in trouble. “We don’t have services to cut,” the CEO said simply. More than a year later, that remains the reality. After all the shouting over Prop. 40, and the massive investment of time and money by California’s billionaire class to defeat the tax , the state’s fragile health care ecosystem is in the same place it was last week: trying, as Salamacha tries every week , to find ways to keep going. That road is about to get rougher. The worst of the new federal Medi-Cal cuts and rules changes (including work requirements) will kick in beginning next year, and from the California vote totals available so far, there won’t be Prop. 40 money coming in to offset any of the reductions. The result: Increased strain on existing hospitals and clinics, fewer services available, and more people going uninsured. As always, this long-term structural problem will land on the state’s most vulnerable. California stands to lose an estimated $30 billion a year in federal funding for Medi-Cal, the low-income program upon which roughly 14 million residents – more than a third of our population, including 4.5 million kids – rely for their health care. Congratulations, Xavier Becerra: This is your problem now. Prop. 40 certainly wouldn’t solve the health care crisis in California, nor was it designed to. It was as it appeared: a one-time 5% wealth tax on the richest 250 people in the state, billionaires all, which would generate up to $100 billion in revenue over about five years. Almost all of the money was to be used to offset federal funding cuts to health care. Not every detail was ironed out, as was known. That included the question of how the state would go about valuing certain assets – art, wine, automobiles – that are part of many ultra-wealthy portfolios, as well as how the new tax revenue would be distributed among health care providers. Also known: The union that spearheaded the Prop. 40 drive represents health care workers whose jobs could be on the line if, as expected, California’s care system contracts in the absence of funding. Now state lawmakers and Becerra, the incoming governor, must search for ways to protect Medi-Cal for as many residents as possible, or else face a wave of uninsured patients who won’t stop needing care just because they can’t pay for services. As many as 1.3 million Californians could lose health insurance as a result of H.R. 1, according to estimates. Related: Trump's ICE Crackdown Sparks California Voter Suppression Fears First as a member of Congress and then as secretary of Health and Human Services under President Joe Biden, Becerra fought for decades to expand Medicaid at the federal level. More recently, he opposed Gov. Gavin Newsom’s move to scale back coverage for undocumented adult immigrants in California, a cause Newsom had championed but retreated from due to budget issues. California’s budget cannot backfill the steep H.R. 1 funding cuts, so it’s a matter of limiting the damage. Becerra, who says he wants the state to be “the backstop for health care,” has a few options. Institute a “fair share” tax. Becerra is a strong supporter of state legislation that would levy fines on businesses that have a large number of employees on Medi-Cal – in other words, tax those companies for paying their workers so little that they qualify for the low-income health care program. The state Department of Finance is studying what such a program would look like, with a presentation to state legislators expected next spring. Reduce waste. As a candidate, Becerra said that California is “losing billions to fraudulent claims and misused benefits.” He said that as governor, he’ll create a dedicated health care fraud task force to go after such activity. Becerra also pledged to modernize Medi-Cal’s’ system to include automatic state-generated renewals, which he said would reduce paperwork errors that lead to eligible patients being mistakenly dropped from coverage – an idea that will be sorely tested by H.R. 1 rules that now require twice-yearly requalifying. Related: California Saves Millions on Home Care: Workers Pay the Price Raise taxes on the wealthy. Becerra opposed the Prop. 40 wealth tax, but he has indicated he’s open to increasing taxes on top income earners. Becerra told KCRA in Sacramento that the idea is to go after that money “if we can prove that you’re not paying your fair share. Austerity Is Now the Central Conflict in Brazilian Politics

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: André Singer ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-06T17:09:42+00:00

On October 4, Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, the son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, finished the first round of Brazil’s presidential election nearly two points ahead of President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva. Three weeks later, Lula turned the result around and won a fourth term in the October 25 runoff. In between, left-led demonstrations filled the streets of hundreds of Brazilian cities. The government also banned online betting, backed a forty-hour workweek, and raised the benefit of Bolsa Família, the cash-transfer program for poor families that Lula created in 2003. Few analysts know Lula’s political project better than André Singer. A professor of political science at the University of São Paulo, Singer served as Lula’s spokesperson from 2003 to 2007 and press secretary from 2005 to 2007. He developed the concept of Lulism in Os sentidos do lulismo (2012) and O lulismo em crise (2018), and his new book, Impasse: O Brasil diante da onda reacionária , came out in August. After the runoff, Singer spoke with Catarina Príncipe and Aldo Cordeiro Sauda for Jacobin about Bolsonarism as autocracy with a fascist bias, the Brazilian bourgeoisie’s silence during the campaign, and why the second-round mobilization was not populism. He also discussed the fight over austerity that awaits Lula’s fourth term. Catarina Príncipe You’ve written about the idea of an “autocracy with a fascist bias” to understand the phenomena of Donald Trump in the United States and Jair Bolsonaro in Brazil. Could you explain this idea further? André Singer Honestly, I can’t speak directly about Trumpism, because I don’t have enough expertise. So I’ll talk about Bolsonarism, the experience from which I formulated the idea of an autocratic tendency with a fascist bias. Studying the Bolsonaro presidency of 2019–2022, one can show empirically that he tried to reshape Brazil’s institutions in the direction of an autocratic regime. One of the major conflicts during his term was over the independence of the Federal Police. For an American audience: The Federal Police is equivalent to the FBI. It is a national police force for the country’s major security problems, because everyday problems fall to the state governments. But the big questions of corruption, organized crime, drug trafficking, and arms trafficking are all the Federal Police’s business. That is why a convention that successive presidents have observed since the return to democracy in the 1980s is so important: guaranteeing the Federal Police’s independence. The president of the republic appoints the director, but presidents have made a practice of appointing directors who have a certain independence from themselves. Bolsonaro made a point of violating that convention and appointing a Federal Police leadership at the service of his own political interests. I’m describing this because it is the most evident case of all: he tried to manipulate the Federal Police to concentrate power. Why do I say this autocratic tendency has a fascist bias? Because I find in it certain elements in common with historical fascism. Why don’t I say it’s fascist tout court? Because there are many elements that don’t correspond. Let me give one element in common: the activation, through propaganda, of the unconscious of the masses. It is a kind of advertising that aims to hook the voter, the citizen, and the supporter through unconscious identification rather than rational argument. That doesn’t mean there aren’t also arguments of a material nature, material interests; I’m not saying it is merely a technique aimed at the unconscious. But I think this is very typical of fascism and not of the nonfascist right. What isn’t in common with fascism? For example, there is no political party organized into militias, as in Italian fascism and then German Nazism. There is also no warlike rhetoric, in the sense of external war: Bolsonarism has a very strange nationalism of submission to the United States — something hard to understand. But it has no rhetoric of confrontation with other countries or of territorial expansion, an important element of historical fascism. Since some elements are in common and many are not, I proposed a way of understanding the Bolsonaro phenomenon. We are facing an autocratic tendency — that is proven — with a fascist tendency that we still need to test. Meanwhile, there is a more general theoretical problem: the concept of fascism isn’t well defined. Strange as it may seem, that is the problem we have to face: arriving at a concept of fascism that lets us understand the present situation. Obviously we are interested in understanding history, but with a view to the present and the future. Catarina Príncipe In an interview, I heard you distinguish between fascism from below and fascism from above. Which class does this autocracy with a fascist bias mobilize most? André Singer Marxist-inspired thought has already made clear the characteristics of historical fascism, and one stands out: fascism is a movement that comes from below, not from above. Leon Trotsky first made that contribution, in his writings on Germany, and it still holds; it is a fundamental distinction. Bolsonarism has this characteristic: it comes from below; it is not a movement of the ruling class. It draws in the middle strata, but it quickly spreads to intermediate popular layers, which form its backbone. So in Brazil today, the far right penetrates from below. Catarina Príncipe How do you analyze the relationship between the ruling class and politics, given the existence of what you call two cross-class coalitions: the Bolsonarist “confederacy” and the Lulist “ concertación ”? What is the composition of these two coalitions? André Singer I call one the “confederacy,” by a certain analogy with the nineteenth-century American case, and the other the “concertación,” by analogy with Chile after Augusto Pinochet. I developed these two hypotheses to understand the 2022 election. So I’ll answer with 2022 in mind, then briefly go through the changes in 2026. The idea of the confederacy was that a coalition of agribusiness entrepreneurs and service sector entrepreneurs in the cities formed. Certain evangelical denominations, which represent a kind of Brazilian Bible Belt, joined them. These denominations account for about 30 percent of the Brazilian electorate, and Bolsonarism has repeatedly won 70 percent of their vote. In the 2022 election, the whole of the security forces joined these two components, the entrepreneurs and the evangelical Bible Belt, from the armed forces down to the regional (state) police forces. These forces represent a large contingent. Besides everyone formally engaged in security work, Brazil has an enormous army of private security guards, because public security can’t cope with the very serious crime problem. These three tracks of the confederacy, so to speak, created an enormous corridor through the interior of the country. It runs from the north of Rio Grande do Sul to the south of the North Region, through the interior of São Paulo and Minas Gerais, the country’s most industrialized states. That corridor aligned with Bolsonarism, which largely explains its significant electoral power. In addition, the evangelical Bible Belt gives Bolsonarism a very strong bridgehead in Brazil’s large metropolitan regions. In 2026, there was a change: because of the failed coup attempt, the armed forces pulled back. They had been very important because Bolsonaro, a former army captain, went through the military academy with the men who, by coincidence, made up the high command when he took office. He brought them into the government, and so the armed forces were deeply committed to the Bolsonaro government. That is no longer the case. You can’t identify anyone of note from the armed forces in the campaign of his son Flávio Bolsonaro. But we know that the police forces backed that candidacy discreetly, rather than explicitly, as they had with Jair Bolsonaro’s government. I call it a confederacy, although it has no connection with slavery. But it does have this connection: it divided Brazil. It united the interior, as if against the big cities, even though, I repeat, the evangelical denominations have influence in the big cities. You also notice a certain prejudice against the Northeast and an underlying ideology that a more modern Brazil would be better off rid of a more backward Brazil. It is a kind of implicit ideology, which sometimes shows up more, sometimes less. As for the concertación: the name came to me because, to build the coalition that sustained him, President Lula made an alliance with sectors of the old Brazilian Social Democracy Party (PSDB). Until 2018, the PSDB had been the main rival of Lula’s Workers’ Party (PT). The current vice president, Geraldo Alckmin, headed the sectors willing to join. The PSDB as a whole has shrunk to a small party, and it stayed neutral: it backed Lula neither in 2022 nor now. But the alliance with Alckmin represented a union with former PSDB sectors willing to form the democratic front. So, as in Chile, the two great adversaries came together: in Chile, the Socialist Party and the Christian Democratic Party; in Brazil, the PT and the PSDB. Along with this party coalition, there was an important class coalition. Lulism represents the base of the Brazilian social pyramid, as everyone knows. But the alliance with this faction of the PSDB also brought in what I call the cosmopolitan bourgeoisie, the most modern sector of the Brazilian bourgeoisie, which backed President Lula in the second round of 2022, not the first. That was very important both for his victory and for the future of the government. In this 2026 election, that sector of the bourgeoisie did not speak out; it stayed neutral and backed neither Flávio Bolsonaro nor President Lula. João Moreira Salles — founder of Piauí magazine, an important filmmaker, and an heir to one of Brazil’s biggest banking families — wrote a very powerful article on the importance of that silence. The consequence of that silence was the acceptance, practically until the last minute, of a Flávio Bolsonaro victory. An extraordinary movement reversed that in this year’s second round. To conclude for now: the cosmopolitan sector of the Brazilian bourgeoisie, which stayed silent, is no longer explicitly present in the concertación. Catarina Príncipe You define Latin American populism as a mass phenomenon: in certain circumstances, classes take the form of multitudes with little or no class organization, connecting people and leader directly. Was this type of populism what secured Lula’s victory? And how can we reconnect this expression of the multitude with more sustained class organization? André Singer I think the concept of populism doesn’t apply to the second-round victory. It’s a very interesting question, and it’s going to give me some work, because I’ll have to develop something more sophisticated to account for the phenomenon. But at first sight, I think we had a social explosion — let’s call it that — without the characteristics of populism. It was more organized and more conscious of its aims than the populist phenomenon implies. To be clear, my theoretical framework is Ernesto Laclau’s old definition, from 1978, not that of the so-called second Laclau. It is a good one for thinking about populism in Latin America and, of course, in Brazil too. Why do I say the explosion is more conscious and more organized? More conscious because it aimed, first, to stop the far right. Flávio Bolsonaro won [the first round] by 2 percentage points and immediately began putting together his government and saying he would change the Constitution. That was a tactical error, even from his own standpoint: it had such an impact that the democratic sectors of Brazilian society immediately began to speak out and organize. That is one side of the phenomena. The other is a feeling that President Lula needed a fourth term, because the government itself began to change during the campaign. It isn’t obvious, but President Lula changed the campaign in mid-September without saying so: he began to take measures. One event seems revealing to me. On September 11, at a rally in Porto Alegre, he said, “I know you are finding products expensive at the supermarket,” referring to coffee and beef. That is very significant, because until then the government had not addressed the population’s material needs directly. From then on, the president not only spoke to those needs but began taking measures. For example, he raised the Bolsa Família benefit, which the government had not adjusted in four years, and he banned online betting, the “bets.” Remember that Brazil is living through a triple crisis: a cost-of-living crisis, a crisis of household indebtedness, and, on top of it, an epidemic of bets. People began gambling wildly, partly to earn extra income to pay off debts. Since betting companies design bets so that the great majority lose, people ended up worse off. President Lula faced great resistance to the ban. First, many companies are involved, and long-term legal contracts were already in place; second, soccer clubs, which are very popular and important in Brazil, depended to some extent on sponsorship from those companies. The other very important decision was the switch from six workdays and one day off (6x1) to five and two (5x2), cutting the workweek from forty-four to forty hours: probably the greatest labor victory since the 1988 Constitution. Congress passed it, but everyone knows it was the president’s initiative and that he committed himself to it. Structurally, of course, it is even more important than the betting ban. But the ban has symbolic meaning, because the president decided on it during the campaign: an act of boldness and courage in defense of the popular economy. Alongside it, the government took two measures to restructure the debts of the popular classes, and others such as free public transit on weekends, which speaks directly to the young people who took to the streets. What I mean is that all this is not populist rhetoric; these are actions. It is what I call a change in the character of the government. Finally, these sectors that took to the streets are not completely disorganized. The demonstrations took place in hundreds of cities, but the largest, and most unprecedented, were in São Paulo, the country’s biggest metropolis. I have been comparing the two October demonstrations in São Paulo, on October 11 and 24, with the great right-wing demonstration for the impeachment of President Dilma Rousseff in March 2016. There had been no demonstration of this kind for at least ten years. The Left led these demonstrations, although they were not only of the Left. They formed a democratic front, with centrist sectors and possibly even a democratic right: individually, people may have gone out to defend democracy. But the leadership clearly belonged to the Left, and these are possibly the largest left-wing demonstrations in Brazil’s history. The Brazilian left has organizations: the PT, the Socialism and Liberty Party (PSOL), the Communist Party of Brazil (PCdoB), the student organizations, the cultural associations, the unions, the neighborhood associations, and the popular movements, especially the Landless Workers’ Movement (MST) and the Homeless Workers’ Movement (MTST). On the center left, you have the Socialist Party of Brazil (PSB) and the Labour Democratic Party (PDT). In short, Brazil has an organizational structure, and it mobilized. So, from these two angles, I wouldn’t describe this as a populist phenomenon; something different happened. Aldo Cordeiro Sauda This year, you observed a pendular movement by the cosmopolitan wing of the bourgeoisie toward the far-right candidacy, the opposite of its support for Lula’s election in 2022. Internationally, US foreign policy shows the same pendular movement: sympathy for Lula under Joe Biden in 2022, explicit support for Bolsonarism under Trump in 2026. To what extent does the Brazilian ruling class’s shift toward Bolsonarism express mere deference to Washington? André Singer As far as I understand, the sector I call cosmopolitan — in the American case, the big technology companies — backed Biden in 2020. In 2024, it wasn’t very clear to me what they were doing, but they didn’t seem to back Trump either, with the exception of Elon Musk. Yet when Trump took office, all of them were there. In the Brazilian case, though, it seems plausible that a conversion of the American cosmopolitan bourgeoisie to the far right influenced the Brazilian bourgeoisie. Until the last minute, there was a certain expectation that this sector might speak out in favor of President Lula. That sector committed itself to democracy in 2022, in a movement broader than support for President Lula. In August 2022, at a demonstration in São Paulo, those business leaders issued a letter. They said they would not accept, under any circumstances, a refusal to recognize the election result — exactly what the White House said. Those two positions were very important in keeping most of the armed forces from joining the coup, because the military knew it could not govern without that support. So you see: there is a Brazilian phenomenon. A link to a transformation in the United States is a possible hypothesis. Catarina Príncipe Our understanding of the question is that the Brazilian cosmopolitan bourgeoisie is staying neutral now because of the political change in the United States from Biden to Trump. At the time of the coup attempt, the Biden presidency was very important in stopping it from going forward. Now, with Trump, there might be some attempt at direct intervention in Brazilian politics. So, part of the cosmopolitan bourgeoisie doesn’t want to take a position today, to avoid a falling-out with US policy and with a possible Bolsonaro victory. It’s less a direct mirror between fractions of the bourgeoisie. André Singer Then I’ll take advantage of your interpretation to add something that makes the problem more complex. The major point of division between that sector of the Brazilian bourgeoisie and Lula’s third government is the fiscal deficit. That sector demands a big cut in public spending, which in its view would have to fall on pension benefits, and it wants to delink them from the minimum wage. The Brazilian Constitution links the two, so raising the minimum wage in real terms also raises all benefits. The other target is the constitutional floor for health and education: the Brazilian Constitution requires that a certain share of annual tax revenue go to health and education, and this cannot go down. The bourgeoisie’s aim is to remove that floor from the Constitution, so that this spending falls. So, we have a very serious issue: on one side the pension system, all the retirees; on the other, the two main social policies that form the social core of the 1988 Constitution, health and education. That is the main problem. We still don’t know whether there is also a certain caution or a desire to align with the American cosmopolitan bourgeoisie. Aldo Cordeiro Sauda National sovereignty took center stage in the campaign, perhaps for the first time since the return to democracy. What role did this issue play in the second-round turnaround against Flávio Bolsonaro? André Singer The Trump administration’s offensive against Brazil — the tariffs and its direct or indirect intervention in the elections — has put national sovereignty on the agenda. Trump justified the first tariffs by saying that Brazil was persecuting his preferred candidate, Jair Bolsonaro. Brazil today does not have a very strong nationalist current. A nationalism is now starting to grow, because the global situation has changed and the country faces political-economic aggression — let’s call it that. So nationalism has come onto the agenda, but in my opinion — and I may be wrong — it has still not become a central issue. Catarina Príncipe Lula wants to transform the Brazilian economy by investing in the green economy, infrastructure, and artificial intelligence (data centers) and by using Brazil’s resources in rare metals and minerals. How much room for maneuver does he have to do this, given the New Fiscal Framework, the Lula government’s 2023 fiscal rule capping annual spending growth at 70 percent of revenue growth? How should we think about his government’s relationship with the bourgeoisie in this fourth term and the new balance of forces? Do you believe the bourgeoisie will tend to deepen its conflict with the PT, intensifying the pendular movement? And in this context, what happens to the mass mobilization from below that we saw in the second round? André Singer I think this is the central point regarding the fourth term: what the outcome of this conflict will be. The stock market fell nearly 14 percent the day after the election. As I have been saying, the market has no vote, but it has money, and its message is clear: if you don’t cut public spending, we’re leaving. The question of fiscal adjustment is truly central, because the bourgeoisie is demanding harsh austerity. In my opinion, the demand for austerity is the fundamental issue in Brazilian politics today, but in general nobody puts it openly: it remains an implicit debate. That is partly because we are not managing to politicize the problem, to make the popular classes, the working class, aware that this is what is at stake. This is the main confrontation. This organizes Brazilian politics. In my recent book, Impasse : O Brasil diante da onda reacionária , I tried to show that this issue splits the democratic front in two. It is, as Americans say, an elephant in the room: everyone treats it as if it didn’t exist. I felt that my role was to bring it to the surface; obviously I can’t do that alone, but I can contribute to some extent. The conflict is immediate, because the transformation Brazil needs, which runs through reindustrialization, is a gigantic task. Brazil is an enormous country, and you don’t change a structure of this order without major investment. This transformation can’t rely on public investment alone; there must also be private investment. But we have to think with the instruments we have. Perry Anderson says Keynesianism no longer works, so we need to create new mechanisms; but until we have them, I’ll use the old ones. The state will have to induce this development process, although it also has to bring in other investors. There is, therefore, an incompatibility between the country’s needs and this radical cut in public spending, which goes in the opposite direction. This is the central point. Even if the terms of the debate were not clear, I think the large demonstrations in hundreds of Brazilian cities point toward the government having this commitment. We’ll have to follow events to see how it unfolds. Catarina Príncipe Your earlier book defined Lulism as “combating poverty without confronting capital.” Can Lulism go beyond itself, and does it want to? Is there room to do so today, given the current party arithmetic in Congress? And how can the Brazilian left organize to force it to take measures that go beyond welfarism? André Singer I’ll start with Congress, which I think is simpler. In the Congress that takes office in 2027, the far right has grown, not at the expense of the Left but of other forces on the Right. In the Chamber of Deputies, it went from 91 to 121 deputies, out of 513 seats. That is significant: since the return to democracy, we would hardly find such a large single-party caucus. But it decides nothing on its own. What we will have is permanent negotiation, which is already happening: Lula’s third government already negotiated permanently with what we in Brazil call the Centrão . To be clear, the Centrão is the established right, the traditional right, so to speak. It generally holds the majority in Brazil’s National Congress, because the Brazilian electoral system reproduces small local oligarchies that tend to dominate legislative elections. This is structural in Brazil, and it will continue. President Lula has specialized in negotiating with those sectors. The third term was the most difficult in this respect, because Congress gained power from what we in Brazil call the “secret budget” ( orçamento secreto ) [budget amendments through which lawmakers steer federal funds without disclosing who requested them]. Even so, the president managed to pass his two boldest proposals. The first was the income-tax exemption for those who earn up to R$5,000 (about US$940) a month, offset by a levy on those who earn more than R$50,000 (about US$9,400). The Chamber of Deputies approved it unanimously. The second was the change of the work schedule from 6x1 to 5x2, which we have already discussed. The Chamber had already approved it unanimously, and the Senate has just passed it by an overwhelming majority. This shows that such negotiation is possible. The traditional right makes concessions on one side and concessions on the other. Its concession to the far right is an effort to reduce the sentences the courts imposed on Jair Bolsonaro and on everyone they convicted of taking part in the 2022 coup attempt, and that effort will expand. It will probably have real consequences: the far right will try to make it possible for Bolsonaro to run in 2030. I think that is its outlook. I repeat, it won’t achieve this without the support of the Right, but depending on the back-and-forth negotiations, it could happen. I now turn to the problem of the Left. The Left should not leave the democratic front. The central problem in Brazil, as in many countries around the world, is to isolate the far right and reverse its advance toward power everywhere. Looking at the long term, we are witnessing a gradual, slow, but constant advance of the far right, sometimes faster, sometimes slower. If the far right comes to power and consolidates itself around the whole world, the working class will suffer a tremendous setback in every respect. Our task, then, is to avoid this by isolating the far right and defeating it as an alternative for power in Brazil. For that, we have to stay in the democratic front. But two incompatible economic programs divide the democratic front. We have to fight explicitly inside this democratic front for our position to win a majority. I’m convinced that democracy depends on the majority of the population recognizing in it an answer to their problems; if it doesn’t, it won’t last. That is the best contribution the Left can and must make to the defense of democracy. Lulism, as I characterized it, is a movement that seeks to combat poverty, which is fundamental in Brazil, without confronting capital. I stand by that definition. But political reality is dynamic. We and all the other actors are facing new situations, so new things are bound to happen. As you know, we can’t predict the future; we can only see tendencies. I would end by saying that a popular commitment is in Lulism’s nature: it depends on a popular base; it is a popular movement. Beyond that, only the political process will tell. Equality Is About Who Rules

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Matt McManus ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-06T17:00:12+00:00

There is a complex debate about the extent to which socialists prioritize equality above the full flowering of each person’s individuality. But you’d have to be quite the contrarian to deny that socialists take equality very seriously; perhaps more seriously than any other major political ideology. Prominent socialist thinkers like G. A. Cohen and Thomas Piketty have dedicated much of their work to defending the philosophical and economic merits of egalitarianism. Socialist Martin Luther King’s “I Have a Dream” speech put equality at the core of his vision: “that one day this nation will rise up and live out the true meaning of its creed: ‘We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men are created equal.’” That said, the commitment of socialists and other egalitarians to equality can often seem frustratingly vague. It invites accusations of ill-defined “social justice” advocacy or even pursuing the much dreaded “equality of outcomes” through a Harrison Bergeron–like willingness to cut everyone down to size. Of course, many of these charges are lobbed in bad faith, and even the more refined versions ignore how other core principles — like liberty or order — are equally contestable and vague until explicitly defined. But having a firmer grasp on what we mean by equality and what we want would help the Left understand itself better. Equality of What? Few living thinkers have done more to answer this question than University of Michigan philosopher Elizabeth Anderson. A former doctoral student of John Rawls, Anderson made her name in the 1990s criticizing contemporary egalitarian philosophy and developing her own version of relational equality. More recently, Anderson has become a vigorous proponent of workplace democracy, contending that there is something deeply tyrannical about the forms of work that exist today. Her most recent book , Hijacked: How Neoliberalism Turned the Work Ethic Against Workers and How Workers Can Take It Back , argues for rehabilitating the tradition of social democratic and socialist political thought as a counter to the Right’s moralizing about work. From the beginning, Anderson was fiercely critical of many dimensions of contemporary capitalism and its inequalities. Her early book Value in Ethics and Economics (1993) is a careful critique of how market relations and their distinct ways of reasoning about ethical questions are increasingly colonizing many spheres of society. She insisted that realizing many forms of “freedom, autonomy and welfare demands that certain goods be produced, exchanged, and enjoyed outside of market relations or in accordance with non-market norms.” While in this book Anderson typically appeals to ideas of autonomy and welfare rather than strict equality, there is a clear egalitarian undercurrent. She criticizes libertarians’ “fetishism” of property and markets as the sole site of freedom, and how this leads them to be blind to the “social relations of domination that exist prior to market transactions and that condition the choices individuals make there.” For instance, a long history of patriarchal subordination means that women may feel compelled to enter into exploitative contracts, say, in the sex industry, to get by day to day. In her important paper “What Is the Point of Equality?” Anderson clarifies the role equality plays in inhibiting domination. It opens with a sardonic question: “If much recent academic work defending equality had been secretly penned by conservatives, could the results be any more embarrassing for egalitarians?” She chastises both Marxists like G. A. Cohen for trivializing equality by reducing it to debates about whether we ought to compensate people for being temperamentally gloomy or easily bored, and liberal egalitarians like Ronald Dworkin for proposing an envy-free distribution of resources — implying conservatives are right that the push for equality is ultimately about nothing more than resentment. Anderson wanted egalitarians to finally get serious and realize that we aren’t, in fact, all that interested in building a society where people get the same bundle of goodies. Inegalitarian societies have built hierarchies whose social relations enabled some people to dominate others, often by insisting that those atop the social hierarchy were intrinsically worth more than those at the bottom. Egalitarians, Anderson argued, are fundamentally committed to undoing those forms of social hierarchy that enabled domination and oppression: Egalitarian political movements oppose such hierarchies. They assert the equal moral worth of persons. This assertion does not mean that all have equal virtue or talent. Negatively, the claim repudiates distinctions of moral worth based on birth or social identity — on family membership, inherited social status, race, ethnicity, gender, or genes. There are no natural slaves, plebeians, or aristocrats. Positively, the claim asserts that all competent adults are equally moral agents: everyone equally has the power to develop and exercise moral responsibility, to cooperate with others according to principles of justice, to shape and fulfill a conception of their good. In The Imperative of Integration , Anderson further defined the parameters of what she called the “relational theory” of equality and inequality. It located the “causes of economic, political, and symbolic group inequalities in the relations (processes of interaction) between groups, rather than in the internal characteristics of their members or in cultural differences that exist independently of group interaction.” Anderson emphasizes that in our society what makes people unequal often isn’t intrinsic to them but the result of the social relations in which they are embedded. But to invoke Karl Marx’s language of fetishism, this is very often obscured by ruling ideologies, which redirect the source of inferiority from social relations onto the individuals and groups themselves. Someone sold into slavery is described, in Aristotle’s terms, as a “natural slave” rather than someone who has been trapped in an oppressively hierarchical social system. Moreover, to reinforce hierarchy we often impute still worse forms of inferiority upon those at the base of the pyramid. For instance, when Senator John C. Calhoun called slavery a “positive good” because slaves were racially inferior, and the best thing for everyone was their paternalistic subordination to the superior race. There is a lot to commend in Anderson’s relational equality. It helped steer egalitarians away from arguing about whether people with highfalutin tastes should be supplied with plovers’ eggs and truffle oil and back toward the hierarchies of oppression that it is the Left’s job to confront. If there is a limitation to her approach, it’s an enduring ambiguity about what a flourishing life ought to look like. This is a necessary part of making the case for equality; namely, why and how would people live better lives if they were more equal through being free of domination. Here Marx provides us with a helpful clue in the third volume of Capital , where he posits that under socialism the cooperative development of human powers and capabilities will become an end in itself for the first time, rather than just a means of training more efficient workers. The development of human powers to foster a rich individuality in community with others ought to be on our horizon, and doing so will mean transcending capitalism. The Tyranny of Private Government and the Conservative Work Ethic In her more recent work, Anderson has become one of the sharpest critics of both capitalist practices and ideology. In Private Government , she asks us to imagine “a government that assigns almost everyone a superior whom they must obey.” This government offers routines but no rule of law: orders can be arbitrary, can change without notice, and can’t be appealed. Worst of all, the bosses answer to no one below them. “They are neither elected nor removable by their inferiors.” Most of us, Anderson point out, don’t have to imagine this. It’s what most of us encounter every day when we go to work. And yet, despite the oppression it entails, we are often told that not only is there no alternative, but that such a system is nothing less than freedom itself. Anderson’s point is well taken. If we were told that tomorrow the federal government was going to tell most Americans when to wake up, what they were going to wear, when they could go to the bathroom and eat lunch, how and when they were going to talk to others, there would be riots in the street. Everyone with a “Don’t Tread on Me!” flag would be making liberal use of their Second Amendment rights, Rand Paul would be weeping on live television, and the Daily Wire would proclaim this the Communist States of America. And yet Anderson observes this is the reality for millions of Americans subject to “private government” every day. She rejects the claim that that the modern commercial firm is a realm of individualism, as opposed to the collectivism of the state. Anderson notes that in the “great contest between individualism and collectivism regarding the mode of production, collectivism won, decisively. Nearly all production is undertaken by teams of workers using large, indivisible forms of capital equipment held in common.” Firms are collective enterprises, where the social relations grant capitalists and managers enormous domineering powers at the expense of their workers. She recommends that the way to respond to these forms of domination is to extend the liberal and democratic norms we already apply to statist collectives to private government: allow workers more democratic rights, greater protections, and the right to assemble and check unaccountable private power. In Hjjacked , Anderson examines some of the ideological reasons many on the Right consider this unacceptable. She points out how from the beginning of the modern era there have been fierce debates over the work ethic and who deserved greater power and rewards: workers or capitalists. Anderson shows how socialists and a surprising number of liberals argued a “progressive work ethic” holding that, since workers did most of the work under capitalism, they were getting a raw deal. But they were always confronted by capitalists advocating a “conservative work ethic” that “stressed discipline, frugality, and asceticism for workers, while reaping the lion’s share of productivity gains for themselves in their capacity as capital owners.” The spirit of this conservative ethic was well captured in Austrian economist Ludwig von Mises’s letter to Ayn Rand, congratulating her on the publication of Atlas Shrugged : You have the courage to tell the masses what no politician told them: you are inferior and all the improvements in your conditions which you simply take for granted you owe to the effort of men who are better than you. If this be arrogance, as some of your critics observed, it is still the truth that had to be said in the age of the Welfare State. Anderson thinks that there are several ways we can confront this ideology. One is by reconstructing and re-canonizing the work of socialist and social democratic thinkers who tried to conceive of better ways to organize the economy. Anderson quips that it is “telling that in the history of political thought, no social democrat has been canonized, despite the huge influence of social democracy in many wealthy capitalist countries.” This is something of an exaggeration outside the United States, where left-wing economic thought has gained more traction, but her point still stands. A major goal of left-wing philosophers ought to be reintroducing large numbers of people to the rich and thoughtful tradition of socialist and social democratic thought, to help inspire thinking about where to go in the future. Anderson is esteemed in academic circles but deserves a much wider popular audience. The times are certainly ripe for her message: in his second administration, Donald Trump has gone on a warpath against workers’ rights and needs, making it harder for public sector employees to engage in collective bargaining. Recently, he even went as far as to adopt an explicit “Let them eat cake” attitude toward rising costs to pay for his siege of Iran: “We’re fighting wars. We can’t take care of daycare.” Anderson offers us ways to both criticize these forms of domination and think through how to envision a better future where workers take back the world they built. But above all else, Anderson reminds us that equality is about more than who gets what. Fundamentally, it’s a question of who rules and who is ruled. For most of us, for most of our waking hours, the answer is that the boss rules and we are ruled. Anderson’s work is an invitation to change that answer. Working-Class Democratic Candidates Won Big in the Midterms

In January, a logger from Maine’s North Woods will take a seat in the US Senate. Troy Jackson spent his adult life cutting timber before he ran for office, and he will be the only Senate Democrat whose working life before politics was spent mostly in a working-class job. But he won’t be alone on Capitol Hill. Across the House, a union ironworker from suburban Cleveland, a Bethlehem firefighter, a Wisconsin waitress, an emergency room nurse, a Montana smoke jumper, a Rochester math teacher, and a former Trader Joe’s worker from Queens are heading to Washington too. By my count, twenty-one Democrats in the next Congress will have spent at least half of their working lives before politics in working-class jobs. Today there are just twelve. Working-class Democrats will still represent a woefully small fraction of the party caucus in the new year, but the 2026 midterms have swelled their ranks fast, and that gives them a real opportunity to come together to lead on a pro-worker agenda. Having more workers in Congress isn’t just a matter of symbolism; it can meaningfully change what government does and how ordinary people feel about their elected officials. Political scientists Nicholas Carnes and Noam Lupu have shown that when workers don’t hold office, economic policy tilts toward the haves and away from the have-nots. Their work demonstrates that when working-class people are underrepresented in office, legislatures are less likely to pass pro-worker policies, governments tend to build less generous welfare states, and citizens have lower trust in their elected officials. A Working-Class Wave Of the over 250 Democrats serving in the current Congress (counting both the House and the Senate), just twelve (4.6 percent) spent most of their pre-political careers in working-class jobs. Meanwhile, roughly half of American workers hold working-class jobs. I count a member of Congress as working class only if they spent at least half of their working life in working-class jobs before entering public office. That means jobs like factory work, waiting tables, office support, teaching, nursing, firefighting, and enlisted military service. A summer job or a few years of college shifts doesn’t count. That rules out many Democrats with real but limited working-class history, whose time in those jobs was too short or too uncertain to count. The ten newcomers who clear that working-class bar mostly won hard-fought, highly competitive races. Indeed, nine of them won or lead in still-uncounted races for seats that Republicans hold today: Brian Poindexter in Ohio’s 7th District; Bob Brooks and Bob Harvie in two Pennsylvania districts; Rebecca Cooke in western Wisconsin; Mitchell Berman in southern Wisconsin; and JoAnna Mendoza, Jake Johnson, and Sam Forstag lead in Arizona, Minnesota, and Montana, respectively. Troy Jackson took a Republican-held Senate seat in Maine. The tenth, Claire Valdez, won an open seat in Brooklyn and Queens, one of the most heavily Democratic districts in the country. Working-Class Democrats Span Every Wing of the Party The working-class Democrats come from a wide range of the party’s caucuses. Among current members, they include Congressional Progressive Caucus stalwarts like Ocasio-Cortez and Maxine Waters, New Democrats like Lucy McBath and John Mannion, and Blue Dog Jared Golden. The incoming class is just as mixed. Valdez was backed by Justice Democrats and the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA). Jackson was Bernie Sanders’s pick. Harvie was endorsed by the New Democrats’ PAC, Johnson and Berman by both the New Democrats and the Blue Dogs. And Poindexter, Brooks, and Forstag managed something that should be impossible under the party’s factional logic: they were endorsed by both the Progressive Caucus PAC and the Blue Dog PAC. But despite their ideological range, this crop of new working-class Democrats is more progressive on economics than it might at first appear. When I coded the campaign positions of the nine newcomers who ran in swing seats, five came out progressive or leaning progressive, including three the Blue Dogs endorsed. Troy Jackson and Bob Brooks both ran on Medicare for All. Sam Forstag proposed a 5 percent wealth tax on fortunes over $1 billion, Bob Harvie backs the PRO Act and a $15 federal minimum wage, and Brian Poindexter wants to make it easier to join a union and to make the wealthy pay their fair share to shore up Social Security funding. Claire Valdez, who ran in a safe seat in New York City, made Medicare for All one of her three platform pillars, alongside “unions for all” and “housing for all.” Even the newcomers who lean moderate or show no clear lean hold robust pro-worker positions. Rebecca Cooke supports a $15 minimum wage and expanding Medicare to cover dental, vision, and hearing. Mitchell Berman calls for a living wage of about $21.54 an hour and joined striking nurses on the picket line. Jake Johnson wants a new top tax bracket for the highest earners and to break up big corporations that exploit workers. JoAnna Mendoza backs the right to unionize and expanding Medicare as well. Whatever wing of the party they come from, this group’s shared working-class background grounds them in the bread-and-butter, kitchen-table issues that working people care about most and makes them ideal tribunes for working people’s interests on a national stage. They share an experience: of hourly pay, of shift work, of what a health insurance deductible means when you’re living paycheck to paycheck, and of the value of a union contract to families’ financial stability. We Need a Working-Class Caucus in Congress Congress has caucuses for nearly every region, industry, and ideological tendency. The Democrats’ main ideological blocs in the House are the Progressive Caucus, the New Democrat Coalition and the Blue Dogs. But Congress doesn’t have a caucus by and for working people, dedicated purely to delivering material gains that improve the lives of ordinary Americans. The Labor Caucus does important work championing workers’ rights on the job and the priorities of the labor movement, from the PRO Act to a higher minimum wage. A working-class caucus would build on that work with a broader vision: raising the standard of living for all working people and making sure every working family has what it takes to thrive. Such a caucus, of course, wouldn’t need to police who does or doesn’t count as working class, but lawmakers with strong working-class backgrounds, who know firsthand the pressures that working families face, would play a central role. That would give working people not only allies in Congress but a voice of their own. A working-class caucus could do things the existing ones cannot. To start, because working-class lawmakers already sit in caucuses across the party’s ideological spectrum, a working-class caucus could build coalitions across factional lines on bread-and-butter issues like wages, labor law, health care costs, and housing. Each faction supports progress on many of these issues in principle, but an organized bloc would be better placed to push back when party leaders are tempted to let those priorities slide down the agenda. Nick French has made a similar case for the record number of socialists heading to Congress: even a small minority can exert meaningful leverage when it organizes as a bloc. Second, the issues that shape working people’s daily lives would sit at the top of the caucus’s agenda: making sure a full-time job pays enough to support a family; bringing down the cost of housing, health care, and childcare; protecting retirement security; strengthening workers’ right to organize; and investing in the towns and regions that decades of bad economic policy have left behind. Finally, there is the bench of future working-class candidates to build. The 2026 results showed that working-class Democrats can win the competitive seats that decide control of the House, and a caucus could recruit, train, and fund more of them, so that a waitress or an electrician thinking about running knows there is a bloc in Washington that wants her to be there. Organizing twenty or so working-class members of Congress into an organized caucus obviously won’t remake the Democratic Party into a party of the working class on its own. It can’t fix the deeper problem that working people have too little organized power in American politics. But it would give workers more leverage inside the party, because an organized group of pro-worker champions in strategic positions across the party can keep its leadership focused on working people’s priorities and hold the party brass accountable when it strays. Steward’s Corner: Rethink the Scripted ‘Ask’

You’re having a conversation with a co-worker, but inside you’re feeling icy dread. You’re talking through a script that ends with asking them to do a specific thing: sign a union card, wear a button, come to a meeting, vote for a strike. And sure enough, when you ask, a wall comes down. The co-worker looks away, makes an excuse, and pins you as someone who is after something. You’re disappointed, confused about why it didn’t work, and afraid you’re not going to meet your goal. Many of us feel nervous or awkward about asking a co-worker to do something. Sometimes we just need to push ourselves to do it. But if you’re being pressured to follow the script and get to a particular “ask” without finding out who this person is and moving at their pace, that’s a problem. A co-worker you don’t yet have much of a relationship with may be turned off that you’re pressuring them to take an action that has already been decided on by others. What can you do instead? Replace the “ask” (to take some predetermined action) with an invitation: “Hey, some of us are getting together to talk about this. You want to join us?” “Can we figure out how you can be part of the solution to this problem that you’re posing?” People are happy to be asked to do something they want to do, or that they’re good at, or that they’re confident about. The Steel Disaster in Taranto, Italy, Is a Warning to Europe

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Valentin Vogl ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-06T13:59:16+00:00

For decades, labor unions and residents of the southern Italian city of Taranto have been mobilizing around the toxic emissions churned out by the local steel mill. A dedicated group fought for their right to good health through a yearslong legal battle that reached all the way to Italy’s top court. On October 29, the court finally ruled: the blast furnaces, which for decades have blanketed Taranto with fine particulate matter, dioxins, benzene, and asbestos emissions, must close with immediate effect. It represents a bitter-tasting victory after one of the great struggles of European industrial history. ILVA, as Europe’s nominally largest steel mill is still called, stands right in the center of Taranto, a city with a population of nearly two hundred thousand. The steel mill was built in the 1960s, when Italy’s capitalist class dreamed of finally industrializing the South of the peninsula. The company is now called Acciaierie d’Italia (“Italy’s Steelworks”) and has been operating under state-imposed emergency measures since 2024. The history of ILVA is one of Europe’s biggest environmental scandals. Local residents, unions, the mayor, and even the local soccer club have all demanded the closure of the blast furnaces. These efforts have consistently failed for want of government plans for financing alternatives. In statistical terms, the people of Taranto suffer from pleural mesothelioma (a cancer caused by asbestos exposure) four times as much as the general population. In addition, the steel mill has been proven to cause elevated levels of particulate matter and emissions of micropollutants such as benzopyrene and dioxins, as determined by official health-impact assessments. Fans of the local Taranto soccer club have also been fighting against the plant for many years. They have written books, sold scarves with slogans such as “Fabbrica assassina, vergogna tarantina” (“Murderous factory, Taranto’s shame”), and rented public ad space to express their discontent. With the abrupt closure of its largest employer, Taranto faces the threat of social crisis. More than ten thousand jobs depend on the steel mill; enormous health care costs and the cleanup of a toxic wasteland are deterring investors. The situation is fertile ground for the authoritarian right and its ability to turn disappointment, uncertainty, and frustration into votes. The much-heralded return of European industrial policy also bears responsibility for ILVA’s demise. In recent years, governments around Western Europe tried to coax “green” investments out of private capital through generous handouts. And yet, oligarchs and steel tycoons were in no hurry to comply. Although the plant is currently state owned, the government has no concrete plan for continuing production. The site’s future appears to be a painful choice between a complete sell-off to oligarchs or else outright collapse. Europe’s Rusty Steel Empire In an age of (sort of) flying cars, steel mills seem like relics from a bygone era. Appearances are not deceiving. The blast furnace process, used worldwide to produce steel for infrastructure, buildings, cars, and weapons, was developed in China over two thousand years ago. Steel truly took off thanks to the self-accelerating economic model of fossil capitalism : machines needed steel and coal; growing coal mining required more machines; and these, in turn, required more steel and coal. Today’s imperial core then began burning ever more fossil coal in ever-larger blast furnaces to produce pig iron — and greenhouse gases, too. Starting in the 1970s, Europe lost ground to Japan’s rising steel power. Japanese steelmakers dared to build blast furnaces of unprecedented size and, in the process, created a global market for fossil metallurgical coal. Today more than one billion metric tons of coal are burned annually to produce steel. This makes the global steel industry responsible for one-tenth of all human-caused greenhouse gases, more than the EU as a whole. By the time they were privatized in the late 1980s and early ’90s, European steelmakers were already lagging behind the competition, particularly their Japanese rivals. Global steel tycoons also took advantage of the wave of privatizations to snap up plants that were being sold off at bargain prices following the collapse of the Soviet Union. They downsized the plants’ workforces, established companies in tax havens, closed unprofitable sites, and generally generated good returns for themselves and minority shareholders. Today Europe’s steel mills are outdated. No new blast furnace plants have been built since the 1970s, and only two blast furnaces have opened in the EU since 2000. From Eisenhüttenstadt to Fos-sur-Mer, Gijón, Kraków, IJmuiden, and Taranto, in a long list of former steel hubs, local residents suffer from air pollution and health problems. Investments to modernize these facilities and curb emissions are long overdue. Derisking Has Failed In Italy, at the height of the European Green Deal investment projects in the early 2020s, the prime minister was none other than Mario Draghi. He had previously been a key figure at Goldman Sachs and, as president of the European Central Bank throughout the 2010s euro crisis, a key figure in the troika that imposed austerity on the Greek government. Since 2024, Draghi’s name has come to symbolize EU competitiveness and the associated watering down of European environmental and human rights policies. In the meantime, in 2021–22, Draghi served as Italian premier and developed a climate road map for the steel plant in Taranto. Beginning in 2019, Europe’s steel manufacturers successively announced decarbonization plans to transition from coal to hydrogen and electric furnaces in order to reduce greenhouse gas emissions. In Taranto, according to Draghi’s plan, €4.7 billion in largely private capital was to be invested to achieve full employment and increase production by 2025. The political ideology in which economic policy levers are adjusted so that unattractive investments like “green steel” become viable business cases is called “derisking.” The European Green Deal aims to make the steel industry climate neutral and competitive again through such means. Specifically, this meant free emissions allowances, billions in state aid for steel and hydrogen, protective tariffs against China, public procurement of “green steel,” energy price subsidies, and more lenient reporting requirements. In an attempt to make green investments attractive to private capital, several EU countries dealt with ArcelorMittal. The world’s second-largest steel company holds a de facto monopoly on blast furnace production in Spain, France, Belgium, and Poland and operates additional blast furnaces in Germany (Bremen and Eisenhüttenstadt). From 2018 to 2024, ArcelorMittal also operated the plant in Taranto. Yet governments underestimated the steel giant’s negotiating position; it abruptly announced that it could not invest at all locations, thereby provoking a subsidy race among member states. Up to €15 billion in low-carbon subsidies has since been awarded to steelmakers, but even that is not enough. As a result of high energy prices following the invasion of Ukraine, ArcelorMittal put all hydrogen projects on hold at the end of 2024. Seven lost years after the Green Deal, only a few Swedish and German steelmakers in the EU are still pursuing significant decarbonization projects. Meanwhile, many locations are in an even worse economic position than before. The EU’s “derisking” project appears to have failed, at least for now. Draghi is quite right to blame the industry’s struggles to a lack of competitiveness. Raw materials and energy mostly have to be imported. Labor costs are comparatively high due to Europe’s past imperial might but also its historic labor struggles. Europe’s historical advantage in innovation has long since been squandered; the innovation hubs of the steel industry are now in Japan, Korea, and above all China. Added to this are persistent global overcapacity and higher returns in the financial sector, which is why ArcelorMittal prefers to carry out share buyback programs rather than make forward-looking investments. Yet instead of EU member states pursuing alternative and more democratic economic models, the lack of competitiveness serves as a pretext to pursue austerity measures and the upward redistribution of wealth. Europe’s steel-producing regions are thus kept in a state of perpetual crisis, which further exacerbates health and climate damage. The authoritarian right skillfully exploits the resulting uncertainty about people’s jobs and the economic survival of their hometowns. This too is a result of timid industrial policies. In turn, these same right-wing parties, building on neoliberal ideological assumptions, further undermine democracy. Socialization Is Not Enough A bold democratic alternative is urgently needed to ensure a stable supply of raw materials. The good news is that we’re not starting from scratch. In June, the French National Assembly voted for the second time to nationalize the French division of ArcelorMittal, but the measure has so far failed to gain Senate approval. In Germany, in a recent position paper, Die Linke advocates for state involvement in the steel industry. Some steel manufacturers, such as the German companies Saarstahl and Salzgitter, are either part-publicly owned or bound to social interests. In Austria, Voestalpine employees own 15 percent of the company. Recent developments in France and Germany make it clear that momentum is finally building around the issue of ecosocialist industrial policy. Yet socialist parties are running late, and nationalization alone is not enough. Rather, the direction of these plants must be anchored in a solid transformation strategy. Democratic management of industry requires that we wrest the systems of provision we need to meet our basic needs from the logic of capital accumulation. Such transformative supply systems must be protected from capital and structured in such a way that social participation has a self-accelerating effect. This presents two fundamental challenges for the democratization of the steel industry. First, solutions are needed to protect socialized steel mills from the capitalist market. Only in this way can economic leeway be created for radical experiments in Europe’s outdated and contaminated facilities. Long-term offtake agreements with public infrastructure operators (such as water or energy utilities) could be a first step. Further socialization of major steel consumers could be the next. The second is that the EU obtains most of its energy and raw materials through imports. Dependence on raw materials makes socialist experiments vulnerable to crisis-induced market fluctuations and acts of economic sabotage. Increased use of scrap steel and renewable energy within the EU can mitigate these risks. In the spirit of “resilience by cooperation,” solidarity-based alliances can be built beyond the EU, with the goal of dismantling colonial patterns in value chains and organizing the procurement of more socially and environmentally sustainable primary raw materials in the longer term. In Taranto, the state and private capital are leaving behind a toxic wasteland that hangs over the future generations. Even beyond the enormous costs of repairing this situation, hopes of industrial transformation are also complicated by the presence of organized crime in every stage of the waste cycle and its frequent involvement in public-procured construction projects, as documented by Italian NGO Legambiente in its No Ecomafia campaign. Given these obstacles, democratizing production is likely the only way for Taranto to retain the site in the long term. Unions and the local population have already mobilized and have the only concrete plan for the plant’s future. For the rest of Europe, Taranto remains a cautionary tale: a foretaste of what might come next. The bees in the city: organizing China’s delivery workers in Ganzhou

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Tings Chak ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-06T22:21:15+00:00

At the entrance to Xingfuli, a residential compound in Ganzhou, Jiangxi province, a bulletin board displays the names, photographs, and phone numbers of the people responsible for managing the neighborhood. Though an ordinary bulletin board at first appearance, among the building representatives and Communist Party members is an unexpected name: Kang Jiafei, a food delivery worker who is also listed as a community “grid worker.” China has more than ten million food delivery workers, part of a rapidly growing global workforce whose lives are organized by algorithms, tight deadlines, and orders that take them across the city. They are among the most visible workers on China’s streets, yet often have no fixed workplace, no place to rest between orders, and limited access to conventional forms of employment protection. Their working hours are fragmented, their movements dictated by the next delivery. What, then, does it mean for one of these workers to have a recognized role in governing a residential community? In China, urban neighborhoods are divided into small geographical units known as grids (网格), with designated workers who monitor local conditions, respond to residents’ concerns, and coordinate with public departments. Kang is one of fifty delivery workers in Ganzhou’s Zhanggong District who have taken on this additional role. During my visit in October, Wuzhifeng Road Community Party secretary Yin Guozhu, responsible for this community, explained how it works. Delivery workers like Kang know the neighborhood through their daily deliveries. When he notices a blocked fire exit, debris near a lift, or another hazard, he reports it to the property management staff. Between orders, he also distributes the community’s anti-fraud leaflets. During recent typhoon weeks, riders who encountered fallen branches blocking drains cleared what they could and reported larger obstructions to the community, which contacted the responsible departments. “Our information is collected up layer by layer,” Yin explained. She calls the riders “mobile probes”: in places community workers cannot see or reach in time, they provide first-hand information. The same mobility that makes these workers difficult to organize also gives them intimate knowledge of the city’s streets and neighborhoods – knowledge that can now serve the wider community. In 2020, the widely read investigation Trapped in the System 《困在系统里》documented the tightening grip of delivery algorithms on these workers in China: a three-kilometer delivery window that fell from an hour in 2016 to only thirty-eight minutes by 2018. Riders described themselves as “racing with death, wrestling with the traffic police, befriending red lights.” The sociologist Sun Ping , after seven years of fieldwork, described the condition as “transitional labor” – work suspended between employment and informality, in which the transition becomes permanent and the delivery economy runs like a great “furnace”, constantly burning as workers rotate through it. Since 2021, national measures have sought to clarify platform companies’ responsibilities and extend labor protections in these “new forms of employment,” notably in the gig economy. An occupational injury insurance program , piloted from 2022 and expanded nationwide in 2026, places its premium obligations on platforms. Days before my visit, a draft regulation on protecting workers in new employment forms opened for public comment. Alongside these regulatory efforts, the Party has been extending its organizational work among workers dispersed across the platform economy. A workforce without a fixed workplace lacks many of the spaces where workers’ welfare and social life have traditionally been anchored. One of the most immediate problems has a simpler name: the riders’ “four difficulties” – eating, drinking water, resting, and finding a toilet, so the infrastructure has moved to the street. The All-China Federation of Trade Unions has helped establish more than 186,000 outdoor worker rest stations nationwide, aiming to ensure that outdoor workers can find a place to rest, drink water, or charge their phones within a fifteen-minute journey. In Zhanggong District, thirty-three Qianxin stations can be found through phone map apps. They take their name from Qian (虔), an old name for Ganzhou, and xin (新), referring to the new employment groups. President Xi Jinping once called delivery workers “hardworking little bees”; here, the community built them resting stations, including the one near the MixC shopping center, shaped like a beehive. When the district launched its Qianxin program, a rider named Zhu Qiang said: “It gives us a home in the city.” Workers waiting between orders had nowhere to shelter from wind and rain. Shopkeepers, worried that soaked riders might disturb their business, were often reluctant to let them inside. The community decided to build a place along the routes the workers already traveled. “With the station, it is as if we built them a warm harbor right where they must pass,” Secretary Yin said of the station built in the Xingfuli residential block. Inside are air conditioning, drinking water, a microwave, charging facilities, first-aid supplies, and a library stocked with books. Around it, the community has organized an alliance of businesses offering services and discounts. A rider can eat his fill at a participating canteen for fifteen yuan, compared with twenty or thirty for other customers. Some education providers offer half-price courses for workers’ children. Lawyers and family-education counselors hold weekly consultations, while online links connect workers to departments responsible for insurance, schooling, and employment. At the compound entrance, a rider-friendly map marks building entrances and nearby places where riders can rest. A few minutes lost searching for an apartment can mean a late order. “For them, time is money,” Yin explained. The community began by making itself easier for the workers to navigate. Kang Jiafei’s name on the grid board represents another step: the recognition that delivery workers are also contributors to collective life of the city beyond the services they perform for customers. For Yin, this is a reciprocal process. “Everything is two-way,” she explained. By making the community more welcoming to riders, she believes their sense of social responsibility has gradually grown. She described how the relationship developed over three years. Initially, riders found it difficult to join community activities, their working hours too fragmented and unpredictable. Today, when the community announces free medical examinations in their WeChat group, workers invite one another and arrive together. Workers also contact Yin directly about developments around the shopping district. Yin says she has gained not only partners in community work, but also “brothers and sisters.” This is not a charity story. A bench, a socket, a microwave, and a bookshelf are material provisions for workers whose lives are organized around delivering the next order. But they also create spaces where otherwise dispersed workers can meet, develop social relationships, and participate in collective activities. The station becomes more than a place of rest: it becomes an organizational center. The methodology is Party-building adapted to a workforce that the factory no longer houses. The trade unions, Women’s Federation, and Communist Youth League are part of this wider effort to extend organization into new employment groups. Where conditions for establishing Party branches do not yet exist, other mass organizations can provide an institutional foundation. 1.6 million join general strike in France against austerity

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Ana Vračar ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-06T04:09:00+00:00

Following weeks of major mobilizations led by high school and university students , hundreds of thousands of people responded to a general strike call by French trade unions. On Thursday, November 5, over 200 events and at least 1,500 industrial action appeals brought the country to a standstill again. According to the General Confederation of Labor (CGT), roughly 1.6 million people took to the streets. The organization reported 350,000 people in Paris, 150,000 in Marseille, 120,000 in Bordeaux, and 100,000 in Lille, among others – despite authorities’ attempts to obstruct the day. Approximately 1.6 million people took part in protests across France on November 5, 2026. Author: Jaylen Strong Participants at strikes and rallies demanded concrete measures to address the cost-of-living crisis and an end to the repressive measures increasingly adopted by the government and law enforcement since early October. “The demands are well known: price freezes, the repeal of the Parcoursup system, more university places, wage increases, and €10 billion in the budget to address the demands of high school and university students,” wrote left media outlet L’insoumission . Refusal of social injustice is driving the mobilization There was little doubt the new day of mobilization would be successful, given the outpouring of support student demands have gathered since the start of the academic year. “What we are witnessing is the summary of ten years of Macronism and anger against that policy,” said Sophie Binet, general secretary of the CGT, in an interview with Mediapart . According to Binet, the convergence of different movements – students, workers, gilets jaunes (Yellow Vests) – is driven in part by a shared refusal of the social injustice that defines President Emmanuel Macron’s legacy. “For ten years, Macronist policy has varied in many respects, but with one constant: its economic choices, which serve the powerful, the business community, and the wealthiest.” Read more: Insurgent left candidate may spoil far right’s plans in 2027 French presidential election This neoliberal agenda is facing resistance not only from students and workers but also from progressive forces in parliament. On Wednesday, November 4, an overwhelming majority – over 450 – of representatives in the National Assembly rejected a budget proposal aiming to cut over €40 billion as concerns over the public deficit in the mainstream mount. While both left- and right-wing representatives rejected the proposal, only the left has outlined a substantial alternative – and has brought that vision along while marching with students over the past weeks. The National Rally, by contrast, has promised no break with the status quo as the 2027 presidential election approaches. Protests to continue on Saturday Preparations for Thursday’s actions were once again marked by a high level of repression , with national authorities announcing the deployment of approximately 70,000 law enforcement agents . At the local level, several demonstrations were rerouted at the last minute, and police continued to use tear gas against demonstrators. Administrative maneuvers, arrests, and violence against students have only added to protesters’ frustration, Binet emphasized, shaping additional demands to uphold and protect the right to protest. Trade unions reported approximately 350,000 protesters in Paris. Author: Jaylen Strong The persistent protests have also produced the first – albeit minimal – breakthroughs . “These past few weeks gave the government space to announce a series of initial measures aimed at young people, in the hope of calming our mobilization,” wrote student unions Union Syndicale Lycéenne (USL) and Union Étudiante. “These announcements represent a first symbolic retreat by a power that had until now appeared unshakeable.” “The balance of power we have built over more than a month is exposing a government – a government incapable of taking responsibility for 10 years of social and racist war policies against young people, and incapable of imagining any response to our demands other than unbridled repression – tremble.” On Thursday, as actions were coming to a close, the government announced it would organize a social dialogue session including trade unions and employers’ representatives next week. Meanwhile, a new general demonstration has been announced for Saturday, November 7.

Building popular power against imperialism in West Africa

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Kate Janse Van Rensburg ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-06T04:09:00+00:00

Under the banner “One People, One Struggle: Against Imperialism for Popular Power in West Africa,” the West Africa People’s Organisation (WAPO) convened its Second Congress at the Amilcar Cabral School in Agyanoa, Ghana, from October 31 to November 1. The two-day event gathered delegates from 29 organizations across 11 countries. Representing political parties, social movements, trade unions, peasant groups, and women’s organizations, participants assembled to construct a unified front against imperialist domination and chart a strategic path toward popular sovereignty across West Africa. Following an opening cultural performance by a local ensemble, Revolutionary Kolomashie, the beat of drums and blare of the trumpet set the scene for vibrant plenary sessions and commission debates focused on the concrete realities of anti-imperialist struggle in West Africa in the current conjuncture. Local ensemble Revolutionary Kolomashie performs at the opening of the WAPO Second Congress, 31 October 2026. Photo: Jnr Johnson Segbefiah / Pan African Television The subregion continues to suffer under severe insecurity and destabilizing violence unleashed by NATO and French military interventions since the fall of Libya. Grounded in this stark reality, the gathering zoomed in on the rising tide of anti-imperialist resistance across the region – expressed through massive mobilizations against foreign military bases that have defined the regional conjuncture over the four years since the founding of WAPO – and notably marked by the expulsion of French troops from Mali, Niger, and Burkina Faso. WAPO President Philippe Noudjenoume described the shifts in the Sahel as a pivotal moment: “The central factor determining any analysis of our West African subregion today is the governments of Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger, united in the Confederation of the Alliance of Sahel States (AES). What is happening in these three countries can only be described as a patriotic and sovereignist revolution.” Noudjenoume added that this movement represents “a giant leap towards realizing the dream of our illustrious Pan-African heroes, such as Kwame Nkrumah, Sékou Touré, Patrice Lumumba and Modibo Keïta.” WAPO President Philippe Noudjenoume delivers the Opening Address of the WAPO Second Congress. Photo: Jnr Johnson Segbefiah / Pan African Television In his welcoming address Socialist Movement of Ghana (SMG) Convenor Kyeretwie Opoku emphasized that this shift resulted directly from grassroots mobilization. Referring to the expulsion of French troops he said, “This didn’t happen because France changed its mind. It happened because people in the streets of Bamako and beyond fought for it, protested, and drove the French out!” Opoku noted that the formation of the Alliance of Sahel States (AES) by Burkina Faso, Mali, and Niger – alongside their departure from ECOWAS – signals a new emancipatory epoch where nations are actively reclaiming sovereignty over their natural resources. This is not unique to the AES, but a symbolic tip of the proverbial iceberg that captures the aspirations of peoples across the African continent. A primary example remains the experience of Niger finally asserting control over its uranium reserves, ending decades of subsidized extraction for France. Delegates rejected imperialist narratives framing regional insecurity as mere ethnic conflicts, identifying imperialist interference and foreign covert destabilization as primary drivers of instability. To secure true self-determination, delegates called for the immediate removal of all foreign military bases from African soil and urged West African nations to establish independent, collective security arrangements. The Congress also examined the divide between formal electoral democracy and actual, popular democratic participation and process. Representatives from Guinea-Bissau, Ivory Coast, and Senegal outlined how external trappings of democracy often mask systemic counteractions against the popular will. Speakers pointed to foreign manipulation – particularly from France – in preserving neocolonial monetary structures like the CFA franc and secret bilateral agreements. Electoral mechanisms, delegates warned, are frequently weaponized to legitimize imperialist control while marginalizing transformative movements. Delegates & observers to the WAPO Second Congress break out into commissions to discuss various themes and topics. Photo: Jnr Johnson Segbefiah / Pan African Television In response, Noudjenoume framed WAPO’s core mandate: “WAPO aims to raise the consciousness of West African workers and peoples, enabling them to undertake their dual liberation: liberation from imperialist domination and social liberation from the ruthless exploitation of capitalism.” This aforementioned erosion of democratic processes coincides with escalating state-sponsored repression in countries such as Benin, Guinea-Conakry, Guinea-Bissau, and Côte d’Ivoire. Delegates reported crackdowns, banishment and exile of opposition figures, illegal arrests, and severe restrictions on civil society. Furthermore, socioeconomic challenges including mounting debt, persistent hunger and famine, and widespread unemployment remain prominent issues impacting the region. Incoming WAPO General Secretary Yaw Appiah-Kubi emphasized that organized popular resistance remains the sole effective defense against authoritarianism and imperialist encroachment. Delegates participate in the plenary discussions of the WAPO Second Congress. Photo: Jnr Johnson Segbefiah / Pan African Television Reflecting on the necessity of grassroots mobilization, SMG Convenor Kyeretwie Opoku emphasized a core principle of revolutionary solidarity: “Sovereignty that relies solely on government power or state power is not enough. Popular power means organized people – not just state power, and not just a single leader.” By uniting social movements, trade unions, and anti-imperialist forces under a common program, the Second WAPO Congress reaffirmed that West Africa’s liberation will not come from foreign patrons or neo-colonial elections, but from the organized power of its people.

South Korea moves to join Iran War under US pressure

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Ju-Hyun Park ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-06T00:10:42+00:00

South Korea’s government has begun procedures to potentially join the US-Israeli war of aggression against Iran. On November 5, a vice-ministerial meeting affirmed a decision to expand the military capacities and operational area of the Cheonghae Unit, a blue-water naval force that has operated in the Gulf of Aden near the Red Sea as part of an “anti-piracy” mission since 2020. The revised mandate of the Cheonghae Unit would double its deployment area up to the waters of the Arabian Sea south of Duqm, Oman. This would place Republic of Korea (ROK) naval units on the fringes of the Gulf of Oman and the Strait of Hormuz – areas Iran has declared closed to foreign military vessels. The proposal would further expand the Cheonghae Unit to include P-8A Poseidon maritime patrol aircraft, an Explosive Ordnance Disposal (EOD) team, and unmanned mine-clearing equipment. An ROK defense ministry official told Yonhap News Agency that the expansion is being undertaken “to protect our citizens and safeguard our economic interests,” adding that the Cheonghae Unit’s new mandate would include support for “efforts to restore freedom of navigation in international shipping routes.” The official added that maritime patrol aircraft would be “prohibited from monitoring Iranian territory, territorial waters, or the interior of the Strait of Hormuz.” Per ROK law, the decision must now be approved by the Cabinet and President, followed by a vote in the National Assembly, South Korea’s parliament. The decision is expected to be made next week. War by deception Word of the ROK’s potential deployment to the Strait of Hormuz leaked in early September, following months of US pressure. Large protests attended by thousands swept the country, with the ROK’s major progressive forces presenting a united front against the war. On September 18, President Lee Jae Myung told media that his government had “ruled out” joining the war – but hinted that expanding the Cheonghae Unit’s deployment could be possible under the auspices of defending freedom of navigation. While it was widely reported internationally that Lee had ruled out support for the war on Iran, progressive parties and anti-war organizations in the ROK continued to raise the alarm. Many pointed out that expanding the Cheonghae Unit to the Gulf of Oman or the Strait of Hormuz would violate Iran’s prior warnings that foreign military vessels in these areas would be treated as enemy combatants. Given the circumstances, these organizations accused President Lee of misleading the public and attempting to proceed towards war by deceptive means. These warnings have proven to be prescient. The Lee administration’s claim to expand the Cheonghae Unit to protect freedom of navigation and commercial vessels is at odds with the details of the proposal. The new weaponry slated for addition to the Cheonghae Unit goes well beyond what would be required for a naval escort. As the Korean Confederation of Trade Unions, a progressive umbrella union organization representing over 1 million workers, put it: “If the true objective were merely to protect vessels, there would be no need for EOD teams, anti-submarine patrol aircraft, or mine-clearing equipment. These are military preparations unrelated to the protection of ships; they clearly reveal an intent to participate in an illegal war led by the United States.” While the Lee administration is maintaining that it would uphold independent command of its troops, the Cheonghae Unit in fact operates as part of the US Naval Forces Central Command’s Combined Maritime Forces. One way or another, the potential deployment shows every indication of either directly or indirectly supporting US military operations. The ROK Progressive Party, which holds four seats in the National Assembly, further pointed out that expanding Cheonghae Unit’s operational area amounts to a “deceptive, indirect deployment – a ploy to insert South Korea into the conflict,” adding that the waters from the Strait of Hormuz to the Gulf of Oman are “effectively a single theater of war…there is no buffer zone whatsoever between the two.” The Party statement also criticized the illegality of the potential decision. The Cheonghae Unit’s legal basis for deployment under international law, United Nations Security Council Resolution 1816, limits its operational mandate to Somalian waters. Furthermore, Article 5, Paragraph 1 of the ROK’s Constitution renounces wars of aggression. Under Washington’s pressure For months, South Korea staved off US pressure to join the war. In August, Trump suddenly announced the partial cancellation of Ulchi Freedom Shield, a major series of joint war drills held annually between the two governments. While citing his “very special relationship” with the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea (DPRK), Trump also delivered invectives against the ROK for its unwillingness to support aggression against Iran. Read more: ‘Unworthy of comment’: The DPRK rebuffs Trump’s overtures These demands have piled additional pressure on Seoul, which has also faced economic coercion under Trump’s tariff wars to deliver a massive $350 billion state-backed investment package into US industry. Lee’s domestic economic agenda is also being obstructed by the US military, and his government is in active dialogue with Washington to relocate the US Air Force Gwangju Base to make room for a proposed semiconductor manufacturing complex. The US and ROK are also negotiating the return of the latter’s operational control (OPCON) over its own military. Recently, US Forces Korea Commander Xavier Brunson declared that the conditions for the transfer had changed, and hinted that a return of OPCON before the end of Lee’s administration in 2030 would be unlikely. Given this context, it is likely that Lee’s proposed expansion of the Cheonghae Unit is an attempted maneuver to gain leverage in advancing his administration’s other goals vis-a-vis Washington. Lee would not be the first ROK leader to partake in US wars of aggression in the hope of advancing domestic pursuits. The ROK’s participation in the Vietnam War and the Second Gulf War were motivated by similar designs. However, Iran today is nothing like the Vietnam of the 1960s or the Iraq of the 2000s. Iran has demonstrated formidable military capabilities that have forced the US into a halting campaign which it can neither win militarily nor exit without facing severe loss of prestige. Regarding Seoul’s potential participation, Tehran has been clear: any foreign military operating in the Strait of Hormuz, the Gulf of Oman, or the Persian Gulf will be regarded as an enemy and its units and economic interests will be targeted. All this has compelled Lee’s government into contortions. Rather than straightforwardly backing the US war, Seoul is now engaged in a rather acrobatic performance that seeks to appease all sides. Technically, the ROK is meeting Washington’s demand, falling short of Tehran’s threshold for retaliation, and packaging it all in such a way that may avoid provoking a renewed wave of domestic outrage. Lee may believe this is the politically prudent thing to do. That illusion will rapidly fade if a single ROK casualty occurs.

Paraguay’s right-wing president resigns as major bank collapses

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Pablo Meriguet ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-05T23:05:00+00:00

Under the anguished, enraged gaze of millions of Paraguayans, the country’s right-wing president, Santiago Peña, announced his resignation amid opposition calls to initiate impeachment proceedings over his alleged responsibility for the closure of Ueno Bank, one of the South American nation’s most important financial institutions. “With the conviction that our republic must always take precedence over any individual or circumstance … I have decided to submit my resignation as president of the republic. I do so with the stability of our country, social peace, and the need to preserve our democratic institutions in mind,” Peña said in a televised address . Peña has been embroiled in serious allegations after his government transferred millions of dollars to Ueno Bank (nearly $850 million in public funds, including money from the Social Security system), an institution that announced its closure this week, despite having boasted for years of financial stability and growth in depositors, according to Manuel Gustale , former president of Ueno. However, on November 3, the Central Bank of Paraguay announced the bank’s dissolution after its solvency ratio fell below the minimum allowed under Paraguayan law. Hundreds upon hundreds of depositors at the bank in question took to the streets and demanded the return of their money, which was not possible due to the government’s freezing of the accounts. The situation is even more serious when one considers that a single business conglomerate managed to control not only the life savings of nearly all pension funds but also the savings of the majority of adults in Paraguay. Thus, the decision to freeze the accounts comes on top of several allegations of possible links between President Peña and the business group that controls Ueno Bank, which has raised suspicions of conflicts of interest involving Peña and Central Bank President Carlos Carvallo. For months, several Paraguayan institutions had been warning of a possible collapse of Ueno when it was discovered that the bank was granting excessive loans to companies linked to its own owners. “And yet, public funds kept flowing in. This isn’t simply the story of a poorly managed bank. It’s a system in which the bank, the regulatory body, and the presidency were so closely intertwined that none seemed capable of holding the others in check,” Orkun Saka, a professor at St. George’s University of London, told the BBC. Peña’s resigns after his party pursues impeachment But the final blow to the current administration came from its own party, the right-wing Colorado Party – which has governed the country for much of the past few decades – when former President Horacio Cartes (a member of the same party) announced that the party would support impeachment proceedings against Peña. “We will not stand idly by while public, private, and pension funds are plundered; we must think of the people and the values we uphold as the Colorado Party. We support the impeachment of the President of the Republic, Santiago Peña, in order to restore the Republic’s institutional framework,” Cartes said in a statement . Once Peña realized that his party (which was clearly aware of his past as a shareholder long before the crisis erupted) had withdrawn its support, he appeared before the cameras and announced that he would step down. However, the opposition and the Colorado Party stated that they would press ahead with the trial to determine the executive branch’s potential responsibility in the financial and political crisis rocking Paraguay. According to some reports , Peña was listed as a shareholder of Ueno Holding, a conglomerate of companies controlled to a greater or lesser extent by the Vázquez Group, the main shareholder group of Ueno Bank. And while Peña announced in early 2025 that he was no longer a shareholder of Ueno Holding, many question whether his political decisions regarding the institution were influenced by his own economic interests and those of the Vázquez Group. Added to this is the fact that the president of the Central Bank was also president of Ueno Bank, and that during Peña’s presidency, the Central Bank approved Ueno’s conversion into a bank in 2023. This highlights a close relationship between the executive branch and the failed bank. That is why opposition Senator Rafael Filizzola has said that the case constitutes: “the largest scam perpetrated by those in political power in the democratic era … It involves a bank created by those in political power to use public funds for private business ventures linked to political power, with the President of the Republic at the helm.” According to Filizzola himself, the losses from this case could amount to some $3 billion, which creates enormous uncertainty for the millions of Paraguayans who do not know when or if they will get their money back.
