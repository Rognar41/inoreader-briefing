The Tasks for DSA After the Blue Wave

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: The Call ／ 著者: Bread &#38; Roses Caucus ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-07T18:20:00+00:00

On November 3rd, the American people sent a decisive message. We are sick and tired of MAGA and the Trump administration, of the cost of living crisis, of war, of corruption, of corporate greed, of billionaires dominating our political system. Alongside an overwhelming rejection of the far-right, voters also chose to send the largest number of democratic socialists in 100 years into the halls of power. Our movement elected over 100 democratic socialists with a clear message: we need a government and economy that works for working people, not the billionaires, capitalists, warmongers, and genocidaires. DSA will now have a bloc of 8 endorsed members in Congress along with a broader bloc to work closely with. Among those elected (and re-elected) were Bread & Roses members Dylan Wegela (Michigan), Richie Floyd (Florida), Robert LeVertis Bell (Kentucky), and Tyler Rooney (Kentucky). We also commend B&R members Andrea Parr (Kentucky) and Todd Chretien (Maine) on their hard-fought races that fell short. In addition to the elections of DSA members throughout the country, historic numbers of rank-and-file union members and leaders ran and won office. Union members played a decisive role in elections all throughout the country, helping defeat Republicans, and electing their union siblings to office. Notably, the UAW elected multiple of its members, including DSA’s Claire Valdez to Congress, as well as Scott Houldieson (a founder and leader of the rank-and-file movement to transform the UAW into a member-led class-struggle union) to the Indiana State Senate. In multiple states — Florida, Michigan, and Maine — voters not only defeated Republican senators, but got to vote for Medicare for All, abolishing ICE, and a total arms embargo on Israel. We’re excited to see Abdul El-Sayed and Troy Jackson buck the so-called “pragmatism” of the Democratic establishment and win seats in the Senate. And we are further impressed by DSA member Angie Nixon’s narrow loss, which suggests that the Democratic Party’s refusal to seriously invest in her campaign was the decisive factor. Their three races show that workers are hungry for transformative change and want a rebellion not only against MAGA but the corporate Democratic Party and even capitalism as well. It is on DSA to organize and lead that rebellion. We also recognize that there were setbacks in this election. Bans on trans athletes won in Arizona, Colorado, Nebraska and Washington. A tax on billionaires was defeated in California — with the help of leading members of the state’s Democratic Party. The ranks of the democratic socialist left in national politics will grow, but many conservative Democrats were also elected, and the pro-business wing of the Democratic Party still dominates the party leadership. Finally, Trump’s response to the election defeat may be to double down on his authoritarian agenda. The choice of Stephen Miller to be his new chief of staff indicates that Trump has no intention of softening his approach. While we celebrate the many defeats handed to MAGA this week, these defeats for our side remind us that we still have a great deal of work to do to build a powerful, majoritarian movement that defends the rights of queer people, can really take on the power of billionaire class, and can remove the far right from power. Once inaugurated in January, it is incumbent on DSA and our socialists in office to seize the initiative, quickly seeking to pass data center moratoriums on the state and federal level, and a war powers resolution in Congress to end the US war on Iran. DSA can coordinate with our state legislators to get enough states to join the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact and effectively abolish the Electoral College as the first step in the political revolution. National DSA’s Federal Socialists in Office Committee should immediately bring together all DSA congresspeople to launch a Democratic Socialist Caucus in the House of Representatives. They should begin a public campaign in the House for either an alternative Speaker to Jeffries or to demand the conditions and concessions with which we would vote for him, without which we’d vote no. A similar campaign against Chuck Schumer’s leadership in the Senate also deserves our support. Our maximal demand should be a rules change to allow our congressmembers to bring forward legislation without leadership approval so we can call votes on Block the Bombs for an arms embargo on Israel, the MELT Act to abolish ICE, Medicare for All, and the PRO Act to reform labor law and unleash the power of organized labor. DSA chapters must immediately onboard the thousands of new members we will receive in the next few months, provide political education and organizing training, and plug them into important issue campaigns and union struggles. And we must begin laying the foundations for AOC 2028 and a massive congressional and downballot slate, today. Alongside our efforts in the labor movement to build toward May Day 2028, DSA and the organized working class will move, and the earth will shake. Enough With the “Public Option” Already

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: John Canham-Clyne ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-07T16:31:45+00:00

The difference between a vampire and a bad health care policy idea is that vampires jump out of their coffins and drink blood at sunset. Undead health care proposals emerge from think tank closets and suck the life out of useful ideas when the sun rises on a national health care debate. As public fury at exploding costs and deadly corporate claims denials boils over into historic Democratic election gains, can someone please find a wooden stake for the public option? Improved and expanded Medicare for All would give everyone full coverage at much lower cost than the combined private premiums, taxes, and out-of-pocket payments that are eating up family income today. But it violates a cardinal rule of federal politics: never do anything that causes corporations or the ultrawealthy any pain. Instead, corporate Democrats wanting to be seen “doing something” about the imploding US health care system have long peddled a vague public option notion because it sounds like the marriage of a big noncorporate vision (it’s public!) with competition and freedom of choice (it’s an option!). Liberal nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) and pundits revived its rotting corpse in response to pro–Medicare for All candidates’ primary victories and Tuesday night’s stunning results. It’s a perfect nonsolution to our three-headed crisis of health care cost, access, and quality. Nobody knows what a “public option” is because there’s no common definition and nothing like it has ever been seen in the wild, so it can be anything weaselly politicians want it to be. It needs to be put out of our misery. The “Right” Path There’s danger in chasing the content of various “public options.” The concept is designed as a distraction. Wasting years on policy rabbit holes is the point. But killing it requires some analysis, so it’s worth pointing out that every one of its shape-shifting forms withers under cursory scrutiny. Rising Democratic Senate star Elissa Slotkin (D-MI) says she “wouldn’t say no forever” to a White House run if “there’s not anyone else who’s on the right path.” Slotkin’s favored path leads to one of the sillier public option proposals. In January, Slotkin, Senator Sheldon Whitehouse (D-RI), and Rep. Jan Schakowsky (D-IL) introduced the Affordable Consumer Health Options and Insurance Competition Enhancement (CHOICE) Act, or the Affordable CHOICE Act (“ACA” — get it?). The CHOICE Act would get the federal government into the business of selling crummy insurance to almost nobody by allowing the feds to market their own versions of “bronze,” “silver,” and “gold” plans on the Obamacare exchanges. Even at record enrollment, the Affordable Care Act exchanges covered less than 7 percent of Americans. Nearly everyone with insurance has Medicare, Medicaid, or private insurance through a job. Three-quarters of US working-age adults are eligible for job-based coverage, nearly all of which is considered legally “affordable” by ACA rules. That means almost all workers are barred from receiving ACA premium subsidies. Presumably they could buy a Slotkinized plan by paying the full premium cost if they really wanted one. Nice option. Subsidized or not, Slotkinized plans would cover only between 58 and 82 percent of the actual cost of health care after premiums, depending on the “metal” label attached to the plan. CHOICE Act public option plans would have to play the same failed “cost control” game as private insurers, building their own private provider networks and trading privileged access to their patients for discounts off market price from providers who join those networks. So, Senator Slotkin’s options will come loaded with the copays, coinsurance, and deductibles that private insurance corporations use to coerce patients into accepting in-network doctors and hospitals. It’s a mockery of “affordability” for patients and would offer neither better nor worse provider payments than UnitedHealth, Aetna, or Cigna. The contrast between Senator Slotkin and the winner of Michigan’s other Senate seat couldn’t be clearer. At his victory celebration, Abdul El-Sayed turned his three-line slogan into a roaring call and response, with Medicare for All as the closer: “Money out of . . . POLITICS! Money in your . . . POCKET! Medicare for . . . ALL!” Corporate Democrats are still trying to paint Medicare for All as too radical and urge progressive winners toward “moderation,” torturing voting data into arguments about electability. The facts: In 2024, Elissa Slotkin defeated Mike Rogers by a 0.3 percent margin. With unlimited American Israel Public Affairs Committee and corporate cash arrayed against him and in the face of nakedly racist opposition, El-Sayed beat Rogers by a full 7 percent. Wait, Not That Public Option Criticize one slippery idea and the optionites will just say, “Hey, I didn’t mean that public option.” In two recent posts , economist Paul Krugman hyped a Medicare “buy-in” version to “allow individuals, and possibly more important, employers, to sign up for Medicare benefits, paying actuarially fair premiums to Medicare reflecting the health status of their workers” (emphasis added). Krugman insists that “many people and many employers would surely take it.” Surely many who need it the most wouldn’t. The US health care system is so broken in part because we’ve asked six million separate employers who don’t know and shouldn’t be expected to know much about health care to buy it for most of us. But even they know that premiums reflecting the health status of workers would immediately price employers who most need an affordable option — those with high-cost employees — out of any “buy-in.” The Krugmanite option violates a core Medicare precept. Of course, individual Medicare premiums shouldn’t exist at all. Paying taxes should buy your health care. Even so, except for a few high-income people, everyone pays the same Medicare premiums right now. “Actuarially fair premiums” tailored to health risks contradict the basic notions of solidarity at the heart of public benefits and the very idea of insurance, which is supposed to pool risk across groups of people, not segregate it. Offering existing original Medicare as a standalone option for employers and nondisabled individuals younger than sixty-five to “buy” is an idea only a Nobel-winning trade economist dabbling in health care could love. Original Medicare’s cost-sharing has already driven most eligible people into Medicare Advantage plans run by the very companies that Krugman dismissed as “parasites.” So, to fully cover their workers, employers would have to — you guessed it — buy supplemental insurance from the private insurance industry. Never underestimate the power of Nancy Pelosi’s Iron Law of Democratic Health Policy: Congress shall make no law respecting expansion of health care access that does not subsidize the private health insurance industry, and all gestures to the contrary shall be performance art. False Equivalence Some people may actually take these ideas seriously, but the essence of public optionism is a bully’s taunt. Virtually every economic analysis of the issue has concluded that a single-payer system would generate large savings to fund both expanding coverage to everyone and dramatically improving existing coverage. Two key sources of those savings are huge, useless administrative costs: the insurance industry’s skim, and the massive bureaucracies that providers have to build just to get paid by thousands of private insurance plans whose terms and members constantly change. Original Medicare is far more efficient than private insurance, spending 97 to 98 percent of revenue on actual health care, while private plans capture more than 10 percent of our premiums as overhead and profit. If public insurance is so darned efficient, the argument goes, a public option should outcompete private insurance. That would have the dual benefit of giving people cheaper, better coverage, and proving to a socialism-averse public that something like Medicare for All could work. So prove it, lefties! The problem, of course, is that operating in existing markets under rules that will force public options to behave like private insurers strips these plans of the permanence and power that make Medicare and truly “public” insurance relatively efficient. Medicare controls prices because it permanently covers everyone in the largest group of people who use a lot of health care. When you turn sixty-five and enroll, you’re in for life. If hospitals want to treat the group of people most likely to need their services, they have to take Medicare prices. The CHOICE Act makes a head fake in that direction. The bill instructs the Health and Human Services secretary to create a provider network and set hospital and physician prices “through a negotiated agreement with health care providers,” without naming which providers or how they’ll negotiate. In the absence of successful negotiations, CHOICE Act plans can just pay any claim at existing Medicare prices. That may sound like a little public muscle flexing to hold prices down, but the CHOICE Act also allows hospitals and doctors to opt out of the public options’ networks, turning Medicare prices into a backstop that applies only to out-of-network claims. Having a guaranteed right to impose low prices on out-of-network care would give public option plans a little pricing leverage. But in the end, the Slotkinized plans would likely wind up creating networks and setting prices in a manner similar to private plans, whose tussles with hospitals are based entirely on market power. Having surrendered the power that allows Medicare to set its own prices and competing as start-ups with private insurance to entice “covered lives” to enroll, the public options would soon find themselves face-to-face with massively powerful regional health systems affiliated with Ivy League schools or the Catholic Church. Have fun. To persuade the necessary “covered lives” to enroll, CHOICE Act plans will have to ape private plans by constantly marketing themselves and enrolling and disenrolling clients. Alongside the headache of managing networks, that work requires skimming billions in premium dollars off the top, just like private insurers. Slotkin, Krugman, and their optional pals have also managed the remarkable feat of finding an idea that would add a little more bureaucracy to the already bloated administrative expenses that choke US hospitals. The CHOICE Act doesn’t create an option. It envisions hundreds of local public option plans with premiums set geographically — ACA plans are offered on a county-by-county basis. Viewed from a hospital’s business office, that means at least three separate local plans, one per “metal,” with each becoming just another payer with its own unique prices, networks, utilization review, and patient cost-sharing to follow, track, bill, and sue when payments don’t show up. Finally, should policymakers and regulators fool themselves into thinking that these options will be efficient because, you know, they’re “public” and offer below-market premiums, the plans face a potential flood of high-risk sign-ups (the “adverse selection” problem). As Jim Kahn and Hank Abrons point out in Health Justice Monitor , even the more-expansive-than-Krugman version offered by The American Prospect is particularly vulnerable on this question. Being stuck with disproportionately high-cost patients wouldn’t be good for plans trying to prove the efficiency of public insurance, but it would be great for politicians seeking to prove that the government really can’t do health care. We could go on forever about the details, but the content doesn’t really matter. These ideas are little more than lumps of lead over which political alchemists like Slotkin and Krugman chant their two magic words to dazzle us into believing they’re creating gold. Public options aren’t incremental reforms toward universal coverage. They’re increments of nothing that enable the status quo. This fantasy nonidea endures because fighting for it achieves a critical bipartisan goal: buying time for corporate control of the rest of the health care system. A legislative timeline would likely look like this: two years until the Democrats have a shot at the White House, followed by a year or two of GOP panic about “communism.” Throw in two to three years to write the regulationss, then five or ten years to roll out the plans and watch them fail. Presto! A decade or two of corporate looting passes, with trillions of dollars squandered and another million or two preventable deaths. There are plenty of meaningful steps on the way to a publicly financed universal health care system that ensures everyone gets the care they need without facing personal bankruptcy or fighting off bloodthirsty private bureaucrats. Politicians who want to improve access or cost control could jump on a few of these sample bandwagons. Don’t expect a rush, though. Most of these ideas take a bite out of the industry’s hide, and letting poor people who make an extra penny keep their health care will give alleged conservatives apoplexy. But here they are: Set fair hospital prices by regulation for all payers, to stop the gouging of employers and patients and end the episodic waves of terror that engulf communities when big insurance companies and hospitals threaten patients with loss of access during local rate negotiations. Lower the age at which everyone becomes eligible for Medicare to fifty or fifty-five. Eliminate the extreme cost-sharing in the original Medicare program. Abolish the Medicare Advantage privatization scheme that lets private insurance companies overcharge the federal government tens of billions of dollars a year, and use the savings to fund said elimination of original Medicare cost-sharing. Buck Washington, DC’s current antitrust fad by lifting restrictions on employers who want to join together to bargain for health care and administer benefits. Look, employer control of health care makes no sense. But if corporate politicians fantasize that it’s a route to better benefits and lower costs, let bosses gather the power to control prices and administrative costs, as long as they do it themselves without the insurance industry, and as long as they’re willing to pay the price of offering uniform plans across their cartel — no more tiny tweaks to networks or boutique cost-sharing provisions for each employer. Dramatically expand the time people can stay on Medicaid. Medicaid’s constant means-testing makes it an unreliable source of coverage. California applied for a waiver to keep kids enrolled through the first five years of their lives instead of making their parents reapply every year. Do more of that and for longer. The worst thing about public optionism is enduring smug lectures that Medicare for All is a political fantasy that could never happen from people promoting a vague, unworkable idea now enjoying its twenty-third year of politically viable nonexistence. Yet The American Prospect casually assumes that a series of essential Medicare for All elements against which the industry will fight like rabid dogs can easily be grafted onto a public option. Health care is a public good. Pretending that having government act like a health insurance corporation will do anything useful is absurd. Let’s kill this putrefying political monster right now. Beyond the Blue Wave

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. To reject fascism is a necessary and good thing. The 2026 election results are part of that. But we should also acknowledge a sad reality. We have not overnight entered another better world. To joyously celebrate having elected some new and quite promising Democrats, some other typical business-as-usual Democrats, and especially to have the Democrats as a whole re-empowered is fully warranted. Glass half full says Trumpism is on the way out. Trump will be but another stain, albeit a major one, on a long-since and repeatedly horribly re-stained history. But Trump will not redirect history to become forever worse. Fascism is stopped. We needed that. Trump is trounced. We celebrate that. Glass half empty says we are celebrating lesser enemies of the people re-empowered, not people’s emissaries finally triumphant. To have lived sixty years after having decided with my generation that my society needed comprehensive revolution, and to be watching election returns, and to smile because the Democratic Party has gotten back in the saddle—yes, it is what we immediately needed, but no it is very far from what we ultimately want. We understandably joyously celebrate tentative survivalism having struck a blow against vicious exterminism. We joyously welcome back into the societal saddle the less vile capitalist, sexist, racist, authoritarianism that birthed the even more vile Trumpism. We celebrate for a day and maybe even for two or three days. It was an essential step. But when we realize the actual character of what we are celebrating, then what? Will we relax? Electorally sated? Or will we escalate our demands and invigorate our energies? Related: Upsurges Bubble Around the Globe but Will it Evolve into Effective Change? Look ahead. It is always problematic to do so. After all, what can we confidently say about a better future? Nonetheless, look ahead. It is often essential to do so. What must we cautiously say about a better future if we are to sensibly seek it? People voted against Donald but if we look a little deeper we see that many people changed their views to do so. Many people didn’t just reject Donald, they reversed prior commitments. And neither Trump’s lies and threats nor, even more instructive, big donor massive money could fully block that. Not could MAGA identity. Does anyone think it was a “we love the Democratic Party” sentiment at work? No, the day’s message was we want real change and we are starting to understand what real change can really mean. To continue that trend we need to now fight to scuttle tariff stupidity. To cancel hyperscaler data centers. To reinstate Trump-terminated social programs. To expand health care. To address atrophying infrastructure. To amply finance education and teachers. To abolish ICE. To re-elevate feminism. To pursue a national wealth tax that precludes billionaires running to escape it. To end the war. To slash the military budget. To impeach Trump, Hegseth, and RFK Jr., and to prosecute corruption. And as wild as all that would be, look closely, it is actually virtually all already popularly desired. But let’s also look ahead. Let’s guess that the upcoming Presidential primary season causes more people to change their views. Let’s hypothesize that 2028 pits AOC against some troglodyte Republican in a race to claim the Oval Office. In that case, what should a serious, committed, anti-authoritarian, feminist, anti-racist, anti-capitalist organization or individual do? And what should that same organization or individual do beyond the next election? To address those questions we should’ve consider what will need to be done and who will be available and even eager to do it. Zohran Mamdani enlisted 90,000 volunteers to become Mayor of New York City. Mamdani won. He is now Mayor. He must now win gains for deserving constituencies while he navigates the pressures of keeping the city functional. At the same time, New York will live on after his tenure. So regarding the city’s future, popular movements need to further raise public consciousness, further galvanize urban energies, further inspire activist constituencies to establish longer run structures, undertake extensive campaigns, and discover and share visionary aims to further pursue. Related: Trump Needs “Good Americans” to Look the Other Way The national situation that AOC or any other progressive candidate who wins the Presidency in 2028 would face will be similar but even more conflicted, diverse, and chaotic than Mamdani’s situation in New York. Yet to win the Oval Office, despite its larger scale and greater stakes, will encounter at least the same two big agenda tasks. Task1: Positively navigate the exigencies of first winning the office and of then governing in the election’s aftermath. And simultaneously, Task 2: Positively establish organizational and programmatic means to arouse diverse constituencies to persistently pursue wider, deeper vision. Electorally win office. Govern. Implement immediate gains. Simultaneously, extra-electorally build activist means and cultivate activist desires to win more and larger gains later. Does any radical much less any revolutionary disagree? If not, is there more we can usefully say? One overarching movement issue we face is that Task 1, which is to deal with winning the Oval Office and to maintain services and meet immediate needs, typically falls short of assisting and can even become incompatible with Task 2 which needs winning and maintenance to deal with present needs in ways that become compatible with and support activism to develop more comprehensive vision and practice for the future. So a progressive electoral project will need to seek needed improvements and maintain and administer systems in 2029, but also to intersect supportively with a parallel activist extra electoral project to transform underlying institutions to create a fundamentally better future? When Hugo Chavez, undeniably charismatic and capable, won presidential power in Venezuela, what happened? Down the road, way less happened than what he and the masses of Chavistas hoped for. Was that just tough luck or did things go wrong that could be corrected in another time and another place? When Chavez became Venezuela’s President, there was one leftist governor, no leftist police departments, and maybe two or three leftist mayors on his side and beyond that there were residual troglodytes and intensely aggressive antagonistic business exploiters and media manipulators who profoundly hated him and his supporters. There were also, throughout Venezuela, many culturally and socially regressive holdover habits. And there was a multiply divided population. A victorious progressive AOC, or whoever might emerge from a wildly successful 2028 election would be in a similar but still worse situation than Chavez endured because where Chavez started with strong support in the military AOC would not likely have that. And where Chavez had the U.S. government and corporate elites as an enemy without—AOC would have that enemy operating from within. So what emerges from considering a still hypothetical electoral project two years before 2028? Those with eyes wide open, or even with eyes not thoroughly occluded, would in this scenario surely vote for AOC. Look at today’s returns. Texas, Michigan, Kansas, and more switched big at every level. Add two more years of Trump. Get way more youth involved like Mamdani did. You can imagine it if you try. Best case, not 90,000 but millions of volunteers open still more eyes to build the massive momentum needed to carry AOC to victory. She and her team and the broad public would, in that best case, induce the needed momentum for that to happen—like Mamdani and his team and supporters induced the needed momentum in NYC. That will be Task 1 mentioned earlier. That will win and then administer. But if we suppose that is attainable, and if we hypothesize it occurs for this possible future, then what about Task 2? What would pressure the new administration from without on behalf of developing further vision and arousing new confidence, desires, and lasting structures able to continue escalating progress year after year until fundamental victory? The aim wouldn’t be to win office and the movement then dissipates. The aim would be to win office and the movement then persists, expands, deepens, and finally wins a fundamentally reconstructed country. Who will prioritize achieving that? Related: From the New Left to Trump: Why Movements of the 1960s Failed to Build Lasting Power And so we come to the choices DSA and other grass roots individuals and local and national progressive and revolutionary organizations will face starting roughly a year from now, and perhaps even sooner—indeed maybe even this week. Whatever specific answers emerge, undoubtedly many nuances and details will arise from new lessons gained and from new conditions that arise as energetic efforts unfold. But even with all that indeterminacy, is there anything we can say now about how to arrive at wise choices able to incorporate new lessons and productively address unfolding conditions? One path is that we ask what needs doing. A partial answer could be that Task 1 and Task 2 need doing. Then we might also ask who will do what is needed, and derivatively, finally, we might ask where will our efforts do the most good? Isn’t that a worthy approach not only for an organization like DSA to decide its role, but also for individuals including writers, activists and organizers to decide theirs? The idea would be determine what is needed and to then decide among all that is needed what we are able to do well, and especially what is otherwise unlikely to be done as well or even just sufficiently if we don’t help do it. The idea would be to do that which we can do that will have the greatest positive desirable impact we can have. Consider DSA engaging with an AOC versus some vile thug election. One observation we might arrive at would be that by virtue of its collective talents and already displayed capacities DSA could certainly attract votes to help AOC win and then help with ideas, volunteers, and staff to administer government. Another observation we might arrive at would be that DSA could productively work to develop vision, infrastructure, and extra-electoral campaigns to raise means and consciousness extending beyond present-day administration to seek future fundamental change. Weighing circumstances, one conclusion as events proceed might be that while DSA could contribute to Task 1 by helping to amass votes needed to win, and could then help conceive steps to win immediate gains, to contribute those efforts would not be particularly decisive. Other actors, less radically aware and less experienced could do those same things. If an AOC campaign was going to win, the energy and insights to accomplish those steps might arise overwhelmingly from previously inexperienced volunteers to such a degree that contributions from long-term more committed and experienced activists and organizations would be at most peripheral. On the other hand, to build momentum for grassroots consciousness raising and to initiate activist campaigns able to push the new administration to build infrastructure and prepare its supporters to seek more and larger gains might fundamentally depend on and reflect efforts by more established actors and so might be where DSA involvement would have most impact on long-term success. Of course, the conclusion could also possibly be the reverse, that just to win requires all hands and all thoughts and all efforts narrowly and immediately applied to, well, just winning. What’s my point? It is that the calculation of what to do is not mainly about what appeals to us. It is not mainly about what feels good for us. And it is not mainly about what displays the most militancy. It is about evaluating what is most needed and who and what can achieve it. It is about exerting energy where one’s efforts will have the most beneficial consequences. It requires a careful accounting of available means, a look-ahead to outcomes needed, and an assessment of the predictable consequences of one’s choices among possible options for what to do. Related: Navigating These Times What if one sees a fuzzy picture? What if there isn’t one single path that nearly everyone in DSA, for example, can agree is best? Then a new choice arises. To vote for one path and require that all members follow it. Or to see more than one advocated way forward and decide to respect different strokes for different folks though with all those involved seeking the same ultimate goal even while pursuing different steps toward it. In other words to decide to acknowledge and respect differences and welcome diversity. So, a group like DSA might agree on the aim, for example to help accomplish Task 1 and Task 2, but respect members who favor two or more ways for its chapters and members to contribute to attaining the aim. Members debate. Partisans of each path respect partisans of the other path. Both paths proceed. Experience evaluates. Znetwork

Plea From Gaza

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: LA Progressive ／ 著者: W. D. Ehrhart ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-07T16:02:48+00:00

The opinions expressed here are solely the author's and do not reflect the opinions or beliefs of the LA Progressive. As those of you who read my writing know, I have had for nearly three years a wonderful, though at times heartbreaking, correspondence with Rawand Gawad abu Ghanem, a young Palestinian woman living in Gaza with her husband and two sons, Yamen, 6, and Mohammed, 1&1/2. I’ve written about her before. I’ve just received another letter from her dated October 21 st , but I only received it a few days ago. She writes: My Dear Friend, I am sorry to write to you of new sorrow, but sometimes just holding this paper and pen in my hands is what reminds me that I am alive. While I am grateful to Allah that he has granted me the blessing of children, I must raise them in an environment that lacks the minimum necessities, here in a city that loves life but cannot live it. What we have instead is a life of continuous resuscitation, day by day, in this beautiful Gaza that is no longer beautiful. Related: The Democrats' Blue Wave Is Real: The Glass Is Still Half Empty I gave birth to my son Yamen before the genocide began and now it is time for him to begin kindergarten. I dream of buying him a backpack with many zippers, a lunch box, and a kindergarten uniform. I imagine putting into this bag his comb, extra socks in case his own get dirty from playing, a towel and water bottle filled with sweet water, not the dirty, poisoned water we drink now. The Trump Era Is Bad. But Is It Evil?

Leftists have an unusual relationship to the language of good and evil. On the one hand, we have a reputation for being righteous. . . . at times even self-righteous. Our publications are saturated with enough morally strident language to make a preacher blush. Even the most “scientific” of scientific socialists aren’t entirely immune, from tireless invocations of Rev Martin Luther King Jr and constant demands for justice and freedom for all to calls to end subordination and marginalization around the globe . On the other hand, leftists tend to be averse to describing structures, let alone people, as evil. Exploitative? Yes. Domineering? Of course. But evil? At best, that’s language reserved for your Hannah Arendt reading group. Philosopher Susan Neiman takes umbrage at this reticence. Author of the acclaimed Evil in Modern Thought and the controversial Left is Not Woke , Neiman thinks we ought to call a spade and spade and describe the Trump administration as what it is: evil. The disturbing question that follows is: If what Donald Trump stands for is evil, why have millions of people chosen to get behind him not once but three times? As Neiman herself notes: Knowing as much as can be known about a public figure whose cruelty and corruption have been on display for a decade, how was it possible that Americans elected him twice? One New York friend pointed out that no one who ever dealt with the man wanted to see him again — except for the American people. But Neiman is less interested in explaining why Americans voted for Trump than in the larger philosophical question of what constitutes evil. As she writes, “My concern is with the moral ideas that Donald Trump embodies and reinforces, ideas that are destructive enough to warrant the word evil .” The Spirit That Negates Neiman’s understanding of modern evil is deeply indebted to Hannah Arendt’s pioneering description of it as “banal.” She acknowledges that Arendt’s description of Adolf Eichmann himself as a thoughtless man devoid of hateful antisemitic feelings is untrue. But Arendt’s broader point about modern evil lacking any “Satanic majesty” remains true. Neiman reminds us that the “Holocaust could never have happened without the willing participation of millions of people who were not particularly committed fascists or fascists of any kind. They were simply willing to accept whatever demands made their lives easier.” This all-too-human tendency to fall not through active malice but selfishness and indifference is one Neiman thinks is very characteristic of modern American history. Neiman notes how since 1945, “it’s hard to find an example of US foreign policy that isn’t principally Schmittian, though they are usually accompanied by the sort of liberal hypocrisy that Schmitt despised.” Carl Schmitt , the German jurist who joined the Nazi party in 1933, understood politics fundamentally as a struggle between friends and enemies and rejected liberal appeals to universal principles as an ideological fiction that masked power. Schmitt would likely be pleased that the second Trump administration has largely dropped even the veneer of liberal hypocrisy. If hypocrisy is the tribute vice pays to virtue, even the Bush administration at least had the decency to labor for months to convince the American people that the Iraq War was justified. When attacking Venezuela and Iran, not to mention saber-rattling at arrogant Greenlanders and Canadians, the Trump administration is typically much more transparent about the unbridled self-interest at play. After deposing Nicolás Maduro, Trump relished America’s access to Venezuelan oil, proclaiming “to the victor belong the spoils.” Neiman’s core worry is that the transparently evil cynicism on display will become so normalized that we ignore its vulgarity. As the “Schmitt-Trump consensus grows, it’s easy to conclude that no other outlook is possible, and cynicism becomes inevitable.” While I sympathize with this outlook, it does seem to run counter to the longer history of American atrocities Neiman chronicles. If it is true that most American foreign policy since World War II has been basically Schmittian, it would seem that Trump simply constitutes a further moral step downward rather than a new kind of wicked hegemony. Sometimes Neiman gestures in this direction as when she insists this is “not a book about Donald Trump” but about “. . . . the conditions that allowed him rise.” These include the “imperialism the US has practiced for over a century,” which she insists can only be called “evil.” If that’s the case, then what is at stake is really more a general American malaise than some transformative and deepening “‘Schmitt-Trump consensus.” Neiman often seems to want to pull back from such a pessimistic thesis. For instance, while she disagrees with the thrust of US foreign policy, she nonetheless expresses deep respect for “individual members of the military.” Neiman admires how their training prizes duty, fellowship, service, and sacrifice — all ideals someone like Trump simply doesn’t understand. It was exactly these principles that led Neiman to hope that “soldiers sworn to defend the Constitution would never follow the worst of his orders after seeing how thoroughly their commander in chief disdained the values they upheld.” Those hopes were dashed when Trump 2.0 strode to power and replaced the military administration with his coterie of brownnoses and appointed Pete Hegseth secretary of war. Neiman insists that we relearn the watchwords of Trump 1.0: “Don’t normalize this.” Doing so helps make the evil deeds of administration seem typical, and consequently over time, not morally objectionable. I think this ambiguity over whether Trumpism constitutes a continuation of, or break with, the past flows from a few sources. As a leftist, Neiman is keen to acknowledge the harm wrought by American foreign and domestic policy. However, I suspect Neiman is reluctant to suggest to her largely American readers that the country has always had a predilection toward Trumpy behavior. More strategically, she may worry that doing so would further legitimate Trumpism by making it seem normal. It would also blunt the force of the immanent critiques often used to mobilize Americans against their government’s abuses: holding up the country’s shining principles as a mirror to its often-brutal practices and saying, “We don’t look like we ought to.” Whatever the case, I think Neiman would be better off following Arendt herself by situating the emergence of Trumpism in a much more concrete historical narrative. There was tendency, until recently, for many — especially conservative anti-Trumpers — to describe the Trump administration as an aberration in the American right’s history. Contemporary historians are increasingly pushing back on this view. In Taking America Back , David Austin Walsh stresses how the American right has always had plenty of overt racists, conspiracy theorists, Augusto Pinochet apologists, and outright fascists. William F. Buckley, the alleged poster boy for a more intellectual and less extreme conservatism, was happy to recommend segregation and defend Francisco Franco to own the libs. Under Trumpism, the more extreme factions of the Right have simply moved from the back seat to the driver’s seat. But they have always been a part of American life. Taking a dialectical approach to American history, however, means recognizing that, just because many Americans have done evil things, the country as a whole is not evil. If anything, the inspiring story of America is of progressive movements successfully mobilizing against wicked practices to bring about a more just society. The arc of the moral universe may be long . . . Evil Goes Global Much of Neiman’s book ranges beyond its central concern, taking on topics from the hegemony of neoliberal ideology to war crimes in Gaza to her long-standing critique of wokism. At their best, these chapters read more like essays that collectively present a panoramic view of the world. The chapter on wokism was especially interesting. Neiman is very critical of wokism for both principled and strategic reasons. She insists that the Left ought to reclaim its defining principle of inclusive universalism. As she puts it, the “idea of universalism once defined the Left; international solidarity was its watchword. This was just what distinguished it from the right, which recognized no deep connections, and few real obligations, to anyone outside its own circle. The Left demanded that the circle encompass the globe.” Neiman is very critical of identity politics, which eschews universalism in order to insist that “you have deep connections and real obligations only to those who belong to your tribe, though others may have limited use as allies.” I have considerable sympathy for this view. Neiman is no doubt historically correct that trading Enlightenment reason and universalism for postmodern skepticism and what the Marxist David Harvey calls “militant particularism” constituted a shift from the Left’s historical stance. Marxist literary theorist Terry Eagleton made a similar observation in The Illusions of Postmodernism , noting the irony that some leftists had come to endorse philosophical positions typically associated with the Right. While motivated by different political concerns, this skepticism shares the conservative privileging of cultural particularity over reason and universalism. A Left that embraced universalism would, of course, be more comfortable with the language of “moral idealism” Neiman affirms, and that would, by and large, be beneficial. That said, I think the question of how to recommit to universalism without falling back into exclusionary modes of thinking that imagine a single perspective ought to speak for everyone remains unresolved. Here, I think the best work has been done by philosophers working at the intersection of liberalism, Marxism, and black radical thought, notably Tommie Shelby and the late Charles Mills. Both Shelby and Mills are comfortable couching demands for racial justice in the universalistic terms of Rawlsian political theory. But they recognize that universalism risks becoming what Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel criticized as an “abstract universalism” if it is insensitive to the particular forms of subordination experienced around the globe. In the United States, securing the basic rights and liberties to which everyone ought to be entitled means renouncing the legacy of systemic racism. The Peril of Apathy I was slightly more frustrated with the Chapter on “Socialism Reconsidered.” Despite the title, much of it reads like a microcosm of the book itself, weighing in on any number of different issues in quick succession. The most interesting sections of the chapter reintroduce readers to the socialism of Albert Einstein. Einstein was, of course, a self-described socialist who was outspoken about his desire for humankind to evolve past the predatory capitalist phase of existence. But, as Neiman points out, these political convictions are rarely addressed by American biographers and commentators, who are usually content to footnote Einstein’s nonprofessional interest in “social justice.” Reacquainting readers with Einstein’s convictions is worthwhile, but the chapter would have been far more interesting if it had remained focused on reconstructing and developing his political ideas more systematically. It’s perhaps inevitable that Neiman’s book sometimes ranges too broadly, given the complexity of the era it attempts to understand and critique. Anyone who wades into American politics these days can feel immediately overwhelmed by the sheer volume of issues demanding attention: wokeness, neoliberalism, identity politics, Trumpism, the rise of fascism, oligarchs’ control of social media, war in the Middle East, and so much more. There is a risk that the crushing weight of all this might lead many to lapse into the apathetic withdrawal Neiman worries about. Or perhaps worse, to reach for simplifying ideologies that offer a retreat from the complexity of the world. It’s to Neiman’s Socratic credit that she offers moral clarity without resorting to the comfort of cheaply bought moral certainties. In Turkey, When Does a Joke Become a Crime?

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Dora Mengüç ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-07T14:40:49+00:00

Have you ever made a joke that offended someone? Maybe they took it personally or stopped speaking to you. A joke can put strain on a relationship or leave someone feeling isolated. But what if the consequences for a joke go far beyond social fallout? What if a comedy performance lands you in a high-security prison cell? For Deniz Göktaş, this question is no longer hypothetical. Until recently, Göktaş performed for relatively small audiences, playing bars in Kadıköy, an Istanbul district known for political satire, and occasionally in university halls elsewhere in Turkey. He was part of a niche comedy scene. Things changed when he took his show to one of Istanbul’s most prominent stages and uploaded the performance to YouTube, opening it to a much wider audience. He knew the show might attract attention. Not just because of the words he spoke onstage, but also because the title and stage image played on a grisly visual gag. Ölü Deniz (“Dead Sea”) was a dark play on his first name, Deniz (“Sea”), while an enormous, sculpted image of his face lay on its side as if decapitated. What began as a night of laughter would end with Göktaş spending eighty-eight days behind bars in a high-security prison. His case exposed not only the limits of free expression in Turkey but also the fraught relationship between political satire and political power. The Charges The thirty-two-year-old comedian was detained at Istanbul airport on July 2, after his ninety-minute special was released on YouTube. Prosecutors cited passages from the performance in bringing charges: insulting President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan, inciting public hatred and hostility, and praising crime or criminals. At his first hearing, on September 28, 2026, Göktaş addressed the court: “As a comedian, seeing that sentences can have such a powerful impact makes me proud. But as a citizen, I am saddened by my country’s record on freedom of expression.” He said he worried the case would discourage others from performing and lead them to censor themselves. The court ordered his release after sentencing. By then, he had spent eighty-eight days in pretrial detention. He told the court he had been held in a cell without sunlight or contact with other prisoners at Çorlu Karatepe High Security Prison, a facility known in Turkey as a well-type prison. The court acquitted him of praising crime or criminals. It convicted him of insulting the president and, because public hatred and hostility were not established, reclassified the incitement charge as insulting religious values. The sentences were eleven months and twenty days and seven months and fifteen days, respectively. The case raised a question beyond the sentences themselves: How had a comedy performance been transformed into a criminal prosecution? Göktaş’s defense argued that prosecutors had removed the alleged offenses from their original context onstage, but the heart of the matter was a dispute over how a comedian’s words should be interpreted. Jokes About Politicians Are Not the Same as Political Comedy While Göktaş was still in prison, I spoke with his friend and fellow stand-up comedian Emre Günsal. Günsal distinguishes Göktaş’s comedy from much of what passes for “political humor” in Turkey. A comedian can get an easy laugh simply by mentioning a politician, referring to a current scandal, or repeating a political cliché the audience already agrees with. In Turkey, Günsal says, that is the least risky kind of political comedy. Göktaş’s work is more deliberate. He combines political satire with social observation, using comedy to say something about the political climate, social tensions, and the shrinking space for free expression. The result can be both funny and cathartic for those who feel alienated or frustrated by the society in which they live. Günsal considers Göktaş one of the leading figures of this tradition in Turkey today. This may help explain Göktaş’s appeal among young people and audiences opposed to the government. Günsal describes Göktaş’s work as a form of emotional release, then trails off: “Seeing someone stand onstage, knowing millions of people will watch, and say the things that others are afraid to say openly because of what might happen to them. . . . ” Göktaş made a similar point in an interview with The New York Times after his release. He recalled a viewer writing beneath his performance that watching it made them feel as though they lived in a free country. The comment, he said, captured exactly what he had hoped to achieve. But he was modest about comedy’s political power. Humor, he suggested, can help people feel understood, given them motivation and the courage to speak, and offer psychological support. Plus Ça Change Political satire and state power has a long and vexed history in Turkey. In 1946, two of the country’s best-known writers, Sabahattin Ali and Aziz Nesin, founded Markopaşa , a socialist-leaning satirical newspaper that sharply criticized class inequality and the government of the day. The paper faced distribution restrictions, pressure on printing houses, confiscations, closures, and criminal prosecutions. Its writers and cartoonists were arrested or forced into exile. Markopaşa remains one of the most important examples of socialist and oppositional humor in Turkey. In later decades, political satire found new forms in cabaret theater and humor magazines, including the work of Ferhan Şensoy and the satirical magazines of the 1990s and 2000s. By the time of the 2013 Gezi Park protests, irony and satire had become prominent forms of political expression. The protests began with opposition to plans to redevelop Gezi Park near Istanbul’s Taksim Square. The government framed the unrest in terms of hooliganism and terrorism. Many protesters, meanwhile, used humor and irony to defy the government’s account of the movement. Researchers have documented how humor and satire became a form of dissent in an environment where traditional media were subject to tight control. In his courtroom defense, Göktaş placed himself in this tradition, invoking Aziz Nesin, Rıfat Ilgaz , Ferhan Şensoy, and Turkey’s major satirical magazines. Then he reached back to Nasreddin Hodja , the legendary Anatolian folk figure whose stories have circulated for centuries. Traditionally dated to 1208–1284, Hodja occupies a place in Anatolian culture somewhat comparable to the traditions of folk wisdom and social satire in American humor. Göktaş distilled the connection between that tradition and contemporary Turkey into a single sentence: “If Nasreddin Hodja were doing stand-up today, half his show would be about President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan.” Göktaş argued that calling Erdoğan, who has governed Turkey for more than two decades, a “dictator” was political characterization, not an insult. As the country’s most powerful political figure, Erdoğan should be expected to face satire and harsh criticism, he said. He argued that politicians must be open to ridicule as well as praise. The language of this debate is not new in Turkey. A few days ago, I came across a street bookseller on the narrow Kadıköy street where Deniz Göktaş had performed stand-up for years. Among the books on the shelves was Görülmüştür , a collection by journalist and writer İlhan Selçuk, published in the 1980s. Leafing through it, I found an essay titled “Freedom of Thought and Tolerance.” One sentence stood out: “Where freedom of thought does not exist, tolerance is nothing more than an empty word.” Selçuk’s point resonates in the Göktaş case. If political or religious satire is tolerated only as long as it offends no one, can that be called freedom of expression? How Does a Court Interpret a Joke? One of the clearest examples of this problem was Göktaş’s joke about “divers.” Prosecutors treated it as an insult to women who wear modest swimwear because of their religious beliefs. In court, Göktaş said he used the audience’s expectations to set up the joke. He described people emerging from the sea with their hair concealed and their bodies covered in black clothing. The audience, he said, assumed that he was describing women wearing modest Islamic swimwear. Then came the punch line : “f–king divers.” The case raised a basic question: Can a stand-up performance be judged solely by a written transcript, stripped of its context onstage? Göktaş invoked American comedian Lenny Bruce, who had faced similar problems. Bruce satirized the courts with the line, “In the Halls of Justice the only justice is in the halls.” He was repeatedly arrested and prosecuted on obscenity charges during the 1960s. In 1964, a New York court sentenced him to four months in a workhouse. He remained free while his appeal was pending and died in 1966 without serving the sentence. Thirty-seven years after his death, in 2003, New York governor George Pataki granted him a posthumous pardon. Bruce’s trials showed how words spoken by a comedian onstage should not be evaluated independently of their delivery, facial expressions, timing, and relationship with the audience. Göktaş argued that tone of voice, pauses, physical performance, audience reactions, and the surprise of the punch line all help make up a joke’s meaning. He compared evaluating a stand-up performance by its transcript to understanding a film by listening only to its soundtrack. Clear and Present Danger To better understand how Turkish law treats the line between satire and incitement, I spoke with Professor Tolga Şirin. Göktaş was still being held in a high-security prison. For Şirin, any legal assessment had to begin with context. “What does it mean within a stand-up performance?” he asked. A legal assessment, he said, must account for irony, exaggeration, satire, and the possibility that a comedian will deliberately lead an audience in one direction before reversing its expectations. Evaluating a few sentences only according to their literal meaning can exclude other reasonable interpretations and privilege the harshest possible criminal reading. Şirin stressed that this was not merely an aesthetic question: “The fact that a statement offends or disturbs some people is not sufficient in itself.” Context matters particularly to the charge of inciting public hatred and hostility. Under the Turkish Penal Code, the offense requires more than a statement that people find offensive. It also requires the emergence of a clear and imminent danger to public safety. Prosecutors cited complaints submitted through CİMER, Turkey’s online public petition and complaints system operating under Turkey’s presidency. In his defense, Göktaş addressed the 185 complaints submitted against him. He argued that the overwhelming majority of complainants had never watched his full performance but had instead reacted to selectively edited clips circulated on social media. He described the backlash as an artificially orchestrated campaign. For Şirin, the complaint count alone could not establish a legally significant danger. Otherwise, organized groups could make speech they disliked into a punishable offense simply by launching petition campaigns. “Public peace is not measured by the number of people who are offended,” he said. Göktaş said he performed Ölü Deniz 198 times over approximately three years, before more than a hundred thousand people in cities across Turkey, without receiving complaints about the statements in question. According to him, the controversy began only after the performance was uploaded to YouTube and short clips started circulating on social media. “What development has caused a statement made and made accessible months ago to suddenly create a clear and imminent danger today?” Şirin asked. When I asked whether the prosecution could affect other comedians and artists, Şirin’s answer was unequivocal: “This is absolutely an act of intimidation.” According to Şirin, Göktaş had pushed against the fear surrounding political figures and sensitive subjects that many people hesitate to joke about. In Şirin’s view, the proceedings could deter other comedians and artists from crossing the same boundaries. “We Have Cancel Culture — and Jail” Günsal’s account shows how the chilling effect described by Şirin can manifest itself on the stand-up stage. Small comedy venues in Istanbul have long provided spaces where comedians can speak relatively freely. The risk changes when a performance circulates on the internet. Jokes lose their original context and reach a much larger audience. After Göktaş’s arrest, Günsal saw comedians grow more reluctant to post political sets. He believes the greater problem is not simply censorship but the inability to tell where the line lies. “There is no clear line telling you, ‘Say this and you go to prison, but you can say that,’” he explained. Even careful comedians cannot know how their words will later be interpreted. The uncertainty can result in performers ruling out jokes before they have even written them. Günsal knows that risk firsthand. In April 2020, he was arrested after a clip from one of his performances circulated on social media. The case involved comments about Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, the founder of the Turkish Republic, and the historical religious figures Rumi and Shams of Tabriz. He spent eleven days in prison. In 2021, he was sentenced to three years and five months; the sentence was suspended. Six years later, while Deniz Göktaş was behind bars, Günsal tells me that political comedy had become both more difficult and more necessary. After his release, Göktaş told The New York Times that he never regretted releasing the performance during his time in prison, not even for a second. As his popularity grew, he said, he had more to lose and began censoring his own material even before his arrest. Asked about American comedians’ complaints about cancel culture and online backlash, he replied: “I don’t want to brag, but we have cancel culture — and jail.” Choosing to Stay There is another revealing detail in the Göktaş case. When the investigation began, he was abroad. According to his courtroom defense, he cut his vacation short and returned to Turkey rather than fleeing. He said that most of his close friends had moved abroad because they no longer saw a future in the country. He had been trying to persuade them to come back. Günsal described this decision as one of the qualities he most admired in his friend. Günsal believes Göktaş could perform stand-up in English and appear regularly on stages abroad. But Göktaş does not want to build a new home somewhere else. That determination to keep calling Turkey home is something Günsal admires. Deniz Göktaş is no longer in prison. But the impact of his case cannot be measured solely by his release on September 28. Perhaps the more telling measure will be the jokes not written, the performances never uploaded to the internet, and the comedians who censor themselves before finishing a sentence because they cannot predict where the legal boundaries lie. The Göktaş case is not merely about whether one comedian crossed the limits of free expression. It is also a struggle over who gets to draw those limits in Turkey. Given austerity measures, it might understandably be seen as gung ho in its courting of capitalist projects that have little consideration for the local environment or workers. Such austerity is arguably responsible for a slowdown of the economy through its negative impact on spending. Finland’s leaders mustn’t seek to compensate by giving hasty permissions for foreign AI companies who ignore ordinary Finns’ interests. They park their money in the stock market. That is not meaningfully investing in opportunity, it’s just investing in other billionaires.” So said Abdul El-Sayed , U.S. Senator-elect from Michigan. He said that he would rather “have an economy that makes a thousand millionaires than one billionaire,” and that money that would otherwise go into the S&P 500 should go instead to “good schools, good infrastructure, health care, child care.” It’s a laudable sentiment, but those thousand millionaires would probably still be parking their money in investments designed to make more money without producing anything. When an investor buys Nvidia shares on the stock market, the money goes to whoever sold the shares, not to Nvidia. A company gets new capital only when it issues new stock. The profits from stock market trades go either to the buyer or the seller, depending on which way the penny falls. And it’s not just the billionaires and millionaires playing those financial markets. Our household savings, corporate funds and pension funds are gambling there as well. State and local pension funds hold $6.2 trillion , more than a fifth of the GDP of the U.S. economy. That massive pool of money is not building housing, infrastructure or local businesses. It is parked in Wall Street’s casino, bidding up the price of existing assets, while asset managers collect their fees whether the pension funds win or lose. And many pundits are predicting an imminent market crash, in which the funds will lose big. Worse, the returns to the pension funds are no better than they could get from a simple index fund without incurring outside management fees, and sometimes they are worse. Meanwhile, the nation’s only state-owned bank, the Bank of North Dakota, earned a 17.2% return on its capital in 2025 while lending into its state’s own economy, and paid $258 million of its profits back to the state. North Dakota’s money is safe, earning, and invested in local productivity. Related: AI Abundance: Meaning Beyond Work The House Always Wins “Everything feels like a casino,” said UK financial commentator Anna Bocca in a March 2026 video titled “ How Financialisation Rewired the Economy .” “ Financialization ” is the process by which financial markets and institutions have gained growing influence over economic policy and investing, shifting the focus from making physical goods and services to using money to make more money. The “house” in today’s financial casino is the asset manager collecting our pension money – Bocca names BlackRock, State Street, and Vanguard – and the house wins even when the pensioners lose. Consider New York City. Its five pension funds held $326.3 billion as of June 30, 2026. The city pays about $10 billion a year into them, even as it struggles to close a projected $5.4 billion budget deficit. What does the city get for its money? In 2015, then-Comptroller Scott Stringer examined ten years of results from the asset managers running the city’s pension funds. Managers of public stocks and bonds had beaten their benchmarks (the returns the same money would have earned in a standard market index such as the S&P 500) by $2.063 billion. But that was before deducting the managers’ fees. The fees consumed more than 95% of the gain, leaving the funds only an estimated $40 million ahead. Managers of the funds’ private investments (stakes in assets not traded on public exchanges, such as private equity and real estate funds) fell $2.6 billion short of their benchmarks after fees. Overall, the outside managers fell more than $2.5 billion dollars short of benchmark returns over the ten year period. The pension funds would have done better in plain index funds. “Right now, heads or tails, Wall Street wins,” Stringer said . Stringer’s study was cited by Scott Baker, managing editor at OpEdNews and a Public Banking Institute expert on pension funds, in a November 2025 article titled “ The Case for Selling off the New York City Pension Funds .” Baker asks, “Why do we need pension funds at all?” More on his answer after a look at pension fund financing as it has evolved over the last half-century. Related: The Digital Dollar That Was Too Democratic to Survive How Our Pensions Got into the Casino The evolution of pension fund financing was traced in a July 2025 report from the American Federation of Teachers (AFT) and other sponsors titled “ From Public Pensions to Private Fortunes: How Working People’s Retirements Line Billionaire Pockets .” The authors observed that before 1980, state and local pension funds were often treated as a “pay as you go” system: a portion of current employee premiums was used to pay benefits to retirees, similar to Social Security. Then a new “actuarial” accounting approach began treating any shortfall between a fund’s assets and its future obligations as an “unfunded” liability. The pressure to close that apparent gap “drove an unprecedented accumulation of assets” – money-producing investments such as stocks, bonds, real estate and stakes in private equity. Between 1980 and 2007, state and local pension assets grew from $183 billion to $3.2 trillion, from 6% to 23% of GDP. By 2024, they stood at $6.2 trillion (an equivalent percentage of GDP, which had also grown). Tom Sgouros, a public finance consultant, former senior policy advisor to the Rhode Island General Treasurer and fellow at Brown University’s Policy Lab, has questioned the need for this accumulation of financial assets at the expense of productive investment. In a 2022 report for the National Conference on Public Employee Retirement Systems , he pointed out that a public pension plan, unlike a private one, “need not worry about the disappearance of its sponsoring employer,” in this case the city. Cities can go bankrupt, but they will always be there. A new government will just replace the old one. Until the 1960s, according to a November 2024 housing report co-authored by Sgouros for Americans for Financial Reform (AFR) and Georgetown University, it was “unremarkable” for pension funds to invest in state and local government bonds. Today, however, it is rare. Most municipal bonds are tax-exempt and pay lower interest rates than corporate bonds; and pension funds, which pay no taxes, get no benefit from the tax break. The older arrangement, which Sgouros calls “fiscal mutualism,” meant that public workers’ savings financed public needs: their pension funds lent to their own cities and states for schools and other projects, and were paid back with interest. This mutually beneficial arrangement unraveled when pension funds began chasing higher yields in corporate bonds, forcing local governments to borrow on the open market at higher rates and fees. Meanwhile, most private sector workers have lost their guaranteed pensions altogether. According to a March 2026 Congressional Research Service report , only 14% of private sector workers had access in 2025 to a “defined benefit” pension (one that guarantees a set monthly payment for life, usually based on salary and years of service), compared to 70% with access to a “defined contribution” plan such as a 401(k), whose value rises and falls with the markets. Related: AI Abundance and the Future of Freedom “Patsies for the Private Equity Industry” According to Reason Foundation data , in 2001 public pension funds held 57.7% of their assets in publicly-traded stocks, 31.4% in bonds, and 9% in “alternatives” — private equity, hedge funds, real estate and commodities. By fiscal year 2024, 34% of pension fund investments were in the alternatives, which carry significantly more costs and risks even than the stock market. Private equity (PE) is particularly risky and controversial. It relies heavily on borrowed money, and investors must commit their money for ten years or more. The management fees are also far higher – typically 2% of asset values plus 20% of profits – and according to the AFT report, “PE managers frequently manipulate reported asset values.” Correcting for these factors, the report concludes, private equity’s “yield advantage is little more than a mirage, facilitated by leverage used for buyouts.” Yet public pension funds are the biggest backers of the private equity industry. According to the AFT report, public pensions “make up almost a third of all investors to private equity funds and contribute an astonishing 67 percent of their capital.” PE firms are notorious for the leveraged buyout, described by the AFR housing report as “using a company’s own assets to finance its takeover by private investors and then looting them after: selling off real estate, closing pension plans, abruptly shuttering less-profitable divisions and major layoffs.” Employment at companies bought by private equity shrinks significantly, and a leveraged buyout can result in bankruptcy. The report points to the devastating collapse of two private equity-owned hospital chains, Steward Health Care and Prospect Medical Holdings. What do the pension funds get for backing PE firms? In a 2020 study titled “ An Inconvenient Fact: Private Equity Returns & The Billionaire Factory ,” Oxford professor Ludovic Phalippou found that private equity funds “have returned about the same as public equity indices since at least 2006.” Yet the performance fees on funds raised from 2006 to 2015 were estimated at $230 billion. In an interview, Phalippou called it a “wealth transfer from several hundred million pension scheme members to a few thousand people working in private equity,” one that “might be one of the largest in the history of modern finance.” In an April 2024 podcast , financial journalist Bethany McLean countered the fiction that pension fund managers are sophisticated investors, stating, “ They’re just the patsies for the private equity industry .” Related: Fed Regime Change: From Big Bank Bailouts to Local Productivity Bailing Out the Casino Privatization, says Bocca, has “shifted all financial risk and reward away from the state and onto the individual pensioner,” while funneling trillions of dollars into the hands of global asset managers. BlackRock alone now manages a record $15.3 trillion . Bocca describes the pursuit of shareholder profit over company productivity as “cost cutting instead of long-term innovation, debt loading instead of reinvestment, outsourcing instead of stable employment, stock buybacks instead of wage growth.” When a private equity firm loads a company with debt and it collapses, “it’s often the workers, residents, or taxpayers who end up bearing the consequences.” Asset managers’ fees grow with the size of the pool, not necessarily with its performance; and when markets crash, the losses are absorbed by the pensioners. A case in point is Blackstone, considered the world’s largest asset manager and the largest landlord in the United States. Blackstone is funded by CalPERS and many other public pension funds. In 2021, it bought 5,600 affordable housing units in San Diego and raised rents by 43% to 65% in two years. “Among the most troubling aspects of the housing crisis,” wrote the authors of the AFR housing report, “is the extent to which workers’ own capital is being used to make it worse.” The pension fund that is supposed to secure a teacher’s retirement may be financing the landlord raising her rent. The asset managers’ fastest growing product is private credit — loans made directly to companies by investment funds rather than banks. According to the AFT report, private credit loans “are not regulated and not covered by securities laws or even rated,” and private credit “also relies heavily on public pensions.” It is now seeing rising defaults on its loans, which are often to companies owned by PE firms. Yet an August 2025 executive order directed regulators to ease the way for private equity and other alternative assets to be included in 401(k) plans. When the whole casino is in trouble, the public again bails it out. When markets froze in March 2020, the Federal Reserve hired BlackRock to manage its new corporate debt-buying programs. The flood of central bank money lifted asset prices, and those who owned the most assets gained the most. According to Oxfam, billionaires’ wealth rose more in the first 24 months of Covid-19 than in the previous 23 years combined. “Investors keep the upside,” says Bocca. “Society absorbs the downside.” Today, Moneywise reports , the U.S. has a record 989 billionaires, and the 400 wealthiest Americans are worth a record $8 trillion , up from $6.6 trillion a year earlier. The city could guarantee retirees their current benefits plus annual cost-of-living increases, and pay them as Social Security does, from current revenues. It could lay off the outside investment managers and sell off the fund assets gradually over about five years. Part of the proceeds could pay down city debt, immediately raising the city’s credit rating and lowering borrowing costs. The rest could capitalize a public bank. Baker envisions a bank with about $50 billion in deposits, lending to local small businesses and individuals that large commercial banks pass over. The stellar U.S. model is the Bank of North Dakota (BND), founded in 1919. By law , state funds are deposited in the BND, which uses them to back loans into the state, mostly in partnership with local banks. As noted above, BND earned net income of $231.8 million in 2025 – a 17.2% return – and paid $210 million into the state’s general fund and another $48.3 million to other state funds. There is no house taking a cut. Momentum for public banks is building around the country, as detailed at a Public Banking Institute conference in Philadelphia on October 22-24. In Washington State and New Mexico , enthusiastic support for public bank bills followed presentations earlier this year by BND president Don Morgan on the mechanics and benefits of the BND. City-owned banks are also in the works. On October 21, the Los Angeles City Council voted 11 to 3 to identify $460,000 for a feasibility study for a city-owned bank; and on November 3, two other California cities voted on public bank initiatives. San Francisco’s Proposition B would create a municipal finance corporation as the first step toward a city-owned public bank, and Berkeley voters will decide on a citizen-initiated parcel tax that would raise $58 million over six years to capitalize a public bank. Neither vote had been called as of November 5; California can take up to 30 days to finalize votes. In Philadelphia, where the City Council voted in 2022 to create a Public Financial Authority as a first step toward a public bank, advocates are pushing “ Project Beloved Community ,” a ten-year plan that includes a city public bank and proposes investing city pension funds in local housing, clean energy and community-owned businesses. In New York City, Mayor Mamdani and Comptroller Levine have begun moving city deposits to community lenders , a first step while advocates press Albany to pass the New York Public Banking Act . And at the national level, The National Infrastructure Bank Act , HR 5356, now has 65 co-sponsors. The money is already ours — our pension contributions, our tax dollars and our public deposits. Rather than handing it to a casino where the house always wins and the pensioners absorb the losses, we can put it to work building homes, businesses and infrastructure in our own communities, and keep the profits for ourselves. Remembering Dolores Huerta (1930-2026)

Dolores Huerta , who died on October 28 at the age of 96, was an honorary chair of DSA from 2012-2016. But her involvement with our organization went back as far as being a speaker at a 1975 convention of one of DSA’s predecessor organizations. My wife, Dolores Delgado-Campbell, and I met her even earlier,when we were co-chairs of the United Farm Workers (UFW) lettuce boycott committee in Sacramento. That commitment to community mobilization against corporate power produced a backlash against her. Texas even banned textbooks on U.S. history because they included her story. On the other hand, when I contributed to a popular history book for middle-school students, The Americans: A History of the United States (1970 & 1979), we included a recording Dolores made in 1968. After leaving UFW leadership in 1994, Dolores traveled the country organizing and encouraging women to step up and take leadership roles in unions , politics, and more. She grew to become one of the leading feminist leaders in the nation focusing particularly on empowering young women and Latinas. Dolores was raised in the Catholic faith, and her lifetime of work demonstrated her commitment to the ideals of the struggle for social justice.Her thinking evolved constantly. Earlier in her life, she was against abortion ; later she was a champion of reproductive justice. Her intensive work, recognized when President Barack Obama presented her with the Presidential Medal of Freedom, illustrated how the fight for union rights and wages also included the fight for women’s leadership in unions, immigrant justice , and reproductive freedom. In the last year of her life she broke a decades-long silence about being assaulted as a young woman by Cesar Chavez and spoke of the misogyny within the farmworker movement, the labor movement, and society in general. We consider ourselves blessed to have had many opportunities to learn from Dolores and to have worked alongside her in campaigns. Israel’s Shaky Democracy Continues to Crumble

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Jared Hillel ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-06T18:19:29+00:00

In July 1950, speaking at the annual Zionist Organization of America convention, US Vice President Alben W. Barkley asserted that Israel was “an oasis of liberty in the desert of despotism,” articulating a precursor to the oft-repeated adage “Israel is the only democracy in the Middle East.” While the expression cannot be traced to a singular person, the sentiment that Israel — a nation with free elections and civil liberties — stands alone amid a sea of authoritarian regimes has been cultivated by Western institutions and Israeli leaders since the state was founded. From the outset, Israel’s earliest laws legalizing persecution against Palestinians showed the incompatibility between Jewish supremacy and democracy. But the subjugation of one group is rarely self-contained, and throughout history, illiberal nations have almost always turned against a portion of their citizens, often as violence against the outgroup reaches its peak. In the midst of the bloodiest three years in the country’s history, and particularly during the election campaign, this tendency began to play out. Israel has disenfranchised voters , at times even contradicting its own discriminatory laws, in efforts to marginalize Jews perceived as a threat to Benjamin Netanyahu and his increasingly violent political agenda. Barriers to the Ballot In the past three years, emigration from Israel has reached record heights. According to a study published by Tel Aviv University, 270,000 citizens have left the country in the past three years. Between 2013 and 2015, the corresponding period in the previous decade, the number sat at 183,000. Like much of the American Jewish diaspora , those who have left in recent years have become deeply critical of Benjamin Netanyahu. While Israel guarantees every citizen over the age of eighteen the right to vote, it has never permitted absentee ballots. With the exception of diplomats , emissaries, and Israel Defense Forces (IDF) soldiers occupying territory in Gaza, Lebanon, and Syria, voters must be in the country on Election Day. With this election largely seen as one of the most consequential in the nation’s history, a survey taken before the election estimated that up to 67,000 expats could be returning home to vote. On October 27, Likud won twenty-seven seats, and Gad Eisenkot’s Yashar party won twenty-one. Netanyahu’s bloc took fifty-three seats, against forty-eight for the Jewish opposition bloc and nineteen for the Arab-led parties. No bloc won the sixty-one seats needed for a majority. The results have left the country facing uncertain coalition negotiations. Leading up to the vote, Likud tried to stop Israelis outside the country from coming back. In September, Likud petitioned the Central Election Committee, demanding it block Fly & Vote, an initiative by the nonpartisan American-Israel Democracy (AID) Coalition, which sought to help Israelis abroad return and cast a vote. Offering logistical help, including eligibility checks and passport renewals, the organization also coordinated six group flights and helped search for commercial flights. While participants were still required to pay their airfare, Likud argued the initiative violated financing laws and constituted election bribery. While the petition was ultimately dismissed, the AID Coalition agreed to not subsidize tickets, organize any additional group flights, or provide transportation and accommodation. This summer, a second spat broke out when hundreds of Israelis living abroad discovered they had been stripped of their voting rights after being classified by the Interior Ministry as nonresidents. The designation, known as Code 51, excluded citizens from the voter registry altogether. Petitioning the Supreme Court, the expats argued that they were not notified of their status change, with several only realizing when checking their electoral eligibility. The courts dismissed the case on procedural grounds, stating the claim was filed too late. While there is no definitive proof linking Likud to the Code 51 scandal, it is clear that the party has repeatedly tried to curtail the expat vote. Israeli newspaper Haaretz reported that informal discussions were held between senior Transport Ministry officials about the possibility of restricting designated landing slots at airports, making it more difficult to fly into the country. Similar to the proposed judicial reforms of 2023, where Netanyahu sought to weaken the power of the Supreme Court, this democratic backsliding has prompted furor among the Israeli left. But as is often the case with liberal Zionists, the far greater repression affecting Palestinians remains largely ignored. Defending the Ethnocracy The AID Coalition describes itself as a group “strengthening, and preserving the democratic foundations of the State of Israel.” While a valiant objective, a more honest statement would be about preserving an ethnocracy. Israeli Professor Oren Yiftachel describes this form of governance as one that “promotes the expansion of the dominant group in contested territory and its domination of power structures while maintaining a democratic facade.” This dynamic has been present since the state’s founding. While the 150,000 Palestinians who remained in their homeland after the Nakba were declared citizens and granted the right to vote from the beginning, the community also faced systematic discrimination from the outset. From 1948 until 1966, Palestinian citizens of Israel were placed under military rule , which included measures that restricted their movement, prohibited political organizing, and imposed arbitrary curfews. Simultaneously, the government confiscated hundreds of thousands of dunams of land, uprooting farmers from their livelihood and forcing many to turn to low-wage labor to survive. In 1967, a year after lifting the military administration, Israel would turn their attention toward Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza after occupying both territories in the Six-Day War. Assuming control of around one million Palestinians, residents of the newly occupied territory were placed under military rule. This time citizenship and the right to vote were withheld from them, since such incorporation would threaten the Jewish demographic majority. As Jewish settlers moved into the occupied territories, aided by generous Israeli government subsidies, an apartheid regime took shape, as authorities applied separate laws, courts, and democratic rights to Palestinians and Israelis living in neighboring areas. Although Israel disengaged from Gaza in 2005, it lay siege to the Strip two years later, imposing a crushing blockade. After the October 7 attacks, it returned yet again as the occupying power, this time unleashing a genocide that has killed more than 74,000 Palestinians. In the West Bank, the apartheid regime continues, as roughly 500,000 settlers, excluding those in East Jerusalem, live with full rights outside of Israel’s internationally recognized border, beside Palestinians who cannot vote, travel freely, or even build on their ancestral land. Amid the dire situation in the West Bank and Gaza, the plight of Palestinians living inside Israel is largely forgotten. Yet these 2.1 million residents continue to live as second-class citizens. In 2018, the Israeli Knesset passed the quasi-constitutional Nation-State Law , stipulating that self-determination is unique to the Jewish people, without guaranteeing equality for its other citizens. Furthermore, Palestinian citizens of Israel continue to face entrenched discrimination in urban planning, economic development, and educational opportunities. The Authoritarian Turn In 2024, the Varieties of Democracy Institute downgraded Israel from a liberal to an electoral democracy. The demotion followed Netanyahu and his coalition’s attempt to overhaul the judiciary, their accusations the Supreme Court was “left-leaning,” and deterioration in protections against torture. This classification did not take into account the active atrocities that Israel was perpetrating in Gaza and the West Bank at the time. Yet since this reclassification, Netanyahu has shed more of the democratic facade he has long sought to uphold. More than five hundred humanitarian workers have been killed since the war in Gaza began. In December 2025, Israel attempted to bar thirty-seven nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) from operating in the West Bank and Gaza. The Supreme Court froze the ban, allowing the organizations to continue operating pending a final ruling. More recently, numerous foreign journalists have been turned away at the border during the election campaign, with others facing arduous interrogations and detention before being allowed entry. Perhaps most revealing is the government’s willingness to break long-standing supremacist laws, which uphold Jews as a privileged class in Israel. Starting in 2025, several Jewish activists were detained and deported for engaging in protective presence, a form of nonviolent solidarity work in which civilians deter violent settlers from harassing Palestinian communities. Earlier this year, a Jewish American academic on a three-year residency visa was denied entry after being accused of being a “left-wing anarchist.” After the backlash prompted by the film NAZA , in which IDF soldiers admitted to knowingly killing innocent civilians in Gaza, Netanyahu announced plans to introduce a bill that would strip those who defame the IDF of their Israeli citizenship. Last month, it was reported that two Jews were denied citizenship for having previously partaken in anti-occupation activism in the West Bank. This was the first known case of the state violating the Law of Return, which stipulates that every Jew is entitled to immigrate to Israel. Many saw Gad Eisenkot as the last chance to halt the country’s authoritarian drift. The election has left open the possibility that he could still lead the government, but he has no clear path to power. While he might reverse the emerging trend of discriminating against Jews and Israelis deemed insufficiently patriotic, none of his proposed policies address the core issues that make Israel an ethnostate. The election has not changed the reality for Palestinians, with or without Israeli citizenship, or the refusal of every major party to address these injustices. What Eisenkot may offer is merely a return to the illusion of democracy. Austerity Is Now the Central Conflict in Brazilian Politics

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: André Singer ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-06T17:09:42+00:00

On October 4, Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, the son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, finished the first round of Brazil’s presidential election nearly two points ahead of President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva. Three weeks later, Lula turned the result around and won a fourth term in the October 25 runoff. In between, left-led demonstrations filled the streets of hundreds of Brazilian cities. The government also banned online betting, backed a forty-hour workweek, and raised the benefit of Bolsa Família, the cash-transfer program for poor families that Lula created in 2003. Few analysts know Lula’s political project better than André Singer. A professor of political science at the University of São Paulo, Singer served as Lula’s spokesperson from 2003 to 2007 and press secretary from 2005 to 2007. He developed the concept of Lulism in Os sentidos do lulismo (2012) and O lulismo em crise (2018), and his new book, Impasse: O Brasil diante da onda reacionária , came out in August. After the runoff, Singer spoke with Catarina Príncipe and Aldo Cordeiro Sauda for Jacobin about Bolsonarism as autocracy with a fascist bias, the Brazilian bourgeoisie’s silence during the campaign, and why the second-round mobilization was not populism. He also discussed the fight over austerity that awaits Lula’s fourth term. Catarina Príncipe You’ve written about the idea of an “autocracy with a fascist bias” to understand the phenomena of Donald Trump in the United States and Jair Bolsonaro in Brazil. Could you explain this idea further? André Singer Honestly, I can’t speak directly about Trumpism, because I don’t have enough expertise. So I’ll talk about Bolsonarism, the experience from which I formulated the idea of an autocratic tendency with a fascist bias. Studying the Bolsonaro presidency of 2019–2022, one can show empirically that he tried to reshape Brazil’s institutions in the direction of an autocratic regime. One of the major conflicts during his term was over the independence of the Federal Police. For an American audience: The Federal Police is equivalent to the FBI. It is a national police force for the country’s major security problems, because everyday problems fall to the state governments. But the big questions of corruption, organized crime, drug trafficking, and arms trafficking are all the Federal Police’s business. That is why a convention that successive presidents have observed since the return to democracy in the 1980s is so important: guaranteeing the Federal Police’s independence. The president of the republic appoints the director, but presidents have made a practice of appointing directors who have a certain independence from themselves. Bolsonaro made a point of violating that convention and appointing a Federal Police leadership at the service of his own political interests. I’m describing this because it is the most evident case of all: he tried to manipulate the Federal Police to concentrate power. Why do I say this autocratic tendency has a fascist bias? Because I find in it certain elements in common with historical fascism. Why don’t I say it’s fascist tout court? Because there are many elements that don’t correspond. Let me give one element in common: the activation, through propaganda, of the unconscious of the masses. It is a kind of advertising that aims to hook the voter, the citizen, and the supporter through unconscious identification rather than rational argument. That doesn’t mean there aren’t also arguments of a material nature, material interests; I’m not saying it is merely a technique aimed at the unconscious. But I think this is very typical of fascism and not of the nonfascist right. What isn’t in common with fascism? For example, there is no political party organized into militias, as in Italian fascism and then German Nazism. There is also no warlike rhetoric, in the sense of external war: Bolsonarism has a very strange nationalism of submission to the United States — something hard to understand. But it has no rhetoric of confrontation with other countries or of territorial expansion, an important element of historical fascism. Since some elements are in common and many are not, I proposed a way of understanding the Bolsonaro phenomenon. We are facing an autocratic tendency — that is proven — with a fascist tendency that we still need to test. Meanwhile, there is a more general theoretical problem: the concept of fascism isn’t well defined. Strange as it may seem, that is the problem we have to face: arriving at a concept of fascism that lets us understand the present situation. Obviously we are interested in understanding history, but with a view to the present and the future. Catarina Príncipe In an interview, I heard you distinguish between fascism from below and fascism from above. Which class does this autocracy with a fascist bias mobilize most? André Singer Marxist-inspired thought has already made clear the characteristics of historical fascism, and one stands out: fascism is a movement that comes from below, not from above. Leon Trotsky first made that contribution, in his writings on Germany, and it still holds; it is a fundamental distinction. Bolsonarism has this characteristic: it comes from below; it is not a movement of the ruling class. It draws in the middle strata, but it quickly spreads to intermediate popular layers, which form its backbone. So in Brazil today, the far right penetrates from below. Catarina Príncipe How do you analyze the relationship between the ruling class and politics, given the existence of what you call two cross-class coalitions: the Bolsonarist “confederacy” and the Lulist “ concertación ”? What is the composition of these two coalitions? André Singer I call one the “confederacy,” by a certain analogy with the nineteenth-century American case, and the other the “concertación,” by analogy with Chile after Augusto Pinochet. I developed these two hypotheses to understand the 2022 election. So I’ll answer with 2022 in mind, then briefly go through the changes in 2026. The idea of the confederacy was that a coalition of agribusiness entrepreneurs and service sector entrepreneurs in the cities formed. Certain evangelical denominations, which represent a kind of Brazilian Bible Belt, joined them. These denominations account for about 30 percent of the Brazilian electorate, and Bolsonarism has repeatedly won 70 percent of their vote. In the 2022 election, the whole of the security forces joined these two components, the entrepreneurs and the evangelical Bible Belt, from the armed forces down to the regional (state) police forces. These forces represent a large contingent. Besides everyone formally engaged in security work, Brazil has an enormous army of private security guards, because public security can’t cope with the very serious crime problem. These three tracks of the confederacy, so to speak, created an enormous corridor through the interior of the country. It runs from the north of Rio Grande do Sul to the south of the North Region, through the interior of São Paulo and Minas Gerais, the country’s most industrialized states. That corridor aligned with Bolsonarism, which largely explains its significant electoral power. In addition, the evangelical Bible Belt gives Bolsonarism a very strong bridgehead in Brazil’s large metropolitan regions. In 2026, there was a change: because of the failed coup attempt, the armed forces pulled back. They had been very important because Bolsonaro, a former army captain, went through the military academy with the men who, by coincidence, made up the high command when he took office. He brought them into the government, and so the armed forces were deeply committed to the Bolsonaro government. That is no longer the case. You can’t identify anyone of note from the armed forces in the campaign of his son Flávio Bolsonaro. But we know that the police forces backed that candidacy discreetly, rather than explicitly, as they had with Jair Bolsonaro’s government. I call it a confederacy, although it has no connection with slavery. But it does have this connection: it divided Brazil. It united the interior, as if against the big cities, even though, I repeat, the evangelical denominations have influence in the big cities. You also notice a certain prejudice against the Northeast and an underlying ideology that a more modern Brazil would be better off rid of a more backward Brazil. It is a kind of implicit ideology, which sometimes shows up more, sometimes less. As for the concertación: the name came to me because, to build the coalition that sustained him, President Lula made an alliance with sectors of the old Brazilian Social Democracy Party (PSDB). Until 2018, the PSDB had been the main rival of Lula’s Workers’ Party (PT). The current vice president, Geraldo Alckmin, headed the sectors willing to join. The PSDB as a whole has shrunk to a small party, and it stayed neutral: it backed Lula neither in 2022 nor now. But the alliance with Alckmin represented a union with former PSDB sectors willing to form the democratic front. So, as in Chile, the two great adversaries came together: in Chile, the Socialist Party and the Christian Democratic Party; in Brazil, the PT and the PSDB. Along with this party coalition, there was an important class coalition. Lulism represents the base of the Brazilian social pyramid, as everyone knows. But the alliance with this faction of the PSDB also brought in what I call the cosmopolitan bourgeoisie, the most modern sector of the Brazilian bourgeoisie, which backed President Lula in the second round of 2022, not the first. That was very important both for his victory and for the future of the government. In this 2026 election, that sector of the bourgeoisie did not speak out; it stayed neutral and backed neither Flávio Bolsonaro nor President Lula. João Moreira Salles — founder of Piauí magazine, an important filmmaker, and an heir to one of Brazil’s biggest banking families — wrote a very powerful article on the importance of that silence. The consequence of that silence was the acceptance, practically until the last minute, of a Flávio Bolsonaro victory. An extraordinary movement reversed that in this year’s second round. To conclude for now: the cosmopolitan sector of the Brazilian bourgeoisie, which stayed silent, is no longer explicitly present in the concertación. Catarina Príncipe You define Latin American populism as a mass phenomenon: in certain circumstances, classes take the form of multitudes with little or no class organization, connecting people and leader directly. Was this type of populism what secured Lula’s victory? And how can we reconnect this expression of the multitude with more sustained class organization? André Singer I think the concept of populism doesn’t apply to the second-round victory. It’s a very interesting question, and it’s going to give me some work, because I’ll have to develop something more sophisticated to account for the phenomenon. But at first sight, I think we had a social explosion — let’s call it that — without the characteristics of populism. It was more organized and more conscious of its aims than the populist phenomenon implies. To be clear, my theoretical framework is Ernesto Laclau’s old definition, from 1978, not that of the so-called second Laclau. It is a good one for thinking about populism in Latin America and, of course, in Brazil too. Why do I say the explosion is more conscious and more organized? More conscious because it aimed, first, to stop the far right. Flávio Bolsonaro won [the first round] by 2 percentage points and immediately began putting together his government and saying he would change the Constitution. That was a tactical error, even from his own standpoint: it had such an impact that the democratic sectors of Brazilian society immediately began to speak out and organize. That is one side of the phenomena. The other is a feeling that President Lula needed a fourth term, because the government itself began to change during the campaign. It isn’t obvious, but President Lula changed the campaign in mid-September without saying so: he began to take measures. One event seems revealing to me. On September 11, at a rally in Porto Alegre, he said, “I know you are finding products expensive at the supermarket,” referring to coffee and beef. That is very significant, because until then the government had not addressed the population’s material needs directly. From then on, the president not only spoke to those needs but began taking measures. For example, he raised the Bolsa Família benefit, which the government had not adjusted in four years, and he banned online betting, the “bets.” Remember that Brazil is living through a triple crisis: a cost-of-living crisis, a crisis of household indebtedness, and, on top of it, an epidemic of bets. People began gambling wildly, partly to earn extra income to pay off debts. Since betting companies design bets so that the great majority lose, people ended up worse off. President Lula faced great resistance to the ban. First, many companies are involved, and long-term legal contracts were already in place; second, soccer clubs, which are very popular and important in Brazil, depended to some extent on sponsorship from those companies. The other very important decision was the switch from six workdays and one day off (6x1) to five and two (5x2), cutting the workweek from forty-four to forty hours: probably the greatest labor victory since the 1988 Constitution. Congress passed it, but everyone knows it was the president’s initiative and that he committed himself to it. Structurally, of course, it is even more important than the betting ban. But the ban has symbolic meaning, because the president decided on it during the campaign: an act of boldness and courage in defense of the popular economy. Alongside it, the government took two measures to restructure the debts of the popular classes, and others such as free public transit on weekends, which speaks directly to the young people who took to the streets. What I mean is that all this is not populist rhetoric; these are actions. It is what I call a change in the character of the government. Finally, these sectors that took to the streets are not completely disorganized. The demonstrations took place in hundreds of cities, but the largest, and most unprecedented, were in São Paulo, the country’s biggest metropolis. I have been comparing the two October demonstrations in São Paulo, on October 11 and 24, with the great right-wing demonstration for the impeachment of President Dilma Rousseff in March 2016. There had been no demonstration of this kind for at least ten years. The Left led these demonstrations, although they were not only of the Left. They formed a democratic front, with centrist sectors and possibly even a democratic right: individually, people may have gone out to defend democracy. But the leadership clearly belonged to the Left, and these are possibly the largest left-wing demonstrations in Brazil’s history. The Brazilian left has organizations: the PT, the Socialism and Liberty Party (PSOL), the Communist Party of Brazil (PCdoB), the student organizations, the cultural associations, the unions, the neighborhood associations, and the popular movements, especially the Landless Workers’ Movement (MST) and the Homeless Workers’ Movement (MTST). On the center left, you have the Socialist Party of Brazil (PSB) and the Labour Democratic Party (PDT). In short, Brazil has an organizational structure, and it mobilized. So, from these two angles, I wouldn’t describe this as a populist phenomenon; something different happened. Aldo Cordeiro Sauda This year, you observed a pendular movement by the cosmopolitan wing of the bourgeoisie toward the far-right candidacy, the opposite of its support for Lula’s election in 2022. Internationally, US foreign policy shows the same pendular movement: sympathy for Lula under Joe Biden in 2022, explicit support for Bolsonarism under Trump in 2026. To what extent does the Brazilian ruling class’s shift toward Bolsonarism express mere deference to Washington? André Singer As far as I understand, the sector I call cosmopolitan — in the American case, the big technology companies — backed Biden in 2020. In 2024, it wasn’t very clear to me what they were doing, but they didn’t seem to back Trump either, with the exception of Elon Musk. Yet when Trump took office, all of them were there. In the Brazilian case, though, it seems plausible that a conversion of the American cosmopolitan bourgeoisie to the far right influenced the Brazilian bourgeoisie. Until the last minute, there was a certain expectation that this sector might speak out in favor of President Lula. That sector committed itself to democracy in 2022, in a movement broader than support for President Lula. In August 2022, at a demonstration in São Paulo, those business leaders issued a letter. They said they would not accept, under any circumstances, a refusal to recognize the election result — exactly what the White House said. Those two positions were very important in keeping most of the armed forces from joining the coup, because the military knew it could not govern without that support. So you see: there is a Brazilian phenomenon. A link to a transformation in the United States is a possible hypothesis. Catarina Príncipe Our understanding of the question is that the Brazilian cosmopolitan bourgeoisie is staying neutral now because of the political change in the United States from Biden to Trump. At the time of the coup attempt, the Biden presidency was very important in stopping it from going forward. Now, with Trump, there might be some attempt at direct intervention in Brazilian politics. So, part of the cosmopolitan bourgeoisie doesn’t want to take a position today, to avoid a falling-out with US policy and with a possible Bolsonaro victory. It’s less a direct mirror between fractions of the bourgeoisie. André Singer Then I’ll take advantage of your interpretation to add something that makes the problem more complex. The major point of division between that sector of the Brazilian bourgeoisie and Lula’s third government is the fiscal deficit. That sector demands a big cut in public spending, which in its view would have to fall on pension benefits, and it wants to delink them from the minimum wage. The Brazilian Constitution links the two, so raising the minimum wage in real terms also raises all benefits. The other target is the constitutional floor for health and education: the Brazilian Constitution requires that a certain share of annual tax revenue go to health and education, and this cannot go down. The bourgeoisie’s aim is to remove that floor from the Constitution, so that this spending falls. So, we have a very serious issue: on one side the pension system, all the retirees; on the other, the two main social policies that form the social core of the 1988 Constitution, health and education. That is the main problem. We still don’t know whether there is also a certain caution or a desire to align with the American cosmopolitan bourgeoisie. Aldo Cordeiro Sauda National sovereignty took center stage in the campaign, perhaps for the first time since the return to democracy. What role did this issue play in the second-round turnaround against Flávio Bolsonaro? André Singer The Trump administration’s offensive against Brazil — the tariffs and its direct or indirect intervention in the elections — has put national sovereignty on the agenda. Trump justified the first tariffs by saying that Brazil was persecuting his preferred candidate, Jair Bolsonaro. Brazil today does not have a very strong nationalist current. A nationalism is now starting to grow, because the global situation has changed and the country faces political-economic aggression — let’s call it that. So nationalism has come onto the agenda, but in my opinion — and I may be wrong — it has still not become a central issue. Catarina Príncipe Lula wants to transform the Brazilian economy by investing in the green economy, infrastructure, and artificial intelligence (data centers) and by using Brazil’s resources in rare metals and minerals. How much room for maneuver does he have to do this, given the New Fiscal Framework, the Lula government’s 2023 fiscal rule capping annual spending growth at 70 percent of revenue growth? How should we think about his government’s relationship with the bourgeoisie in this fourth term and the new balance of forces? Do you believe the bourgeoisie will tend to deepen its conflict with the PT, intensifying the pendular movement? And in this context, what happens to the mass mobilization from below that we saw in the second round? André Singer I think this is the central point regarding the fourth term: what the outcome of this conflict will be. The stock market fell nearly 14 percent the day after the election. As I have been saying, the market has no vote, but it has money, and its message is clear: if you don’t cut public spending, we’re leaving. The question of fiscal adjustment is truly central, because the bourgeoisie is demanding harsh austerity. In my opinion, the demand for austerity is the fundamental issue in Brazilian politics today, but in general nobody puts it openly: it remains an implicit debate. That is partly because we are not managing to politicize the problem, to make the popular classes, the working class, aware that this is what is at stake. This is the main confrontation. This organizes Brazilian politics. In my recent book, Impasse : O Brasil diante da onda reacionária , I tried to show that this issue splits the democratic front in two. It is, as Americans say, an elephant in the room: everyone treats it as if it didn’t exist. I felt that my role was to bring it to the surface; obviously I can’t do that alone, but I can contribute to some extent. The conflict is immediate, because the transformation Brazil needs, which runs through reindustrialization, is a gigantic task. Brazil is an enormous country, and you don’t change a structure of this order without major investment. This transformation can’t rely on public investment alone; there must also be private investment. But we have to think with the instruments we have. Perry Anderson says Keynesianism no longer works, so we need to create new mechanisms; but until we have them, I’ll use the old ones. The state will have to induce this development process, although it also has to bring in other investors. There is, therefore, an incompatibility between the country’s needs and this radical cut in public spending, which goes in the opposite direction. This is the central point. Even if the terms of the debate were not clear, I think the large demonstrations in hundreds of Brazilian cities point toward the government having this commitment. We’ll have to follow events to see how it unfolds. Catarina Príncipe Your earlier book defined Lulism as “combating poverty without confronting capital.” Can Lulism go beyond itself, and does it want to? Is there room to do so today, given the current party arithmetic in Congress? And how can the Brazilian left organize to force it to take measures that go beyond welfarism? André Singer I’ll start with Congress, which I think is simpler. In the Congress that takes office in 2027, the far right has grown, not at the expense of the Left but of other forces on the Right. Equality Is About Who Rules

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Matt McManus ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-06T17:00:12+00:00

There is a complex debate about the extent to which socialists prioritize equality above the full flowering of each person’s individuality. But you’d have to be quite the contrarian to deny that socialists take equality very seriously; perhaps more seriously than any other major political ideology. Prominent socialist thinkers like G. A. Cohen and Thomas Piketty have dedicated much of their work to defending the philosophical and economic merits of egalitarianism. Socialist Martin Luther King’s “I Have a Dream” speech put equality at the core of his vision: “that one day this nation will rise up and live out the true meaning of its creed: ‘We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men are created equal.’” That said, the commitment of socialists and other egalitarians to equality can often seem frustratingly vague. It invites accusations of ill-defined “social justice” advocacy or even pursuing the much dreaded “equality of outcomes” through a Harrison Bergeron–like willingness to cut everyone down to size. Of course, many of these charges are lobbed in bad faith, and even the more refined versions ignore how other core principles — like liberty or order — are equally contestable and vague until explicitly defined. But having a firmer grasp on what we mean by equality and what we want would help the Left understand itself better. Equality of What? Few living thinkers have done more to answer this question than University of Michigan philosopher Elizabeth Anderson. A former doctoral student of John Rawls, Anderson made her name in the 1990s criticizing contemporary egalitarian philosophy and developing her own version of relational equality. More recently, Anderson has become a vigorous proponent of workplace democracy, contending that there is something deeply tyrannical about the forms of work that exist today. Her most recent book , Hijacked: How Neoliberalism Turned the Work Ethic Against Workers and How Workers Can Take It Back , argues for rehabilitating the tradition of social democratic and socialist political thought as a counter to the Right’s moralizing about work. From the beginning, Anderson was fiercely critical of many dimensions of contemporary capitalism and its inequalities. Her early book Value in Ethics and Economics (1993) is a careful critique of how market relations and their distinct ways of reasoning about ethical questions are increasingly colonizing many spheres of society. She insisted that realizing many forms of “freedom, autonomy and welfare demands that certain goods be produced, exchanged, and enjoyed outside of market relations or in accordance with non-market norms.” While in this book Anderson typically appeals to ideas of autonomy and welfare rather than strict equality, there is a clear egalitarian undercurrent. She criticizes libertarians’ “fetishism” of property and markets as the sole site of freedom, and how this leads them to be blind to the “social relations of domination that exist prior to market transactions and that condition the choices individuals make there.” For instance, a long history of patriarchal subordination means that women may feel compelled to enter into exploitative contracts, say, in the sex industry, to get by day to day. In her important paper “What Is the Point of Equality?” Anderson clarifies the role equality plays in inhibiting domination. It opens with a sardonic question: “If much recent academic work defending equality had been secretly penned by conservatives, could the results be any more embarrassing for egalitarians?” She chastises both Marxists like G. A. Cohen for trivializing equality by reducing it to debates about whether we ought to compensate people for being temperamentally gloomy or easily bored, and liberal egalitarians like Ronald Dworkin for proposing an envy-free distribution of resources — implying conservatives are right that the push for equality is ultimately about nothing more than resentment. Anderson wanted egalitarians to finally get serious and realize that we aren’t, in fact, all that interested in building a society where people get the same bundle of goodies. Inegalitarian societies have built hierarchies whose social relations enabled some people to dominate others, often by insisting that those atop the social hierarchy were intrinsically worth more than those at the bottom. Egalitarians, Anderson argued, are fundamentally committed to undoing those forms of social hierarchy that enabled domination and oppression: Egalitarian political movements oppose such hierarchies. They assert the equal moral worth of persons. This assertion does not mean that all have equal virtue or talent. Negatively, the claim repudiates distinctions of moral worth based on birth or social identity — on family membership, inherited social status, race, ethnicity, gender, or genes. There are no natural slaves, plebeians, or aristocrats. Positively, the claim asserts that all competent adults are equally moral agents: everyone equally has the power to develop and exercise moral responsibility, to cooperate with others according to principles of justice, to shape and fulfill a conception of their good. In The Imperative of Integration , Anderson further defined the parameters of what she called the “relational theory” of equality and inequality. It located the “causes of economic, political, and symbolic group inequalities in the relations (processes of interaction) between groups, rather than in the internal characteristics of their members or in cultural differences that exist independently of group interaction.” Anderson emphasizes that in our society what makes people unequal often isn’t intrinsic to them but the result of the social relations in which they are embedded. But to invoke Karl Marx’s language of fetishism, this is very often obscured by ruling ideologies, which redirect the source of inferiority from social relations onto the individuals and groups themselves. Someone sold into slavery is described, in Aristotle’s terms, as a “natural slave” rather than someone who has been trapped in an oppressively hierarchical social system. Moreover, to reinforce hierarchy we often impute still worse forms of inferiority upon those at the base of the pyramid. For instance, when Senator John C. Calhoun called slavery a “positive good” because slaves were racially inferior, and the best thing for everyone was their paternalistic subordination to the superior race. There is a lot to commend in Anderson’s relational equality. It helped steer egalitarians away from arguing about whether people with highfalutin tastes should be supplied with plovers’ eggs and truffle oil and back toward the hierarchies of oppression that it is the Left’s job to confront. If there is a limitation to her approach, it’s an enduring ambiguity about what a flourishing life ought to look like. This is a necessary part of making the case for equality; namely, why and how would people live better lives if they were more equal through being free of domination. Here Marx provides us with a helpful clue in the third volume of Capital , where he posits that under socialism the cooperative development of human powers and capabilities will become an end in itself for the first time, rather than just a means of training more efficient workers. The development of human powers to foster a rich individuality in community with others ought to be on our horizon, and doing so will mean transcending capitalism. The Tyranny of Private Government and the Conservative Work Ethic In her more recent work, Anderson has become one of the sharpest critics of both capitalist practices and ideology. In Private Government , she asks us to imagine “a government that assigns almost everyone a superior whom they must obey.” This government offers routines but no rule of law: orders can be arbitrary, can change without notice, and can’t be appealed. Worst of all, the bosses answer to no one below them. “They are neither elected nor removable by their inferiors.” Most of us, Anderson point out, don’t have to imagine this. It’s what most of us encounter every day when we go to work. And yet, despite the oppression it entails, we are often told that not only is there no alternative, but that such a system is nothing less than freedom itself. Anderson’s point is well taken. If we were told that tomorrow the federal government was going to tell most Americans when to wake up, what they were going to wear, when they could go to the bathroom and eat lunch, how and when they were going to talk to others, there would be riots in the street. Everyone with a “Don’t Tread on Me!” flag would be making liberal use of their Second Amendment rights, Rand Paul would be weeping on live television, and the Daily Wire would proclaim this the Communist States of America. And yet Anderson observes this is the reality for millions of Americans subject to “private government” every day. She rejects the claim that that the modern commercial firm is a realm of individualism, as opposed to the collectivism of the state. Anderson notes that in the “great contest between individualism and collectivism regarding the mode of production, collectivism won, decisively. Nearly all production is undertaken by teams of workers using large, indivisible forms of capital equipment held in common.” Firms are collective enterprises, where the social relations grant capitalists and managers enormous domineering powers at the expense of their workers. She recommends that the way to respond to these forms of domination is to extend the liberal and democratic norms we already apply to statist collectives to private government: allow workers more democratic rights, greater protections, and the right to assemble and check unaccountable private power. In Hjjacked , Anderson examines some of the ideological reasons many on the Right consider this unacceptable. She points out how from the beginning of the modern era there have been fierce debates over the work ethic and who deserved greater power and rewards: workers or capitalists. Anderson shows how socialists and a surprising number of liberals argued a “progressive work ethic” holding that, since workers did most of the work under capitalism, they were getting a raw deal. But they were always confronted by capitalists advocating a “conservative work ethic” that “stressed discipline, frugality, and asceticism for workers, while reaping the lion’s share of productivity gains for themselves in their capacity as capital owners.” The spirit of this conservative ethic was well captured in Austrian economist Ludwig von Mises’s letter to Ayn Rand, congratulating her on the publication of Atlas Shrugged : You have the courage to tell the masses what no politician told them: you are inferior and all the improvements in your conditions which you simply take for granted you owe to the effort of men who are better than you. If this be arrogance, as some of your critics observed, it is still the truth that had to be said in the age of the Welfare State. Anderson thinks that there are several ways we can confront this ideology. One is by reconstructing and re-canonizing the work of socialist and social democratic thinkers who tried to conceive of better ways to organize the economy. Anderson quips that it is “telling that in the history of political thought, no social democrat has been canonized, despite the huge influence of social democracy in many wealthy capitalist countries.” This is something of an exaggeration outside the United States, where left-wing economic thought has gained more traction, but her point still stands. A major goal of left-wing philosophers ought to be reintroducing large numbers of people to the rich and thoughtful tradition of socialist and social democratic thought, to help inspire thinking about where to go in the future. Anderson is esteemed in academic circles but deserves a much wider popular audience. The times are certainly ripe for her message: in his second administration, Donald Trump has gone on a warpath against workers’ rights and needs, making it harder for public sector employees to engage in collective bargaining. Recently, he even went as far as to adopt an explicit “Let them eat cake” attitude toward rising costs to pay for his siege of Iran: “We’re fighting wars. We can’t take care of daycare.” Anderson offers us ways to both criticize these forms of domination and think through how to envision a better future where workers take back the world they built. But above all else, Anderson reminds us that equality is about more than who gets what. Fundamentally, it’s a question of who rules and who is ruled. For most of us, for most of our waking hours, the answer is that the boss rules and we are ruled. Anderson’s work is an invitation to change that answer. The bees in the city: organizing China’s delivery workers in Ganzhou

フォルダ: 06_左派ニュース・社会主義戦略 ／ 媒体: BT News ／ 著者: Tings Chak ／ 公開日時: 2026-11-06T22:21:15+00:00

At the entrance to Xingfuli, a residential compound in Ganzhou, Jiangxi province, a bulletin board displays the names, photographs, and phone numbers of the people responsible for managing the neighborhood. Though an ordinary bulletin board at first appearance, among the building representatives and Communist Party members is an unexpected name: Kang Jiafei, a food delivery worker who is also listed as a community “grid worker.” China has more than ten million food delivery workers, part of a rapidly growing global workforce whose lives are organized by algorithms, tight deadlines, and orders that take them across the city. They are among the most visible workers on China’s streets, yet often have no fixed workplace, no place to rest between orders, and limited access to conventional forms of employment protection. Their working hours are fragmented, their movements dictated by the next delivery. What, then, does it mean for one of these workers to have a recognized role in governing a residential community? In China, urban neighborhoods are divided into small geographical units known as grids (网格), with designated workers who monitor local conditions, respond to residents’ concerns, and coordinate with public departments. Kang is one of fifty delivery workers in Ganzhou’s Zhanggong District who have taken on this additional role. During my visit in October, Wuzhifeng Road Community Party secretary Yin Guozhu, responsible for this community, explained how it works. Delivery workers like Kang know the neighborhood through their daily deliveries. When he notices a blocked fire exit, debris near a lift, or another hazard, he reports it to the property management staff. Between orders, he also distributes the community’s anti-fraud leaflets. During recent typhoon weeks, riders who encountered fallen branches blocking drains cleared what they could and reported larger obstructions to the community, which contacted the responsible departments. “Our information is collected up layer by layer,” Yin explained. She calls the riders “mobile probes”: in places community workers cannot see or reach in time, they provide first-hand information. The same mobility that makes these workers difficult to organize also gives them intimate knowledge of the city’s streets and neighborhoods – knowledge that can now serve the wider community. In 2020, the widely read investigation Trapped in the System 《困在系统里》documented the tightening grip of delivery algorithms on these workers in China: a three-kilometer delivery window that fell from an hour in 2016 to only thirty-eight minutes by 2018. Riders described themselves as “racing with death, wrestling with the traffic police, befriending red lights.” The sociologist Sun Ping , after seven years of fieldwork, described the condition as “transitional labor” – work suspended between employment and informality, in which the transition becomes permanent and the delivery economy runs like a great “furnace”, constantly burning as workers rotate through it. Since 2021, national measures have sought to clarify platform companies’ responsibilities and extend labor protections in these “new forms of employment,” notably in the gig economy. An occupational injury insurance program , piloted from 2022 and expanded nationwide in 2026, places its premium obligations on platforms. Days before my visit, a draft regulation on protecting workers in new employment forms opened for public comment. Alongside these regulatory efforts, the Party has been extending its organizational work among workers dispersed across the platform economy. A workforce without a fixed workplace lacks many of the spaces where workers’ welfare and social life have traditionally been anchored. One of the most immediate problems has a simpler name: the riders’ “four difficulties” – eating, drinking water, resting, and finding a toilet, so the infrastructure has moved to the street. The All-China Federation of Trade Unions has helped establish more than 186,000 outdoor worker rest stations nationwide, aiming to ensure that outdoor workers can find a place to rest, drink water, or charge their phones within a fifteen-minute journey. In Zhanggong District, thirty-three Qianxin stations can be found through phone map apps. They take their name from Qian (虔), an old name for Ganzhou, and xin (新), referring to the new employment groups. President Xi Jinping once called delivery workers “hardworking little bees”; here, the community built them resting stations, including the one near the MixC shopping center, shaped like a beehive. When the district launched its Qianxin program, a rider named Zhu Qiang said: “It gives us a home in the city.” Workers waiting between orders had nowhere to shelter from wind and rain. Shopkeepers, worried that soaked riders might disturb their business, were often reluctant to let them inside. The community decided to build a place along the routes the workers already traveled. “With the station, it is as if we built them a warm harbor right where they must pass,” Secretary Yin said of the station built in the Xingfuli residential block. Inside are air conditioning, drinking water, a microwave, charging facilities, first-aid supplies, and a library stocked with books. Around it, the community has organized an alliance of businesses offering services and discounts. A rider can eat his fill at a participating canteen for fifteen yuan, compared with twenty or thirty for other customers. Some education providers offer half-price courses for workers’ children. Lawyers and family-education counselors hold weekly consultations, while online links connect workers to departments responsible for insurance, schooling, and employment. At the compound entrance, a rider-friendly map marks building entrances and nearby places where riders can rest. A few minutes lost searching for an apartment can mean a late order. “For them, time is money,” Yin explained. The community began by making itself easier for the workers to navigate. Kang Jiafei’s name on the grid board represents another step: the recognition that delivery workers are also contributors to collective life of the city beyond the services they perform for customers. For Yin, this is a reciprocal process. “Everything is two-way,” she explained. By making the community more welcoming to riders, she believes their sense of social responsibility has gradually grown. She described how the relationship developed over three years. Initially, riders found it difficult to join community activities, their working hours too fragmented and unpredictable. Today, when the community announces free medical examinations in their WeChat group, workers invite one another and arrive together. Workers also contact Yin directly about developments around the shopping district. Yin says she has gained not only partners in community work, but also “brothers and sisters.” This is not a charity story. A bench, a socket, a microwave, and a bookshelf are material provisions for workers whose lives are organized around delivering the next order. But they also create spaces where otherwise dispersed workers can meet, develop social relationships, and participate in collective activities. The station becomes more than a place of rest: it becomes an organizational center. The methodology is Party-building adapted to a workforce that the factory no longer houses. The trade unions, Women’s Federation, and Communist Youth League are part of this wider effort to extend organization into new employment groups. Where conditions for establishing Party branches do not yet exist, other mass organizations can provide an institutional foundation. Party members among the delivery workers, like Kang, retain their primary organizational affiliations while participating in activities near their workplaces. The station holds “mini-Party lessons,” between orders – adapting political education to workers whose “time is fragmented and space is fluid,” as Yin put it. The challenge is not only to extend social protections, but also to overcome the atomization produced by platform labor. The algorithms organize riders as individual units of labor, each racing against the clock. The community attempts to bring them into social relationships and institutions that extend beyond the commercial transaction. The contradictions have not disappeared. A subsidized meal does not determine how much a rider earns per delivery, and a rest station cannot replace enforceable labor rights. Those questions remain central to China’s evolving regulation of still-new platform employment. But the community addresses another dimension of the problem: how workers who spend their days moving through a city can become recognized participants in its collective life. The community where Kang Jiafei runs his route is called Xingfuli – “Happiness Lane.” Ninety-two years after the Red Army set off on the historic Long March from this very region, the long work of socialist construction continues in different forms: investigating new conditions, building new institutions, and organizing a changing working class, one neighborhood and one hive at a time.
