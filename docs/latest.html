Jonathan Demme’s Wild, Humanist Cinema Deserves a Reappraisal

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Eileen Jones ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-06T20:41:40+00:00

There’s a Criterion Channel series paying tribute to director Jonathan Demme that, as short as it is, makes clear that Demme is due for a major reappraisal. It includes only four films — Something Wild (1986), Married to the Mob (1988), The Silence of the Lambs (1991), and Rachel Getting Married (2008) — but each demonstrate both his confidence in mixing genre elements freely as well as his commitment to centering his films on engrossing characters and the actors who play them. This means his early movies especially seem loose and rangy but also kinetic and exciting. Much like his peers Peter Bogdanovich, Francis Ford Coppola, and Martin Scorsese, Demme started his career shooting exploitation movies like Caged Heat (1974) and Crazy Mama (1975) for producer Roger Corman. He found an early champion in critic Pauline Kael, who began the trend of defining Demme as a “humanist” director in the mold of Jean Renoir ( The Rules of the Game ), one who tended to portray a wide range of characters of various classes, races, and backgrounds without condescension, appreciating their struggles and idiosyncrasies. Demme, who died in 2017, soon broke out of the exploitation underground and had a long career with a major influence on contemporary filmmakers. Paul Thomas Anderson was once asked during a Criterion Channel interview what filmmakers had influenced him the most, and he named as his model and hero “Jonathan Demme, Jonathan Demme, and Jonathan Demme.” Even while omitting a few of Demme’s greatest works, such as Melvin and Howard (1980) and the Talking Heads concert film Stop Making Sense (1984), Criterion’s series attests to boldly creative qualities Demme had that seem to be in short supply in recent Hollywood cinema. Demme’s Big Prestige Detour It can be argued that The Silence of the Lambs , Demme’s biggest hit and the film he’s best known for, plus his other Oscar winner Philadelphia (1993), overshadow his livelier, more inventive work. More than one critic has argued that Demme lost his way after this period, with one offering a startling explanation: that Demme the proud progressive was so distressed by LGBTQ-advocacy groups’ protests over Silence of the Lamb ’s cross-dressing serial killer Jame Gumb that he spent years anxiously trying to shore up his progressive bona fides. His attempts to “atone” included Philadelphia , featuring Tom Hanks as an attorney with AIDS. A “passionate liberal Hollywood drama” seemingly designed to win awards, Philadelphia is a well-made film that “never quite transcended the message movie category.” Demme also made a “stilted and overly ‘poetic’” adaptation of Toni Morrison’s Beloved (1998), which bombed even with Oprah Winfrey in the lead. Both films are more “classic” prestige studio projects, reining in Demme’s freewheeling tendencies. Though even when filming in a chillier, more controlled style, Demme and longtime cinematographer Tak Fujimoto created memorable imagery, such as an inventive variation on Demme’s “ signature shot ” in which characters speak directly into the camera: The subjective close-up, where a character addresses the other person in a scene while staring straight into the lens [is] a rarely deployed piece of film grammar, thanks to its disruptive, fourth wall–smashing qualities. But Demme employs the technique to quite the opposite effect: he uses it to create intimacy. This shot was used most powerfully in The Silence of the Lambs , where it transforms the encounters between Jodie Foster’s Clarice Starling and Anthony Hopkins’s Hannibal Lecter: They share barely ten minutes together on screen, but so intense are their conversations, and so close is the camera to the actors’ faces, that it feels half the movie is just them talking. Demme’s Pre- Silence Genius To get a bead on Demme’s more adventurous filmmaking pre– Silence of the Lambs , it helps to go back to one of his earlier breakout films, such as Something Wild . An astutely reconceived screwball comedy with a bopping rhythm keeping time with the infectious and liberatory reggae score, Something Wild is about the transformative impact on a repressed corporate cog named Charlie Driggs (Jeff Daniels) when he meets a free spirit with a dramatic Louise Brooks bob who calls herself Lulu (Melanie Griffith). Almost immediately, he gets pulled along in her sexy, lawless, adventuresome wake. This includes constant shifts in identity and appearance typical of screwball comedy, a genre in which spontaneous role-playing is both a way to revive people who’ve been leading deadened lives and a way to zigzag through the fast-paced, obstacle-filled, deeply insane modern world. Lulu has the endearing zaniness and spontaneity of a “madcap heiress” type such as Carole Lombard in My Man Godfrey or Katharine Hepburn in Bringing Up Baby , but Demme brings her down to earth by giving her working-class roots, a realistically troubled past when she was actually named Audrey, and a dangerous ex-con ex-husband named Ray Sinclair who’s obsessed with her (Ray Liotta). This was Liotta’s first major role and practically steals the film with his electrifying turn as Ray, a menace with a sharklike smile who once shared an edgy outlaw life with then wife Lulu/Audrey. Meeting Ray at Lulu/Audrey’s high school reunion, Charlie picks exactly the wrong time to commit himself to wild Lulu-like ad-libbing, spinning fantastical tales about his new romance that Ray knows are lies. The tension ratchets up to excruciating levels as Ray circles the oblivious, nattering Charlie, radiating lethal vibes, and the screwball-comedy-turned-road-movie shifts into scary thriller terrain. Demme’s political consciousness was reflected in his view of what Something Wild was really about after he read the refreshing E. Max Frye script: It kept surprising you at every twist and turn, and there was this theme of the flipside of putting on your neat suits and committing a certain kind of financial violence as a successful yuppie in a corporation. The dark side of that is a guy like Ray Liotta, who resorts to a more fundamental kind of violence to solve his problems and to get ahead. I liked this collision and I liked what the script said about violence. Something Wild helped to salvage Demme’s career after the disastrous production of Swing Shift (1984) threatened to bring its momentum to a complete halt. With Swing Shift , Demme went up against entrenched star power in the form of Goldie Hawn — still hot off her own daffy hit Private Benjamin (1980) — and lost. According to most accounts, Hawn — who was also producing the film, which costarred her longtime life partner Kurt Russell — watched Demme’s initial cut of the film and hated it. So did the studio executives at Warner Brothers. The film was completely recut and some reshoots were ordered to put the focus more squarely on the narrative arc of Hawn’s character, Kay Walsh. Ironically, this weakened what had been closer to an ensemble film, with several major characters — played by a marvelous cast including Christine Lahti, Holly Hunter, and Fred Ward — caught up in a familiar historical development of World War II: women entering factory jobs to replace the men who had gone off to fight and discovering, through the highest-paying work they had ever done, the joys and challenges of financial and personal independence. When the war ended, those same women were pushed out of their jobs as part of a national campaign insisting that their patriotic duty was to return to the domestic sphere and make way for the returning GIs. The studio cut also sanitized the importance of Kay’s affair with a trumpeter in a local band (Kurt Russell) and its impact on her marriage to a GI (Ed Harris). The new emphasis was on Kay’s remorse and her return to her husband, rather than on how much she has grown as a person — and how that growth suggests she has outgrown him. Hawn insisted that the changes she and the studio wanted were simply meant to clarify the main storyline, but it’s hard not to see a second motive: protecting her star image and her “likability” with audiences who might view Kay’s affair as simply immoral. This drastically reedited version bombed with both critics and the public. Demme’s cut, which is still not available through any official channels, has been declared a masterpiece by the few who’ve seen it. Accounts of its intricately interwoven plot strands, with several characters influencing and shaping one another’s stories, fit with Demme’s broader tendencies as a filmmaker. He had a particular gift for bringing colorful characters to the foreground, whether in small but brilliantly cast pivotal roles or even brief bits, until audiences felt they could follow almost any of them into a completely different but equally engrossing film. Demme’s Improvisational Genius You can see this kind of multicharacter narrative edited in 2008’s Rachel Getting Married , when Demme turbocharged his earlier filmmaking tendencies by borrowing some of the methods and raw aesthetics of the Danish avant-garde movement known as Dogme 95 . Shooting in sequence without a preplanned shot list, Demme filmed a multiday family gathering with such a commitment to realism, Rachel Getting Married was described as a “beautiful home movie” that just so happened to be an actual movie starring Anne Hathaway. Of course, the very first Dogme 95 film, The Celebration (1998), directed by Thomas Vinterberg, felt more like a very unbeautiful home movie. Vinterberg’s film is about a traumatic family gathering to celebrate the sixtieth birthday of the rich patriarch who gets accused by his son of sexually abusing his own children. The plot of Rachel Getting Married also swirls around family trauma, involving the Buchmans, an affluent clan headed by a music industry mogul and devoted family man played by Bill Irwin, the Cyclops from Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey . The whole clan is triggered by the arrival of one erratic, resentful, guilt-ridden, drug-addicted family member — ex-model Kym (Anne Hathaway). She’s let out of rehab just long enough to attend the wedding of her sister Rachel (Rosemarie DeWitt), who’s the beloved family favorite. Tunde Adebimpe, Anna Hathaway, and Rosemarie DeWitt star in Rachel Getting Married . (Clinica Estetico) After the shoot, the actors all marveled at how much making Rachel Getting Married felt like attending an actual wedding, wearing mics and staying in character throughout, laughing, chatting, drinking, dancing to the live music of various musicians drawn from Demme’s vast music world connections like Robyn Hitchcock from the Soft Boys and TV on the Radio’s Tunde Adebimpe, who plays Sidney Williams, the groom. Meanwhile Demme’s multiple cameras prowled around among them all getting long takes of their often totally unscripted and unrehearsed interactions. This kind of improvisation was definitely central to the project — even the real-life musicians were improvising the score on the spot. They played so incessantly that Anne Hathaway complained about the noise during an intense scene of family confrontation. In the spirit of the project, Demme told her to have her character Kym do something about it. Hathaway then ad-libbed a snappish line to the actor playing her father: “Can you tell them to knock it off?” This kind of risk-taking by a major, established director is a rare thing, especially today, and Demme’s approach generally produced great performances, sometimes career bests. Hathaway, for instance, has never again created so raw, intense, and nerve-wracking a character as Kym. Every one knows how Anthony Hopkins reached a late-career peak of stardom with his indelible Hannibal Lecter, but just look at the work Jason Robards did under Demme’s direction, with his portrayal of a wild-haired and deeply strange but oddly likable Howard Hughes in Melvin and Howard , perhaps his very best performance. And then later, in just a few scenes, as the elderly senior partner in a top law firm in Philadelphia where Robards is terrifically powerful and concentrated, conveying the phony bonhomie and sick-souled malice underneath it all. The Women of Demme’s Cinema Demme had a special talent for working with women, and he repeatedly brought out the best in such actors as Melanie Griffith, Jodie Foster, and Michelle Pfeiffer. He acknowledged that one of his primary drives as a director was an unusual one — making women-centered films: Ever since my days of working with Roger Corman, and perhaps before that, I’ve been a sucker for a woman’s picture. A film with a woman protagonist at the forefront. A woman in jeopardy. A woman on a mission. These are themes that have tremendous appeal to me as a moviegoer and also as a director. I still remember seeing Pfeiffer playing the lead in Demme’s zany gangster comedy Married to the Mob , and — being no fan of Pfeiffer at that point — feeling startled at how charming and funny she was. Playing Angela De Marco, she’s the only big-haired mob wife of the whole Long Island bunch who’s miserable about her seemingly luxurious life because “everything we own fell off the back of a truck” and it’s all, of course, tainted with blood. She’s married to Frankie “the Cucumber” De Marco (Alec Baldwin), the handsomest of the goons working for the local don, Tony “the Tiger” Russo (Dean Stockwell). A perpetual horndog whose only real fear is his jealous wife, Connie (Mercedes Ruehl), Tony murders Frankie when he catches him with one of his girlfriends. Immediately after that, at the funeral, he’s making aggressive passes at newly widowed Angela, who rebuffs him and tries to strike out on her own, free of the mob. At one point, she tells her neighbor Mike Downey (Matthew Modine), who’s actually an undercover FBI agent trying to bring Tony to justice, about her travails, especially being relentlessly pursued by Tony. She says, “I guess he’s been trying to screw me ever since,” and at the word “screw” Pfeiffer crossed her eyes, an endearing way of conveying Angela’s embarrassment, disgust, and incredulity at the madness of her own situation. Michelle Pfeiffer stars in Married to the Mob . (Orion Pictures) “Now I see why she’s a star,” I thought then. And that wasn’t the only time. Melanie Griffith’s star qualities — the matter-of-fact sense of vast sexual experience, the strange, raspy little voice — were perfectly showcased in Something Wild and rarely if ever presented as effectively again. There’s one moment demonstrating Demme’s devotion to actors and performance that sums up all the rest. At the end of Married to the Mob , he includes a series of shots of actors performing in character that, for whatever reason, didn’t make the final cut of the film. But Demme clearly wanted these moments to be seen and appreciated anyway. My favorite one shows Dean Stockwell, as ever-stylish, impeccably dressed, and egocentric mob boss Tony Russo, in the simple act of getting out of a car. Stockwell, always an inspired actor, gets out, pauses, and does a little flourish with his hand, as if Tony is presenting himself to the world with a visual “ta-da!” It encapsulates a quality of Tony’s that’s always been there, except when he’s being terrorized by his formidable wife, Connie: he’s always playacting an exaggerated version of the Mafia don. He’s a little overdressed for the part, forever adding some extra flourish of menace or unctuous elegance to his behavior, the perpetual star of his own private movie playing in his head. Demme’s films are full of these moments that crystallize characters, making them startlingly alive. And because, at his best, he let his characters lead the way through offbeat, unpredictable, adventurous plots, his movies remain fresh and vivid in ways that some of our more ponderous filmmakers could learn a great deal from.

Hollywood Is Losing Not Just Jobs but Entire Lineages

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Clara Aranovich ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-06T17:44:46+00:00

The flight of film and television production out of the United States is usually framed in terms of jobs and dollars. And no wonder: entire careers are evaporating at a nauseating rate, with US film and television production down 40 percent in 2024 from its 2022 peak. What gets less attention is a quieter loss: the erosion of an intergenerational workforce.In 2021, I directed an episode of The CW’s Nancy Drew. Like many productions of its scale, it shot in Vancouver. It was an ambitious show — narratively dense with five lead characters, technically demanding with supernatural elements, all within the constraints of a modest broadcast budget. It was also a blast to make. As with most episodic directing jobs, I parachuted in as a short-term transplant, folding into the well-established ecosystem of a multiseason show.While on Nancy Drew, I met Hannah, a script supervisor who stood all of four feet eleven with a voice to match. We spent long days side by side at the monitors, as directors and script supervisors do. Close in age and aligned on which jokes were absolutely hilarious, we became fast friends.“You’re Jim’s girl, aren’t you?” someone asked her one day, referring to her father, a longtime camera operator and cinematographer. This led to stories of being brought to set as a child and coming of age under the watchful guard of a workforce who “knew her when.”I’ve since learned this kind of intergenerational collaboration is not uncommon in Canada, and working in Toronto and Vancouver, I’ve been repeatedly struck by it — along with a pang of envy and grief for our ailing Hollywood. What emerges is an on-set experience buoyed by a network of shared history and continuity passed from older workers to younger ones, not uncommonly in the same family.Before you write this off as garden-variety nepotism, I’d argue it’s actually trade succession, similar to what you see in the building trades. These are not the film industry’s rarefied or glamorous above-the-line roles. They are the physically demanding, highly specialized trades that form the backbone of film and TV production, and in that context the term “nepotism” loses its stink.The benefits of working with multiple generations of craftspeople are impossible to quantify. The combined knowledge, dedication, and identification with craft and community are greater than the sum of their parts.Trades have long functioned this way, at least since the guild era of the Middle Ages. If you grew up around a craft, you absorbed it by default — awakening in a cloud of sawdust or to the sound of a hammer on an anvil, you were a de facto apprentice. But beyond the nuts and bolts of the trade, the younger generation gets to observe the ineffable soft skills of the work and the countenance required for it.It’s for precisely this reason that the Writers Guild of America fought in 2023 to get writers back on set as producers of their own episodes. The new contract restored a version of that expectation — central to how the next generation of writers learns to be showrunners — but only for some shows, after it had fallen away almost entirely during COVID-19.When Nancy Drew wrapped, Hannah and I stayed loosely in touch. Over the next few years, I worked on a handful of shows in different cities — Santa Fe, Atlanta, even Los Angeles — most of which didn’t make it past their first season as the industry contracted. But when I returned to Vancouver for work, I ran into something I hadn’t experienced in a long time: a lot of the same crew members.Among them was Hannah, now with her married name, directing her own shorts between script supervising gigs. I was reunited with an A-camera dolly grip who’d had a baby, and a producer whose son had started in the locations department. It was frankly utopian. “Now this is an industry town,” I thought.But if Vancouver is a true industry town, what does that make Los Angeles? Increasingly, film is becoming an industry in which the decision-makers are headquartered at a distance from production itself — and in that gap, to paraphrase Antonio Gramsci, morbid symptoms appear.When I recently wrote Hannah to get her blessing to tell her story, she replied, “Literally standing next to another second-gen camera op right now. Didn’t know that wasn’t a thing in LA.” Indeed, it is increasingly uncommon in this age of footloose capital, which uproots industries from the communities that built them and sets them down wherever labor comes cheapest, often thanks to tax breaks and government subsidies.A stable industry that treats its workers well does more than reliably turn out art the public loves. It can be a scaffold on which entire generations grow and flourish. It allows workers not merely to make a living but to actually live — to hit life’s milestones with the work as a steady backdrop, marrying and buying homes and raising the next generation with the confidence that the work will still be there for them. It allows older workers to stick around and pass knowledge down as an uncle might on a Saturday afternoon in his garage wood shop.This is the future I dream of for our industry.

Unions Can Save Tech Workers and Tech Work

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Dan Russell ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-06T16:46:06+00:00

A dramatic shift has taken place in the tech industry over the last several years. Industry leaders who once had a reputation as innovative forward thinkers are more familiar as misanthropic paranoiacs. The playful atmosphere of tech offices flush with investment, enjoying luxe catered lunches to attract programming talent, has been replaced by a looming fear that layoffs are just around the corner as artificial intelligence (AI) company executives make repeated public statements that any day now, everyone will be replaced by superintelligent chatbots. The once-sage advice to “learn to code” isn’t heard much these days. One could hardly be blamed for feeling pessimistic about the tech industry. But last May, 2,100 information technology employees at the University of California (UC) voted to join the union of University Professional and Technical Employees (UPTE-CWA Local 9119). Among the reasons for joining, members have pointed to the layoff protections the union won in its last contract, as well as the positive social vision of technology for which organized workers can fight. This was the largest effort to organize technology workers in the history of the US labor movement. While the tech industry might still be new ground for unions, the methods it took to win were familiar as ever. Jacobin contributor and tech worker Aaron Petcoff spoke with Local 9119 President Dan Russell and University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) Unit Cochair Max Belasco about the campaign, what it took to win, and how workers can organize to build technology that serves people rather than the bosses. Aaron Petcoff To begin, could you each tell me about what you do for work and your role relative to the union? Dan Russell I’m in IT, so I do desktop support and server administration. I got hired at UC in 2012. I wasn’t represented at the time, and I’d been hoping there would be a union for IT workers at UC; it was one of the reasons I was excited about the job. Then I saw a flyer for UPTE, which was trying to organize IT workers into a new bargaining unit. I came to find out that was not going very well. So I got involved. My role was added to the unit around 2016, and at that point I got on the executive board and on the bargaining team for the contract fight that started in 2016. I was part of the officer team of a reform slate that got elected in 2018. We ran another leadership slate in 2022. Max Belasco I’ve been working at UCLA for fourteen years, since right after I graduated from UCLA. I’m a tech worker too. My title is business systems analyst, but my working title is manager of BruinLearn and clinical IT. I’m in charge of instructional technology and IT infrastructure at the UCLA School of Law, for our instructional technology and our clinical programs. The School of Law runs several clinics that interface with clients and the public — housing justice, support for veterans navigating social services, and an immigrant family legal clinic that provides wraparound immigration services for families at LAUSD’s Robert F. Kennedy schools. My job is to support these clinics — make sure they have computers, printers, wireless, and access to the databases and tools they need to provide quality legal representation. It’s an important feature of how UCLA engages with the public in LA County. When the pandemic hit, I pivoted toward developing workshops and helping faculty with varying levels of digital literacy use remote instructional technology. I got credentialed in a lot of that technology through EDUCAUSE, and the work became specialized — instructional design, helping law faculty keep their classes Americans with Disabilities Act–compliant, things like that. Eventually UCLA wanted to spin that work off into a nonunion title. I was in a union position, and it wanted to roll up what I was doing into a new title and asked me to apply for it, calling it a promotion — but it would take me out of the bargaining unit. So it was a decision: keep progressing in my career, or go back to square one at the help desk. I chose my career, and I’ve been telling people I’ve regretted it ever since — I lost a lot of the contract protections, I lost out on raises that colleagues who stayed union caught up to within a year, and I pay more in health care premiums. I found that frustration in a lot of other folks too — this two-tier system where some unionized tech workers like Dan benefit from the collective bargaining agreement, but others like us, who have similar specialties and work side-by-side with them, have a much worse tier of benefits and more precarity. That’s not how you build long-term longevity or institutional memory. The stellar contract UPTE won really motivated us to think about what it would mean for all of us if we didn’t have to worry about the layoffs we keep reading about in the private sector — if we could get raises that keep up with inflation, pay less on health care, and actually have a voice in our workplace. That was a big galvanizing moment: we should come together and join a union that’s shown it’s out there fighting for tech workers. Aaron Petcoff I’m curious to learn more about the contract. Max, you’ve already hinted at the layoff protections it secured. What else did the contract win, when was it won, and what’d it take? Dan Russell The raises were pretty huge. A lot of folks were left behind by inflation during the pandemic. I think it was 22 percent over three years. Members save up to $300 a month on health insurance premiums. On layoffs: we got a commitment that before someone is laid off, if there’s a vacant position they’re qualified for, they’d be offered it. And we expanded the existing preferential rehire rights from your own campus to any UC campus. That’s especially appealing for people who can work remotely. It doesn’t matter if you’re at UC San Diego or UC Sacramento. That’s a big thing for tech workers. Aaron Petcoff Max, can you talk about how the contract and those protections played into some of your organizing conversations? Max Belasco Practically speaking, unionized and nonunion tech workers were treated very differently at UCLA, and I saw it when I was in the bargaining unit. The US Beats Europe — in Wasteful Health Care Spending

When arguing that the US is much richer than Western Europe or other peer nations, there is one class of people who point to measures of gross domestic product (addressed yesterday ) and then another, higher, class of people who point to something called actual individual consumption (AIC). This latter group seems to think that AIC paints a truer picture of the difference between these economies and that this picture is even rosier for the United States. This is also wrong. What Is AIC? Officially, AIC is defined as household and nonprofit consumption plus government services provided in-kind (e.g., health care and education). As a matter of accounting identity, AIC can also be calculated by taking GDP and subtracting from it capital investment, collective government consumption (e.g., police spending), and net exports. The graph below illustrates these two different ways of arriving at AIC in the United States. Already, you should probably be wondering whether this is actually a better measure than GDP. As constructed, the best way for a country to excel at AIC, at least in the short term, is to cut capital investment and run up a trade deficit. For a given amount of domestic production, these moves do generate extra household consumption but not in a way that is necessarily desirable, especially over the long term. With the exception of the UK, the United States does convert more of its GDP to AIC than other countries. The US has $73.90 of AIC for every $100 of GDP. The same number for Denmark is $58.60. In Germany, it’s $66.30. The reason the United States has a higher AIC/GDP ratio than its peer nations is almost entirely because the US has a large trade deficit while its peer nations typically have trade surpluses. So when someone switches the conversation from GDP measures to AIC measures, all they are really saying is that we should credit the US for importing more than it exports and ding other countries for doing the opposite. What the proper balance of trade ought to be is a worthwhile topic of debate, but covertly burying a pro-deficits position in a discussion about relative economic performance is a pretty silly way to have it. Nonhealth AIC Per Hour For the reasons stated above, the AIC measures favor the US more than the GDP measures do. One common graphic you see is AIC per capita, which I have reproduced below. As noted in my post about GDP , my initial objection to this is that it needs an hours-worked adjustment. Different countries select different labor/leisure trade-offs, and it is question-begging to rely on measures that implicitly treat nonwork time — vacations, holidays, retirement, student life, time with friends and loved ones — as worthless. When we plot AIC per hour worked, the gaps shrink, but the United States still appears to be ahead of the rest except for Norway (for this comparison, it is actually fair to mention Norway because the AIC measures subtract out its huge oil-derived trade surplus). Even after this hours-worked adjustment, this measure still has a glaring problem. One of the defining differences between the US and these other countries is that the United States has a wildly expensive and dysfunctional health care system while these other nations, all of which have universal health care and better health outcomes, do not. The extreme cost of the American health care system distorts its AIC figures, vastly overstating actual American consumption. To see the difference health care makes, in this next graph I have calculated each country’s AIC excluding health care expenditures. When we look at nonhealth AIC per hour worked, the US, once again, becomes totally unremarkable. Germany beats the United States on this measure, while France is only a few cents behind. Finally, it is always important to remember that these measures do not account for the inequality differences between these countries. AIC is distributed much more evenly in Western Europe than it is in the US. Due to diminishing marginal utility, this means that Western Europe gets more utility out of a given unit of consumption than the United States does. So when you put it all together, those pushing AIC are effectively arguing the US is better than its peer nations because: It has a big trade deficit. It favors labor in the labor/leisure trade-off. It spends a bunch of money on health care administration and health care provider rents. A dollar of consumption for the upper class is as valuable as a dollar of consumption for the lower and middle class. Perhaps some writers actually believe these things. I don’t. And I doubt these other countries find any of this very appealing. Do the French want to work a lot more? Do the Danes want American-style health care? Do the Germans want to run up a big trade deficit? If you think they are wrong about those things, then you should at least focus on the actual components of this divide — deficits, less time off, health care waste, inequality — and argue for them, not just post AIC graphs that obscure what is actually driving them. One Last Note on Health Care Spending Since I am already on the topic, I want to respond to a piece that has probably been sent to me one hundred times over the years. The piece, which was written pseudonymously, purports to show that US health care spending is actually not that anomalous once you compare it to American AIC rather than American GDP. The piece is long, but this is meant to be the big payoff graph. This analysis does not hold up. In the graph below, I plot per-capita health expenditure against AIC per capita for thirty-eight Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development countries in 2023. The United States is clearly well above the trend. But there is also something a bit circular about this graphic. Health care spending is plotted on the vertical axis, but is also contained in the horizontal axis because it is a component of AIC. This means that a country that spends excessively on health care sees its dot pushed up but also to the right, understating just how aberrant it really is. So for my final graph, I have plotted per-capita health expenditure against nonhealth AIC per capita for these same countries. In this graphic, the United States sits $4,365 above the trend line. Multiplied across the 2023 American population (336.8 million people), that’s $1.47 trillion of excess health care spending. If we fit a line without the US (so as to exclude the effect it has on the trend), we find the United States sitting $4,872 above that line, which is equal to $1.64 trillion of excess health care spending. As with my previous piece , I don’t think I am really saying anything particularly groundbreaking here. Americans and Western Europeans have similarly advanced economies and similar levels of hourly productivity. Americans cash out their productivity with more stuff, while Europeans cash out theirs with more time. What to Do When Your Union Contract Is Voted Down

フォルダ: 00_毎日確認 ／ 媒体: Jacobin ／ 著者: Chris Brooks ／ 公開日時: 2026-08-05T20:39:36+00:00

Like stumbling across a yeti or flying saucer, I was experiencing the seemingly impossible: a conversation between HR executives was warming the depths of my heart and giving me hope for the future. It was December 2022, and I was attending a convening of company executives and labor leaders hosted by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology’s Sloan School of Management. A line of corporate bigwigs from long-unionized Fortune 500 companies were heatedly voicing their exasperation over the new bargaining climate in the country. Apparently, all of these companies were suffering from the same affliction: tentative agreements were being voted down by union members. In some cases, contracts went down by large margins several times before finally passing. “It’s especially bad with the young workers, who have completely unreasonable expectations,” said one HR executive from a company that would go on to make nearly a billion dollars in net income that year. “They want the return of cost-of-living adjustments and pensions. It’s completely unrealistic with where the economy is today,” added another executive from a company with a market capitalization of more than $30 billion. “The union leaders are often just as upset as we are about how out of touch their membership is.” For years now, workers’ expectations have been rising across the country. As they should be. Corporate America rebounded after the Great Recession, and corporate profits have soared over the past decade, reaching an all-time high in the first quarter of 2026. In a recent article headlined “Corporate profits were already at historic highs. They shot even higher in Q1,” Yahoo Finance journalist Ben Werschkul writes that “US business profits are booming no matter how you measure it,” with corporate balance sheets remaining above historic norms for more than a decade. For the working class, the story is very different. Our standard of living has declined under the combined pressure of stagnant wages and rising prices. Income inequality in the United States has reached heights not seen since the Great Depression. According to the Wall Street Journal , inflation-adjusted corporate profits increased by 50 percent between the end of 2019 and early 2026. Hourly wages increased by a pathetic 3 percent over the same period. Workers everywhere should be demanding more — and that is especially true for unionized workers at highly profitable companies. I applaud workers who are making company executives wring their hands over their refusal to accept that status quo in bargaining. But just because we demand more does not mean we can easily win it. And we can’t afford to be naive about how asymmetric the power imbalance is between workers and employers, or how unprepared many unions are for a real fight. There are real dangers when a tentative agreement is voted down. Workers might go out on a long and painful strike only to return with an offer little better than the one they originally shot down. The experience can divide and demoralize both the membership and the bargaining team. Bargainers can feel abandoned by the members who elected them. Members can feel betrayed by the bargainers they elected. In the end, the union feels weaker, and the boss appears stronger. One of my proudest accomplishments as the United Auto Workers’ (UAW) top strategist was creating the Department of Bargaining Strategies. We didn’t know it at the time, but one of the new department’s top priorities would quickly become assisting locals whose contracts had been voted down. Time and again, we saw two interrelated problems driving no votes: local bargaining teams were not ambitious enough in bargaining, and they had not built the high-participation campaigns necessary to win more. Thankfully, the road to winning a stronger contract and building a more powerful union is also the road to winning enthusiastic and overwhelming contract ratifications. Through this new department — staffed with a combination of experienced bargainer-organizers hired from other unions and some of the most talented staff from the rank and file — we showed bargaining teams and members that a contract rejection does not have to become a source of division. It can instead become an opportunity to change how the union bargains and fights. By making negotiations more transparent and turning the entire membership into active players in the fight for a strong contract, a local can lay the foundation to win more while building greater trust between the bargaining team and the shop floor. The Three Axioms of Bargaining After more than a decade of bargaining, I have boiled down the basics to three axioms that I repeat over and over when training bargaining teams: Bargaining Axiom no. 1: Bargaining is when we go to the table with proposals to improve our working conditions, pay, and benefits, and management says no. Bargaining Axiom no. 2: What we win at the table reflects the power we build in the workplace. Our power is rooted in our unity and our ability to act collectively. Bargaining Axiom no. 3: Meaningful progress in negotiations most often happens when we create a crisis for the employer — typically through a credible strike threat. The first axiom is my definition of bargaining, but don’t take it too literally. Management rarely just says no at the bargaining table. They’re often more sophisticated than that. Instead, employers prefer to cultivate a bargaining environment of exhaustion and isolation. The company stretches out negotiations, warns that ambitious demands will cost jobs, and portrays a potential strike as reckless. At the same time, management tries to confine the bargaining process to a small room occupied by professional representatives. The more isolated the union’s bargaining committee becomes, the easier it is for management to persuade negotiators that nothing better is possible — and for members to conclude that their own negotiators failed them. The phrase “collective bargaining” is often treated as a legalistic term. It should instead describe how negotiations are actually conducted: collectively, with members shaping the goals, tracking the fight, and taking responsibility for building leverage. Members are far more likely to support an agreement when they helped decide what the union would fight for, understood how management responded, and participated in the pressure campaign that produced the final settlement. Under those conditions, ratification is the conclusion of a collective struggle rather than a blind vote on a document negotiated behind closed doors. Famed UAW President Walter Reuther once said that “power is the ability of a labor union like the UAW to make the most powerful corporation in the world — General Motors — say ‘yes’ when it wants to say ‘no.’” His point is well taken. Ask anyone who has served on a union negotiating team, whether at a zealously anti-union manufacturing plant in the South or a supposedly bighearted nonprofit hospital in New England, and they will tell you that making persuasive arguments to management won’t get you anywhere. Unless unions collectively build the capacity to make management’s refusal costly, the boss can and will keep saying no. And workers may ultimately blame their own negotiators and union leadership as a result. Member participation is not merely a good idea or the right thing to do. It is the source of our power. How to Build a Powerful Contract Campaign The second axiom of bargaining is that what we win at the table depends on the power we build in the workplace. That power is not built in a day and can’t be rushed in the final days before contract expiration. A strong contract is won by a membership that has been involved long before bargaining begins and continues to play a meaningful role until the agreement is ratified. We do this by organizing a contract campaign. A contract campaign is a collective action plan with a beginning, middle, and end, organized around specific demands that workers are trying to win. It has a target: someone who has the power to give us what we want. And it involves a series of escalating actions that progressively turn up the heat on that target and create the conditions for victory. Those actions should be timed to support what is happening in bargaining. Ideally, they build in intensity and participation, culminating at the union’s peak of strength. This is where the third axiom comes in. We build enough power to finally make the boss say yes when they want to keep saying no. We do that by creating a crisis for the employer, typically (though not always) by organizing a supermajority strike. Returning to the UAW’s “stand-up strike” as an example: this strike kept the union at the peak of its strength, using the credible threat of additional strikes over and over again to drive concessions out of Ford, General Motors, and Stellantis. It was a unique strategy that kept the companies guessing, captivated the media’s attention, and forced politicians to pick a side (resulting in then President Joe Biden walking a UAW picket line , a historic first in the United States). Ultimately, though, this strategy brought the companies to their knees because of the power of UAW members who were ready and willing to walk off the job at a moment’s notice — and proves the point that our power in negotiations rests on our power in the workplace. Through the UAW’s Department of Bargaining Strategies, we started training locals to think of the bargaining team and the shop floor like jumper cables: they only work when they are connected. That connection has to be maintained through representative structures, constant two-way communication, and workplace actions that have an effect that members can see at the bargaining table. We can build those jumper cables by democratizing the bargaining process. Don’t Keep Negotiations Behind Closed Doors Keeping members in the dark about bargaining is a recipe for disaster. Bargaining teams should never agree to ground rules that bar them from talking to the membership or the public about the status of negotiations. That language only helps management. Management knows everything that is happening in bargaining — they’re in the room! So secrecy simply denies workers on the shop floor the information they need to organize around what is happening in negotiations. Transparency strengthens trust between negotiators and the membership, and demonstrates to management that the bargaining team genuinely speaks with the authority of its coworkers. And it helps move members to take the actions necessary to win stronger contracts. Survey the Membership Before bargaining begins, use a survey to have members rank their top issues, define their priorities, and generate ideas for new proposals. Surveys should systematically gather input from a large majority of the bargaining unit. Bargainers should not just be collecting ideas for proposals but having their coworkers rank their priorities, so negotiators can accurately assess what matters the most. It is critical to know the difference between a demand that a supermajority of workers will strike over and another that might be popular but most aren’t willing to miss a paycheck over. Crucially, it’s important to not merely pass out surveys but also to use them as conversation starters. The bargaining survey is often the first opportunity to identify and recruit volunteers who can help write proposals, distribute future bargaining updates, and organize potential actions. Recruit Subject-Matter Experts The “official” bargaining team may be limited in size, but anyone and everyone can be involved in developing bargaining proposals. Recruit coworkers into working groups to hammer out proposals addressing issues that are widely and deeply felt in the workplace. Then have those workers present their proposals directly to management at the bargaining table. Is there a particular group of workers — cafeteria workers at the school, photographers at the magazine, lab techs in the hospital — who feel like they are always overlooked in bargaining? Have them collectively present their demands to management, and keep those demands as a highly visible union priority throughout negotiations. This not only can help restitch long-standing holes in the quilt of workplace solidarity but publicly reinforces to the boss that the workforce is united and invested in the bargaining process. Clearly Communicate Bargaining Goals Workers should know what the union is fighting for before negotiations begin. Some unions go so far as to distribute their bargaining proposals to members before presenting them to the company, then keep workers informed about how management responds. During the stand-up strike at the Big Three, UAW President Shawn Fain repeatedly used Facebook Live to explain the union’s demands and the companies’ responses. Members knew why they were being called on to strike and why they were being asked to stay out on the picket line. When management responded to the UAW’s initial proposals with an insulting first counter, Fain famously “filed” them in a garbage can on video. Members around the country began making memes of that garbage can. One local actually built him one and presented it to him for use in future negotiations. This is the kind of buy-in and member-initiated activity that good communication generates. Members come to understand that winning a strong contract does not fall solely on the bargaining team or union leadership. The fight belongs to all of us. Ironically, many longtime bargainers are afraid of publicizing the union’s demands and making ambitious proposals, because they fear their coworkers will vote it down for falling short of the union’s shared goals. This has it all backward. Our coworkers aren’t crazy or unreasonable. When workers are asked to make a decision without any insight into what was being demanded or how hard we had to fight to win it, it makes sense that they would make decisions based on the information they do have: comparing the employer’s massive profits and rising executive salaries against their own paltry wages and benefits. By that logic, it makes total sense to vote down every contract — exactly the situation I was happily listening to company executives complain about at the Sloan School event. The only way to cultivate mature, strategic judgment is through the kind of deep participation that a contract campaign with clear communication provides. And not for nothing, the fear of fighting for more has produced a vicious downward spiral of concessions in many unions. The only way we will raise standards in our industries is by abandoning the old philosophy of “aim low and settle lower” for a new approach: aim high, unite, and fight like hell to get everything we can. Send an Energizing Update After Every Session Bargaining updates — often written distributed through emails and flyers — should be short, informative, agitational, and persuasive. The purpose is not merely to report on a specific article that was tentatively agreed to or describe the boss’s latest hijinks at the table. Every update should help members understand what is at stake and what they can do next. It should be a call to action. Use Bargaining Caucus Time to Communicate With Coworkers Bargaining often involves a lot of downtime. Put it to use. Identify the key message members need to hear, create a phone tree, and use caucus time to call them. Or send out mass texts. Or write that shop paper to send out to everyone. Just make sure these communications are a two-way street. They should allow the bargaining team to gather feedback and help their coworkers understand the relationship between the actions they are taking on the shop floor and the company’s reactions at the table. Bring Members to Bargaining Allowing members to attend negotiations is often called “open bargaining,” an increasingly popular approach to negotiating a contract. Open bargaining shows management that the union is united, it shows members that their union has nothing to hide, and it provides a valuable learning experience for workers by giving them the opportunity to hear firsthand what management thinks of their own employees — and what they think our labor is worth. When possible, bargain at the workplace and invite members to attend before or after their shifts or during lunch breaks. This allows workers to move in and out of the room without losing an entire workday. Members who attend should follow a common set of rules: keep a straight face, do not speak unless it is part of a prepared plan, and follow up with negotiators afterward with questions. Pack Bargaining at Pivotal Moments Even when bargaining is always open, there may be dozens of sessions in a typical campaign. The union should identify pivotal moments when it makes sense to mobilize their coworkers in a show of force. Pack the first bargaining session . Turn the opening session into a bargaining kickoff. Systematically gather commitments to attend from the entire bargaining unit. Use the opening of negotiations for the bargaining team to explain the union’s priorities and why they matter. This demonstrates that the membership is united behind its goals and starts to signal how far everyone is willing to go to win them. Pack bargaining when members present an important proposal . Prepare workers who care deeply about a particular issue to speak at the table, and mobilize their coworkers to attend in support. This shows management that the proposal is not the personal project of one negotiator but a widely shared demand. Pack bargaining when management presents an important counterproposal . Do not allow management to insult only the bargaining team when it rejects workplace improvements or meaningful raises. Give the boss an audience. Force management to look a sea of their employees in the face while explaining why they are not worth what they are owed. What if management refuses to let members attend ? How we bargain is as much a reflection of our power as what we bargain. I’ve seen employers refuse to attend sessions when large numbers of employees were present, then try to turn the membership against their own negotiators by claiming the company would discuss wages if only the bargaining team stopped “irresponsibly” insisting that coworkers be allowed in the room. Unions have the legal right to decide who serves on their bargaining team — even if that means the entire bargaining unit. But anyone waiting for the National Labor Relations Board to enforce that right anytime soon is likely out of luck. If management refuses to enter the room when employees are present, then how we bargain becomes the first test of the union’s strength. Turn that refusal into an organizing opportunity. Bargaining on our terms — at the worksite, open to members, and without unnecessary secrecy — may itself require escalating action. The union has to unite everyone around that fight and keep them moving forward together. When You’re Stuck, Talk to Your Coworkers Bargaining is hard. We cannot always anticipate every trick management will use to confuse, divide, and demoralize us. Whenever negotiations get stuck, put your trust in your coworkers by holding one-on-one conversations, organizing department meetings, or hosting a town hall. Our collective intelligence and creativity are greater than those of any single individual. Collective problems require collective solutions. Strength in Solidarity A tentative agreement is most likely to be voted down when members feel disconnected from the bargaining process, distrust the people negotiating on their behalf, or believe the union settled for less without building the power necessary to win more. The answer is not to lower workers’ expectations. It is to organize those expectations into collective power. Through surveys and conversations, workers can develop proposals that address the most widely and deeply felt issues in the workplace. But winning those demands requires much more than strong arguments or powerful worker testimonies. We have to fight. And the bigger the demands, the bigger the fight, and the more powerful our union needs to be. That’s why contract campaigns are essential not only to winning strong agreements but to building stronger unions. When members help establish the demands, understand the obstacles, participate in the campaign, and experience the fight together, the final ratification vote is not treated like a referendum on how well the bargaining team did. It becomes the culmination of a struggle the membership helped lead.

Every day, millions of Americans get out of bed, tune into the news, and ask themselves a simple question: How the hell did the Republican Party become so spectacularly unhinged? The GOP is fully off the rails, unbothered by any seeming concern for basic political economic stability, the empirical reality of things we can see with our own eyes, and the fundamentals of human decency. Donald Trump has remade the Republican Party in his image over the past decade, creating a kind of personalist strongman rule that’s without exact precedent in American history. But Paul Heideman argues that the sorry, scary state of today’s Republican Party cannot be solely attributed to one strange, horrible man. Rather, it’s the product of a range of features of the US political system, some long-standing, others more recent. Heideman’s book Rogue Elephant: How Republicans Went from the Party of Business to the Party of Chaos argues that the United States’ weak party system played a big role in creating the conditions for today’s derangement and the specific forms that derangement has taken. For Jacobin Radio ’s podcast The Dig , guest host and Jacobin editor Micah Uetricht sat down with Paul Heideman to discuss why business could destroy Joseph McCarthy but not Trump, the service-party model that turned both parties into fundraising networks, Newt Gingrich’s remaking of the House around money, big tobacco’s fingerprints on the Bill Clinton impeachment, the Tea Party revolt against the bailout, and why the coming fight to succeed Trump is unlikely to leave the party any less chaotic. The GOP Strength Hidden in the Chaos Micah Uetricht One of the key paradoxes of the situation in the Republican Party today seems to be, as your book has in its subtitle, how Republicans went from the party of business to the party of chaos. There is indeed much chaos within the Republican Party. We see it on a daily basis, and you often see a kind of gleeful response from liberals and leftists witnessing that chaos, the kind of joy at watching this party that has wreaked so many horrors at home and abroad be wracked by all of this internal fighting, because they assume this portends poorly for the party’s fortunes in the future. Paul Heideman There are a couple aspects to that. The internal divisions in the party, I think, have been quite consequential over the last decade and a half, let’s say. Micah Uetricht So the two main factors that your book covers in charting the breakdown of the Republican Party are the weakening of the party in the context of an already weak party system and the disorganization of American capital that you were just speaking to. What are stronger political parties elsewhere around the world capable of doing that US parties are not? Paul Heideman Probably the most concrete way to think about this is that, in the United States, parties don’t directly control who appears on their ballot line. And that’s maybe the main function of political parties, to produce candidates to run for office. And so the fact that in the United States that process is done through this primary system means that something like Zohran Mamdani’s win can happen even though most of Democratic Party officialdom in New York absolutely did not want that to happen. Similarly, with Trump’s victory in 2016, the Republican Party did not want Trump to win, but because of the institutional setup of American politics, they were really powerless to prevent it. That’s a fundamental dimension of party weakness in the United States. If you compare it with the Labour Party in the UK, the Labour Party as an institution decides who is going to run in what constituency, who’s going to stand where, right? It’s impossible for someone to run on the Labour Party ballot against the will of the Labour Party leadership. There are elections within the Labour Party for leadership, but once the leadership is in place, they ultimately control who runs where. The party gets to decide. In the United States, the party does not decide. And really it’s mandated by state law in most of the United States that, for state-level positions, the parties have to hold primaries. And that means that the party as an institution doesn’t get to control this most basic function of deciding who will represent it in an election. That’s part of why parties are weaker, and it means that parties are vulnerable to insurgencies in a way that parties elsewhere in the world are just much, much less vulnerable to. Micah Uetricht To continue with the Labour Party example, obviously you had Jeremy Corbyn standing for prime minister for the party. Paul Heideman Yeah, exactly right. Even if they had wanted to completely purge Sanders supporters from the party, they had no way to do it, because they can’t stop someone from running on their ballot line. And if that person does a good enough job of organizing, they can win. So that renders American parties more gelatinous, I think you could say, than parties elsewhere. The borders are not hard. They’re always vulnerable to people entering, mounting different kinds of campaigns, and I think that’s really key to what’s happened in the Republican Party over the last few decades as well: these insurgents coming up in the party challenging the leadership, and the leadership just having no kind of organizational resources in the party itself that can stop them. The American Party Insurgent Strategy Micah Uetricht You’re someone who’s been on the Left for a long time and has participated in these debates about what the Left should be doing within the Democratic Party. Is it wise for us to be doing that or not? Those debates seem more settled at this point. The Left has now settled on this kind of insurgent strategy within the party that remains independent from the party and its leadership but is mostly operating within the party. And I wonder if this porousness that you’re talking about that American political parties in general have is precisely why that is the strategy that makes sense in this moment. Paul Heideman I think that’s absolutely right, and I think those two facts are deeply related. The primary system in the United States creates these weak parties, and at the same time it creates a strong party system in which the two parties are extraordinarily secure in their places. In the UK, the party system is kind of disintegrating right now. That’s not happening at all in the United States despite many predictions that something like that would come to pass. One reason for that is simply that, if you want to challenge the Democratic Party leadership, you have a choice: Do you do that as a third party, or do you do that within the party? Which one looks like it’s going to have more of a route to success? Over the last decade, the verdict is pretty clear that you can have a tremendous amount of success actually challenging the party from within. Insurgents in the Republican Party have faced the same choice and have come to a similar conclusion for most of the last century: if you want to pull the Republican Party further right, the way to do it is to run in the Republican primary. If you want a new right-wing pole in American politics, the way to do it is to run in Republican Party primaries and try and pull that party further right. The other part of the equation is that the United States has all kinds of laws that make it almost impossible to run as a third party. So, for example, restrictions on ballot access: in the United States, if you want to get on the ballot, in some places you have to collect signatures equal to around 5 percent of the electorate in a region. In most other democratic countries, those kinds of laws are considered authoritarian. They are explicitly banned by the European Union. So you have these ballot access laws, and then the fact that elections in the United States are privately financed means that if you want to raise money to run for office, and you want to do that as a third-party candidate, good luck! The resources that are available to someone running in a Democratic primary in terms of fundraising and all of that kind of stuff are absolutely massive — just things like ActBlue, access to fundraising platforms like that. The kinds of instruments that allow insurgent candidates to overcome the advantages of big money are themselves partially linked to running within Democratic Party primaries. I do think that the weakness of American parties and the corresponding strength of the American party system is a major influence on what has led the American left over the last decade to, as you say, largely choose the path of running insurgent campaigns in the Democratic Party. The Rise of the Modern Party System Micah Uetricht Another element of this weak party system and the increasing weakness of both parties is actually the process of parties becoming ideologically coherent in the first place, which actually was not the case for the majority of American political history. That has to do with party realignment, which really takes off in the New Deal era. Can you talk about the development of ideological coherency within the Republican and Democratic parties and why that matters for understanding the Republican Party today? Paul Heideman For most of American history, American political parties have not really been these cohesive organizations promoting a given ideological worldview. Instead, they’ve been coalitions of different regional elites who have come together out of common interest and to try and find a common program but have not been linked to a more all-encompassing worldview of any sort. A great exception to this is, of course, the Republican Party of the Civil War era, which was united by this ideological antislavery, free labor vision. Though after the party abandons Reconstruction, they move away from that and you get a return to these more nonideological politics. And by 1900 or so, a lot of observers are looking at American politics and being like, “The parties just don’t really believe in anything.” Max Weber, the Prussian social scientist, is watching from across the Atlantic, and he’s like, “In the United States, the parties are just gangs of office seekers. They’re just rival gangs. It’s not like they’re united by different interpretations of the Constitution, grand ideological visions, or anything like that.” And this pattern really continues up to the New Deal. At the same time, parties were incredibly organizationally strong for most of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Voting in the late nineteenth century was something of a combination between Mardi Gras and a gang fight where the parties organized these giant parades to march people down to the polls. And participation rates were north of 70 or 80 percent in elections in these years. So the parties were organizationally extremely strong, even as they were ideologically indistinct. The New Deal begins a process of transformation of this when you get this social democratic pole created in American politics. At the same time, that was congenitally deformed because the Democrats, at the same time that they’re the party of social democracy in the United States, and socialists in Europe are looking at the New Deal being like, “this is what we should do,” the party is also the party of Jim Crow. So it becomes increasingly clear over the course of the 1930s that the Democratic Party has become an unstable amalgam of the most progressive people in the country, these northern liberals, the most left-wing in mainstream politics at least, and the most reactionary: the Dixiecrats in the Jim Crow South. And so the next thirty years are, within the Democratic Party, an attempt to resolve that coalition or that contradiction, or to preserve it and to stabilize it. And that basically ends in the 1960s with the passage of the Voting Rights Act, which demolishes Jim Crow in the South, and, on the other hand, begins this process of realignment of Southern white voters. And there’s a corresponding process happening in the Republican Party at this time, too. There was a large branch of the Republican Party that was really quite liberal in some ways, going back to Theodore Roosevelt and the Bull Moose Progressives, that whole wing of the party. So western Republicans in the 1910s and ’20s were often quite progressive. And going forward, you have people like Earl Warren, who was governor of California and then on the Warren Court. He was a Republican governor. People like Thomas Dewey, the governor of New York, who are really these liberal Republicans who attached themselves to New Deal institutions and often said, “Look, we don’t want a big expansion of the New Deal, but we also don’t want to roll it back.” And from the 1930s to the 1960s, this is the fight going on in the Republican Party, between “Are we the party of rolling back the New Deal?” or “Are we the party of just stopping further growth?” And in some ways, Barry Goldwater’s victory in the 1960s marks the victory of the rollback faction. Richard Nixon is something of an interregnum there, but that faction really wins, and that victory is, of course, consolidated when Ronald Reagan wins the presidency in the 1980s. In 1932, about 70 percent of black Americans voted for Herbert Hoover. Why? Because he was a Republican. It’s the party of Abraham Lincoln. It’s who black Americans in the North, where black Americans could vote, had voted for since the Civil War. In 1936, two-thirds of black Americans voted for Franklin Delano Roosevelt. That’s one of the biggest shifts in voting in American history. And it happens in four years, extraordinarily quickly. Why? Because the New Deal did a huge amount for black Americans. The Two Parties Consolidate Micah Uetricht How does this establishment of coherency of the two parties play out once the process of realignment is completed at the end of the 1960s? And what does that have to do with the party weakness that you write about so much in the book that the 1970s are a key period for? Paul Heideman So, in both parties, there are people who political scientist Sam Rosenfeld calls the polarizers , the people who say, “No, no, no, we want the parties to be coherent. This is the fight we’re fighting.” And that’s happening in both parties in different ways. In the Democratic Party, a huge battle is the battle against seniority in Congress, because these Southern Democrats preside over one-party dictatorships, and so they have the most seniority, because they’re basically never voted out of office. The Northern liberals who want to pass civil rights legislation realize that if they want to do so, they have to get the Dixiecrats out of leadership. And to do that, they have to dethrone seniority. This was a big battle in the 1950s and ’60s, to get rid of the seniority principle, and it was replaced with a stronger congressional leadership. Now committee membership is based on what the congressional leadership decides, right? What the majority leader or the minority leader really wants. And you can say this is strengthening that party leadership, and that’s how it was perceived at the time. But in the 1970s, this really interacts with an absolutely staggering rise in campaign costs, as political campaigns really start to embrace television and radio advertising as a primary means of campaigning. Before the 1970s, the most important input in campaigns is labor. It’s volunteers and party workers. That’s who wins campaigns. In the 1970s, it shifts to advertising, and that means a huge, huge increase in costs. Now the most important input is money. This combines with this more open approach to selecting party leadership to create a situation that really consolidates by the 1990s. Gingrich is the one who really pioneers this, in making it so that whoever raises the most money is now the de facto leader of the party. Nancy Pelosi’s reign as head of House Democrats was really undergirded by her incredible success as a fundraiser. She raised and redistributed throughout the party massive, massive amounts of money. And so this effort to produce a more coherent party by dethroning seniority and getting rid of the Dixiecrats interacted with the changed campaign finance environment to create a situation where now, actually, money just rules the day in the party. And actually, the party as an autonomous institution able to make decisions of its own has been diminished. Because now it’s just, well, whatever the funders decide is what’s going to happen. Who they’re giving money to is going to be the leader. In the Republican Party, you get a very different but analogous development in that, in the wake of Goldwater’s defeat, there was a huge fight in the party over which direction it would go. And part of the way this played out was over who would be head of the Republican National Committee. And they basically nominated a compromise candidate whose outlook was, “Look, the job of the party is not to take a side in ideological disputes within the party.” It’s to provide services to whomever the candidate would ultimately be. And this became known as the service model of politics. And so this nomination comes as a result of a stalemate in the party. Goldwater’s been defeated. There are all of these Republican liberals and moderates who want to get rid of Goldwater but aren’t quite strong enough to push the Right all the way out of the party. And so you get this service party conception, which again weakens the party. Now the party doesn’t decide questions of policy, of politics, anything like that. The party only provides services to candidates. And then the Democrats actually start copying this from the Republican Party, and both parties really adopt this service party model, which is really the party model that continues to reign to this day, where parties are networks of campaign service providers and channels through which money flows. And that’s what the party as an institution really is. It’s not making any kind of decisions about policy and politics. Parties as Bit Players Micah Uetricht You have a subheading in one of your chapters called “Parties as Bit Players,” which is a sort of astonishing phrase to think about. The idea that parties themselves matter less and less at this moment is probably counterintuitive for many people. But this is in many ways a result of the flood of big money into politics, right? Most people listening to this podcast understand the distorting effect of big money in politics at the most basic level, that big money and special interests can throw their money around at will to get their will enacted through legislation and enforcement, or lack thereof, instead of what benefits the common good. And that’s true, but also the story you’re telling here is one in which big-money institutions like super PACs essentially come to supplant the party itself. Then the party leaderships are increasingly those who can wrangle the most money, and those who are able to fundraise significant amounts are those who get leadership positions in the parties. And even disruptive candidates like Trump, who are able to raise large amounts of money, can essentially flip the bird to party elites. Is that correct? Paul Heideman Yes, and the 2024 election is actually a perfect example of this. The 2024 election was the first time a major party’s campaign has been primarily funded by super PACs rather than through the candidate committee that is registered with the Federal Election Commission and associated with the party. Most of Trump’s money didn’t flow through his official candidate committee. It flowed through super PACs like Elon Musk’s super PAC or various other groups. And so most of the money isn’t even going through the party now. Now most of the money is going outside of the party as well. And so on questions about campaign strategy, the party increasingly doesn’t have a major say over it. Elon Musk spends $250 million; he can decide what the main public-relations, advertising, and geographic focus of the campaign is going to be. So yeah, I wrote that section before the election, but parties are increasingly becoming bit players. Why American Capital Never Had to Organize Micah Uetricht Let’s talk about the disorganization of big business. Given the power of the American capitalist class as a whole, the record levels of economic inequality, the desiccation of the American welfare state, the attacks on the labor movement that have basically left it on life support, all the things that we know about the state of corporate power and working-class weakness in the United States today, I always assumed that it was a given that capital would be able to organize and advocate on its own behalf. But this is actually, in your telling, not the case throughout postwar American history up to the present. In fact, there are only a few moments in that period, most notably in the 1970s, in which capital does actually organize to defend and advocate for itself as a class, and you say that that has always been in response to worker organizing. So lay out this seemingly paradoxical situation of unprecedented corporate power and economic inequality with weak capitalist organizing. Paul Heideman I think the way to think about it is that it is precisely because capital has been so dominant in American history that it hasn’t needed to be as organized as capital elsewhere. In the United States, as listeners will know, movements of the Left have generally been weaker, unions have been weaker and smaller than elsewhere. The United States has never produced an electorally viable socialist party, unlike most of the rest of Western Europe. All of that has meant that capital in the United States has not faced the same scale of challenges as elsewhere, and, because of that, capital hasn’t really needed to organize on the same scale as elsewhere. They’ve — Micah Uetricht Had it too easy. Paul Heideman They’ve had it too easy. Exactly. They haven’t been disciplined. It’s a little bit like a monopolist in the market. Monopolists don’t face the discipline of competition, and so they can afford to be wasteful in various ways and slothful. American capital is much the same. They don’t face the political competition of a highly organized and combative working class outside of a few really specific moments. And so going back to the early twentieth century, the weakness of American labor has meant that capital can get what it wants for the most part without really needing to be highly organized. They can defeat their workers, they can get the policy they want without these big class-wide organizations of the kind that you really see emerging in force in Western Europe after World War II, when you have socialist parties or labor parties at least on the verge of power in much of Western Europe. You have these labor movements that are much, much bigger than in the United States. In those countries, capital needed to figure out what its class-wide demands for contesting with labor as a class were going to be. Not what individual businesses might want here or there, but how they can organize the class as a whole to make sure that they win what were almost existential battles at the time. In the United States, capital has really not faced those kinds of stakes most of the time, and so the organization of capital has correspondingly been more sectional and partial. Businesses has often been more focused on their narrow interests as individual firms or industries rather than as a class. They’ve been focused on what they can get for themselves, not what they can all agree is needed vis-à-vis labor. Micah Uetricht You know, in publishing, in Jacobin , I’m often left feeling like a particularly vulgar Marxist most of the time, because the conclusion to many things is like, “Yeah, because we don’t have an organized working class, this is why XYZ bad thing is happening.” And so when I got to this part of the book, I was like, oh, yet again . . . We actually can blame that weakness on the weakness of organized labor. Just chalk another thing up to the weakness of organized labor. Paul Heideman If you wanted to boil the book down to the essence, it would be that American exceptionalism and a weak American working class are ultimately responsible for the insanity of the Republican Party today. Micah Uetricht Right. What does this lack of capitalist organizing as a class mean for American capitalism in the longer term? Surely this must have some negative effect for capitalist profit-making, for the shape of the economy as a whole. If other countries have more coherent business-class mobilization in defense of their interests, what are they getting for their capitalists that American capitalists are not getting because they’re not mobilizing in that same way? Paul Heideman I think it’s helpful to think about the post–World War II United States to understand this. In the postwar United States, there were a number of prominent organizations of American business. There was the Committee for Economic Development, which I mentioned earlier. There was the National Association of Manufacturers and the Chamber of Commerce. And essentially, the Committee for Economic Development was an organization of some of the CEOs of a lot of the biggest, most profitable corporations in the United States, the really big multinational companies that were emerging or reaching new heights after World War II. And these were companies that thought, “Okay, the New Deal has been established. We don’t want it to go further, but we also don’t think it’s worth the fight of trying to roll it back. We think that could be chaotic, messy, and really could cost us more than it’s worth. So we really don’t want that.” So they represented the moderate wing of capital. The Chamber of Commerce and the National Association of Manufacturers were interlinked with the John Birch Society in these years, and they were the ones who were like, “No, the New Deal is communism. We need to roll it back. And in fact, Committee for Economic Development, you guys are probably communists too if you don’t want to roll it back.” And the Committee for Economic Development was very politically powerful in these years. So again, it’s not that capital lacked influence. It’s just that that influence was not representing capital as a class, but was mostly representing the biggest firms, the most powerful firms, and what they wanted. In Europe, capital is organized, but their power is more constrained, because they’re contending with a very powerful labor movement, with very powerful electoral socialist movements. And so they have to be organized to kind of tread water, in some sense. In the United States, they’re so completely dominant that they’re able to get along quite well without any kind of class-wide perspective, and sometimes that means that some small capitalists are losers. So in the post–World War II era, when free trade is really in the ascendance in the United States and big capital loves free trade, well, a lot of small and medium-sized capital loses out from that. And that was actually a major force in the Goldwater campaign. The Goldwater campaign represented an eruption of protectionism from small and medium-sized businesses who were defecting from the free trade consensus. So in that sense, the fact that there’s not a class-wide perspective within capital means that the less powerful bits of capital might get the shaft a little harder here than elsewhere, because there’s not a class-wide movement trying to create a consensual political agenda. Instead the big capitalists are often able to set the agenda. Gingrich Builds the Money Machine Micah Uetricht You say in the book that Newt Gingrich’s Republican revolution was in many ways actually more important than the party’s transformation under Ronald Reagan.
